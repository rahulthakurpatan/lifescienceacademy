1. Why cells need catalysts

A living cell must break down nutrients, copy DNA, build membranes and respond to environmental changes at temperatures compatible with life. Many reactions are thermodynamically possible but too slow to support growth. A peptide bond, for example, can undergo hydrolysis, yet spontaneous hydrolysis under ordinary cellular conditions is extremely slow. A protease makes the same net chemical transformation rapid and selective. The distinction between possibility and speed is the foundation of enzymology.

The preceding nodes established three ideas: molecules encounter activation barriers; equilibrium and reaction rate are separate; folded proteins arrange side chains into functional three-dimensional structures. Together these explain an enzyme: a biological catalyst that creates a favourable chemical pathway for a particular reaction.

An enzyme is regenerated over the complete catalytic cycle. This does not mean it is unchanged at every instant: it can bind substrate, change conformation, gain or lose protons, transiently form a covalent bond or alter oxidation state. Most enzymes are proteins, but catalytic RNAs (ribozymes) are exceptions. A catalyst increases the rate without changing the equilibrium constant. Continuing substrate input, product removal or coupling to a favourable process can maintain net cellular flux.

From fermentation to molecular mechanisms

In 1894 Emil Fischer proposed the lock-and-key model of enzyme specificity. In 1897 Eduard Buchner demonstrated fermentation in cell-free yeast extracts, establishing that intact cells were not required for every biochemical transformation. James Sumner crystallised urease in 1926, supporting the protein nature of enzymes. Daniel Koshland proposed induced fit in 1958, emphasising conformational flexibility. Discovery of catalytic RNA in the early 1980s showed that catalysis is not exclusive to proteins. The modern picture unites chemical recognition, molecular dynamics and preferential stabilisation of reactive configurations.

Learning route

Classify the reaction, understand substrate recognition, identify the energy barrier, then examine cofactors, catalytic strategies and actual mechanisms. Quantitative saturation kinetics will follow in 1.D.02.

2. The seven enzyme classes

Names such as urease and lactate dehydrogenase describe substrates or reactions; names such as trypsin are historical. The International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology uses Enzyme Commission numbers to classify the overall chemical transformation rather than protein shape or ancestry. An EC identifier has four numbers: principal class, subclass, sub-subclass and individual entry. Hexokinase, EC 2.7.1.1, is a transferase transferring a phosphoryl group to an alcohol acceptor.

Older textbooks list six classes because translocases were added later. Fumarase uses water but is a lyase because it catalyses addition across a double bond, not hydrolytic cleavage. ATP usage alone does not define a ligase: a kinase transfers phosphate, while a pump transports solutes. Synthetase generally refers to energy-dependent joining, whereas synthase is less restrictive. ATP synthase is classified among translocases.

Unrelated proteins can catalyse the same reaction. Isozymes are molecularly distinct forms catalysing the same overall reaction; they may have different tissue distribution or regulation. Detailed regulation belongs to 1.D.05.

3. Active sites and the catalytic cycle

Substrate diffuses toward the enzyme, binds to form ES, undergoes one or more chemical steps, forms an enzyme–product complex and finally leaves. Binding, chemistry and release are different events; any may limit overall turnover. The simplest representation E + S ⇌ ES → EP ⇌ E + P hides potentially many intermediates, but it provides a useful framework.

A small region built from the whole protein

An active site is a three-dimensional pocket, cleft or interface. Residues separated by hundreds of positions in the primary sequence may meet when the protein folds. Binding residues recognise shape, charge, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, and hydrophobic surfaces. Catalytic residues supply nucleophiles, proton donors or acceptors, metal ligands and electrostatic stabilisation. One residue may fulfil more than one role. The remaining protein supports geometry, movement, and communication between binding sites.

An enzyme may have one active site per subunit, fewer sites than subunits, or multiple distinct sites. A mutation at the catalytic centre may abolish turnover without eliminating binding. A distant mutation may reduce activity indirectly through unfolding. Hence low activity alone cannot identify a catalytic residue.

The active-site microenvironment

Nearby charges, hydrogen bonds, limited solvent access and desolvation shift side-chain pKₐ values. Histidine has a free side-chain pKₐ near 6, making proton donation and acceptance plausible near physiological pH, but its actual pKₐ in an enzyme is context-dependent. Serine and cysteine can be nucleophiles, lysine can form transient imines, carboxylates can act as acids, bases or nucleophiles, and arginine can stabilise negative charge. A residue's role must be inferred from the actual mechanism rather than memorised from its name.

4. Specificity and conformational change

A substrate must not merely attach: it must bind with reactive atoms appropriately aligned. The wrong stereoisomer may bind weakly or bind in a nonproductive orientation. Substrate recognition and chemical selectivity are thus related but distinct.

Lock and key, induced fit, conformational selection

The lock-and-key model explains pre-existing complementarity, but a protein is not a rigid object. In induced fit, binding promotes a conformational rearrangement. Hexokinase closes around glucose, helping organise phosphoryl transfer and limiting competing water access. In conformational selection, the free enzyme samples multiple conformations and substrate preferentially binds a compatible state. Subsequent rearrangement can further optimise that state. Both dynamic processes can coexist.

Specificity need not be absolute. Enzyme promiscuity describes secondary substrate or reaction activities, often much weaker than the physiological activity, which can provide a basis for evolution. High binding affinity does not guarantee high turnover: an excessively stable ES ground state can become an energetic trap unless the transition state is preferentially stabilised.

5. Reaction free energy and activation free energy

A negative reaction free energy does not imply a fast reaction. The reactants must pass through a transition state, a fleeting high-free-energy configuration with bonds partly formed or broken. An intermediate is different: it occupies a local energy minimum and can sometimes be detected or trapped. A catalyst supplies an alternate pathway whose relevant activation barriers are lower.

ΔG‡ denotes activation free energy, measured from a relevant starting state to a transition state. ΔG denotes the difference between products and reactants. For an enzyme-bound reaction step, the reference may be ES; for an overall pathway comparison the reference can be free enzyme and substrate. These references must not be mixed without accounting for binding free energy.

How barrier changes translate into rate changes

For an elementary reaction, transition-state theory gives approximately k = κ(kBT/h)e−ΔG‡/RT. Here κ is a transmission factor, kB is the Boltzmann constant, h is Planck's constant, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. For comparable prefactors the rate ratio is approximately exp[(ΔG‡uncat − ΔG‡cat)/RT].

Worked example: At 298 K, RT = 2.478 kJ mol⁻¹. Reducing a barrier by 34.2 kJ mol⁻¹ gives exp(34.2/2.478) ≈ 9.9 × 10⁵, about one millionfold enhancement. About 5.71 kJ mol⁻¹ corresponds to a tenfold change at this temperature. This illustrative comparison is not automatically the Michaelis–Menten parameter kcat/Km.

ΔG°′ = −RT ln K′eq expresses biochemical standard-state thermodynamics. Enzymes do not alter ΔG°′ or K′eq. They accelerate approach to equilibrium from either side, consistent with microscopic reversibility. A reaction with positive actual ΔG requires changed concentrations or coupling to a favourable reaction for sustained forward flux.

Exam trap

A catalyst lowers barriers, not the free-energy difference between the starting and ending states. Adding extra enzyme may change how quickly equilibrium is reached, not where it lies.

6. Preferential transition-state stabilisation

Pauling's transition-state concept explains why simple binding is insufficient. Consider hydrolysis of an ester or peptide carbonyl. Nucleophilic attack produces a tetrahedral configuration and developing negative charge on oxygen. A catalytic pocket can form hydrogen bonds that stabilise this configuration more strongly than the ordinary planar carbonyl. The energy difference between ES and the transition state decreases.

Binding energy can help compensate for loss of translational and rotational freedom, substrate distortion, and desolvation. The enzyme may already provide a preorganised electrostatic environment so that fewer solvent molecules need to rearrange. These contributions are intertwined and should not be treated as independent universal numbers.

A transition-state analogue is a stable molecule resembling important aspects of a reaction-state geometry or charge distribution. It may bind tightly and inhibit the enzyme. Boronate-based serine-protease inhibitors, for example, mimic tetrahedral features. Such an analogue is not a genuine transition state; the latter is a fleeting barrier configuration. Not all strongly binding inhibitors resemble transition states. Catalytic antibodies raised against analogues demonstrate the principle, although their catalytic performance often differs from that of natural enzymes.

Think mechanistically

If a mutation removes a hydrogen bond that stabilises the transition state more than ES, activation free energy rises and rate decreases. Substrate binding could remain almost normal.

7. Apoenzymes, holoenzymes and cofactors

Protein side chains provide many chemical functions but do not cover all reactions. Enzymes recruit inorganic ions or organic molecules to transfer electrons, carbon groups, amino groups or activated intermediates. A cofactor is a nonprotein component required for activity. An apoenzyme is the protein portion without a required cofactor; a holoenzyme contains the required component or components and is catalytically competent when conditions are suitable.

Organic cofactors are often called coenzymes. A cosubstrate binds, undergoes a chemical change and leaves; a separate reaction regenerates it. A prosthetic group is tightly associated, sometimes covalently attached. These terms describe how the molecule participates, not rigid categories based on its chemical identity. FAD, for instance, is tightly associated in many enzymes, but its binding mode varies.

Metal binding and vitamin dependence

A metalloenzyme commonly contains a relatively tightly bound metal, while metal-activated enzymes may associate reversibly with metal ions or metal–substrate complexes. Carbonic anhydrase contains zinc; many kinases use MgATP. The distinction is operational. A substitution can affect ionic radius, coordination geometry, acidity or redox potential.

Many B vitamins are precursors of coenzymes, so deficiency may impair several pathways. Not all vitamins are coenzymes, and not all coenzymes derive from vitamins. A coenzyme may be chemically transformed during one enzyme reaction and regenerated by a different enzyme; NAD⁺ regeneration in fermentation allows continued glycolysis. Adding cofactor may rescue intact apoenzyme but cannot be expected to repair an unfolded protein or missing catalytic residue.

8. Six connected catalytic strategies

Proximity and orientation

Random collisions are often unproductive. Binding two reactants near each other with suitable alignment increases effective local concentration and reduces the entropic cost of assembling a reactive configuration. Mere proximity does not guarantee catalysis: the orbital geometry and chemical environment must also be appropriate.

General acid–base catalysis

A general base accepts a proton to activate a nucleophile; a general acid donates a proton to stabilise a leaving group or intermediate. Histidine can alternate roles within one cycle. In specific acid–base catalysis the relevant proton donors or acceptors are hydronium or hydroxide in solution, whereas general catalysis involves other proton donors or acceptors. A buffer-concentration dependence at fixed pH may indicate general acid–base chemistry, provided ionic strength and binding effects are controlled.

Covalent catalysis

A nucleophilic enzyme group forms a temporary bond to substrate, creating an alternate pathway. Serine, cysteine and lysine are common participants. The enzyme–substrate intermediate must be processed to regenerate the active enzyme. A permanent covalent modification instead may cause irreversible inhibition. Chymotrypsin's acyl-enzyme exemplifies productive covalent catalysis.

Metal-ion catalysis

Metals can activate bound water, position substrates, stabilise developing charge or transfer electrons. Zinc can act as a Lewis acid without changing oxidation state; magnesium moderates phosphate charge; iron and copper can support redox cycling. The same metal may have different roles in different enzymes.

Electrostatic catalysis

Charged and polar groups preferentially stabilise charge development in a reaction state. Serine protease oxyanion holes use backbone NH hydrogen bonds to stabilise the tetrahedral oxyanion. Preorganisation of the protein electrostatic field can reduce the energetic cost of rearranging the reaction environment.

Distortion and desolvation

An enzyme may bias substrate geometry toward a reactive shape or alter solvent access and protonation. Lysozyme distorts a sugar at the cleavage site; hexokinase closes around its substrate. Distortion costs energy and can be supported by favourable binding. Water exclusion may favour one reaction but impair a hydrolysis; its usefulness depends on the actual chemistry.

Real enzymes often combine several mechanisms; naming one strategy does not exclude the others. The next examples show how to reconstruct the complete cycle rather than merely identify a catalytic label.

9. Chymotrypsin: serine-protease mechanism

Chymotrypsin preferentially cleaves peptide bonds on the carboxyl side of bulky aromatic residues such as Phe, Tyr and Trp. Its hydrophobic specificity pocket recognises the side chain, while the catalytic triad attacks the backbone carbonyl. In conventional numbering, Ser195 is the nucleophile, His57 acts as acid and base, and Asp102 helps orient and stabilise histidine. Backbone NH groups of Gly193 and Ser195 form the oxyanion hole.

Acylation

Substrate binds with its scissile carbonyl close to Ser195. His57 accepts a proton from the serine hydroxyl as the oxygen attacks the carbonyl carbon. The carbonyl π electrons shift onto oxygen, forming a tetrahedral oxyanion intermediate stabilised by the oxyanion hole. The intermediate collapses, reforming the carbonyl and breaking the peptide C–N bond. His57 donates a proton to the departing amine. The amine fragment leaves and the acyl fragment remains covalently attached to Ser195 as an ester.

Deacylation

Water enters. Histidine activates water by accepting a proton as water attacks the acyl-enzyme carbonyl. A second tetrahedral oxyanion intermediate forms and collapses. The acyl–serine ester bond breaks, histidine returns a proton to serine, and the carboxyl-containing product leaves. The original active site is regenerated. Net hydrolysis adds water across the peptide bond.

The same chemistry, different specificity

Trypsin's Asp189 at the bottom of the S1 pocket favours positively charged lysine and arginine side chains. Elastase has a shallower pocket, favouring small neutral side chains. The catalytic chemistry is broadly similar. Subtilisin, from a different protein structural family, independently evolved a comparable Ser–His–Asp catalytic arrangement, illustrating convergence.

With suitable chromogenic ester substrates, a rapid first burst of leaving-group product can be followed by slower steady-state release. If acylation is fast and deacylation is slow, enzyme accumulates as acyl-enzyme. A well-resolved burst amplitude may approximate the concentration of active sites. Not every substrate yields such a burst; observed behaviour depends on relative rates and the product measured.

Mechanism checklist

Two tetrahedral intermediates, one acyl-enzyme, His57 alternates acid and base roles, Asp102 supports histidine, oxyanion hole stabilises negative charge. Covalent, acid–base, electrostatic and orientation strategies cooperate.

10. Lysozyme: glycosidic bond cleavage

Lysozyme hydrolyses β(1→4) glycosidic linkages in bacterial peptidoglycan between N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetylglucosamine. Weakening susceptible cell walls explains its antimicrobial role. The preceding carbohydrate node established why glycosidic stereochemistry matters; here that chemistry becomes a catalytic problem.

Hen egg-white lysozyme uses Glu35 and Asp52. Binding distorts the sugar near the scissile linkage. Glu35 protonates the glycosidic oxygen, helping the leaving group depart. Asp52 attacks the anomeric carbon, giving a covalent glycosyl-enzyme intermediate. In the second displacement Glu35 acts as a base, activating water to hydrolyse the intermediate. Two inversions yield net retention of anomeric configuration. The transition states have substantial oxocarbenium-ion-like character; this does not imply a long-lived free carbocation.

Older simplified descriptions emphasised electrostatic stabilisation by Asp52. Experimental evidence supports a covalent intermediate in the retaining mechanism. Some other glycosidases instead perform a single displacement and invert configuration. Thus the substrate class alone does not determine the detailed mechanism.

Compare lysozyme and chymotrypsin: both employ acid–base catalysis and a transient covalent intermediate, but the former uses an aspartate nucleophile and glycosyl-enzyme, while the latter uses serine and an acyl-enzyme. Water regenerates the catalyst in both cases.

11. Carbonic anhydrase and metal-assisted catalysis

CO₂ generated by metabolism must interconvert rapidly with bicarbonate. Carbonic anhydrase catalyses CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H⁺. In human carbonic anhydrase II, Zn²⁺ is coordinated by His94, His96 and His119, with water or hydroxide occupying a fourth coordination position.

Zn²⁺ withdraws electron density from coordinated water and makes deprotonation easier. Zinc-bound hydroxide attacks CO₂ positioned in a neighbouring pocket. Bicarbonate forms and is displaced by water. His64 participates in a proton-transfer network that transfers a proton toward solvent or buffer, regenerating zinc-bound hydroxide. Zinc stays in the +2 oxidation state. Proton transfer can limit turnover even if the initial carbon–oxygen bond formation is rapid.

In tissues high CO₂ favours hydration; pulmonary conditions favour conversion back to CO₂ for exhalation. The enzyme accelerates both directions without altering equilibrium.

Magnesium and phosphate chemistry

MgATP is the relevant phosphoryl donor for many kinases. Mg²⁺ coordinates phosphate oxygens, moderates electrostatic repulsion and assists transfer geometry. The nucleophile attacks the terminal phosphorus and ADP leaves. Nucleic-acid polymerases commonly use a two-metal-ion mechanism, activating the 3′ hydroxyl and stabilising the leaving pyrophosphate. Full replication chemistry belongs in molecular biology.

These examples separate non-redox metal catalysis from redox metals. A chelator that reduces activity may suggest metal dependence, but the correct metal rescue, structural evidence and controls are needed before assigning an exact catalytic role.

12. Coenzyme mechanisms and RNA catalysis

PLP-dependent aminotransferases

PLP initially forms an internal aldimine with an active-site lysine. An incoming amino acid replaces the lysine linkage, forming an external aldimine. Removal of the α-carbon proton generates an intermediate stabilised by the conjugated pyridinium system, which acts as an electron sink. Rearrangement and hydrolysis produce an α-keto acid and pyridoxamine phosphate (PMP). In the second half PMP transfers the amino group to another α-keto acid, regenerating PLP. Net transamination usually needs no external reducing equivalent.

PLP can also support decarboxylation and racemisation because each protein positions a particular substrate bond for reaction. Cofactor identity alone does not uniquely specify the transformation.

NAD-dependent dehydrogenases

Lactate dehydrogenase catalyses pyruvate + NADH + H⁺ ⇌ lactate + NAD⁺. During pyruvate reduction a hydride equivalent moves from NADH to the carbonyl carbon, while a proton donor helps protonate the oxygen. Positioning makes the reaction stereoselective. NAD⁺ leaves and is regenerated in a separate redox reaction. NADH absorbs strongly at 340 nm compared with NAD⁺, enabling continuous assays. Direction depends on the actual concentrations and coupled reactions.

Catalytic RNA

RNA folding can position functional groups and metal ions to facilitate catalysis. Some self-cleaving ribozymes promote intramolecular phosphodiester cleavage by aligning a 2′-OH nucleophile to attack adjacent phosphorus. Acid–base and metal participation vary among ribozymes. RNase P has a catalytic RNA component in many organisms, although protein-only RNase P exists. The ribosomal peptidyl-transferase centre is primarily rRNA, demonstrating RNA-mediated peptide bond formation.

These observations connect nucleic-acid folding to enzyme fundamentals: the essential property is a catalytic molecular architecture, not a particular polymer chemistry. Not every RNA is catalytic and not every ribozyme uses the same metal or proton-transfer mechanism.

13. pH, temperature and catalytic competence

pH changes protonation

A catalytic group may need to be deprotonated to accept a proton or protonated to donate one. Two groups that must occupy opposite protonation states can produce a bell-shaped pH–activity profile. For ideal independent groups with pKₐ values 6 and 9, the active fraction is approximately [1/(1+106−pH)] × [1/(1+10pH−9)]. At pH 7.5 each factor is 0.969, so the active fraction is 0.939. At pH 6 the first factor equals 0.5; at pH 9 the second factor equals 0.5.

Real profiles include substrate protonation, binding, conformational changes and multiple rate-limiting steps. A pH optimum is the maximum measured under defined conditions, not a direct measurement of a single residue's pKₐ. Pepsin works in acidic gastric conditions; other enzymes have very different profiles.

Temperature affects rate and stability

Within a suitable range, higher temperature generally increases elementary reaction rates. It may also accelerate unfolding, aggregation or irreversible damage. An activity curve can therefore rise and fall. A short assay at high temperature may show rapid initial activity, while a long assay reveals loss of active enzyme. The apparent optimum depends on assay duration and conditions. Thermophilic enzymes often retain activity at higher temperatures but are not necessarily more active than mesophilic enzymes at low temperature.

Ionic strength affects charge interactions, while solvents and detergents can influence protein folding or membranes. Reducing conditions may maintain reactive cysteines or disrupt important disulfides. Substrate, product and cofactor concentrations affect net rate. Compare activities only under matched conditions.

14. Enzyme assays and activity units

Activity measures the amount of substrate converted or product formed per unit time under defined conditions. One international unit (U) equals 1 μmol min⁻¹. One katal (kat), the SI unit, equals 1 mol s⁻¹; 1 U = 16.67 nkat. Specific activity is activity divided by total protein mass, commonly U mg⁻¹. During purification it can rise even as total activity falls because enzyme is lost or inactivated. Turnover number is the number of catalytic cycles per active site per second at saturation; its quantitative treatment follows in 1.D.02.

Initial rates minimise substrate depletion, product accumulation and reverse reaction. Continuous assays follow a signal such as absorbance or fluorescence. Discontinuous assays collect aliquots or stop reactions at specified times. A coupled assay converts a difficult-to-measure product into a detectable species. The coupling step must be sufficiently fast and not rate limiting.

Worked example: NADH assay

Beer–Lambert law gives A = εlc. A is absorbance, ε is extinction coefficient, l is path length and c is concentration. NADH has ε₃₄₀ approximately 6.22 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹. A blank-corrected absorbance decrease of 0.1244 per minute at 1.00 cm corresponds to 0.0200 mM min⁻¹. In a 1.00 mL assay, this equals 0.0200 μmol min⁻¹ or 0.0200 U, assuming 1:1 reaction stoichiometry. With 0.0100 mg protein, specific activity is 2.00 U mg⁻¹. Microplate path length should be calibrated rather than assumed to be 1 cm.

Purification fold = 50/5 = 10. Activity yield = 250/500 × 100 = 50%. Specific activity increased tenfold despite losing half the total measured activity. 15. How catalytic mechanisms are tested

A structural model must make experimentally testable predictions. No individual mutation, crystal structure or pH profile proves an entire mechanism. Converging evidence is stronger.

ApproachEvidenceInterpretation limit
Site-directed mutagenesisSubstituting a candidate residue reduces turnoverCheck folding, binding, stability and active enzyme concentration
Cofactor removal and rescueChelation lowers activity, correct ion restores itMetal dependence is not proof of redox chemistry
pH-rate profileIonisation affects activityApparent pKₐ cannot alone identify a residue
Isotope effectsSubstitution of heavier isotope changes rateObserved effect depends on rate-limiting steps
Rapid-mixing / quenchBurst or intermediate before steady stateProve the species lies on the productive pathway
Structural methodsCatalytic geometry and bound analoguesA static snapshot cannot establish the complete time sequence

A solvent isotope effect comparing H₂O and D₂O may suggest proton-dependent chemistry, but viscosity and equivalent pH/pD conditions must be considered. A substrate isotope effect can support the involvement of a particular bond if its change contributes to the measured rate. Combining structural evidence with kinetics and chemical perturbation provides a mechanistic explanation.

For example, a protease mutant that retains binding and folding but loses catalytic activity may affect chemical turnover. A specificity-pocket mutant that changes substrate preference while saturated turnover remains high suggests a recognition effect. These are hypotheses, not absolute assignments. Distinguish loss of enzyme abundance from loss of activity per active molecule.

16. Connecting catalytic mechanisms to the next node

Folding builds a functional site; binding collects and aligns substrate; catalytic residues and optional cofactors lower the relevant activation barriers; intermediates are processed; release regenerates enzyme. These events are the molecular basis for a measurable rate. As substrate concentration increases, more active sites are occupied until further substrate may have little effect on initial rate. This motivates the Michaelis–Menten model.

A strong-binding enzyme need not turn over rapidly. Km is not universally equal to equilibrium Kd; equality requires special kinetic conditions. The detailed derivation, steady-state assumptions, kcat, efficiency, and plots belong to 1.D.02. Quantitative inhibition, multisubstrate and allosteric kinetics, and regulation belong to 1.D.03–05 respectively. This separation keeps the learning sequence progressive rather than repetitive.

17. Part B — conceptual and assertion–reason questions

Original CSIR-NET/SET-style practice. Attempt each question before opening the answer.

1. Which quantity remains unchanged after adding catalyst? A. Forward rate B. Reverse rate C. Equilibrium constant D. Activation barrier

C. Catalysis changes rates, not equilibrium thermodynamics.

2. Fumarase adds water across a double bond. Class? A. Hydrolase B. Lyase C. Ligase D. Transferase

B. Hydration across a double bond is a lyase reaction, not hydrolytic cleavage.

3. Which is not universal? A. Catalysis B. Regeneration C. Protein composition D. Rate enhancement

C. Ribozymes are catalytic RNA.

4. Distant primary-sequence residues form an active site because of A. cleavage B. folding C. cofactor consumption D. transcription

B. Folding brings distant groups into three-dimensional contact.

5. Best catalytic design? A. Preferential ES stabilisation B. Preferential TS stabilisation C. Permanent product trapping D. Increased K′eq

B. Lowering TS relative to ES reduces the relevant activation barrier.

6. Induced fit implies A. absolute rigidity B. binding-associated rearrangement C. identical binding to all shapes D. no product release

B. Binding can reorganise the catalytic geometry.

7. Ser195 of chymotrypsin is a A. nucleophile B. zinc ligand C. phosphate donor D. proton shuttle

A. Serine oxygen attacks the peptide carbonyl; histidine shuttles protons.

8. Oxyanion hole stabilises A. aromatic side chains B. negative charge C. ATP D. metal oxidation

B. Backbone NH groups support the tetrahedral oxyanion.

9. Zinc in carbonic anhydrase A. undergoes redox cycling B. activates water C. is consumed D. donates ATP

B. Zinc acts as Lewis acid and stays Zn²⁺.

10. PLP commonly supports A. amino-acid transformations B. DNA base pairing C. oxygen transport D. all lipid hydrolysis

A. The pyridoxal conjugated system stabilises amino-acid reaction intermediates.

11. Prosthetic group means A. freely diffusible product B. tightly associated cofactor C. always free amino acid D. substrate only

B. Tight association is defining; covalent linkage is not mandatory.

12. Trypsin and chymotrypsin chiefly differ in A. net hydrolysis B. catalytic serine absence C. specificity pocket D. RNA composition

C. Trypsin's negatively charged S1 pocket favours basic residues.

13. Covalent catalysis is illustrated by A. diffusion B. acyl-enzyme formation C. ionic attraction alone D. substrate release

B. A temporary bond creates an alternate chemical route.

14. An intermediate differs from a transition state by occupying A. local minimum B. barrier maximum C. reaction axis D. equilibrium constant

A. Intermediates can have finite lifetimes.

15. NADH is followed at A. 260 B. 280 C. 340 D. 600 nm

C. Reduced nicotinamide absorbs strongly at 340 nm.

16. Assertion: High temperature can decrease activity. Reason: thermal inactivation may accelerate. A. Both true, reason explains B. Both true, unrelated C. A true R false D. A false

A. Loss of active molecules can outweigh faster chemical rates.

17. Assertion: Apoenzyme and holoenzyme are identical. Reason: cofactors can be required. A. Both true B. Both false C. A true R false D. A false R true

D. Holoenzyme includes the required cofactor, apoenzyme does not.

18. Which RNA-containing centre directly catalyses peptide bond formation? A. Ribosomal peptidyl-transferase centre B. DNA ligase C. Hexokinase D. Catalase

A. The catalytic centre is primarily rRNA.

18. Part C — analytical and numerical problems

All datasets are hypothetical and designed to test reasoning, not recalled examination questions.

1. At 300 K a catalyst lowers ΔG‡ by 28.7 kJ/mol. Estimate rate enhancement assuming similar prefactors.

RT = 2.494 kJ/mol. exp(28.7/2.494) ≈ 10⁵. No change to the equilibrium constant follows.

2. A simple A ⇌ B reaction has K′eq = 100. Add enzyme to a mixture initially containing A. What changes at equilibrium?

Equilibrium is reached sooner. The product/reactant activity ratio remains 100 at the same temperature and conditions.

3. Mutant A retains normal binding but turnover drops 10,000-fold. Mutant B binds 100-fold more weakly but saturated turnover is nearly unchanged. Infer roles.

A likely affects a post-binding step, perhaps chemistry; B likely affects recognition. Folding and active concentration must be controlled, and further kinetic analysis is needed.

4. A rapid burst releases 0.80 μM product from 1.00 μM enzyme molecules with one potential site each. Estimate active fraction.

Assuming one burst molecule per active site, 0.80/1.00 = 80%. The slower phase may be deacylation or another later step.

5. Crude fraction has 30 U in 3 mg protein; final has 12 U in 0.20 mg. Find purification fold and yield.

Initial 10 U/mg; final 60 U/mg. Fold = 6; yield = 40%.

6. A 2.0 mL assay, 1 cm path, has NADH absorbance increase 0.311/min. Find activity.

0.311/6.22 = 0.050 mM/min. Multiply by 2 mL: 0.100 μmol/min = 0.100 U.

7. A base must be deprotonated and has pKₐ 7. Find deprotonated fractions at pH 6 and 8.

1/(1+10^(pKₐ−pH)). At pH 6 = 0.091; at pH 8 = 0.909. Tenfold difference, not necessarily a tenfold rate difference if other steps matter.

8. Chelation lowers activity to 5%, Zn²⁺ restores 92%, Mg²⁺ restores 8%. Does this prove a redox role?

No. Specific zinc requirement is supported, but structural or Lewis-acid roles remain possible.

9. A protease S1 pocket becomes negatively charged. Predict a possible change in preference.

Lys/Arg side chains may become favoured, but direct assays are needed; a single mutation does not guarantee complete trypsin-like specificity.

10. A stable tetrahedral analogue binds tightly. Does this identify the genuine transition state?

No. It resembles aspects of the reaction configuration and may support a model, but a genuine transition state is fleeting.

11. D₂O decreases reaction rate threefold. Interpret and specify a control.

A proton-dependent step or equilibrium may contribute. Control equivalent pH/pD, viscosity and stability. The result alone does not identify the step.

12. At 60 °C the first ten seconds are faster than at 40 °C, but after ten minutes activity is lower. Explain.

Temperature accelerates chemistry but also inactivation. Compare stability by preincubating at different temperatures and assaying at the same reference temperature.

13. Explain retention of configuration during lysozyme catalysis.

Asp52 attack creates a covalent intermediate by first displacement; water attacks in the second displacement. Two inversions yield net retention.

14. A coupled assay speeds up when more reporter enzyme is added while primary enzyme concentration stays fixed. Why?

The reporter step may be rate limiting. Increase coupling capacity until measured initial rate is independent of reporter concentration, and check blanks and stoichiometry.

19. Revision map and references

Folded catalyst → selective binding → productive orientation → preferential transition-state stabilisation → reaction intermediates when needed → product release → enzyme regenerated. Cofactors expand the chemistry; environmental conditions influence catalytic competence; thermodynamics determines net direction.

Common traps

Not every enzyme is a protein, not every cofactor is a vitamin, not every enzyme consumes ATP, and not every active-site residue directly catalyses chemistry. High substrate affinity does not prove rapid catalysis. A covalent intermediate is not necessarily inhibitory; a temperature optimum is not a universal constant. Do not confuse a specificity pocket with a catalytic triad, or assume that a crystal structure alone proves a reaction mechanism.
