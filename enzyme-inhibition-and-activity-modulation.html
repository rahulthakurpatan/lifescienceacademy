1. Why an enzyme needs a brake

Imagine a cell that has already made enough of an amino acid. Its enzymes can still convert more starting material into that product, but doing so would consume ATP, reducing power and carbon skeletons that other reactions require. The cell needs a brake, not simply a faster accelerator. Enzyme inhibition is one of the ways to impose that brake. The same molecular principle explains why a compound in a laboratory assay reduces product formation, why many medicines target enzymes, and why certain chemicals permanently damage catalytic proteins.

In the previous node, Michaelis–Menten kinetics described how an enzyme accelerates a reaction through a transient enzyme–substrate complex. At low substrate concentration the active sites are mostly empty, and adding substrate increases the frequency of productive binding. At high substrate concentration the available sites spend much of their time occupied, and catalysis approaches a limiting rate. We now ask what changes when another molecule is introduced into that system. Does the inhibitor compete for free enzyme, bind the occupied enzyme, alter catalysis, or remove active enzyme from the population? Each mechanism leaves a different quantitative signature.

Activity, amount and affinity are not interchangeable

An enzyme assay measures a reaction rate, such as micromoles of product formed per minute. Activity is not automatically a measurement of protein mass. Two tubes can contain the same mass of enzyme but very different amounts of catalytically competent enzyme. Conversely, a reversible inhibitor may lower the measured rate while every enzyme molecule remains folded and chemically intact. Inhibition is therefore a decrease in catalytic rate relative to an appropriate control, not necessarily protein destruction.

The maximum rate Vmax depends on enzyme concentration and catalytic turnover: Vmax = kcat[E]T. Km is the substrate concentration that gives half Vmax in a simple Michaelis–Menten system; it is not generally a dissociation constant. For E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, Km = (k−1 + kcat)/k1, whereas Kd = k−1/k1. They approach one another under rapid-equilibrium conditions when catalysis is slow relative to substrate dissociation. This distinction becomes especially important when interpreting inhibitor-induced changes in apparent Km.

Learning route

Start with the uninhibited reference reaction, build the reversible inhibition model, derive all four classical cases from one equation, learn to read experimental plots, and finally distinguish reversible binding from time-dependent inactivation. The next nodes extend these ideas to multisubstrate enzymes, cooperativity and physiological regulation.

2. Establish the uninhibited reference reaction

A meaningful inhibition experiment begins with a reliable rate measured without inhibitor. Consider an enzyme catalysing S → P. Under initial-rate conditions, product is negligible, substrate concentration changes little during the measurement interval, and reverse reaction from P is ordinarily ignored. A steady state is reached when the rate of ES formation approximately balances the rate of ES disappearance. This does not mean that chemical equilibrium has been reached; product continues to form.

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P    v₀ = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S])

Here [S] denotes free substrate concentration, usually approximated by total added substrate because substrate is in large excess over enzyme. The enzyme mass balance is [E]T = [E] + [ES]. The initial velocity is v₀ = kcat[ES]. At [S] = Km, half the enzyme's maximal catalytic rate is observed. At [S] much smaller than Km, v₀ ≈ (Vmax/Km)[S], so the initial slope of the saturation curve reports Vmax/Km. At [S] much larger than Km, v₀ approaches Vmax and is approximately independent of substrate concentration.

Suppose Vmax = 120 µmol min⁻¹ and Km = 4 mM. At 1 mM substrate the rate is 24 µmol min⁻¹; at 4 mM it is 60; at 12 mM it is 90. A single measurement of 30 µmol min⁻¹ after inhibitor addition tells us that inhibition occurred, but not how it occurred. Measuring rates across a range of substrate concentrations reveals whether the inhibitor shifts the curve horizontally, lowers its plateau, or changes both features.

Substrate regimeRate approximationWhat the observation tests
[S] ≪ Kmv ≈ Vmax[S]/KmLow-substrate catalytic efficiency
[S] = Kmv = Vmax/2Midpoint relative to the enzyme's own plateau
[S] ≫ Kmv ≈ VmaxMaximum catalytic capacity

The comparison must keep total enzyme amount, temperature, pH, ionic strength, solvent composition, and assay timing constant. Changing any of these can produce an apparent inhibition pattern without a specific inhibitor mechanism. For instance, a pH shift may alter catalytic residue protonation, and an organic solvent used to dissolve an inhibitor may itself alter enzyme activity. A solvent-matched control is therefore essential.

3. Reversible binding versus enzyme inactivation

The first useful classification asks whether enzyme activity can return when free inhibitor is removed. A reversible inhibitor associates through an equilibrium or kinetic binding process and can dissociate, allowing enzyme molecules to resume catalysis. Noncovalent forces such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, hydrophobic contacts and dispersion often mediate binding. An irreversible inhibitor instead produces a very persistent inactive state, frequently through covalent modification of a crucial residue. Operationally, no substantial activity recovers during a suitable observation period after removal of free inhibitor.

Why covalency is not the definition

Some covalent inhibitors can be reversible, while extremely slow-dissociating noncovalent inhibitors can appear irreversible during a short assay. A stable complex after a quick dilution is evidence of persistent inhibition, not by itself proof of a covalent bond. Distinguish the chemical mechanism from the timescale of recovery. A tightly bound reversible inhibitor can require extensive dilution or extended recovery time; an irreversibly modified enzyme may require synthesis of new protein in a living cell.

The four classical reversible models

For a single-substrate enzyme, classical models distinguish the inhibitor's preference for free enzyme E and substrate-bound enzyme ES. Competitive inhibition involves binding to E but not productive ES under the model. Uncompetitive inhibition involves binding to ES but not E. Mixed inhibition permits both complexes with different affinities; pure noncompetitive inhibition is the equal-affinity special case of mixed inhibition. These are kinetic categories, not universal descriptions of the inhibitor's structural binding location.

We will assume one binding site for substrate and one relevant inhibitor-binding event, a fully inactive EI and ESI, initial rates and approximately constant free inhibitor concentration. These simplifying assumptions are the reason that the equations below are useful at CSIR-NET level. Real enzymes can show partial inhibition, cooperativity or time-dependent changes, which will be treated separately.

4. One equation explains the four reversible cases

Rather than memorising four unrelated equations, build a single enzyme mass balance. The reversible inhibitor I can associate with E to form EI or with ES to form ESI. Define Ki as the dissociation constant for EI and Ki′ as that for ESI, using the standard simple binding model. The prime marks binding to ES; it does not mean a derivative.

Ki = [E][I]/[EI]    Ki′ = [ES][I]/[ESI]

Rearrangement gives [EI] = [E][I]/Ki and [ESI] = [ES][I]/Ki′. Introduce two dimensionless factors that express how much each inhibitor-binding route contributes to the population of enzyme species:

α = 1 + [I]/Ki    α′ = 1 + [I]/Ki′

The total enzyme concentration includes all four forms. Under the classical steady-state mixed-inhibition model, in which the inhibitor-bound forms are catalytically inactive and the binding steps satisfy the simple equilibrium assumptions used here, the unbound productive states obey [ES]/[E] = [S]/Km. Combining these relations yields:

[E]T = [E] + [EI] + [ES] + [ESI] = α[E] + α′[ES]
[E]T = [ES](α Km/[S] + α′)

Multiplying by [S] and isolating [ES] gives [ES] = [E]T[S]/(αKm + α′[S]). Since only ES is productive in this complete-inhibition model, v = kcat[ES]. Thus the mixed-inhibition rate equation is:

v = Vmax[S]/(αKm + α′[S])

Divide numerator and denominator by α′ to recover the familiar hyperbolic form. This is the bridge between the microscopic binding preference and the experimentally observed apparent Michaelis–Menten constants.

Vmax,app = Vmax/α′    Km,app = (α/α′)Km

An apparent parameter is the parameter obtained when an inhibited rate curve is fitted to the ordinary Michaelis–Menten form. It is not necessarily an intrinsic change in the enzyme's original substrate-binding chemistry. For a given free inhibitor concentration, α and α′ are constant. In the absence of inhibitor both equal one and the original equation returns. Stronger binding (smaller Ki or Ki′) makes the corresponding factor larger at the same inhibitor concentration.

The derivation is a standard simple steady-state treatment, not a guarantee that every physical four-state mechanism has this exact form. If inhibitor association is slow, a bound form retains appreciable activity, inhibitor concentration is depleted by enzyme binding, or a more elaborate kinetic pathway is operating, the assumptions must be revised. In this node we use the classical model whenever no additional complication is specified.

Exam anchor

α tells how strongly inhibitor favours free enzyme; α′ tells how strongly it favours ES. The plateau depends only on α′; the apparent midpoint depends on their ratio. At low substrate, the rate ratio is approximately 1/α. At saturating substrate, it approaches 1/α′.

5. Competitive inhibition: competition for free enzyme

Consider succinate dehydrogenase, which oxidises succinate to fumarate. Malonate resembles succinate sufficiently to bind in the substrate-recognition region, but its structure does not permit the same productive reaction. When malonate occupies the relevant binding site, succinate cannot occupy it productively at the same time. Raising succinate concentration increases the chance of forming ES rather than EI. This classical example connects molecular recognition with a kinetic pattern.

In ideal competitive inhibition, inhibitor binds E but does not bind ES. Ki is finite and Ki′ is effectively infinite; hence α > 1 and α′ = 1. Substitution into the general equation gives:

v = Vmax[S]/(αKm + [S]);   Km,app = αKm;   Vmax,app = Vmax

Why is the plateau unchanged? When the substrate becomes very abundant relative to the competing inhibitor, essentially all active enzyme can again be recruited into the productive state. No active enzyme molecules have been permanently removed. Why does apparent Km increase? A higher substrate concentration is needed to reach half of the same maximal rate because inhibitor competes for free enzyme. It is a shift in apparent substrate requirement, not necessarily a direct change in the affinity of unoccupied enzyme for substrate.

Worked example: substrate rescue

An enzyme has Vmax = 100 units, Km = 2 mM, and a competitive inhibitor with Ki = 1 mM. At [I] = 3 mM, α = 1 + 3/1 = 4. The apparent Km is 8 mM. At [S] = 2 mM, v = 100 × 2/(8 + 2) = 20 units, compared with 50 units without inhibitor. At [S] = 80 mM, the inhibited rate is 90.91 units and the control is 97.56 units. The difference becomes relatively small at high substrate, though it is not exactly zero at a finite concentration.

Biological and pharmacological examples

Methotrexate binds dihydrofolate reductase and inhibits reduction of dihydrofolate, limiting tetrahydrofolate-dependent one-carbon transfer reactions. Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase and reduce mevalonate formation; they are well-known examples of competition with the enzyme's HMG-CoA substrate in the relevant kinetic analysis. Detailed drug kinetics may be more complex than a single rapid-equilibrium experiment, so classify a particular assay from the actual substrate and rate data rather than merely from its drug name.

Misconception

A competitive inhibitor need not be a visible structural mimic of substrate. Mutually exclusive binding may also arise from overlapping interactions or conformational coupling. Conversely, a compound that binds at a distant site is not automatically noncompetitive.

6. Uncompetitive inhibition: trapping the occupied enzyme

Now imagine a molecule whose preferred binding pocket becomes available only after the substrate has bound. It has little or no affinity for the free enzyme under our model, but it can associate with ES and produce inactive ESI. The substrate, paradoxically, creates the very species that the inhibitor needs. Raising substrate concentration therefore does not rescue the reaction as it does in ideal competitive inhibition.

For ideal uncompetitive inhibition, α = 1 and α′ > 1. The denominator contains the normal Km term and an amplified substrate term:

v = Vmax[S]/(Km + α′[S]);   Vmax,app = Vmax/α′;   Km,app = Km/α′

Both apparent parameters decrease by the same factor. The maximum rate falls because even at abundant substrate some occupied enzyme is trapped in the inactive ternary complex. The apparent Km also falls because the new plateau is reached halfway at a lower substrate concentration; inhibitor stabilises the pool of substrate-associated enzyme states. This is sometimes described as increased apparent substrate affinity, but the lower Km,app alone does not establish a smaller microscopic substrate Kd.

At very low substrate, little ES exists and the inhibitor has little opportunity to act. Algebraically v ≈ Vmax[S]/Km, which is the same low-substrate slope as the uninhibited curve. At high substrate the rate is limited to Vmax/α′. This increasing relative inhibition with substrate is characteristic of the simple uncompetitive model.

Worked example: a low Km is not always good

Let Vmax = 120 µmol min⁻¹, Km = 6 mM, and Ki′ = 2 mM. At [I] = 4 mM, α′ = 3. The apparent maximum is 40 µmol min⁻¹ and the apparent Km is 2 mM. At 2 mM substrate the new rate is 20, which is half the inhibited plateau. The control at that substrate is 30. At 60 mM substrate, the inhibited rate is approximately 38.7, whereas the control is approximately 109.1. Adding substrate cannot restore the original capacity.

Certain enzyme–substrate complexes expose binding opportunities that free enzyme does not offer. Lithium inhibition of inositol monophosphatase can exhibit uncompetitive behaviour relative to substrate under appropriate assay conditions; the detailed kinetics also depend on metal ions and reaction mechanism. Use such examples to understand binding order rather than assuming every condition gives exactly the same inhibition class.

7. Pure noncompetitive inhibition: equal preference

Suppose the inhibitor binds both free enzyme and ES with exactly equal dissociation constants. It does not discriminate between the two substrate occupancy states. Under the complete-inhibition model, binding prevents productive catalysis whenever inhibitor is present, yet the proportion by which it redistributes free and substrate-bound enzyme is the same. This is pure noncompetitive inhibition.

Set Ki = Ki′. Then α = α′ > 1. Dividing the general equation by α gives:

v = (Vmax/α)[S]/(Km + [S]);   Vmax,app = Vmax/α;   Km,app = Km

The rate at every substrate concentration is reduced by the same fraction 1/α. The apparent Km remains unchanged because the shape of the saturation curve relative to its own new plateau is unchanged. The curve is scaled vertically, not shifted horizontally. At saturating substrate the inhibitor still binds ES, so the original Vmax cannot be recovered simply by adding substrate.

Worked example: separate occupancy from capacity

An enzyme has Km = 5 mM and Vmax = 200 units. A pure noncompetitive inhibitor has Ki = Ki′ = 2 µM. At 6 µM inhibitor, both factors equal four; apparent Vmax is 50 units while Km stays 5 mM. At [S] = 5 mM the uninhibited rate is 100 and the inhibited rate is 25. At [S] = 50 mM the rates are 181.82 and 45.45 respectively. In both cases activity remaining is exactly 25%.

The textbook phrase 'inhibitor binds elsewhere and changes enzyme shape' is at most a possible molecular explanation. Pure noncompetitive kinetics specifically requires equal binding preference in the simple model. Many ligands that bind outside the active site show mixed inhibition, partial inhibition, or allosteric behaviour instead. Do not label heavy-metal poisoning or covalent enzyme modification as pure noncompetitive merely because an active-site competitor cannot reverse it.

8. Mixed inhibition: unequal affinity for E and ES

Real enzymes frequently lie between the simple limiting cases. Substrate binding changes the arrangement, hydration and flexibility of enzyme residues; an inhibitor may therefore bind free enzyme and substrate-bound enzyme with different strengths. Both EI and ESI form, but one is thermodynamically preferred. Mixed inhibition is the general classical case and pure noncompetitive inhibition is a special limit.

v = Vmax[S]/(αKm + α′[S]);   Vmax,app = Vmax/α′;   Km,app = (α/α′)Km

Case 1: stronger binding to free enzyme

If Ki < Ki′, α > α′ at a nonzero common inhibitor concentration. The apparent Km rises while Vmax falls. The enzyme is competitively disadvantaged at low substrate, but high substrate only partially rescues activity because ESI can still form. For example, let Ki = 1 µM, Ki′ = 4 µM, and [I] = 4 µM. Then α = 5, α′ = 2. With original Km = 3 mM and Vmax = 80 units, apparent Km is 7.5 mM and Vmax is 40 units. At 7.5 mM substrate, the inhibited rate is 20 units.

Case 2: stronger binding to the ES complex

If Ki > Ki′, α < α′. Both apparent Km and Vmax decrease, but unlike pure uncompetitive inhibition they generally decrease by unequal factors. With Ki = 4 µM, Ki′ = 1 µM and [I] = 4 µM, α = 2 and α′ = 5. The same original enzyme now has Km,app = 1.2 mM and Vmax,app = 16 units. The ratio Vmax,app/Km,app is half the original value, demonstrating that the low-substrate slope is no longer unchanged.

Thermodynamic consistency of the four-state cycle

If all ligand binding steps can be described by equilibrium dissociation constants, the two routes E → ES → ESI and E → EI → ESI must have the same overall free energy change. If Ks is the microscopic dissociation constant for substrate binding to E and Ks′ is its dissociation constant for binding to EI, then Ks Ki′ = Ki Ks′. Binding preference is reciprocal: when substrate changes the inhibitor's affinity, inhibitor also changes substrate affinity in the corresponding thermodynamic cycle. Do not substitute steady-state Km for Ks without justifying a rapid-equilibrium assumption.

Distinguish two patterns

Decreases in both Vmax and Km are compatible with uncompetitive or ES-favouring mixed inhibition. Only an equal fractional decrease, or the unchanged reciprocal slope predicted by the simple model, supports the ideal uncompetitive case.

The word 'partial rescue' refers to increased relative activity, not an absolute rate equal to the uninhibited maximum.

v(I)/v(0) = (Km + [S])/(αKm + α′[S])

This single expression gives a second route to classification. If the rate ratio increases with substrate, α > α′; if the ratio decreases, α′ > α. A ratio independent of substrate implies equal factors. To discriminate ideal competitive from E-preferring mixed, examine whether the high-substrate ratio approaches one. To discriminate ideal uncompetitive from ES-preferring mixed, examine whether the low-substrate ratio approaches one.

At the same free inhibitor concentration a lower Ki means stronger binding to E, while a lower Ki′ means stronger binding to ES. However, a smaller Ki does not automatically imply a greater decrease in Vmax because Vmax depends on Ki′. A very strong competitive inhibitor may have no effect on the theoretical maximum at sufficiently high substrate.

10. Lineweaver–Burk plots: read the geometry

A Michaelis–Menten saturation curve is hyperbolic. Taking the reciprocal of the classical mixed-inhibition equation converts it into a straight line, a form historically useful before routine nonlinear fitting. The Lineweaver–Burk plot displays 1/v on the vertical axis and 1/[S] on the horizontal axis. It is not the same as plotting v against 1/[S].

1/v = (αKm/Vmax)(1/[S]) + α′/Vmax

Compare with y = mx + c. Slope = αKm/Vmax, y-intercept = α′/Vmax, and x-intercept = −α′/(αKm) = −1/Km,app. In the control, these become Km/Vmax, 1/Vmax and −1/Km. The negative x-intercept is an extrapolated value; it does not represent a physically negative substrate concentration.

UncompetitiveUnchangedIncreasesMore negative; parallel lines
Pure noncompetitiveIncreasesIncreases proportionallyUnchanged; lines meet on x-axis
MixedUsually increasesIncreasesChanges; family intersects left of y-axis away from axes in the general case

Where do mixed-inhibition lines meet?

For several inhibitor concentrations at the same intrinsic constants, write the line as 1/v = (Km/Vmax)x + 1/Vmax + [I]((Km/(Vmax Ki))x + 1/(Vmax Ki′)). The inhibitor-dependent term vanishes at x = −Ki/(Km Ki′). The corresponding y is (1 − Ki/Ki′)/Vmax. Thus E-preferring mixed inhibition (Ki < Ki′) produces an intersection above the x-axis, ES-preferring mixed inhibition produces an intersection below it, and equal affinities produce an intersection on it. For pure uncompetitive inhibition Ki is effectively infinite and the finite intersection disappears: lines are parallel.

A numerical reading

A control reciprocal line has slope 0.04 and y-intercept 0.02 (in consistent units). Then Vmax = 1/0.02 = 50 rate units and Km = 0.04/0.02 = 2 concentration units. An inhibited line has slope 0.12 and y-intercept 0.04. Thus α = 3, α′ = 2, Vmax,app = 25 and Km,app = 3. This is mixed inhibition with stronger binding to free enzyme. Notice that Km is obtained by slope divided by intercept, not by slope alone.

A double-reciprocal plot magnifies error at low substrate concentrations: a modest error in small v becomes a large error in 1/v. Linear extrapolation can therefore misclassify noisy experiments. The plot remains excellent for interpreting exam diagrams, but nonlinear regression of untransformed initial rates is generally preferred for estimating constants.

11. Hanes–Woolf, Eadie–Hofstee and secondary plots

Other algebraic rearrangements encode the same model parameters in different visual forms. Each transformation has a different error structure. Hanes–Woolf places [S]/v on the y-axis and [S] on the x-axis; Eadie–Hofstee plots v against v/[S]. Substituting apparent parameters gives the following equations.

In Eadie–Hofstee, competitive lines have the same y-intercept, while pure noncompetitive lines have the same slope. In this plot the measured rate appears on both axes, so errors are correlated. Recognise which plot a question supplies before applying familiar intercept rules.

Extract Ki and Ki′ from secondary plots

If several Lineweaver–Burk lines are fitted at different inhibitor concentrations, their slopes and intercepts can be plotted against [I]. For the simple mixed model the slope increases linearly with [I], as does the intercept when inhibitor binds ES:

m(I) = (Km/Vmax)(1 + [I]/Ki);   b(I) = (1/Vmax)(1 + [I]/Ki′)

Extrapolating the slope-versus-[I] line to zero gives −Ki. Extrapolating the intercept-versus-[I] line to zero gives −Ki′. The ratio of the secondary line's y-intercept to its slope also equals the relevant constant. If one of the secondary slopes is zero, the corresponding inhibitor-binding route is absent or not detectable within the conditions tested. As before, these transformations are primarily diagnostic teaching tools.

Dixon plot

The Dixon plot uses 1/v against [I] at fixed substrate concentrations. Rearrange the general equation to obtain a straight line whose slope is (Km/(Vmax[S]Ki) + 1/(VmaxKi′)) and intercept is (Km/[S] + 1)/Vmax. For ideal competitive inhibition, lines from different substrate concentrations intersect at [I] = −Ki and 1/v = 1/Vmax. For mixed inhibition the intersection x-coordinate is also −Ki in this simple model, with y = (1 − Ki/Ki′)/Vmax. In ideal uncompetitive inhibition the lines are parallel. Negative inhibitor concentrations are mathematical extrapolations, not measurements.

Best experimental practice

Fit all initial-rate data across substrate and inhibitor concentrations to candidate models directly, assess uncertainty and residual patterns, then use plots to communicate or diagnose the fitted behaviour.

12. Ki, Ki′, IC50 and inhibition strength

Ki is a mechanistic binding constant within a specified inhibition model. IC50 is the inhibitor concentration that reduces the measured response to 50% of the matched uninhibited response in a particular assay. IC50 depends on substrate concentration, time, enzyme concentration when ligand depletion matters, and other assay conditions. It is not inherently an affinity constant. IC50 values from two experiments cannot be compared as if they were intrinsic constants without examining conditions.

Derive the substrate dependence of IC50

In the classical reversible complete-inhibition model, divide the uninhibited rate by the inhibited rate and set v(I)/v(0) = 1/2. The resulting equation is αKm + α′[S] = 2(Km + [S]). Substitute α and α′ and solve for free inhibitor concentration:

IC50 = (Km + [S])/(Km/Ki + [S]/Ki′)

For competitive inhibition, Ki′ is effectively infinite, which yields the familiar Cheng–Prusoff relationship for a simple competitive enzyme assay. It states that IC50 increases as substrate concentration increases.

Competitive: IC50 = Ki(1 + [S]/Km);   Ki = IC50/(1 + [S]/Km)

For uncompetitive inhibition, Ki is effectively infinite. IC50 = Ki′(1 + Km/[S]); raising substrate concentration lowers IC50 toward Ki′. For pure noncompetitive inhibition Ki = Ki′, so IC50 = Ki independent of substrate in this model. These relationships require free inhibitor concentration to be approximately known, initial steady-state rates and a complete inhibitory effect at sufficiently high inhibitor. They cannot be applied blindly to irreversible, slow-binding, tight-binding or partial-inhibition measurements.

MechanismIC50 in the ideal modelEffect of increasing [S]
CompetitiveKi(1 + [S]/Km)Increases
UncompetitiveKi′(1 + Km/[S])Decreases
Pure noncompetitiveKiNo change
Mixed(Km + [S])/(Km/Ki + [S]/Ki′)Depends on relative binding preference

Worked example: an inhibitor has IC50 = 30 µM when [S] = 4Km. If competitive and the assumptions hold, Ki = 30/(1 + 4) = 6 µM. At [S] = Km its predicted IC50 is 12 µM. The numerical value changes because the substrate concentration changed, not because the compound acquired a different chemical identity. In another assay a compound with Ki′ = 6 µM would have an uncompetitive IC50 of 7.5 µM at 4Km and 12 µM at Km; at a single substrate concentration the two mechanisms can even have identical IC50 values.

Fractional activity and percent inhibition

% inhibition = 100 × (1 − v(I)/v(0))

If the control rate is 80 and the inhibited rate is 28, fraction remaining is 0.35 and inhibition is 65%. Use blank-corrected rates rather than raw endpoint absorbance. If an assay has substantial background signal, calculating ratios from uncorrected measurements can underestimate inhibition.

13. Irreversible inhibition: remove active enzyme with time

The previous mechanisms reduce the productive fraction reversibly. An irreversible inhibitor changes the problem: enzyme molecules become persistently inactive, often through a covalent reaction with a strategically placed catalytic residue. Substrate may protect an enzyme from initial attack if the inhibitor needs access to the active site, but once the chemical modification occurs, adding more substrate generally does not restore those inactive molecules.

A simple one-step inactivation process can be written E + I → E–I. When inhibitor is in great excess, its concentration is effectively constant and active enzyme disappears with pseudo-first-order kinetics. For many enzyme-directed irreversible inhibitors a reversible recognition step precedes inactivation:

E + I ⇌ EI → E–I    (inactivation rate constant kinact)

The reversible complex forms first; the second step converts it into a persistently inactive species. Under a standard saturable two-step inactivation model with inhibitor in excess and appropriate kinetic assumptions, the observed first-order rate constant is:

kobs = kinact[I]/(KI + [I])

KI here denotes the inhibitor concentration giving half-maximal inactivation rate in this model; it is not automatically identical to the equilibrium dissociation constant Ki of a reversible inhibitor. At low inhibitor concentration, kobs ≈ (kinact/KI)[I], and the ratio kinact/KI has units of concentration⁻¹ time⁻¹. At saturating inhibitor, kobs approaches kinact, a first-order rate constant. This plateau reflects saturation of the recognition step, not substrate saturation.

[E]active(t) = [E]active(0)e−kobs t;   t½ = ln(2)/kobs

If activity is assayed after inactivation under identical conditions and residual free inhibitor does not distort the rate, remaining activity reports the active enzyme fraction. A graph of ln(activity remaining/initial activity) against time has slope −kobs for simple first-order inactivation. Multiple phases or a plateau of residual activity suggest additional complexity, such as resistant enzyme populations or reversible inhibition.

Worked example: distinguish time from binding equilibrium

Let kinact = 0.20 min⁻¹ and KI = 5 µM. At [I] = 5 µM, kobs = 0.10 min⁻¹. Half-life is 0.693/0.10 = 6.93 min. After 20 minutes, the active fraction is e⁻² ≈ 0.135, so about 13.5% activity remains. At [I] = 15 µM, kobs = 0.15 min⁻¹, and after 20 minutes about 4.98% remains. These results depend on preincubation duration, unlike ideal fast reversible inhibition after equilibration.

What happens to apparent Km and Vmax?

Suppose a fraction f of enzyme remains active, and the surviving enzyme has the same catalytic properties as untreated enzyme. Vmax,app = fVmax, while Km is unchanged. This resembles a pure noncompetitive pattern in a substrate series, yet the cause is entirely different. Dilution, washout and time courses distinguish loss of enzyme molecules from rapid reversible binding. If a chemical modification also changes the properties of partially active surviving molecules, Km can change; there is no universal irreversible-inhibition Km rule.

14. Molecular examples: covalent, mechanism-based and persistent inhibition

Covalent inhibition often targets residues whose side chains are essential for catalysis. The molecular effect is understandable from earlier nodes: a serine hydroxyl can act as a nucleophile, a cysteine thiol may participate in catalysis, and a lysine amino group may orient or transfer chemical groups. Modification can block these roles even when the overall fold remains largely intact.

ExampleEnzyme / molecular effectConcept illustrated
AspirinAcetylates cyclooxygenase; inhibits its normal prostaglandin-producing reactionPersistent covalent enzyme modification
Penicillin and related β-lactamsAcylate the catalytic serine of bacterial penicillin-binding transpeptidasesSubstrate-like recognition followed by stable acyl-enzyme formation
AllopurinolConverted by xanthine oxidoreductase to oxypurinol, which forms a tightly bound inhibitory complex with the reduced enzymeEnzyme-dependent generation of a persistent inhibitor; not simply a generic covalent reaction
Eflornithine (DFMO)Mechanism-based inactivation of ornithine decarboxylaseThe target enzyme processes the inhibitor into a reactive inhibitory species

A mechanism-based inhibitor, often called a suicide substrate or suicide inhibitor when inactivation is effectively irreversible, is processed by the target enzyme's normal catalytic machinery into a species that traps or inactivates it. The important distinction is that catalytic processing generates the inhibitory species. Not every covalent inhibitor is mechanism-based: a reactive compound may modify a residue directly without undergoing catalytic transformation.

An affinity label instead exploits selective reversible binding to position a reactive group near a target residue; a covalent modification may follow without normal catalytic conversion. A transition-state analogue mimics important geometric or electrostatic features of the catalytic transition state. Such molecules can bind strongly but need not react covalently. These categories describe different aspects of inhibitor mechanism and should not be treated as synonyms.

Recovery in an organism

A reversible drug's effect often declines when free drug concentration falls, subject to binding kinetics and pharmacokinetics. Persistent inactivation can last beyond the period during which free inhibitor is present. For instance, platelet cyclooxygenase activity after aspirin exposure recovers mainly as new platelets enter circulation, whereas nucleated cells may produce replacement enzyme. This is a biological illustration of enzyme turnover, not a dosing recommendation.

The antibiotic example also connects inhibition to cell structure: bacterial transpeptidases form cross-links in peptidoglycan. Blocking their function impairs cell-wall construction in susceptible growing bacteria. Resistance can arise through altered target proteins, reduced access, or enzymes that hydrolyse the antibiotic. The main examination insight is the distinction between reversible competition, stable acylation of the catalytic target, and destruction of the inhibitor itself.

15. Beyond the ideal models: tight binding, slow binding and partial inhibition

The four classical models assume that free inhibitor is essentially equal to the added concentration. This fails when the inhibitor binds so strongly that a significant fraction of it is captured by the enzyme, especially when total inhibitor concentration is comparable to total enzyme concentration. In that situation, doubling enzyme concentration may shift the measured IC50. The dose–response curve is then influenced by stoichiometry as well as affinity.

Tight-binding mass balance

For a simple 1:1 inactive complex E + I ⇌ EI at a fixed assay condition, let ET and IT denote total concentrations and x denote bound complex concentration. Using free E = ET − x and free I = IT − x gives Kd = (ET − x)(IT − x)/x. Rearrangement yields the physically meaningful quadratic root:

x = (ET + IT + Kd − √((ET + IT + Kd)² − 4ET IT))/2

If EI is completely inactive and no other complications are present, activity remaining is approximately 1 − x/ET. In a substrate-containing assay the effective binding parameter may need adjustment for substrate competition; this is the basis of tight-binding or Morrison-type analysis. For ET = 10 nM, IT = 5 nM and Kd approaching zero, almost every inhibitor molecule is bound and only half the enzyme can be inactivated. A simple equation assuming free inhibitor = 5 nM would fail in this limit.

Slow-binding inhibition

Some reversible inhibitors associate slowly or first form EI and then rearrange into a more stable EI* state. During an assay the rate may gradually fall from an initial velocity to a lower steady-state velocity. Preincubating enzyme with inhibitor can therefore lower the measured initial rate, even though the interaction is reversible on a longer timescale. Unlike genuinely irreversible inactivation, prolonged removal of inhibitor can allow activity to return. Distinguish the initial and final steady rates, and do not fit a single straight line to an entire curved progress trace.

Partial inhibition

The classical mixed model assumed ESI is completely inactive. What if ESI can still make product, but at a fraction β of the uninhibited turnover? In a simple binding model, v = kcat([ES] + β[ESI]), with 0 < β < 1. The numerator then acquires an extra factor (1 + β[I]/Ki′) when other assumptions remain valid. A nonzero limiting activity at high inhibitor does not automatically mean inhibitor failed to bind. It may represent catalysis by inhibitor-bound enzyme, a resistant isozyme, incomplete solubility, or another experimental complication.

Substrate inhibition is a separate idea

Sometimes the substrate itself inhibits the enzyme at high concentration by occupying a second nonproductive binding position. A simple model assumes ES is active and ESS is inactive, with substrate inhibition constant KSI. The rate is:

v = Vmax[S]/(Km + [S] + [S]²/KSI)

At low substrate the extra term is negligible and the enzyme behaves approximately as Michaelis–Menten. At very high substrate, v ≈ Vmax KSI/[S], so the rate falls instead of approaching a fixed plateau. Differentiating the simple equation with respect to [S] shows the maximum occurs at [S] = √(Km KSI). If Km = 2 mM and KSI = 18 mM, the peak occurs at 6 mM. This phenomenon cannot be diagnosed from a low-substrate series alone. A fall in rate at high substrate could also arise from assay interference, substrate precipitation or other causes; verify before invoking a second site.

Product inhibition occurs when reaction product binds enzyme and reduces the forward rate. Product can compete with substrate or bind additional enzyme states, depending on the mechanism. This is distinct from the slowing caused by depletion of substrate or the reverse reaction becoming significant as equilibrium is approached. Multisubstrate product-inhibition patterns will become useful in Node 1.D.04 for distinguishing ordered, random and ping-pong mechanisms.

16. From a test tube to a living cell: activity modulation

Inhibition in a purified assay is a useful model of molecular control, but cell metabolism is a network of interacting reactions. A metabolite that reduces an enzyme's rate can be an inhibitor in a biochemical sense while functioning as a beneficial regulator in the organism. Conversely, an activator can increase activity by stabilising a productive conformation, assisting metal-dependent catalysis, or changing substrate response. The relevant effect is always defined under specified conditions.

Feedback versus direct product inhibition

Consider a pathway A → B → C → D catalysed by different enzymes. If D binds the first committed enzyme and reduces its activity, the cell can limit unnecessary formation of D. This is feedback inhibition. D need not be the immediate product of that first enzyme. Direct product inhibition instead involves the product of the same catalytic step. The two phenomena can coexist but are not identical. The principle helps explain how amino acid synthesis and nucleotide synthesis respond to cellular demand.

Aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase) in bacteria illustrates allosteric feedback: CTP inhibits its activity, whereas ATP can activate it, helping coordinate pyrimidine and purine nucleotide availability. Its cooperative substrate response is not fully described by a one-site Michaelis–Menten equation; this is precisely why the next node treats Hill-type and allosteric models. A modulator binding a regulatory site can affect substrate affinity, maximal activity, cooperativity, or combinations of these features.

Activity is not identical to thermodynamic driving force

An enzyme or inhibitor changes the rate at which a reaction proceeds, not the standard free energy difference between substrate and product. Catalysis lowers activation barriers; inhibition reduces access to productive catalytic pathways or changes the effective enzyme population. Neither changes the intrinsic equilibrium constant of the uncoupled reaction. If an inhibitor binds substrate or product appreciably, the distribution of bound and free species can change, but the intrinsic chemical equilibrium for free reactants remains governed by thermodynamics.

In cells, changes in flux can alter metabolite concentrations and thereby alter actual reaction free energy or the extent of coupling. This does not mean that the inhibitor changed the standard reaction free energy. Keep the chemical reaction, the rate of its catalyst and the behaviour of the surrounding open network conceptually separate.

Specific inhibition versus environmental modulation

Temperature changes both collision frequencies and the conformational stability of enzymes. pH changes protonation of catalytic residues and substrates. Ionic strength changes electrostatic interactions, and the availability of a metal cofactor can limit catalytic capacity. An apparent loss of enzyme activity after adding a metal-chelating compound could reflect removal of an essential cofactor rather than direct competitive binding to the substrate site. Restoring the cofactor under controlled conditions helps test that hypothesis. Detailed phosphorylation, zymogen activation, enzyme abundance and isozyme regulation belong to Node 1.D.05.

17. Design and interpret an inhibition experiment

A convincing mechanism assignment begins with experimental design. Prepare a substrate series spanning below Km, near Km and above Km, and measure initial product formation at each substrate concentration. Use several inhibitor concentrations including zero. Keep enzyme concentration, pH, temperature, ionic composition, solvent and total assay volume constant. Ensure that absorbance or fluorescence signals remain in the detector's linear range and that substrate consumption is small during the selected initial-rate window.

Step 1 — establish controls and baseline

Use a no-enzyme or appropriate no-substrate blank to detect nonenzymatic signal, an untreated or vehicle control to measure reference activity, and where applicable a known positive inhibitor as a method control. If the inhibitor is coloured, fluorescent, precipitates, or interferes with a coupled reporter enzyme, the readout can change without inhibition of the intended target. A second orthogonal readout or direct product measurement can resolve suspected interference. Verify that enzyme activity is proportional to enzyme amount in the working range.

Step 2 — inspect the time course

Plot product versus time for control and treated samples. A linear initial segment supports calculation of initial velocity. A treated curve that begins at the control slope and later flattens could reflect time-dependent inhibition, substrate depletion, product accumulation or instability. Repeat with matched control and different preincubation times. A change in the initial slope after preincubation is evidence of a time-dependent process, not proof of irreversible chemistry.

Step 3 — quantify the substrate dependence

Fit v versus [S] using the uninhibited model first. Then fit all datasets to candidate competitive, uncompetitive, pure noncompetitive and mixed models. Shared intrinsic Vmax and Km across inhibitor concentrations impose useful consistency. Inspect whether residuals appear random, estimate uncertainty in Ki and Ki′, and recognise when one parameter is poorly constrained. An apparent plateau estimated from data that never reach saturation is unreliable; a falsely reduced Vmax may make a competitive inhibitor look mixed.

Step 4 — test recovery and binding

Rapid dilution of the enzyme–inhibitor mixture lowers free inhibitor concentration; activity recovery supports reversible inhibition. Dialysis or gel filtration can remove small free molecules. Allow adequate time for slow-dissociating inhibitors to leave. A lack of recovery after appropriate removal and recovery time suggests persistent inactivation, but direct structural or mass evidence is needed to establish covalent modification. Substrate protection experiments can indicate overlapping access to the active site; they do not uniquely prove a competitive mechanism.

Step 5 — support molecular interpretation

Kinetic patterns report functional relationships between substrate, inhibitor and catalytic activity. Structural observations, binding measurements, mutagenesis or residue modification studies can support a molecular explanation. A mutant that loses inhibitor sensitivity may have lost direct contacts, or it may have changed conformational equilibria; check that basal enzyme activity and folding remain intact. A structural image of a ligand in a site by itself does not establish its kinetic category.

Scientific reasoning

A pattern suggests a model under its assumptions. A mechanism claim requires consistency between rate data, experimental controls, recovery behaviour and, when available, molecular evidence.

18. Data interpretation workshop: solve an unknown inhibitor

An enzyme is studied at 0, 2 and 6 µM inhibitor. Nonlinear fits to substrate series give the following idealized data. Treat the apparent parameters as accurate for this exercise; real estimates would have uncertainty.

[I] (µM)Vmax,app (µmol/min)Km,app (mM)Vmax,app/Km,app
0120430
2805.33315
6486.47.5

First infer the two factors independently

From the 2 µM experiment, α′ = Vmax/Vmax,app = 120/80 = 1.5. The Km ratio is 5.333/4 ≈ 1.333, which equals α/α′; hence α ≈ 2. At 6 µM, α′ = 120/48 = 2.5 and α = (6.4/4) × 2.5 = 4. Both factors increase with inhibitor and α is larger than α′, so the inhibitor binds both E and ES but prefers E.

Then calculate binding constants

Ki = [I]/(α − 1);   Ki′ = [I]/(α′ − 1)

At 2 µM, Ki = 2/(2 − 1) = 2 µM, and Ki′ = 2/(1.5 − 1) = 4 µM. At 6 µM, Ki = 6/(4 − 1) = 2 µM and Ki′ = 6/(2.5 − 1) = 4 µM. The independent datasets agree, supporting the simple mixed model.

Predict a new rate

At [I] = 4 µM, α = 3, α′ = 2. At [S] = 8 mM, v = 120 × 8/(3 × 4 + 2 × 8) = 960/28 ≈ 34.29 µmol/min. The uninhibited rate is 120 × 8/(4 + 8) = 80 µmol/min. Inhibition is approximately 57.14%. Predicted plateau is 60 µmol/min; apparent Km is 6 mM. The low-substrate rate ratio tends to 1/3, while the high-substrate ratio tends to 1/2.

Read the same dataset as a reciprocal plot

The control slope is 4/120 = 0.03333 and intercept is 1/120 = 0.008333. At 2 µM, slope is 5.333/80 ≈ 0.06667 and intercept is 0.0125. At 6 µM, slope is 6.4/48 = 0.13333 and intercept is 0.020833. Both increase; x-intercepts move from −0.25 to −0.1875 to −0.15625 mM⁻¹. The lines extrapolate to a common point x = −2/(4 × 4) = −0.125 mM⁻¹ and y = (1 − 2/4)/120 = 0.004167 min/µmol, above the horizontal axis. The numerical and graphical arguments tell the same story.

The workflow generalises: determine Vmax ratio, determine Km ratio, recover α and α′, calculate binding constants, and check whether new data follow the inferred model. This is more robust than memorising which line looks like which pattern.

19. CSIR-NET Part B practice — concept checks

These original practice questions are aligned to the conceptual level of CSIR-NET Life Sciences and State SET; they are not claimed to be previous-year questions. Attempt each before opening its explanation.

1. A competitive inhibitor in the classical model changes which parameter?
  1. Vmax only
  2. Km,app only
  3. Both equally
  4. Neither

Answer: B

α increases and α′ remains one. Apparent Km rises by α but the same maximal rate can be approached at high substrate.

2. An inhibitor binds exclusively to ES. Which pattern follows?
  1. Competitive
  2. Pure noncompetitive
  3. Uncompetitive
  4. Irreversible

Answer: C

ESI formation sequesters occupied enzyme. Both Km,app and Vmax,app fall by α′.

3. A pure noncompetitive inhibitor has which binding relationship?
  1. Ki = Ki′
  2. Ki′ = ∞
  3. Ki = ∞
  4. Ki = 0

Answer: A

Equal affinity for E and ES gives equal α and α′, so apparent Km is unchanged.

4. Which plot gives parallel lines for ideal uncompetitive inhibition?
  1. v versus [S]
  2. Lineweaver–Burk
  3. Eadie–Hofstee
  4. Hanes–Woolf

Answer: B

The reciprocal slope is αKm/Vmax. Uncompetitive inhibition has α = 1, so the slopes remain equal while intercepts rise.

5. A smaller apparent Km necessarily demonstrates stronger microscopic substrate binding. True or false?
  1. True in every enzyme system
  2. False; Km is a kinetic parameter
  3. True only for competitive inhibition
  4. True only for irreversible inhibition

Answer: B

Km is not generally Kd. Uncompetitive inhibition can lower Km,app by sequestering ES without altering the original microscopic substrate dissociation constant.

6. Which quantity is dimensionless?
  1. Ki
  2. kcat
  3. α
  4. Vmax

Answer: C

α = 1 + [I]/Ki; both concentrations have the same units. kcat is time⁻¹ and Vmax is rate.

7. A reversible inhibitor binds free enzyme much more strongly than ES, but binds both. What is expected?
  1. Vmax decreases, Km increases
  2. Vmax increases, Km decreases
  3. Vmax unchanged, Km unchanged
  4. Both increase

Answer: A

Ki is smaller than Ki′, so α > α′ > 1. Vmax falls due to α′, Km rises due to α/α′.

8. Which experiment most directly distinguishes rapid reversible inhibition from persistent inactivation?
  1. Measure enzyme mass only
  2. Remove free inhibitor and test activity recovery
  3. Raise enzyme temperature to boiling
  4. Measure substrate colour

Answer: B

Recovery after removal supports reversibility, provided sufficient time is allowed for dissociation.

9. What is the usual effect of high substrate on competitive IC50?
  1. Decreases
  2. Unchanged
  3. Increases
  4. Always becomes zero

Answer: C

IC50 = Ki(1 + [S]/Km) in the ideal competitive case.

10. Which property distinguishes a mechanism-based inactivator from a generic affinity label?
  1. It must bind outside the active site
  2. The enzyme's catalytic processing generates the inactivating species
  3. It never binds reversibly
  4. It changes the reaction equilibrium constant

Answer: B

Catalytic processing is the defining feature. A recognition step may be reversible before inactivation.

11. At [S] much smaller than Km, which ideal inhibitor retains the original slope of the saturation curve?
  1. Competitive
  2. Pure noncompetitive
  3. Uncompetitive
  4. Any mixed inhibitor

Answer: C

Vmax,app and Km,app decrease by the same factor, so Vmax/Km remains unchanged.

12. Which statement about a distant regulatory binding site is correct?
  1. It always produces pure noncompetitive inhibition
  2. It never affects substrate binding
  3. Its kinetic category must be determined experimentally
  4. It always creates irreversible inhibition

Answer: C

Structural location alone does not establish inhibitor preference or kinetic pattern.

13. The equilibrium constant for the free S ⇌ P reaction is changed by a simple reversible enzyme inhibitor.
  1. True
  2. False

Answer: B

Catalysis and inhibition change rates and catalytic pathways, not the intrinsic thermodynamic equilibrium constant for free reactants.

14. An enzyme shows 40% activity remaining in a blank-corrected assay. Percent inhibition is:
  1. 40%
  2. 60%
  3. 140%
  4. Cannot be calculated

Answer: B

Percent inhibition = 100 × (1 − 0.40) = 60%.

15. A decrease in rate at very high substrate with no added inhibitor suggests which model, if artifacts are excluded?
  1. Pure competitive inhibition
  2. Substrate inhibition
  3. Simple Michaelis–Menten saturation only
  4. A higher Vmax

Answer: B

A second nonproductive substrate-binding event can create an inactive ESS species, causing the high-substrate rate to decrease.

20. CSIR-NET Part C practice — analytical and numerical reasoning

These original problems require interpreting assumptions, calculating parameters, and comparing mechanistic alternatives. All numeric values are hypothetical teaching data unless explicitly identified otherwise.

1. Vmax = 90 units and Km = 3 mM. A competitive inhibitor has Ki = 2 µM, [I] = 4 µM. Find rate at 9 mM substrate.
  1. 22.5
  2. 45
  3. 67.5
  4. 90

Answer: B — 45

α = 3 and Km,app = 9 mM. At [S] equal to apparent Km, v = Vmax/2 = 45.

2. Control Km = 8 mM and Vmax = 100. Inhibitor changes them to 2 mM and 25. What is the most consistent classical model?
  1. Competitive
  2. Pure noncompetitive
  3. Uncompetitive
  4. E-preferring mixed

Answer: C

Both fall fourfold, so α′ = 4 and α = (2/8) × 4 = 1. ES-only binding gives the ideal uncompetitive signature.

3. At [I] = 6 µM, Vmax changes 80 → 20 and Km changes 4 → 3. Determine Ki and Ki′.
  1. Ki = 3 µM, Ki′ = 2 µM
  2. Ki = 2 µM, Ki′ = 3 µM
  3. Ki = Ki′ = 2 µM
  4. Ki = 6 µM, Ki′ = 6 µM

Answer: A

α′ = 4, α = (3/4) × 4 = 3. Ki = 6/(3 − 1) = 3, Ki′ = 6/(4 − 1) = 2. The inhibitor prefers ES.

4. A reciprocal plot has control slope 2 and y-intercept 0.5. The treated line has slope 6 and the same y-intercept. Calculate apparent Km.
  1. 1
  2. 4
  3. 8
  4. 12

Answer: D

The control Km is slope/intercept = 4. Treated Km,app = 6/0.5 = 12. Unchanged intercept means unchanged Vmax.

5. A competitive inhibitor has IC50 = 36 µM at [S] = 5Km. Predict IC50 at [S] = Km.
  1. 6 µM
  2. 12 µM
  3. 18 µM
  4. 36 µM

Answer: B

Ki = 36/(1 + 5) = 6 µM. At [S] = Km, IC50 = 2Ki = 12 µM.

6. Uncompetitive inhibitor Ki′ = 4 µM. At [S] = Km/2, find IC50.
  1. 2 µM
  2. 4 µM
  3. 8 µM
  4. 12 µM

Answer: D

IC50 = Ki′(1 + Km/[S]) = 4(1 + 2) = 12 µM. This is the opposite substrate trend to competition.

7. A persistent inactivator has kinact = 0.30 min⁻¹, KI = 10 µM. At [I] = 20 µM, what remains after 10 min?
  1. 13.5%
  2. 36.8%
  3. 50%
  4. 86.5%

Answer: A

kobs = 0.30 × 20/(10 + 20) = 0.20 min⁻¹. Remaining fraction = e⁻² = 0.135.

8. An inhibitor lowers Vmax by 50% while leaving Km unchanged. Activity fails to recover shortly after dilution. Which conclusion is justified?
  1. Pure noncompetitive inhibition is proved
  2. Covalent inhibition is proved
  3. Persistent loss of activity is supported; further recovery and molecular tests are needed
  4. The inhibitor must be competitive

Answer: C

Both reduction in active enzyme amount and equal-affinity noncompetitive binding can give this parameter pattern. Slow dissociation may also explain failure of rapid recovery.

9. A mixed inhibitor has α = 5 and α′ = 2. What fraction of control activity remains in the two substrate limits?
  1. Low 0.2, high 0.5
  2. Low 0.5, high 0.2
  3. Both 0.2
  4. Both 0.5

Answer: A

The activity ratio tends to 1/α at low substrate and 1/α′ at high substrate. The inhibitor prefers free enzyme.

10. The simple substrate inhibition equation has Km = 4 mM and KSI = 25 mM. At what substrate concentration does rate peak?
  1. 4 mM
  2. 10 mM
  3. 25 mM
  4. 29 mM

Answer: B

For the specified model, peak [S] = √(Km KSI) = √100 = 10 mM.

11. An enzyme concentration is 20 nM. A tight-binding 1:1 inhibitor is added to 8 nM with negligible Kd, assuming inactive EI. Approximate maximal immediate inhibition?
  1. 8%
  2. 20%
  3. 40%
  4. 100%

Answer: C

In the tight-binding stoichiometric limit at most 8 of 20 nM enzyme can be occupied, or 40%. The free inhibitor approximation is invalid.

12. Assertion: Increasing substrate cannot restore the original Vmax under ideal uncompetitive inhibition. Reason: The inhibitor binds ES and traps it as inactive ESI.
  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true

Answer: A

High substrate generates ES but inhibitor can bind that ES; α′ remains greater than one and maximal rate remains reduced.

13. A secondary plot of Lineweaver–Burk slopes versus inhibitor concentration is horizontal, while the intercept plot increases. Which ideal mechanism is indicated?
  1. Competitive
  2. Uncompetitive
  3. Pure noncompetitive
  4. Mixed with equal binding affinities

Answer: B

Slope is proportional to α. No change in slope means no measurable binding to free enzyme under the classical model, but the increased intercept indicates ES binding.

14. Control initial slope Vmax/Km is 24. An inhibitor gives apparent Vmax = 48 and Km = 4. If [I] = 3 µM, find Ki from these data.
  1. 1 µM
  2. 3 µM
  3. 6 µM
  4. Ki′ only can be found

Answer: B

Inhibited initial slope is 48/4 = 12, half control. α = 24/12 = 2. Ki = 3/(2 − 1) = 3 µM. Ki′ requires the original Vmax.

15. For a mixed inhibitor, the Lineweaver–Burk family crosses the horizontal axis at a point left of the origin. Under the ideal general model what does this imply?
  1. Ki = Ki′
  2. Ki′ = ∞
  3. Ki = ∞
  4. Ki > Ki′ necessarily

Answer: A

At intersection y = (1 − Ki/Ki′)/Vmax = 0, requiring equal finite binding constants in the simple model.

16. A reaction with control Vmax 100 and Km 5 is assayed after adding an inhibitor. Vmax,app = 50 and Km,app = 5 at [I] = 10 µM. Predict v at [S] = 15 mM using the ideal reversible fit.
  1. 25
  2. 37.5
  3. 50
  4. 75

Answer: B

Apparent Vmax is 50, and v = 50 × 15/(5 + 15) = 37.5. The control would give 75. The kinetic fit is pure noncompetitive-like, but the underlying chemistry still requires a recovery test.

17. A data series has control v = 20 at low [S] and 95 near saturation; inhibitor gives 19 at low [S] and 32 near saturation. Which follow-up best tests an uncompetitive explanation?
  1. Measure only one extra point near saturation
  2. Fit a wide substrate series and compare Vmax/Km ratios
  3. Remove substrate from the assay entirely and compare final colour
  4. Assume covalent reaction

Answer: B

Little relative inhibition at low substrate and strong inhibition at high substrate suggest ES preference. Test proportional decreases of the two apparent parameters and the initial slope across concentrations.

18. Two inhibitors have equal IC50 at [S] = Km. One is competitive, one uncompetitive. Which will have lower IC50 when substrate increases to 9Km?
  1. Competitive
  2. Uncompetitive
  3. Both remain equal
  4. Cannot compare in the ideal model

Answer: B

At Km both IC50 values are twice their respective binding constants, so Ki = Ki′ in this example. At 9Km competitive IC50 = 10Ki, uncompetitive IC50 = (10/9)Ki′.

21. Revision map, common mistakes and references

Reconstruct the entire chapter from the enzyme mass balance. Free enzyme and ES form the productive pathway. Inhibitor binding to E contributes α and binding to ES contributes α′. The classical equation v = Vmax[S]/(αKm + α′[S]) immediately determines apparent parameters, graphical signatures and substrate rescue. A time-dependent decline in active enzyme requires a separate kinetic description. When inhibitors alter more complex enzyme states, the simple hyperbola may no longer be adequate.

Key questionWhat to recall
What sets the plateau?Vmax,app = Vmax/α′
What sets the apparent midpoint?Km,app = αKm/α′
What sets the low-substrate slope?Vmax/(αKm)
What distinguishes a competitive inhibitor?α > 1, α′ = 1
What distinguishes uncompetitive inhibition?α = 1, α′ > 1
What distinguishes pure noncompetitive?α = α′ > 1
What distinguishes general mixed?Both factors > 1, unequal
What sets persistent inactivation half-life?ln 2/kobs for a simple first-order process
When is IC50 not Ki?Generally when assay conditions influence response; equality requires special assumptions

Common examination traps

Do not equate a low Km with high catalytic efficiency without considering Vmax or kcat. Do not infer a covalent reaction from an irreversible-looking short assay. Do not infer pure noncompetitive inhibition from the fact that a compound binds outside the active site. Do not use Lineweaver–Burk slope as Km directly. Do not interpret an extrapolated negative concentration as a physical measurement. Do not apply Cheng–Prusoff to every inhibitor type. Do not identify a mechanism from one substrate concentration or one inhibitor concentration. Do not confuse inhibition of catalytic rate with changing the intrinsic equilibrium constant.
