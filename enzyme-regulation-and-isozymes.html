1. Why catalysis alone is not enough

Imagine a muscle cell that has just begun climbing a staircase. A few seconds ago, its demand for ATP was low. Now ATP is being consumed by contraction, and the cell must increase the supply of usable fuel. It cannot wait for a new set of enzymes to be manufactured. Yet when the exercise stops, continuing to break down stored fuel at the same rate would waste resources. The machinery must be adjustable while it is working. This is the problem solved by enzyme regulation.

The preceding chapters explained how an active site binds substrates, lowers the activation barrier and produces a measurable reaction rate. They also showed how the rate changes when substrate concentration, inhibitors or the conformation of an enzyme change. Here we use those ideas in a living system. The question is no longer simply how quickly a purified enzyme can work, but how the cell decides which reaction should work, how fast it should work, where it should work and when it should stop.

A metabolic pathway is a sequence of reactions connected through shared intermediates. In a pathway A → B → C → D, B is both the product of one reaction and the substrate of the next. An enzyme making B cannot be understood entirely in isolation: if B is consumed slowly, its concentration may rise, and that accumulation can affect both the preceding and following reactions. The useful output is the net rate of material flowing through the sequence, called metabolic flux. An increased concentration of an intermediate does not necessarily mean an increased flux. A blocked drain can raise the water level even when the incoming flow has fallen.

Cells regulate flux for several related reasons. They avoid wasteful production of metabolites that are already plentiful; coordinate competing branches that use the same substrate; preserve ATP and reducing equivalents; and direct reactions into the compartments and tissues where their products are needed. These objectives are linked because molecules are limited resources. Making an unnecessary amino acid not only accumulates that amino acid but also consumes precursors, nitrogen and energy that could support other cellular processes.

It is useful to distinguish regulation from a mere change of rate. Heating a purified enzyme may increase its rate over part of its temperature range; that does not by itself establish a physiological regulatory mechanism. Regulation means that a biological input such as a metabolite concentration, a signal, a modification or a change in enzyme amount controls the behaviour of the system. The same physical laws of chemistry govern both situations, but a regulated pathway connects the chemical response to a biological purpose.

There are three questions to keep asking throughout this chapter. Has the number of enzyme molecules changed? Has the activity of each molecule changed? Has the enzyme gained or lost access to its substrates? An answer to one of these questions is not automatically an answer to the others. A cell may contain a large amount of an inactive precursor, a normal amount of a strongly inhibited enzyme, or a highly active enzyme that is separated from its substrate by a membrane. All three may show little reaction flux for different reasons.

Learning goal

Connect molecular changes to reaction rates and then to pathway behaviour. Separate rapid regulation of existing enzyme, slower control of enzyme abundance and tissue-specific expression of isozyme forms.

2. From enzyme molecules to measurable activity

For a simple enzyme under initial-rate Michaelis–Menten conditions, the rate is v = kcat[E]active[S]/(Km + [S]). Here [E]active is the molar concentration of catalytically competent sites, [S] is substrate concentration, kcat has units of inverse time, and Km has concentration units. This relation applies to a simple saturable system under the assumptions developed earlier; it is not a universal rate law for cooperative, multisubstrate or rapidly interconverting enzymes.

Suppose the cell contains 100 nanomolar enzyme sites, but only 40% are in a state that is competent for catalysis. The active concentration is 40 nanomolar. If kcat is 20 s−1 and the substrate saturates the enzyme, the maximum velocity is 800 nanomolar product per second, or 0.8 micromolar per second. The protein concentration can remain unchanged while the active fraction rises to 80%; the maximum velocity would then double to 1.6 micromolar per second. If an activity measurement alone were made, this could be mistaken for a doubling of enzyme abundance.

Not every regulated enzyme has a strictly inactive and active form. Often two forms each catalyse the same reaction at different rates or with different apparent substrate affinities. If the forms act independently and their fractions do not change during an initial-rate assay, their velocities add. For two forms A and B, v = [E]T(fAkcat,A[S]/(Km,A+[S]) + (1−fA)kcat,B[S]/(Km,B+[S])). The total enzyme concentration [E]T is fixed in this example. A modification that changes the fraction fA can therefore change the shape of the rate curve as well as its plateau.

Consider a simple example in which both forms have the same Km of 2 mM but A has turnover 30 s−1 and B has turnover 6 s−1. At [S] = 2 mM each operates at half its own maximum. For [E]T = 1 μM and fA = 0.25, the velocity is (0.25 × 30 + 0.75 × 6)/2 = 6 μM s−1. At fA = 0.75 the velocity is (0.75 × 30 + 0.25 × 6)/2 = 12 μM s−1. We have doubled activity without increasing protein abundance or changing substrate supply. A population of enzymes is often better understood as a collection of molecular states than as an on/off switch.

There is another important distinction: the maximum velocity of a purified reaction is a measure of capacity, not necessarily the rate of that reaction in vivo. In a real cell, concentration of substrate, product, cofactors, local pH, competing reactions and compartment barriers matter. Even if an enzyme is almost fully active in a test tube, it may carry little net flux in a cell whose substrate is scarce or whose reaction is close to equilibrium.

An enzyme changes the speed with which a reaction approaches equilibrium but not the equilibrium constant. Regulation may shift the balance between different reactions or alter concentrations so that a pathway operates further from equilibrium. It does not allow an enzyme to violate thermodynamics. ATP-consuming covalent modification is an additional coupled chemical process; energy is expended to control the state of the protein, rather than to change the intrinsic equilibrium constant of its target reaction.

Activity is often expressed as enzyme units, where one conventional U is one micromole of product formed per minute under stated assay conditions. Specific activity in U per milligram total protein is useful for extracts and purification, but its denominator includes many proteins. A rise in specific activity can reflect greater relative abundance of the enzyme, activation of the enzyme, or removal of irrelevant proteins. When the amount of the particular enzyme is independently known, activity per unit of that enzyme gives a better basis for distinguishing abundance from catalytic modulation.

3. Pathway organisation: feedback, feedforward and branch points

Consider a four-step pathway that makes a required end product P. When demand for P increases, enzymes in the pathway replenish it. Once demand falls, P accumulates. If high P reduces the activity of an upstream enzyme, its own production slows. When P is consumed again, its inhibitory effect diminishes and production increases. This arrangement is called negative feedback because the effect of the response opposes the original change. It is particularly useful because the pathway senses the supply of the very molecule it is producing.

In simple unbranched pathways, the first committed step is often a good place for regulation. It converts a precursor into a molecule that is substantially committed to a particular biosynthetic route. It is not necessarily the first reaction drawn on a diagram. If precursor A is used for several products and A → B is shared by several pathways, inhibiting that reaction every time one product is abundant could deprive the other pathways. A reaction B → C that enters only the pathway of interest may be the more appropriate target. Regulation at the committed step prevents costly accumulation of unnecessary intermediates while preserving precursor availability for other purposes.

Feedback inhibition must be distinguished from immediate product inhibition. In the reaction A → B, B may bind the enzyme that makes B and slow the reaction. In end-product feedback, the inhibitor may be several reactions downstream; D might bind the enzyme converting A to B even though D is neither its substrate nor its immediate product. The two can overlap when the product being sensed is produced directly by the regulated enzyme. Identifying the inhibitor's position in the pathway resolves much of the confusion.

Feedback usually acts on existing enzyme molecules. A high end-product concentration can also reduce transcription of genes encoding enzymes of its own synthesis. That is feedback repression at the level of expression. It may have the same overall consequence but a different time course: an existing pool of active enzyme does not vanish simply because transcription stops. Cells often use the two mechanisms together, rapidly reducing activity while later reducing the amount of protein they must maintain.

Now consider the opposite direction of communication. An upstream metabolite can stimulate a downstream enzyme before large amounts of its direct substrate arrive. This is feedforward activation. The abundance of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate in glycolysis stimulates certain pyruvate kinase isoforms; it signals that carbon is moving through the upper pathway and prepares the final ATP-producing step. Feedforward can coordinate sequential rates but does not automatically stabilise an end product. Feedback and feedforward are distinguished by the direction of information relative to material flow, not by whether the regulator is an activator or inhibitor.

A branched pathway must satisfy two demands at once. In a schematic A → B → X and B → Y, high X should inhibit the branch leading to X more strongly than the branch leading to Y. If both X and Y are abundant, the common upstream pathway can be suppressed to conserve A. Real pathways achieve such control through separate branch enzymes, separate feedback-sensitive isoforms of a common enzyme, or multiple effectors acting on an enzyme. These designs are useful because the same precursor can support several essential products without granting any single product unlimited control over the shared supply.

Classical microbial aspartate-family amino-acid metabolism provides a concrete example. In many bacteria, aspartate kinase commits aspartate toward a family of biosynthetic routes producing amino acids such as lysine, threonine and methionine. In Escherichia coli, multiple aspartate kinase forms have distinct regulatory responses: one is associated with threonine-sensitive control, another with lysine-sensitive control, and expression-level control also participates. The point is not to memorise every enzyme of those pathways at this stage. It is to understand how separate molecular forms at a common reaction can allow one biosynthetic product to limit its own supply without completely shutting off the others.

Feedback may be graded rather than complete. If a residual flux remains after inhibition, it may be sufficient to compensate for continued consumption of the product. A regulatory mutation can also make an enzyme insensitive to its end product without destroying catalysis. Such a mutation can cause overproduction when other conditions permit. The direction of change in product pool is often predictable; the exact magnitude is not, because substrate supply and other controlled steps may become limiting.

Do not confuse first step and first committed step

At a branch, the best regulatory target may be a later branch-specific reaction. The first step on a pathway diagram need not be exclusively committed to that product.

4. Allosteric regulation: how a distant ligand changes the active site

The physical basis of many rapid feedback systems is allostery. Proteins are flexible structures that can occupy several conformations. When an effector binds a site that is functionally coupled to the catalytic site, its binding can change the probability of those conformations, alter local geometry or influence substrate binding and turnover. The ligand need not resemble the catalytic substrate. The relevant property is energetic coupling: a binding event at one site changes what happens at another.

In the earlier chapter on cooperative kinetics, the concerted and sequential models described how occupancy and conformation could influence one another. That foundation helps interpret regulatory effectors, but cooperative substrate binding and allostery are not identical concepts. A homotropic effect occurs when a substrate or ligand influences the binding or activity of additional molecules of the same kind. A heterotropic effect occurs when a different molecule influences the response. A monomeric enzyme can be regulated allosterically; multiple identical subunits are not a requirement for communication between distinct sites. Likewise, a sigmoidal activity curve alone is not proof of one particular structural model.

Allosteric effectors may affect the apparent substrate concentration required for half-maximal velocity, the maximal rate, or both. For a cooperative enzyme, S0.5 or K0.5 is often a more suitable empirical parameter than Km, because the Michaelis–Menten mechanism is not assumed. Consider an enzyme with equal maximum velocity in the absence and presence of an activator, but with half-maximal activity at 4 mM and 1 mM substrate, respectively. At 1 mM substrate the activator increases the rate, whereas at sufficiently high substrate the curves may converge. This is primarily a shift in substrate responsiveness. If a regulator changes the plateau even at saturating substrate, it affects maximum catalytic capacity under those assay conditions. Many real enzymes display a mixture of effects.

A common examination error is to equate allosteric inhibition with pure noncompetitive inhibition. Allosteric is a description of a molecular regulatory mechanism. Competitive, uncompetitive, mixed and pure noncompetitive are kinetic patterns defined under a particular rate model. A regulatory ligand may produce a competitive-like shift, a mixed change, altered sigmoidicity or a more complex response. A remote binding site is not sufficient evidence that apparent Km will remain unchanged. Conversely, equal apparent Km with reduced Vmax under a simple model does not reveal the structural location of the inhibitor.

There are two useful ways to test allosteric coupling. First, compare activity as a function of substrate concentration at several effector concentrations while keeping enzyme amount, pH and temperature fixed. This separates changes in capacity from changes in responsiveness. Second, perturb a regulatory site, ideally without disrupting the catalytic site or overall fold. If the mutant retains basal catalysis but no longer responds to the regulator, that provides evidence for separation of catalytic and regulatory functions. If the mutant loses all activity, one must first exclude protein misfolding or loss of catalytic-site integrity before inferring a regulatory role.

For cooperative enzymes, one can use the empirical Hill equation v/Vmax = [S]n/ (S0.5n + [S]n) to compare responses over a limited range. It describes curve steepness using n, the apparent Hill coefficient; it does not establish a detailed mechanism. For an illustrative enzyme with n = 2, S0.5 = 4 mM and Vmax = 100 units, at [S] = 2 mM the rate is 100 × 4/(16 + 4) = 20 units. If an activator reduces S0.5 to 2 mM without changing the other parameters, at the same concentration the rate becomes 50 units. A two-fold shift in S0.5 here produces a 2.5-fold rate increase. The model is useful for this comparison, but do not treat n = 2 as proof of exactly two substrate-binding sites.

A small metabolite can consequently act as information rather than simply as reactant. ATP, AMP, citrate and various amino acids are examples of molecules that participate in chemistry in some reactions and serve as effectors in others. Whether the molecule is consumed or simply binds reversibly depends on the enzyme and site. This dual use allows the cell's own chemical composition to communicate its metabolic state without a separate signalling system.

5. ATCase: classical evidence for feedback and allostery

A particularly informative example comes from bacterial pyrimidine biosynthesis. Aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase) catalyses the reaction of carbamoyl phosphate and L-aspartate to produce N-carbamoyl-L-aspartate and inorganic phosphate. This reaction begins the committed sequence leading to pyrimidine nucleotides in E. coli. If the cell already has abundant pyrimidine nucleotides, continued high flux through ATCase would consume precursors without meeting an immediate need.

Classical studies by Yates and Pardee in the 1950s helped establish end-product feedback in this pathway. Subsequent work on purified ATCase revealed that CTP, a pyrimidine nucleotide produced downstream, inhibits the enzyme. ATP, a purine nucleotide, can activate it. This is conceptually striking: CTP is not one of the two reacting substrates, yet it changes the enzyme's rate. ATP is not consumed in the ATCase reaction; it acts here as a regulatory ligand. The opposing responses help coordinate pyrimidine synthesis with purine availability, although the full intracellular response depends on several metabolites and effectors.

The E. coli enzyme is a dodecamer with six catalytic and six regulatory chains organised as two catalytic trimers and three regulatory dimers. Aspartate and carbamoyl phosphate bind catalytic sites, whereas CTP and ATP bind nucleotide regulatory sites. The oligomer can adopt less active T-like and more active R-like conformations; binding substrates favours catalytically competent conformations, CTP tends to favour the inhibited state and ATP favours activation. The exact structural and kinetic details are richer than a two-state diagram, but the model captures the essential connection between ligand binding and regulation.

The classic separation of the catalytic and regulatory components made it possible to test whether catalysis and feedback were separable functions. The catalytic trimers retain catalytic activity without the regulatory dimers, but lose the characteristic nucleotide regulation and the cooperative behaviour of the intact enzyme. This finding is more powerful than an observation that CTP slows a reaction: it links the regulatory response to a distinct structural component. The conclusion depends on confirming that the isolated catalytic component remains correctly folded and active.

A simple experiment helps translate this into examination reasoning. Prepare equal amounts of catalytic sites in intact enzyme and isolated catalytic trimers. Measure initial velocity at a range of aspartate concentrations, with fixed carbamoyl phosphate, with and without CTP. An intact enzyme with intact regulation may show decreased velocity in the presence of CTP over a range of substrate concentrations. A catalytically competent isolated trimer that does not bind CTP in its regulatory sites should show little of that characteristic inhibition. If both preparations are inactive, the experiment does not distinguish regulation from destruction of catalytic activity.

This example also shows why an increase in substrate concentration may partly compensate for an allosteric inhibitor without making the inhibitor a competitive substrate analogue. The substrate and effector influence the same conformational ensemble from different sites. When substrate stabilises an active state, high substrate can oppose an inhibitor that favours an inactive state. The shape of the curve reflects these coupled equilibria and catalytic steps; a simple reciprocal plot alone may not identify the physical mechanism.

ATCase is a bacterial example. Mammalian pyrimidine biosynthesis has a different organisational context, with several early activities present in the multifunctional CAD protein and regulation distributed differently. Do not carry every bacterial subunit count or nucleotide effect over to all organisms. For NET-level reasoning, the enduring lesson of bacterial ATCase is that separate catalytic and regulatory sites connect pathway demand to a controllable reaction rate.

6. Metabolite signals and energy demand

An enzyme can respond directly to the chemical state of its environment. ATP is both a major energy-transfer molecule and, at suitable concentrations, an indicator that energy supply is relatively abundant. ADP and AMP tend to rise relative to ATP when energy is being used. Through adenylate kinase, 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP, modest changes in ADP relative to ATP can lead to pronounced changes in AMP. A protein that is responsive to AMP can thus detect energy demand without measuring ATP hydrolysis directly.

The adenylate energy charge provides a useful numerical description: energy charge = ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]). It is dimensionless and lies between zero and one for nonnegative adenylate concentrations with a nonzero total pool. ATP contributes one, ADP one-half and AMP zero. It summarises the distribution of phosphorylated adenylates; it is not itself a reaction equilibrium constant or a direct substitute for the free energy of ATP hydrolysis.

If ATP, ADP and AMP are 8, 1.5 and 0.5 mM, the energy charge is (8 + 0.75)/10 = 0.875. Suppose ATP falls to 6 mM while ADP and AMP become 3 and 1 mM. The charge is (6 + 1.5)/10 = 0.75. The total adenylate concentration is unchanged, but the distribution is different. A cell facing such an energetic change may activate fuel breakdown while reducing energy-consuming biosynthesis. The actual response also depends on compartment and organism.

Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) is a useful illustration. It catalyses the ATP-dependent phosphorylation of fructose 6-phosphate to fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. In common mammalian contexts, ATP is a substrate at the catalytic site but can also inhibit at high concentrations through a regulatory interaction. AMP and fructose 2,6-bisphosphate promote activity, and citrate can support inhibition. A single molecule can therefore have opposite effects depending on site and concentration. This does not mean that ATP binding to the catalytic site prevents catalysis; rather, ATP participates in the reaction while its regulatory binding signals a different metabolic condition.

Imagine plotting initial velocity against ATP at fixed fructose 6-phosphate. Very low ATP limits reaction supply. Intermediate ATP provides the substrate needed for phosphorylation. At sufficiently high ATP, regulatory inhibition can reduce the rate in a responsive enzyme preparation. Such a curve is not described by a simple one-substrate saturation equation across the whole range. It also does not imply that ATP always inhibits all enzymes, or that a low ATP concentration is intrinsically favourable. A suitable regulatory response must balance the need for ATP as a chemical substrate with ATP as a signal.

A second example is the muscle form of glycogen phosphorylase, which can be activated by AMP. When demand is low, ATP and glucose 6-phosphate oppose this activation. When energy is consumed, AMP can stimulate breakdown of glycogen stores by favouring a more active state of the enzyme. This can occur even before a hormone-dependent phosphorylation cascade has reached its full effect. The detailed reactions of glycogen and glycolysis belong to the next subunit; here the important point is that metabolites can directly and rapidly alter the activity of existing enzymes.

PFK-1 and glycogen phosphorylase also illustrate the importance of context. Effectors have tissue- and isoform-specific actions; for example the liver glycogen phosphorylase has a particularly important response to glucose. A general rule about one muscle enzyme should not be extrapolated blindly to all homologous proteins. Regulation must be inferred from the actual enzyme, regulatory inputs and biological conditions.

7. Reversible covalent modification: building a molecular switch

Allosteric binding changes activity as long as the ligand and enzyme remain associated or conformational effects persist. A different strategy is to change the covalent structure of the enzyme itself. In reversible covalent modification, a chemical group is added to or removed from particular residues. The modified and unmodified proteins can differ in conformation, charge, interaction partners, localisation, stability or catalytic properties. Their relative amounts can be controlled by distinct enzymes, creating a regulated interconversion cycle.

The most familiar example is phosphorylation of proteins. A protein kinase transfers the terminal phosphoryl group of ATP to a suitable acceptor, commonly the hydroxyl group of serine, threonine or tyrosine in eukaryotic signalling. The simplified net reaction is protein–OH + ATP → protein–O–PO32− + ADP, with protonation states depending on pH. A protein phosphatase hydrolyses the phosphate ester, producing the unmodified hydroxyl group and inorganic phosphate. It does not generally reverse the kinase reaction by converting ADP back into ATP. In bacteria, additional signalling mechanisms can involve phosphorylated histidine and aspartate; their chemistry differs from the stable O-phosphorylation example.

The addition of phosphate introduces a bulky, negatively charged group. It can form new ionic interactions with basic amino acids, disrupt a previously favourable contact, alter loop positions, expose a binding surface or rearrange a regulatory domain. Sometimes it opens an active site; sometimes it constrains the enzyme in a less active conformation. There is no universal law that phosphorylated means active. The modification must be interpreted relative to the structure of the specific protein.

A phosphorylation cycle also differs from simple equilibrium binding. Maintaining a steady distribution of phosphorylated and unphosphorylated protein can require continued ATP consumption because kinase and phosphatase may both be active. A stationary fraction of modified protein does not mean molecules have stopped interconverting. In the simplest cycle, one ATP is consumed for each completed phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle. The chemical free energy supports directional control of protein state. This explains how a cell can maintain a regulated state even when it is not the passive equilibrium state of the unmodified protein.

The classic experimental work on glycogen phosphorylase established the physiological importance of phosphorylation and dephosphorylation. In the 1950s, studies by Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs showed that ATP-dependent conversion of phosphorylase between forms could be understood as a reversible chemical modification. These findings made it possible to explain how hormones and intracellular signals change enzyme activity without replacing the existing protein. The key conclusion was based on chemical conversion and reversible activity changes rather than on an assumption that all regulated enzymes must have different amino-acid sequences.

Other reversible covalent mechanisms include adenylylation, acetylation and certain redox modifications. E. coli glutamine synthetase is a textbook example of regulation by reversible adenylylation: AMP groups can be added to subunits, altering enzyme activity and sensitivity to effectors in response to nitrogen status. This is chemically distinct from addition of a phosphoryl group. In plants, light-dependent thioredoxin systems can reduce regulatory disulfide bonds in several chloroplast enzymes, coordinating activity with the supply of light-generated reducing power. The detailed signalling networks and pathways can be studied in their appropriate units; the common principle is that a chemically modified enzyme can carry a regulatory state.

An activity change after ATP addition does not alone establish protein phosphorylation. ATP might be a direct substrate or effector. Evidence for phosphorylation includes detection of the modified protein, dependence on a kinase or phosphate donor, and reversal by an appropriate phosphatase. A mutation replacing the target serine with alanine can prevent phosphorylation at that site, but it may also alter local structure. Replacing serine with aspartate or glutamate sometimes approximates a negative charge, yet it is not a faithful chemical replacement for phosphate in every protein. A strong interpretation combines these perturbations with controls for protein amount, folding and direct catalytic activity.

8. Quantifying a phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle

A useful model treats an enzyme pool as two interconverting states, E and EP. Suppose the total concentration ET = E + EP remains constant over the measurement, and the effective first-order conversion constants are a for phosphorylation and b for dephosphorylation, both in s−1. This approximation may be reasonable when the modifying enzymes act in a range where conversion rate is proportional to their target state concentration. It is not a full description of saturating kinases and phosphatases.

Define f = [EP]/[E]T. The unmodified fraction is 1−f. In an interval dt, a fraction a(1−f)dt is added to the modified pool and a fraction bf dt leaves. Therefore df/dt = a(1−f) − bf = a − (a+b)f. At steady state, df/dt = 0, so a(1−f) = bf. Solving gives fss = a/(a+b). Notice that both rates matter: a fixed kinase activity can support different steady-state phosphorylation levels depending on phosphatase activity.

If a = 0.06 s−1 and b = 0.02 s−1, fss = 0.75. If the phosphatase is activated so that b becomes 0.06 s−1 with the kinase unchanged, the modified fraction falls to 0.50. The result does not say whether total target-enzyme activity rises or falls; that depends on which state has the higher activity. In glycogen phosphorylase the phosphorylated form is generally more active under the relevant conditions, whereas for glycogen synthase phosphorylation generally reduces activity.

Subtract the steady-state fraction from f and the equation becomes d(f−fss)/dt = −(a+b)(f−fss). Integrating yields f(t) = fss + (f(0)−fss)e−(a+b)t. The time constant is 1/(a+b), and the time to halve the distance from the new steady state is ln2/(a+b). These are response times, not the half-life of the target protein. If a+b = 0.08 s−1, the response half-time is 0.693/0.08 ≈ 8.66 seconds.

At steady state, each direction still has a nonzero rate. In the first numerical example, if [E]T = 2 μM, the unmodified concentration is 0.5 μM and the modified concentration is 1.5 μM. The phosphorylation rate is 0.06 × 0.5 = 0.03 μM s−1; the dephosphorylation rate is 0.02 × 1.5 = 0.03 μM s−1. Under the simple assumption of one ATP per phosphorylation, ATP is consumed at 0.03 μM s−1, despite no net change in EP. Equal opposing rates indicate a dynamic steady state, not absence of chemistry.

Another system might have a = 0.6 s−1 and b = 0.2 s−1. The steady-state fraction is still 0.75, but its response half-time is about 0.866 seconds and the cycling flux is ten times greater at the same ET. Thus a measurement of steady-state phosphorylation cannot by itself identify response speed or ATP expenditure. Time-resolved measurements or independent kinase/phosphatase assays are required. This distinction between a state variable and flux is a recurring theme in metabolism.

9. Coordinating glycogen breakdown and synthesis

Glycogen is a glucose polymer stored in liver and muscle. When the body needs rapidly available fuel, glycogen phosphorylase releases glucose 1-phosphate from nonreducing ends by phosphorolysis. Glycogen synthase catalyses a major synthetic step using UDP-glucose. Both reactions can occur in the same tissue, so their activities must be coordinated; otherwise simultaneous strong synthesis and breakdown could consume energy without accomplishing useful net storage or mobilisation.

In the classical muscle example, glycogen phosphorylase exists as a relatively less active b form and a phosphorylated a form. Phosphorylase kinase catalyses phosphorylation of a regulatory serine (Ser14 in the classical muscle enzyme), converting b to a. This addition shifts the enzyme's conformational preferences toward higher activity. Protein phosphatase 1 can remove the phosphate, converting a back to b. Both forms can still respond to metabolites, so the letters a and b do not define absolute on and off states.

During a hormonal signal, epinephrine can stimulate a signalling cascade involving cAMP and protein kinase A (PKA). PKA activates phosphorylase kinase by phosphorylation; phosphorylase kinase then phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase. Calcium also helps activate phosphorylase kinase through its calmodulin component, connecting muscle contraction to fuel mobilisation. A small upstream signal can therefore change the activity of many downstream enzyme molecules, because each active kinase can modify more than one target molecule over time. This is amplification through catalysis, not the creation of ATP or protein from nothing.

In parallel, phosphorylation of glycogen synthase at regulatory sites generally reduces its activity, while dephosphorylation by phosphatases can promote glycogen synthesis. When conditions favour storage, insulin-dependent signals and phosphatase activity favour synthesis and oppose breakdown. This reciprocal control limits unnecessary cycling. Do not confuse the phosphate on the regulatory enzyme with inorganic phosphate used as a substrate by glycogen phosphorylase. The former changes the enzyme's state; the latter participates directly in cleavage of glycogen.

Allostery operates alongside covalent modification. Muscle phosphorylase b can become substantially more active when AMP binds, even if it remains dephosphorylated. Conversely, the liver a form responds to glucose in a way that can favour its dephosphorylation and reduced glycogen breakdown. Glycogen synthase is also responsive to glucose 6-phosphate. Thus a hormone is not the only controller of the rate; direct metabolites adjust the response to local demand. Different signals can converge on one protein because they act through different but coupled molecular mechanisms.

Consider a sample before and after epinephrine treatment. The total amount of glycogen phosphorylase is unchanged, its phosphorylation increases, and its activity in a standardised assay increases. A phosphatase treatment of the sample reduces both the modification and the activity. This combination supports reversible covalent regulation. If the cellular glycogen concentration subsequently falls, that observation is consistent with mobilisation, but the enzyme assay is not sufficient by itself to prove the precise in vivo flux. The amount of glycogen, availability of phosphate, activity of other enzymes and tissue demand all contribute.

Signal termination is just as important as signal initiation. cAMP can be hydrolysed by phosphodiesterases, kinase activities can decline, and phosphatases can restore a different distribution of enzyme states. The cell does not need to degrade every phosphorylated molecule when the signal ends. The reversible modification mechanism allows the existing enzymes to be used repeatedly, avoiding the time and biosynthetic cost of replacing them after each brief period of activity.

10. Proteolytic activation: making an irreversible local decision

A third regulatory strategy solves a different problem. Digestive proteases are capable of hydrolysing peptide bonds in proteins, including proteins in the cells that manufacture them. If they were produced in fully active form at all locations, they could damage their surroundings. One solution is to synthesise and transport inactive or weakly active precursor proteins, called zymogens or proenzymes, and convert them into active enzymes only at the appropriate site.

Activation generally involves cleavage of one or more peptide bonds by a protease. Removal of a segment or creation of a new chain end changes the structure of the target protein, exposing or organising its catalytic machinery. It does not necessarily remove a physical lid from the active site. In several serine proteases, the newly generated amino terminus makes specific interactions that reorganise loops and the substrate-binding pocket. The catalytic residues may already be present in the precursor, yet the complete active geometry is not established until cleavage.

Trypsinogen is produced by pancreatic acinar cells and transported toward the intestinal lumen. Enteropeptidase in the small intestine cleaves its activation peptide to produce active trypsin. Trypsin can activate additional trypsinogen molecules and several other digestive zymogens, including chymotrypsinogen and procarboxypeptidases. This creates a cascade: one proteolytic activation step initiates further activations, producing a coordinated set of digestive enzymes. The cascade is regulated by compartmentalisation, inhibitor proteins, controlled secretion and conditions at the activation site.

Chymotrypsinogen illustrates how limited cleavage can alter catalysis without breaking the protein into completely separate pieces. Initial trypsin cleavage generates a new amino terminus (Ile16 in the classical chymotrypsin numbering) whose positively charged amino group can form a salt bridge with Asp194. This interaction helps organise the specificity pocket and oxyanion hole. Additional processing yields mature α-chymotrypsin, whose peptide chains remain connected by disulfide bonds. The structural consequence, rather than mere removal of mass, is the critical reason for activation.

Another common example is pepsinogen, the precursor of pepsin. Acidic conditions in the stomach facilitate activation, and active pepsin can participate in further cleavage of precursor molecules. pH therefore helps localise activation, but the eventual change includes proteolytic processing. An increase in enzyme activity upon lowering pH does not by itself prove that a zymogen has been cleaved; evidence of altered protein processing distinguishes the irreversible mechanism from reversible pH effects.

Unlike simple phosphorylation, proteolytic activation cannot ordinarily be reversed by a dedicated enzyme that pastes the peptide segment back on. Individual molecules are irreversibly processed under physiological conditions. Activity can nevertheless be stopped at the cellular level by inhibitors, clearance, degradation, dilution, restricted substrate access or cessation of new zymogen secretion. Irreversible activation at the molecular level does not mean uncontrolled catalysis forever. The cell uses different mechanisms to control the beginning and end of the enzyme's action.

Limited proteolysis is not universally activating. Cleavage can inactivate an enzyme, remove a regulatory domain, change localisation, or target the protein for further degradation. The term zymogen is reserved for a precursor that becomes a more active enzyme after appropriate processing. In some cases precursors have measurable residual activity. An examination question that describes an inactive precursor becoming active after a specific cleavage is testing the principle of proteolytic activation rather than the claim that every cleavage always activates a protein.

Other biological systems also use proteolytic cascades, including blood coagulation and the activation of certain apoptotic proteases. Their detailed physiology belongs to later units. At this stage, recognise why cascades provide strong amplification and why their spatial restriction and inhibitors are essential. A kinase cycle can be reversed on the same protein molecule, whereas a proteolytically activated enzyme often requires inhibition or turnover to end its active lifetime.

11. Slower regulation: controlling enzyme abundance

Rapid mechanisms act on a protein already present. If environmental conditions change for hours or days, the cell may also adjust the quantity of the enzyme. Protein synthesis and protein degradation determine the total amount of an enzyme, while localisation and secretion can redistribute it. Changing abundance requires cellular resources and usually operates more slowly than simple metabolite binding, but it can provide persistent changes in metabolic capacity.

Consider bacteria growing on a readily utilisable sugar. Producing large amounts of an enzyme that is only needed to metabolise a different absent sugar wastes amino acids, ribosomal capacity and energy. When the alternative nutrient becomes available and the appropriate regulatory conditions are met, expression of the corresponding metabolic proteins can rise. When the nutrient disappears, expression can fall. The detailed logic of bacterial operons belongs to Unit 3; here the important principle is that enzyme induction and repression alter the supply of catalytic molecules instead of directly altering the active sites of pre-existing molecules.

Enzyme abundance also depends on degradation. A protein with a short lifetime can be adjusted rapidly by changing its synthesis, because the old protein pool disappears quickly. A very stable protein may persist after expression stops. Conversely, accelerated degradation can decrease an enzyme's amount even while its mRNA remains abundant. In eukaryotic cells, proteasomal degradation and lysosomal pathways can contribute to protein turnover; the detailed mechanisms are studied later. Do not assume that mRNA concentration and enzyme activity are always proportional.

A minimal quantitative model is d[E]/dt = s − kd[E], where s is the synthesis rate in concentration per time and kd is a first-order degradation constant in inverse time. At steady state the rates balance, so [E]ss = s/kd. This assumes a constant synthesis rate, proportional removal and constant volume; in dividing cells dilution can be incorporated into the effective removal constant. A higher synthesis rate or a lower degradation rate increases steady-state abundance.

When synthesis is stopped, the equation reduces to d[E]/dt = −kd[E]. Integration gives [E](t) = [E](0)e−kdt. The half-life is ln2/kd. For an enzyme with a half-life of 4 hours, after 12 hours without new synthesis the fraction remaining is (1/2)3 = 1/8, or 12.5%. An inhibitor of transcription would not instantly eliminate the active enzyme; its effect depends on the persistence of the existing mRNA and protein, and on whether additional translation continues.

For a synthesis rate of 12 nM h−1 and a removal constant of 0.3 h−1, the steady-state enzyme concentration is 40 nM. If the synthesis rate doubles while removal is unchanged, the new steady state is 80 nM. It is approached gradually: the concentration at t hours following the change is 80 + (40−80)e−0.3t. After ln2/0.3 ≈ 2.31 hours, the concentration is 60 nM, halfway from the old value to the new one. The equation has a similar form to the phosphorylation-cycle model, but the variable now describes protein amount rather than a fraction of an unchanged pool.

A useful experiment compares enzyme activity, total protein amount and the relevant modification state over a time course. A rate rise in minutes with unchanged protein amount suggests rapid regulation of existing molecules. A rate rise over hours accompanied by an increase in protein abundance suggests induction or stabilisation. Both mechanisms may operate simultaneously. For example, an enzyme can be rapidly activated by phosphorylation and subsequently accumulate because gene expression changes. No single timing threshold proves a mechanism, so the conclusion should be supported by direct measurements.

The term constitutive is often used for an enzyme produced under a wide range of conditions, whereas inducible expression increases in response to a regulatory input. These terms describe expression behaviour, not the absolute absence or presence of enzyme regulation. A constitutively expressed enzyme can be strongly allosteric, and an inducible enzyme may remain inactive until its required cofactor binds. It is the combination of amount and molecular state that establishes catalytic capacity.

12. Compartmentalisation, cofactors and access

Imagine that an enzyme and its substrate are both abundant in a cell, but a membrane separates them. A measurement of the total amount of each molecule would suggest a potentially rapid reaction. Yet unless the substrate can cross the membrane, or the enzyme is present in the same compartment, the reaction may be very slow. Living cells exploit such separation to control metabolism.

In eukaryotic cells, enzymes of different metabolic routes occupy different locations. Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol, whereas major steps of the citric acid cycle occur in the mitochondrial matrix, with succinate dehydrogenase associated with the inner membrane. Fatty-acid synthesis and mitochondrial β-oxidation are largely separated in typical mammalian cells. Transport of intermediates and cofactors connects these processes and helps determine which substrates are accessible. This organisation also allows distinct local pH, redox state and metabolite concentrations.

A relevant example is the control of long-chain fatty-acid entry into mitochondrial oxidation. Malonyl-CoA, an intermediate associated with fatty-acid synthesis, inhibits carnitine palmitoyltransferase I in the outer mitochondrial membrane. This reduces mitochondrial entry of long-chain fatty acyl groups through the carnitine-dependent transport system when synthesis is favoured. The regulated enzyme is a gatekeeper for substrate access to downstream reactions. The resulting decline in β-oxidation can occur even if the amount and intrinsic catalytic capacity of matrix β-oxidation enzymes remain unchanged.

The availability of cofactors can be equally important. An apoenzyme lacking a required cofactor may have little activity, whereas the holoenzyme can catalyse the reaction. Metal ions, NAD+, NADH and other coenzymes participate in or enable reactions. A change in the NAD+/NADH ratio can alter the rates and thermodynamic driving forces of dehydrogenase reactions. This should not always be described as allosteric regulation: in many cases the cofactor binds a catalytic site and is chemically transformed during the reaction. The role of a molecule is determined by the mechanism, not by whether it has a regulatory consequence.

Changes in localisation can also regulate proteins themselves. An enzyme may move to a membrane after a signal, associate with a substrate-rich complex or be sequestered away from a target. Association with another protein can activate or inhibit catalysis or recruit the enzyme to its substrate. Calcium-dependent binding of calmodulin to target proteins provides a classical example of a regulatory protein transmitting a signal. Calcium is not necessarily a direct substrate of the target enzyme; it changes the state of the regulatory component.

Localisation experiments must distinguish the total cell pool from the accessible pool. A whole-cell extract disrupts membranes and mixes compartments; the activity observed after lysis may be greater than that possible in the intact cell. An enzyme that appears normal in an extract may carry low in vivo flux because substrate transport has been disrupted. Conversely, loss of activity after cell fractionation can result from loss of cofactors or damage during preparation rather than from absence of the enzyme. Marker proteins and appropriate recovery controls help distinguish these explanations.

These mechanisms are connected to, but not identical with, the rapid and slow regulatory levels already discussed. An altered transporter can change effective substrate concentration; phosphorylation may affect localisation; synthesis can produce an isozyme targeted to a particular organelle. A cell often achieves control through combinations rather than choosing exactly one regulatory strategy.

13. Isozymes: different molecular forms of the same reaction

Not every cell needs the same version of an enzyme. A liver cell exposed to large changes in blood glucose has a different task from a cell that must use glucose reliably even at modest concentrations. Both may catalyse the same chemical transformation, but benefit from enzymes with different substrate responses and regulatory properties. Isozymes, also called isoenzymes, are distinct molecular forms of an enzyme that catalyse the same overall reaction. They can differ in amino-acid sequence, subunit composition, kinetic properties, sensitivity to effectors, stability or tissue distribution.

Isozymes commonly arise from different genes encoding related catalytic proteins. Gene duplication and divergence can preserve the catalytic reaction while changing regulatory regions or substrate affinity. Alternative splicing can also yield functionally distinct forms, and combinations of related subunits can form multiple oligomeric isozymes. The key criterion is shared catalytic reaction with distinct molecular forms; unrelated activities merely occurring in the same pathway are not isozymes.

Several nearby terms must be separated carefully. An allosteric T state and R state of one enzyme are interconverting conformations, not automatically distinct genetically encoded isozymes. Phosphorylated and dephosphorylated forms are covalent states of a protein; they are not ordinarily what is meant by classical gene-encoded isozymes, although broader isoform terminology can include post-translational variants. A zymogen and its mature enzyme represent processing states of a precursor. An allelic variant is produced from a different allele at the same locus; allelic enzyme variants are sometimes called allozymes. These distinctions matter when a question asks what produced two enzyme bands or changed their activities.

Because isozymes catalyse the same overall reaction, they generally share an enzyme classification for that reaction. They need not have identical amino-acid sequences or identical Km, Vmax or inhibitor sensitivities. Vmax depends on how much active enzyme is present, so meaningful comparisons of intrinsic catalytic capacity require equal active-site concentrations or comparison of kcat. A statement that one isozyme has a higher maximum velocity in a tissue extract is not by itself evidence that its turnover per molecule is greater.

Isozymes may have different pH optima, temperature stability or susceptibility to substrate inhibition. A tissue that changes its gene expression can change the mixture of isozyme activities without changing total amount of protein assigned to that reaction. This gives the organism another way to adapt metabolic behaviour to local demands. Different forms also offer an experimental opportunity: if they have different charge, size or stability, an assay may separate and identify their contributions.

The word isozyme does not imply that one form catalyses a permanently forward reaction and another a permanently reverse reaction. For an identical net chemical transformation at the same conditions, the equilibrium constant is a thermodynamic property of reactants and products. Different enzyme forms may support different rates in either direction or have different responses to concentrations, but they cannot establish different equilibrium constants. In a cell, the direction and magnitude of net flux depend on the reaction quotient, thermodynamics and the kinetic properties of the available enzyme forms.

We can now connect the earlier regulatory mechanisms to isozyme expression. Allosteric activation changes the response of an existing molecule, covalent modification changes its chemical state, and abundance control changes the number of molecules. Isozyme regulation can change the composition of the enzyme pool itself. A tissue with two forms may switch toward one that responds strongly to an energy signal, while another tissue expresses mainly a form suited to a relatively constant demand. These strategies are not mutually exclusive: each isozyme can also be phosphorylated or respond to metabolites according to its own structure.

14. Lactate dehydrogenase: five classical tetrameric isozymes

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) catalyses the reversible reaction pyruvate + NADH + H+ ⇌ L-lactate + NAD+. In conditions of high glycolytic flux, conversion of pyruvate to lactate regenerates NAD+, which is required by glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase. Lactate can also be oxidised to pyruvate in tissues that use it as fuel. Both directions involve the same chemical equilibrium and enzyme class; the cellular context determines the net direction.

The classical mammalian LDH forms are tetramers assembled from two major related polypeptide chains: H, encoded by LDHB, and M, encoded by LDHA. Four positions can contain H or M subunits. The five possible compositional classes are H4, H3M, H2M2, HM3 and M4. Historically they are numbered LDH-1 through LDH-5 in that order. Other LDH forms exist, including testis-associated LDHC; the five-class model concerns the classical H/M series.

H-rich forms are prominent in tissues such as heart and red blood cells; M-rich forms are prominent in skeletal muscle and liver. These are distributions, not exclusive assignments. H-rich and M-rich tetramers can differ in kinetic properties, including susceptibility to substrate inhibition. The historical association of H-rich forms with lactate utilisation and M-rich forms with lactate production is a useful introductory tendency, but it must not become the false rule that H4 can only oxidise lactate and M4 can only reduce pyruvate. Both catalyse the reversible reaction, and substrate/product ratios and NADH/NAD+ strongly influence net direction.

Classical electrophoretic separation exploits differences in net charge under a specified buffer condition. In typical alkaline electrophoresis, LDH-1 is relatively anodic and LDH-5 less anodic; intermediates migrate between them. Detection of activity after separation produces bands in which active enzyme generates a detectable reaction product through a coupled staining system. The intensity of such a band reflects activity under the staining conditions, not automatically the number of protein molecules. A weakly staining isozyme could be less abundant, less active under those conditions or partly denatured during separation.

The existence of five bands from two genes can be understood using combinatorics. If an H subunit is selected with probability p, an M subunit with probability 1−p, and four subunits assemble independently at random, the probability of a tetramer containing k H subunits is C(4,k)pk(1−p)4−k. The binomial coefficient C(4,k) counts the ways k H positions can be chosen among four positions. Different arrangements with the same H:M composition are grouped into the same compositional class in this elementary model.

For equal availability p = 0.5, the five predicted proportions from H4 to M4 are 1/16, 4/16, 6/16, 4/16 and 1/16. The central class H2M2 is most frequent because six arrangements share this composition. The outer classes each have only one arrangement. If H subunits account for 75% of the available subunits, the H4 probability is (0.75)4 = 0.3164 and the H3M probability is 4(0.75)3(0.25) = 0.4219. More H-rich tetramers are expected, but H4 is not necessarily the single most frequent class.

These calculations assume random assembly, equal accessibility and stability of the resulting tetramers. Real cells can depart from the binomial distribution because of selective association, differences in degradation, assembly history or expression timing. Electrophoretic band intensities may also depart from predicted molecular fractions if the individual forms have different catalytic rates. A discrepancy between observed bands and the random model therefore suggests a need to examine the assumptions rather than an automatic contradiction of tetrameric structure.

LDH provides a powerful distinction between genotype, molecular assembly and biochemical phenotype. Two genes contribute to a pool of related subunits; assembly produces several molecular forms; tissue-specific expression and biochemical conditions determine their observed activities. This logic applies broadly to multimeric proteins. One does not need five independent genes to produce five compositionally distinct tetrameric forms from two related subunits.

15. Hexokinase and glucokinase: tuning a common reaction to demand

The phosphorylation of glucose to glucose 6-phosphate consumes ATP and helps retain glucose within the cell for metabolism. In mammals, hexokinase isoforms I–III generally have relatively high affinity for glucose and are inhibited by their product glucose 6-phosphate. Glucokinase, also called hexokinase IV, is expressed particularly in liver and pancreatic β cells. It catalyses the same overall reaction, but has a higher glucose concentration requirement for half-maximal velocity and is not directly feedback-inhibited by glucose 6-phosphate in the same way.

Why is this difference useful? Many tissues require glucose phosphorylation at ordinary glucose concentrations. A low apparent Km allows an enzyme to operate near its maximal rate even when glucose is not abundant. If downstream metabolism slows and glucose 6-phosphate accumulates, product inhibition can restrain further phosphorylation, conserving ATP and limiting metabolite accumulation. In liver, by contrast, glucokinase activity increases substantially as glucose rises, enabling the tissue to process more glucose after a meal. In β cells, glucose phosphorylation is an important part of glucose sensing. Detailed endocrine physiology is beyond this chapter, but the kinetic distinction illustrates why tissue-specific enzyme forms are valuable.

Glucokinase has a sigmoidal response to glucose under relevant conditions despite being monomeric, because its kinetic behaviour involves slow conformational transitions. Thus its glucose response is often described by S0.5 rather than a simple Michaelis constant. This is a useful exception to the idea that every sigmoid enzyme curve requires multiple equivalent binding sites. In contrast, many classical low-Km hexokinase forms display a more nearly hyperbolic substrate response. The comparison involves both substrate responsiveness and regulation, not merely one affinity value.

For illustrative arithmetic, suppose hexokinase A follows Michaelis–Menten kinetics with Km = 0.1 mM and Vmax = 100 arbitrary activity units. At glucose concentrations of 0.5 and 5 mM, its velocities are 100(0.5)/(0.6) = 83.3 and 100(5)/(5.1) = 98.0 units. Most of its potential capacity is already used at the lower concentration. If a second hypothetical form follows an empirical Hill response with S0.5 = 8 mM, n = 1.5 and the same maximal rate, the velocities at 0.5 and 5 mM are approximately 1.54 and 33.1 units, respectively. At 16 mM it reaches about 73.9 units. These numbers are model values, not a measurement of specific mammalian enzyme preparations. They show why a lower-affinity, glucose-responsive form can act over a wider concentration range.

Liver glucokinase is also regulated by interaction with glucokinase regulatory protein (GKRP), which can sequester it in the nucleus under particular metabolic conditions. Fructose 6-phosphate favours the inhibitory association, whereas fructose 1-phosphate can oppose it. As glucose and other regulatory conditions change, glucokinase availability in the cytosol changes. Here the distinctive isozyme properties are integrated with protein–protein interaction and localisation. A question describing high glucose concentration, movement between compartments and changed activity is not necessarily asking about gene expression alone.

Another example of isozyme specialisation is creatine kinase. Its cytosolic forms arise from M and B subunits that assemble as dimers, producing MM, MB and BB forms, and mitochondrial creatine kinase forms also exist. They catalyse reversible phosphoryl transfer between phosphocreatine and ADP/ATP and differ in tissue distribution. The important contrast with LDH is that two types of subunits in a dimer give three compositional classes, whereas two types in a tetramer give five. Under the simplified random assembly model with equal subunit pools, a dimer would give a 1:2:1 distribution. The type of assembly determines the count, not merely the number of genes.

In evaluating isozyme examples, compare the actual reaction first, the molecular source of variation next, and the functional consequence last. Two enzymes catalysing different reactions in the same pathway are not isozymes. Two proteins that catalyse the same reaction can be isozymes even if their kinetic curves, localisation and tissue roles differ substantially.

16. Integrated control: why metabolic flux is not a single-enzyme property

We now return to the pathway as a whole. In a simple unbranched route, the concentration of an intermediate B changes according to d[B]/dt = vin − vout. At metabolic steady state, d[B]/dt = 0, so vin = vout. Both rates may be large and equal. If an inhibitor reduces the downstream reaction before the upstream reaction responds, B accumulates. As B rises, upstream feedback or greater substrate availability for the downstream enzyme may establish a new steady state. A steady concentration does not mean that B is no longer being produced and consumed.

In a branched route, the balance includes all inputs and outputs. Suppose an intermediate is produced at 12 μM min−1 and consumed by two branches at 7 and 5 μM min−1. Its pool is initially steady. If a branch-specific inhibitor reduces the first branch to 3 while the others initially remain unchanged, d[B]/dt = 12 − 3 − 5 = 4 μM min−1. Over the first 30 seconds, the pool would increase by 2 μM if those rates stayed constant. Later, changes in concentrations and feedback may alter the rates. This initial calculation is not a prediction of indefinite linear accumulation.

Far-from-equilibrium steps are often important regulatory targets because their net rates can be adjusted substantially and a strongly favourable reaction can help direct material into a pathway. Near-equilibrium reactions frequently respond to changes in substrate and product concentrations. However, it is too simplistic to say that every irreversible step is rate-limiting or that a single enzyme permanently determines the entire pathway flux. A pathway can have distributed control, and which reaction exerts the greatest influence depends on the cell's state and other constraints.

A simple way to evaluate flux sensitivity is to change one enzyme amount slightly and observe the fractional change in steady-state pathway flux. An approximate relative sensitivity is (ΔJ/J)/(ΔE/E), where J is steady-state flux and E is enzyme amount. For a small change from E = 10 to 11 arbitrary units, with flux rising from 100 to 102, the approximate sensitivity is 0.02/0.10 = 0.2. A 10% increase in enzyme abundance has produced only a 2% flux increase. This is not an intrinsic constant of the isolated enzyme; it is a property of the particular system under those conditions. Formal metabolic control analysis defines corresponding derivatives for infinitesimal changes. The simple finite-change calculation is sufficient to recognise distributed control at this level.

Why can an enzyme with a large Vmax have little control over flux? If the pathway is limited by substrate transport or a different reaction, increasing the capacity of an already abundant enzyme may have little effect. Conversely, a change in an enzyme at a branch may redistribute flux strongly even if total entry flux changes little. Control of total throughput and control of product allocation are not always the same.

Another useful principle is reciprocal regulation of opposing pathways. Glycolysis and gluconeogenesis share several reactions, but distinct enzymes bypass strongly directional steps. Coordinating activities of these opposing reactions helps prevent the expenditure of ATP in a futile cycle. Some substrate cycles do operate physiologically for purposes such as responsiveness or heat production, so the presence of simultaneous opposing flux is not automatically pathological. Net flux is the difference between opposing one-way fluxes; both can be substantial even if the net is small.

The integration of control mechanisms can be seen in a cell entering a period of increased energy demand. Metabolite changes such as increasing AMP can rapidly activate suitable enzymes. Hormonal signals can alter the phosphorylation states of enzymes and shift glycogen mobilisation. Compartmental substrate transport influences mitochondrial energy production. Over longer periods, gene expression and enzyme turnover adjust capacity. Tissue-specific isozymes set the background response. These mechanisms form a hierarchy of responses over time, rather than competing explanations of one event.

The next subunit develops the chemical accounting behind these responses. Enzyme regulation explains how the cell controls the rate at which a transformation happens; bioenergetics explains why transformations can proceed in a particular direction and how their energy is captured or coupled. To understand ATP production, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, we will need both: the thermodynamic driving forces of reactions and the kinetic control of the enzymes that catalyse them.

17. How to distinguish regulatory mechanisms experimentally

A reliable investigation begins with the observation to be explained. Suppose an enzyme activity rises after a nutrient is added to cells. The first useful step is to decide whether activity refers to the rate measured in intact cells, the catalytic capacity of an extract or the rate of a purified enzyme. These measurements answer different questions. A higher in vivo product formation rate could result from altered substrate supply, a higher amount of enzyme or altered catalytic state. An extract assayed at identical saturating substrate concentrations can help distinguish substrate availability from intrinsic capacity, although extraction itself can alter regulatory states.

Measure total target protein in matched samples using an appropriate antibody or other quantitative protein method. If the target protein amount remains constant while maximal activity changes, altered catalytic competence is plausible. Check the specific activity relative to target enzyme amount rather than simply total extract protein. A rise in target amount with unchanged activity per target molecule supports abundance control. If both change, quantify their individual contributions. For instance, a two-fold rise in protein combined with a three-fold rise in total maximal activity means activity per target protein rose 1.5-fold.

Next, test reversibility and dependence on an effector. Add the candidate metabolite directly to a purified or well-defined preparation under controlled conditions, then remove it by suitable dilution or separation and reassay. Rapid restoration of the original activity supports reversible binding. Persistent effects can arise from covalent modification, tight binding or slow conformational equilibration, so persistence alone does not prove covalency. A measured shift of a velocity curve can identify functional effects, but structural or binding evidence is required to locate the regulatory site.

When covalent modification is suspected, assay the modification state and total enzyme in parallel. Test whether a suitable modifying enzyme changes both the modification and activity, and whether an appropriate reverse modification restores the original state. Include controls for ATP acting as a catalytic substrate, for changes in pH and ionic strength, and for loss of protein during handling. A phosphorylation-dependent antibody can report a particular site, but the signal must be normalised to the total amount of target protein; a stronger band could otherwise reflect more protein rather than a higher modified fraction.

When proteolytic activation is suspected, examine the precursor and processed species by electrophoresis or an appropriate protein analysis together with activity. If cleavage precedes activation, the observed pattern is consistent with processing. A cleavage-resistant mutant retaining correct folding may help test the necessity of cleavage. A smaller apparent mass alone does not identify the cleavage site or prove activation, and a mature disulfide-linked multi-chain enzyme may migrate differently under reducing and nonreducing conditions. The relevant conclusion must follow from the combined evidence.

For isozyme analysis, use a method that retains or reveals the different molecular forms. Native electrophoresis followed by an activity stain can resolve forms according to their mobility, whereas denaturing SDS–PAGE generally separates individual polypeptides by apparent molecular mass rather than preserving the native tetramers. Equal-mass H and M subunits can therefore yield a simple denaturing band pattern even when several native isozymes are present. Protein identification or gene-expression evidence can help associate bands with molecular forms. An activity stain measures enzyme function under the staining conditions, not automatically equal molar quantities of each isozyme.

Finally, consider experimental time. Direct metabolite binding can act within the time required for binding and conformational adjustment. Covalent modification often acts rapidly too, but depends on the modifying enzymes. Proteolysis can be rapid and irreversible for an individual molecule. Expression and turnover often operate over longer periods, although some proteins turn over quickly. The time course is useful evidence, but it is not a substitute for testing the molecular mechanism. Combine activity, abundance, molecular state, substrate access and reversibility whenever an explanation is ambiguous.

18. A unified map of the regulation mechanisms

The mechanisms studied in this chapter all change the relationship between a cell's needs and its enzyme-dependent reactions, but they act on different variables. Direct allosteric regulation changes catalytic behaviour through reversible binding and conformational coupling. Covalent modification changes a chemical group on an existing molecule, frequently with a separately controlled enzyme for the reverse reaction. Proteolytic activation alters the protein chain and is normally irreversible for that molecule. Expression and degradation control the number of enzyme molecules. Compartmentalisation controls contact with reactants. Isozyme composition adjusts which molecular version carries out the reaction.

The following comparisons are useful only when interpreted mechanistically. For example, a rapid rise in activity does not by itself identify allostery because phosphorylation can be equally rapid. A low total enzyme activity could result from reduced protein amount, an inhibited form, an inactive precursor, or lack of an essential cofactor. An enzyme with a remote regulatory site is not necessarily a pure noncompetitive enzyme in the kinetic sense. A phosphorylated molecule is not necessarily more active. A pair of isozymes cannot have different equilibrium constants for the same net reaction under identical thermodynamic conditions.

When solving a problem, identify what was measured before choosing a mechanism. If the question provides protein concentration, initial velocity, substrate concentration and modification state, use all four. If it provides only a steady-state metabolite concentration, do not claim a specific flux unless you have additional information about input and output rates. Distinguishing what is observed from what is inferred is one of the central skills needed in the analytical portion of the examination.

19. Part B — conceptual and assertion–reason practice

These original questions test the distinction between molecular mechanisms, kinetic patterns and pathway behaviour. Choose the best option before opening the explanation.

1. A feedback inhibitor is the final product D of a pathway A → B → C → D. Which target best avoids wasting precursor committed only to D?

A. A transporter unrelated to the pathway; B. First committed step toward D; C. All reactions that consume A; D. An enzyme that degrades D.

Answer and explanation

B. Suppressing the first committed step reduces unnecessary intermediates and preserves precursor for other routes. A is unrelated, C can disable other pathways, and inhibiting degradation (D) would tend to increase the product.

2. Which observation establishes that an allosteric inhibitor is pure noncompetitive?

A. Binding away from active site; B. Inhibits at high substrate; C. Under a simple Michaelis–Menten model, unchanged Km with reduced Vmax across inhibitor concentrations; D. Sigmoidal curve.

Answer and explanation

C. Pure noncompetitive inhibition is a special kinetic pattern. A describes location, B is not sufficient to distinguish mixed or irreversible mechanisms, and D indicates a complex response rather than this specific pattern.

3. Which statement about phosphorylation is correct?

A. Always activates enzymes; B. Phosphatase regenerates ATP; C. Can activate or inhibit depending on target; D. Requires new target synthesis every time.

Answer and explanation

C. The phosphate changes molecular interactions in a protein-specific way. A is contradicted by glycogen synthase; B confuses hydrolysis with ATP synthesis; D ignores modification of existing protein.

4. What is the principal distinction between product inhibition and end-product feedback?

A. Both always irreversible; B. End-product feedback can target a distant earlier reaction; C. Product inhibition changes equilibrium; D. End-product feedback always represses genes.

Answer and explanation

B. A downstream product can inhibit an upstream enzyme that does not directly produce it. A and D confuse possible regulatory mechanisms; C violates the thermodynamic role of a catalyst.

5. Which is an example of feedforward activation?

A. CTP inhibits bacterial ATCase; B. End product represses synthesis of its first enzyme; C. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate activates a responsive pyruvate kinase; D. Trypsin activates more trypsinogen.

Answer and explanation

C. An upstream glycolytic intermediate stimulates a later glycolytic enzyme. A and B are negative feedback; D is a proteolytic autocatalytic cascade, not the specified metabolic feedforward example.

6. The two catalytic trimers of bacterial ATCase retain activity after separation from regulatory dimers. Which inference is best?

A. Catalytic and regulatory functions can be separated; B. ATP is an ATCase substrate; C. CTP is hydrolysed by the catalytic chain; D. Catalytic trimers must have the same cooperative response as intact enzyme.

Answer and explanation

A. The catalytic trimers retain catalysis while characteristic nucleotide control is lost. B and C contradict the reaction; D ignores the regulatory architecture.

7. Which statement distinguishes zymogen activation from a kinase switch?

A. Both require proteolysis; B. Zymogen cleavage ordinarily cannot be reversed on the same molecule; C. Phosphorylation destroys all catalytic activity; D. Zymogen activation changes equilibrium constant.

Answer and explanation

B. Peptide cleavage is normally irreversible for the processed molecule. Phosphorylation usually involves group addition/removal; C is not general; D is thermodynamically incorrect.

8. Two gene products catalyse the same net reaction but differ in tissue expression and Km. They are most appropriately called:

A. Substrates; B. Isozymes; C. Zymogens necessarily; D. Competitive inhibitors.

Answer and explanation

B. Molecular forms sharing a reaction can differ kinetically and in distribution. A and D refer to small molecules or binders in a reaction context; C describes precursors, not different gene products as such.

9. Which property must be shared by classical LDH H4 and M4?

A. Identical kcat; B. Identical tissue distribution; C. Same equilibrium constant for the same reaction at fixed conditions; D. Identical charge.

Answer and explanation

C. Equilibrium belongs to the reaction chemistry, not the catalyst. Kinetics, expression and net charge can differ, making A, B and D false.

10. Assertion: a steady pool of phosphorylated protein can consume ATP. Reason: kinase and phosphatase can operate at equal nonzero rates.

A. Both true, reason explains; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Answer and explanation

A. Each phosphorylation consumes ATP; simultaneous dephosphorylation maintains a constant pool without stopping turnover. The equal rates explain the assertion.

11. Assertion: an increase in total enzyme activity proves increased gene transcription. Reason: covalent modification can change activity at fixed protein amount.

A. Both true; B. Both false; C. Assertion false, reason true; D. Assertion true, reason false.

Answer and explanation

C. Activity is not a direct measurement of transcription. A modification can raise activity without any increase in protein amount; altered turnover or expression can also contribute.

12. Which enzyme is a classical exception to the idea that sigmoid kinetics necessarily imply several equivalent binding subunits?

A. Mammalian glucokinase; B. Every enzyme with a prosthetic group; C. Any zymogen; D. All phosphatases.

Answer and explanation

A. Monomeric glucokinase shows glucose-dependent kinetic cooperativity. The other categories do not define a sigmoidal kinetic mechanism.

20. Part C — analytical, numerical and data interpretation practice

Work through each problem using the stated assumptions. Check units, distinguish observed data from interpretations, and compare your reasoning with the explained solution.

1. Active fraction and saturation

A 2 μM enzyme pool contains 25% active sites. kcat = 40 s⁻¹ and Km = 2 mM. Calculate v at 2 mM substrate and after active fraction rises to 75%.

Worked solution

At 2 mM, substrate factor = 2/(2+2)=0.5. Active concentrations are 0.5 and 1.5 μM. Rates are 0.5 × 40 × 0.5 = 10 and 1.5 × 40 × 0.5 = 30 μM s⁻¹. Three-fold activation does not require greater total enzyme.

2. Two catalytic states

An enzyme has two independently acting forms with equal Km. At saturating substrate, E-P has kcat 12 s⁻¹ and E has kcat 48 s⁻¹. The total pool is 1 μM. What happens when the phosphorylated fraction increases from 0.25 to 0.75?

Worked solution

Initial Vmax = 0.25×12+0.75×48 = 39 μM s⁻¹. Final Vmax = 0.75×12+0.25×48 = 21 μM s⁻¹. Phosphorylation reduces capacity in this example; a universal activation rule would predict the wrong direction.

3. Hill response

A cooperative enzyme follows n=2, S₀.₅=6 mM, Vmax=90 units. An activator changes S₀.₅ to 3 mM with unchanged n and Vmax. Find both rates at 3 mM.

Worked solution

Control v = 90×9/(36+9)=18 units. Activated v = 90×9/(9+9)=45 units. Rate ratio = 2.5. The data establish an empirical shift but not a precise count of binding sites.

4. ATP as substrate and signal

At fixed fructose 6-phosphate, PFK-1 initial rates are 2, 18 and 7 units at ATP concentrations 0.01, 1 and 10 mM. Total enzyme is unchanged. Explain the pattern and propose a control.

Worked solution

At low ATP, catalytic substrate supply limits the rate. The intermediate concentration supplies ATP; at high concentration a regulatory inhibitory effect is plausible. Measure a broader ATP titration at fixed pH/free Mg²⁺, test relevant effectors and a regulatory-site perturbation. Changes in free Mg²⁺ or other assay conditions could also contribute; a three-point curve alone is not proof of a binding site.

5. Kinase/phosphatase balance

a = 0.12 s⁻¹, b = 0.08 s⁻¹, total target = 5 μM. Find steady phosphorylated fraction, concentration and ATP consumption in the simple cycle.

Worked solution

fss = 0.12/0.20 = 0.60; E-P = 3 μM and unmodified = 2 μM. Forward rate = 0.12×2 = 0.24 μM s⁻¹ and reverse = 0.08×3 = 0.24 μM s⁻¹. With one ATP per phosphorylation, consumption is 0.24 μM s⁻¹, not zero.

6. Dynamic switch

System A has a=0.09, b=0.03 s⁻¹; system B has a=0.9, b=0.3 s⁻¹. Compare steady fraction and response half-time.

Worked solution

Both have fss=0.75. Half-times: A 0.693/0.12=5.78 s; B 0.693/1.2=0.578 s. B responds ten times faster and cycles faster at the same enzyme pool. Equal endpoints cannot identify equal dynamics.

7. Abundance and turnover

An enzyme has a half-life of 6 h and synthesis is completely blocked. Starting at 160 nM, what concentration remains at 18 h? If turnover and synthesis both double before blockade, how does steady-state abundance change?

Worked solution

Three half-lives pass, leaving 160/8=20 nM. Before blockade, Ess=s/kd; doubling numerator and denominator leaves Ess unchanged but shortens the response half-time. After doubling kd, a new half-life would be 3 h.

8. Synthesis versus activation

Control extract has 10 ng target enzyme and activity 40 U. Treated extract has 20 ng and activity 120 U under the same conditions. Calculate relative activity per ng and interpret.

Worked solution

Control = 4 U/ng; treated = 6 U/ng, a 1.5-fold rise. Abundance doubled and activity per amount increased 50%, jointly giving three-fold total activity. More protein alone cannot account for all of the increase; determine active fraction and assay state.

9. LDH random assembly

If p(H)=0.6 and assembly is random, calculate H₄ and H₂M₂ proportions.

Worked solution

H₄ = 0.6⁴ = 0.1296 or 12.96%. H₂M₂ = 6×0.6²×0.4² = 0.3456 or 34.56%. The binomial coefficient is required for the mixed class; its six arrangements are grouped by composition.

10. LDH band intensity

Equal molar amounts of H₄ and M₄ give bands of 80 and 40 intensity units in a linear activity stain. A tissue gives equal intensity bands. Under the same linear conditions, what is its H₄:M₄ molar ratio?

Worked solution

Per molar amount H₄ yields twice the signal of M₄. For equal intensity, 80H = 40M, so H:M = 1:2. Equal activity-band intensity need not mean equal molecular abundance; the interpretation assumes calibrated linear staining.

11. Branch-point regulation

Intermediate X is formed at 15 μM/min and consumed by branches at 9 and 6. An inhibitor reduces the first branch to 4. Predict initial accumulation and change over 12 seconds if rates stay fixed.

Worked solution

d[X]/dt=15−4−6=5 μM/min. Twelve seconds = 0.2 min; increase = 5×0.2=1 μM. Later feedback can change the fluxes; the result applies to the stated initial period.

12. Distributed flux control

Increasing enzyme A by 5% changes pathway steady flux from 200 to 203 units; increasing enzyme B by 5% in a separate experiment changes flux to 220. Which has greater local influence?

Worked solution

A: (3/200)/0.05 = 0.30. B: (20/200)/0.05 = 2.0 as finite-change sensitivity. B has greater influence under these test conditions. This is not proof that B always controls flux, nor a claim that these finite estimates obey any formal infinitesimal control theorem.

13. Identifying a zymogen

A 50-kDa precursor has little activity. Treatment with a protease yields a 43-kDa band and strong activity; dilution does not reverse the effect. What conclusion is supported, and what is a good additional control?

Worked solution

Limited proteolytic activation is supported. Include a no-protease control, demonstrate that activity belongs to the processed target rather than the added protease, and test cleavage-resistant target while checking its folding. Persistent activation alone is not conclusive, but processing plus activity strengthens inference.

14. Energy charge

ATP, ADP and AMP are 5, 3, and 2 mM. Then they become 7, 2 and 1 mM. Calculate energy charges.

Worked solution

First: (5+1.5)/10=0.65. Second: (7+1)/10=0.80. The adenylate distribution changes despite constant total concentration. This is a useful energetic indicator but does not independently specify ATP hydrolysis ΔG or pathway flux.

15. Feedback-resistant enzyme

A mutant ATCase retains basal catalysis and normal expression but no longer responds to CTP; cells supplied abundant precursors accumulate excess pyrimidine product. Explain and predict a limitation.

Worked solution

Loss of end-product inhibition permits continued entry into pyrimidine synthesis when product is high. Precursor shortage, downstream enzyme capacity and other regulation can limit overproduction. The mutation does not alter the reaction equilibrium; it changes kinetic regulation of pathway entry.

16. Location versus intrinsic activity

After inhibition of long-chain fatty-acid mitochondrial entry, intact-cell β-oxidation declines, but an assay of isolated matrix enzymes at saturating substrates gives normal rates. Explain.

Worked solution

The evidence supports limited substrate delivery rather than loss of intrinsic matrix enzyme capacity. Additional transporter and substrate measurements are needed to establish the exact cause. Lysing or isolating components changes access and can remove the intact-cell restriction.

17. Reciprocal regulation

A phosphatase inhibitor increases phosphorylation of glycogen phosphorylase and glycogen synthase. Assuming the classical regulatory effects, predict changes in catalytic capacities and explain why actual glycogen flux might differ.

Worked solution

Phosphorylase capacity generally rises and synthase capacity falls, favouring mobilisation. Actual flux also requires glycogen and phosphate, suitable metabolite signals and the rest of the pathway. Phosphorylation measurements alone do not quantify net glycogen loss.

18. Equilibrium trap

Isozyme A produces lactate faster than isozyme B under identical initial substrate conditions. A student concludes that A shifts equilibrium toward lactate. Evaluate.

Worked solution

Higher initial velocity reflects kinetics under those conditions. The reaction quotient and equilibrium constant are unchanged for the same chemistry at fixed temperature, pH convention and composition. Both enzymes speed approach to the same equilibrium; net flux in a cell depends on concentration ratios and coupling.

19. Two forms, different substrate response

At 1 mM substrate, isozyme A has Km=0.25 mM and B has Km=4 mM. Both follow Michaelis–Menten and have Vmax=50 units at equal site concentrations. Compare rates.

Worked solution

A = 50×1/1.25=40 units; B=50×1/5=10 units. A is four times faster at this substrate concentration. At sufficiently high substrate both approach 50 units; the difference at low substrate is not evidence of different turnover at saturation.

20. Native versus denaturing bands

A native LDH gel shows five bands, but denaturing electrophoresis shows only two bands corresponding to H and M subunits. Is this contradictory?

Worked solution

No. Native electrophoresis separates assembled tetramers by properties such as charge; denaturing conditions dissociate the oligomers, revealing subunits. Two types of subunit in a four-subunit enzyme can form five composition classes. Actual migration depends on the electrophoresis method and subunit mass differences.

21. Revision and the bridge to bioenergetics

Begin revision by imagining a pathway as a connected network with limited resources. Identify which step commits a precursor to a product, what signal indicates demand, and which control mechanism adjusts activity or amount. In a quantitative problem, decide whether you need active enzyme concentration, enzyme turnover, half-saturation concentration, phosphorylation fraction, protein lifetime, reaction flux or isozyme composition. Each describes a different aspect of the same biochemical system.

For ATCase, recall the separation of regulatory and catalytic chains and the opposing effects of CTP and ATP. For glycogen metabolism, remember that phosphorylation generally promotes phosphorylase activity but reduces synthase activity, and that allostery provides an additional layer. For zymogens, distinguish precursor processing from reversible modulation. For LDH, understand the five H/M tetrameric compositions and the assumptions behind binomial assembly. For hexokinase and glucokinase, connect distinct glucose responses to tissue function without implying that they catalyse different net reactions.

Four equations provide useful checkpoints: v = kcat[E]active[S]/(Km+[S]) for an appropriate Michaelis–Menten system; fss = a/(a+b) for a first-order modification cycle; Ess = s/kd and t½ = ln2/kd for first-order enzyme turnover; and d[X]/dt = total inputs − total outputs for a metabolite pool. For random assembly of n positions from two subunit types, use C(n,k)pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ only when the assumptions of independent assembly and comparable availability apply.

The story of enzymes began with catalysis and ended with control. A living cell does not simply accumulate catalysts; it continually adjusts their behaviour to the availability of nutrients, energy requirements and the demands of different tissues. In Subunit 1.E, these regulatory principles become essential for understanding ATP, coupled reactions and metabolic pathways. The next chapter begins with biological energy transduction, linking chemical driving forces with controlled cellular work.
