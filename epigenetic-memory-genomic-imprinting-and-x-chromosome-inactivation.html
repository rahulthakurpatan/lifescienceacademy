1. The problem of remembering a cell identity

A fertilized mammalian egg contains a set of chromosomes inherited from each parent. As its descendants divide, they acquire increasingly different functions. A muscle cell produces proteins needed for contraction, whereas a liver cell produces a different collection of enzymes. Except for particular biological exceptions, both possess the same underlying genetic instructions. The difference therefore cannot be explained simply by counting genes or identifying their nucleotide sequences. It requires an account of how a cell selects instructions and how its descendants continue to make appropriate choices.

A distinction must be made between a gene's existence and its expression. An intact gene may be transcribed frequently, rarely or almost never. Even when transcription occurs, its RNA may have different lifetimes or translational efficiencies. These possibilities were developed in earlier chapters. Here the central question is narrower: how can an expression state persist after the condition that established it has disappeared, or after a cell has replicated its genome and divided? This is the problem of biological memory at the level of gene regulation.

Imagine two cultures of genetically identical cells, each kept under the same final conditions. In one culture a regulatory signal was supplied briefly before the experiment, while in the other it was never supplied. If both cultures now produce the same RNA output, the measured gene shows no persistent memory of the signal under these conditions. If the treated cells continue to produce a different output, the investigator must ask how long the difference lasts and what carries it. A difference that lasts only as long as a stable RNA molecule survives is not necessarily a heritable regulatory state. A difference maintained through many rounds of DNA replication and division demands a stronger explanation.

The word epigenetic has been used in several ways in biology. In this chapter it refers principally to stable or heritable changes in gene activity that are not explained by a change in the base sequence. Not every histone modification is proof of epigenetic inheritance; modifications may be transient consequences of transcription. Conversely, a stable regulatory state can involve interacting DNA-binding proteins, chromatin factors and feedback circuits rather than a single chemical mark. The important experimental criterion is persistence and the mechanism responsible for it, not the name of the molecule found at a locus.

A genetic mutation and an epigenetic change can both reduce gene expression, but they predict different molecular observations. A promoter mutation may prevent binding of an essential activator because the recognition sequence has changed. A methylated promoter may retain exactly the original sequence yet become less accessible or recruit proteins that favor repression. Removing the methylation might restore transcription if the necessary activating machinery is available. Removing methylation cannot repair a deletion of the recognition sequence. These differences make sequencing, chromatin measurements and expression measurements complementary rather than interchangeable.

The term mitotic inheritance describes persistence from a cell to its daughters during ordinary proliferation. Transmission through meiosis and fertilization is a different question. Many epigenetic features are extensively reorganized during mammalian germ cell formation and early embryogenesis. Consequently, a state that is reliably inherited within a skin cell lineage need not persist in offspring. Genomic imprinting is particularly informative because particular parental marks survive a period when much of the genome is reprogrammed, yet these marks are normally reset in the germline of the resulting individual.

There are three related but separable problems in this chapter. First, a cell must maintain appropriate regulatory states as it replicates its DNA and chromatin. Second, selected autosomal genes distinguish alleles according to their parental origin. Third, cells with more than one X chromosome generally establish a compensatory pattern of X-linked gene expression through chromosome-wide regulation in placental mammals. Each is a biological solution to a different problem, even though all three can involve methylated DNA, modified histones and regulatory RNA.

The concept of a regulatory state is useful because a chromosome cannot be understood as a uniform strip of DNA. Promoters, enhancers, insulators and nucleosomes form local regulatory environments. In one state, an enhancer can contact a promoter and transcription factors can recruit polymerase. In another state, the same promoter may be inaccessible or physically insulated from an enhancer. An expression difference can therefore arise without a difference in the coding sequence and may remain linked to a particular chromosome within one nucleus.

A persistent state is not an immutable state. If chromatin could never be changed, cellular differentiation itself would be difficult to explain. Development requires establishment of new patterns, maintenance of appropriate ones and removal of others. The molecular machinery for maintenance works in a regulated environment; its ability to preserve a state depends on cell type, developmental stage and the continued availability of its substrates and interacting proteins. This balance between continuity and plasticity is more informative than the simplified idea that epigenetics means permanent gene silencing.

2. How DNA replication challenges epigenetic memory

DNA replication preserves base sequence by using complementary strands as templates. Consider one CpG dyad, in which a cytosine followed by guanine on one strand corresponds to a cytosine followed by guanine when the complementary strand is read in its own five-prime to three-prime direction. Both cytosines can carry methyl groups. When replication separates the parental strands and synthesizes their partners, each daughter duplex initially contains an old methylated cytosine and a newly synthesized cytosine that need not be methylated. This asymmetric condition is called hemimethylation.

Maintenance DNA methylation resolves the asymmetry. In mammals, DNMT1 preferentially acts on hemimethylated CpG sites, with UHRF1 contributing to recognition of the chromatin environment and recruitment of the methylation machinery. The new strand thereby acquires a methylation pattern related to the old one. The parental DNA strand supplies positional information, whereas a separate enzymatic reaction restores the chemical mark. DNA polymerase alone cannot guarantee this result because methylation is not a fifth complementary base incorporated whenever polymerase encounters a methylated template.

A methyltransferase transfers a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine to a cytosine in DNA. The methyl group becomes part of a covalent modification; it is not a regulatory protein that simply binds temporarily and dissociates. This helps explain why the mark can persist physically on old strands after replication. It also explains why maintenance requires suitable metabolic substrates and active enzymes. Although a methylated CpG may serve as a cue for copying the regulatory state, restoring it involves biochemical work rather than spontaneous duplication of a modification.

The maintenance reaction has a spatial problem as well as a chemical one. The genome contains a large number of CpG sites, and replication occurs at many locations. Recognition and recruitment near newly copied chromatin increase the chance that restoration occurs where the parental pattern calls for it. A regulatory defect affecting recruitment may therefore produce methylation loss even if the catalytic domain of the enzyme remains functional in a purified assay. Conversely, the presence of DNMT1 protein in a cell is not proof that every methylated site will be copied correctly.

A different activity, de novo methylation, can establish methylated sites that were not previously modified on either strand. DNMT3 family enzymes are important contributors to this process in mammals. The functional distinction is between initiating a pattern and restoring an existing pattern. It is not an absolute prohibition against overlapping activities. If an experiment removes a maintenance enzyme and methylation persists at certain loci, de novo methylation and other context-specific mechanisms may contribute. One should not conclude that the maintenance enzyme never acts at those sites merely because the final methylation measurement is unchanged.

To appreciate the consequences of maintenance failure, begin with a fully methylated population of CpG dyads and suppose that replication occurs without new methylation or active demethylation. Every original methylated cytosine can remain on its original strand, but the number of total cytosines doubles at each division. Thus the proportion of methylated cytosines among all cytosines at those sites decreases by half after each round. The loss is called passive demethylation because the original methyl groups need not be chemically removed. The population gains unmodified sites as DNA molecules multiply.

This idealized mechanism differs from active demethylation, which can alter methylated cytosines through chemical reactions and associated processing rather than requiring successive dilution through replication. An observed reduction in methylation is therefore not sufficient to decide which route occurred. If methylation decreases substantially in a nondividing cell, a pure replication dilution model is inadequate. If the reduction closely follows the number of completed cell divisions and old methylated strands remain chemically intact, passive loss is a plausible explanation. Evidence for one mechanism should be evaluated under conditions that distinguish it from the other.

Even perfect maintenance at selected CpG sites would not preserve the entire regulatory state unaided. A transcription factor may recognize methylated DNA differently from unmethylated DNA. Methyl-binding proteins can recruit other factors that modify nucleosomes or restrict promoter accessibility. A maintained mark is biologically meaningful insofar as some component interprets it. If a cell retains methylation but loses the protein required to translate it into repression, expression can change while the DNA methylation assay looks normal. This separates mark propagation from mark interpretation.

The physical environment around the replication fork creates an additional challenge. DNA is wrapped around histone proteins in nucleosomes, and replication cannot proceed with every original nucleosome remaining intact and stationary. Parental nucleosomes are disrupted or reorganized and their histones are recycled, while newly synthesized histones fill out the increased amount of chromatin. The resulting daughter chromatin contains both inherited and new components. An original modification therefore does not automatically occur on every new histone located at the corresponding chromosomal region.

This provides a second type of dilution. Suppose an idealized domain had a particular repressive histone modification on every nucleosome before replication, and its parental histones are distributed between daughter DNA molecules while new histones initially lack that modification. In a simple average model, the fraction of modified histones falls after replication. The distribution is not necessarily uniform or random at each real locus; the model illustrates the need for restoration, not a law about how individual nucleosomes must segregate. Cells can rebuild patterns using neighboring parental nucleosomes, regulatory proteins and continuing sequence-dependent recruitment.

Histone readers bind particular chemical features of histones or chromatin. Writers introduce modifications, and erasers remove them. When a reader associated with an existing repressive mark recruits a writer capable of producing that mark on neighboring nucleosomes, a positive feedback loop is possible. Existing modified chromatin helps recruit the machinery that creates additional modified chromatin. Newly assembled nucleosomes become substrates for restoration. This is an attractive mechanistic explanation for persistence through the temporary dilution caused by replication.

Two standard examples illustrate why mark identity matters. H3K9 trimethylation is associated with many constitutively repressed chromatin domains and can be recognized by heterochromatin protein 1 family proteins. H3K27 trimethylation is associated with Polycomb-mediated repression in many developmental contexts. These marks involve different lysines on the same histone family and are interpreted by different regulatory machinery. Saying that histone methylation is always a single universal silencing mechanism would obscure both distinctions and the existence of other methyl marks associated with transcriptionally active regions.

A reader-writer interaction is not equivalent to the DNA template mechanism. DNA polymerases choose complementary nucleotides at individual positions. Histone-modifying complexes act in a larger physical environment where contact probability, sequence-specific recruitment, chromatin boundaries and competing enzymes affect where they work. A parental histone is evidence of a prior state, not an exact independent instruction for every new nucleosome. It is therefore possible for some chromatin states to be robustly recovered while others are substantially reorganized during differentiation.

During mitosis much transcription is reduced and many transcriptional regulators disengage from chromatin. Nevertheless, a subset of proteins may remain associated with particular genomic regions or return rapidly after mitosis. Such behavior is referred to as mitotic bookmarking when the retained association facilitates restoration of regulatory activity. The key question is functional: does retention help restart the appropriate program? Mere detection of a protein on mitotic chromosomes does not prove that it is required for memory. Removing it specifically during mitosis and examining subsequent transcription is more informative.

DNA methylation, histone modification and bookmarking can cooperate or compensate for one another. For instance, a repressed domain could retain methylation on parental DNA, retain some modified histones and recruit repressors after mitosis. Failure of one component might cause a modest change because the remaining mechanisms still favor repression. Failure of two components could yield a larger effect. Such combined perturbations help distinguish redundancy from a strictly linear pathway in which losing either of two essential sequential steps should eliminate the same downstream effect.

3. Quantitative principles of maintenance and persistence

A mathematical model can help separate the number of cell divisions from the chemical efficiency of maintenance. Let f be the fraction of individual CpG cytosines methylated at a defined set of sites immediately before a division. Assume all cells divide once, every parental cytosine remains chemically unchanged, and every new cytosine opposite a methylated parental cytosine is methylated with probability p. Assume that new methylation opposite an unmethylated parental cytosine does not occur and that no cell is selectively lost. Of the original methylated cytosines, all remain present and an expected fraction p of that number is added to newly synthesized strands.

The number of potential cytosines doubles. The resulting methylated fraction becomes f times (1+p)/2. After n equivalent rounds, the expected fraction is f0[(1+p)/2]^n. Here n is a dimensionless division count, f0 is a fraction between zero and one, and p is a probability in the same interval. This expression describes an average over a population; it does not mean that one cytosine has become eighty percent methylated. An individual cytosine is either methylated or not in this simplified model. Intermediate fractions arise because different copies have different states.

If p is zero, the formula gives pure dilution. If p is one, methylation is perfectly maintained under the stated assumptions. For a hypothetical starting fraction of 0.8 and a maintenance probability of 0.9, the fraction after four divisions is 0.8 times 0.95 to the fourth power, or about 0.652. The absolute loss is about 0.148, while the loss relative to the original fraction is about 18.5 percent. The distinction between absolute percentage points and proportional loss is important when comparing methylation experiments.

The model is not a general equation for every methylation system. If some sites undergo de novo methylation, previously unmodified strands can acquire marks independently of inheritance from their partners. If different cell types have different probabilities or growth rates, the population average can change even when the methylation kinetics of each type are constant. Active demethylation, selective cell survival, incomplete division and site-specific recruitment also violate the assumptions. Observing a falling methylation fraction does not by itself establish that the value of p has decreased.

An expression state may also be maintained by a self-reinforcing regulatory circuit. Suppose a transcription factor activates its own gene in addition to downstream target genes. A temporary signal may raise the factor above a level where its production compensates for degradation. If the relationship between concentration and production is sufficiently nonlinear, the system can possess a low stable state and a high stable state under the same external conditions. This is called bistability. A transient input may switch the cell between states rather than merely cause a proportional transient increase in expression.

A simple dynamic description is that the rate of change of the factor concentration equals its production rate minus its removal rate. If degradation is first order, removal is proportional to concentration. A plot of production and removal versus concentration may show intersections corresponding to steady states. An intersection is stable if a small increase in concentration makes removal exceed production and a small decrease makes production exceed removal. In an unstable intermediate state the deviations instead grow. The direction of the two rate curves around an intersection, not merely their equality, identifies stability.

Positive feedback does not guarantee bistability. Weak feedback may produce one stable expression level, and excessive fluctuations may make two predicted states difficult to maintain in small cells. Experimental evidence of two populations with different expression levels could reflect different cell types, asynchronous responses or measurement variation. Testing whether the same conditions support alternative persistent states and whether prior stimulation changes the eventual output is essential. Hysteresis, in which increasing and decreasing the input produces distinct switching thresholds, can support a memory model, but its interpretation requires attention to the time allowed for equilibration.

Feedback can link chromatin to gene expression even if no single molecular feature remains permanently present. A transcription factor recruits an enzyme that opens chromatin, and open chromatin permits further binding of the factor. When DNA replicates, some of these interactions are disturbed; surviving components and the sequence context can help reconstruct them. The resulting cell state is a dynamic steady state sustained by molecular turnover. This is why maintenance of a differentiated phenotype does not imply that the same molecules have been physically retained throughout the lifetime of the lineage.

This systems-level view also helps distinguish memory from selection. If a treatment kills cells in the low-expression state while sparing high-expression cells, the population after treatment appears to retain the high state. No individual cell need have switched or acquired memory. Following lineages before, during and after treatment helps determine whether pre-existing states were selected or new states were induced. Measuring viability and cell proliferation alongside expression is particularly important whenever a treatment perturbs the genome or chromatin.

4. What experiments revealed about parental genomes

Mendelian inheritance established that alleles can pass from either parent to offspring. For many autosomal genes, an allele's sequence and its interaction with other genes are more relevant to its expression than whether it came through sperm or egg. This symmetry suggested that two complete haploid genomes should provide comparable sets of essential instructions, regardless of their origin. In mammals, however, embryological experiments demonstrated that equal chromosome number is not enough to guarantee equivalent development.

In 1984, work by Surani, Barton and Norris and independent experiments by McGrath and Solter examined mouse embryos with different parental contributions to their nuclear genomes. Their experimental strategy separated nuclear parentage from simple diploid chromosome dosage. Embryos reconstructed to contain two maternal nuclear genomes or two paternal nuclear genomes differed markedly from appropriately reconstructed embryos containing one of each. The former combinations failed to support normal development to term. These experiments provided evidence that mammalian maternal and paternal genomes are functionally nonequivalent in development.

The power of the experiment comes from its comparison, rather than from the observation that manipulated embryos sometimes fail. If manipulation alone were responsible, comparable embryos with both parental contributions should also fail. The biparental reconstruction therefore serves as a crucial reference. The same logic applies to ploidy: if absence of a chromosome set were the explanation, two haploid sets should correct the problem regardless of parentage. Because the uniparental combinations were diploid, a simple deficiency in chromosome number could not account for the result.

The distinct developmental effects of maternal-only and paternal-only nuclear contributions suggested different roles of genes whose expression depended on parent of origin. In mouse experiments paternal genome contribution was particularly important for normal extraembryonic development, but it would be incorrect to conclude that all placental genes are paternal or that all embryonic genes are maternal. Most genes do not show an absolute parent-of-origin expression rule. The experiments pointed to a subset of critical regulators whose combined dosage matters.

Subsequent identification of individual imprinted loci made the idea testable at a molecular level. Instead of asking only whether an embryo develops normally, investigators could distinguish the two alleles of a gene and ask which is transcribed. A genetic polymorphism is particularly valuable in this context because it allows the RNA product to be assigned to its chromosome of origin without assuming that methylation or sequence differences necessarily cause the expression pattern. Crosses in both directions can then separate the effect of a sequence variant from the effect of being inherited through a particular parent.

An imprint is a molecular state established in association with parental origin that influences later expression. The resulting expression pattern need not involve both copies of every gene. At a paternally expressed imprinted locus, the paternal copy usually makes the relevant transcript in the tested tissue while the maternal copy is relatively silent. At a maternally expressed locus the relationship is reversed. These descriptions refer to the expression of the gene, not necessarily the location of a methyl group. A methylated paternal control region can, depending on its regulatory role, enable expression of a nearby paternal gene.

It is useful to separate the imprint itself from the phenotype it produces. In many mammalian examples the primary mark is DNA methylation at a regulatory region, but its consequences can include altered binding of transcription factors, altered long noncoding RNA production and changes in chromatin structure. A single methylated region can influence several nearby genes. Consequently, an investigator should not infer that every imprinted gene contains a differentially methylated promoter or that the methylated allele of every gene is necessarily the inactive one.

The word parental in genomic imprinting refers to the immediate parental origin of an allele in the individual being studied. It does not mean that an allele retains the same expression status forever because it originally came from a distant ancestor of one sex. During germline development, the inherited marks at many imprinting regions are erased and new marks are established according to the sex of the individual producing gametes. Therefore an allele can pass through different imprint states in successive generations while its nucleotide sequence remains unchanged.

5. Establishing, protecting and resetting parental imprints

The two copies of an autosomal chromosome in a diploid cell are homologues. They contain corresponding genes, though they may carry different alleles. For an ordinary autosomal gene, the cell's transcription factors can potentially act on both homologues. Genomic imprinting creates an asymmetry between homologues within the same cellular environment. Because the two copies share the same nucleus, a difference in their expression can be especially useful for demonstrating that the regulatory state has a chromosome-local component rather than being explained entirely by a diffusible factor.

One important type of regulatory sequence is an imprinting control region, abbreviated ICR. A control region coordinates allele-specific expression of one or more genes within its neighborhood. A differentially methylated region, or DMR, is a region showing different methylation states between alleles, tissues or developmental stages. These terms overlap in many classic examples but are not synonyms. A DMR is defined by an observed methylation difference; an ICR is defined by regulatory function. Some DMRs arise after imprinted expression has already been established. Establishing that a particular DMR is the primary control element requires evidence beyond correlation.

The life cycle of a mammalian germline imprint begins with erasure of earlier parental information in developing germ cells. Consider an individual whose cells contain a maternal allele and a paternal allele of an imprinted gene. During the production of gametes, both homologues must become appropriate for transmission through the individual's germline. If the individual produces sperm, the relevant loci acquire paternal-type marks; if the individual produces eggs, they acquire maternal-type marks. The old grandparental distinction is not generally the determining variable for the imprint in a newly formed gamete.

This resetting principle resolves a classic pedigree puzzle. A chromosome inherited by a woman from her father may have carried a paternal imprint in her somatic cells. If she transmits that chromosome to her child through an egg, the inherited sequence is now of maternal origin in the child. In a normally reset locus, the regulatory state is appropriate to egg transmission rather than to the chromosome's earlier history in her father's germline. The same chromosome can therefore be active in one generation and inactive in another without a change in its allele sequence.

Establishment of an imprint is not simply a consequence of the general concentration of methyltransferase in a germ cell. Different genomic regions must be selected, and the timing and local chromatin environments of germline development contribute to this selection. De novo methyltransferases, notably DNMT3A with its regulatory partner DNMT3L in classic mouse germline models, participate in laying down many germline DNA methylation imprints. The exact requirements depend on the locus and developmental context. Their role should be distinguished from the maintenance action of DNMT1 after DNA replication.

At fertilization the two parental genomes arrive from highly specialized cell types. The newly formed embryo must extensively remodel both genomes to support new patterns of transcription. Much DNA methylation is lost and new patterns are subsequently established. If every imprint were erased in the embryo at this point, parent-of-origin expression could not be reliably maintained through development. Many classical germline DMRs therefore have mechanisms that preserve their differential methylation despite widespread changes elsewhere in the genome.

Protection involves sequence-selective recognition as well as enzymatic copying. For example, mammalian proteins that recognize particular methylated imprint-associated sequences can recruit repressive chromatin factors and support maintenance methylation. The conceptual point is that a genome-wide tendency toward demethylation does not imply that every locus experiences identical kinetics. Regions with protective machinery can resist reprogramming better than neighboring regions. A defect in a protection mechanism can thus disturb selected imprints without requiring uniform erasure throughout the entire genome.

Once a protective state has survived early development, the ordinary challenges of DNA replication remain. Each somatic cell division must restore the relevant methylation asymmetry. If a paternal ICR is normally methylated while the maternal ICR is unmethylated, maintenance methylation must restore methylated sites on the paternal chromosome without indiscriminately methylating the maternal copy. Selectivity is as important as efficiency. An enzyme system that methylated both homologues uniformly could preserve total methylation levels while destroying the parental distinction.

This illustrates why a measurement of bulk methylation near fifty percent can be ambiguous. In a population of cells with one fully methylated parental allele and one completely unmethylated allele, approximately half the assayed sites may be methylated. But a similar average can arise if each allele has a patchwork of modified and unmodified sites, or if some cells have both alleles methylated and others have neither. The biological consequences of these cases differ. An assay that identifies the parental allele of individual DNA molecules is needed to discriminate between them.

The final phase of the imprint cycle occurs in the germline of the next generation. The marks that supported stable monoallelic expression in the individual's soma are not necessarily marks that should be transmitted unchanged. Their removal allows establishment of the new sex-specific gametic states. Thus genomic imprinting simultaneously demonstrates epigenetic persistence and programmed reversibility. Without maintenance, parental information would be lost too early; without resetting, the information would refer to the wrong parent in later generations.

A locus can also show secondary changes that are induced by the primary imprint rather than installed in the gamete. Imagine that methylation of an ICR prevents expression of a nearby noncoding RNA, and transcription of that RNA would otherwise recruit repression to another promoter. The methylation pattern at the RNA promoter is upstream of the downstream promoter state. A perturbation of the downstream mark may modify expression temporarily, while the primary control region remains capable of reestablishing the normal regulatory pattern. Recognizing the order of causation helps explain why simply measuring many methylated regions does not reveal the central regulatory lesion.

6. The Igf2 and H19 locus: a methylation-sensitive regulatory switch

The mouse Igf2 and H19 gene cluster offers a particularly instructive example because two neighboring genes respond differently to the same parental control region. Igf2 encodes insulin-like growth factor 2, an important regulator of growth during development. H19 produces a long noncoding RNA. In the classical tissues used to teach this locus, Igf2 is expressed predominantly from the paternal allele and H19 from the maternal allele. The arrangement makes clear that an imprint can coordinate expression of multiple genes without assigning one chromosome a uniform active or inactive status.

The simplified regulatory map contains the Igf2 promoters, the H19 imprinting control region, the H19 promoter and shared downstream enhancers. A useful way to read the map is to ask which promoter can communicate productively with an enhancer. An enhancer can influence a promoter over a substantial DNA distance, often through chromatin contacts involving regulatory proteins. The intervening DNA is not just inert space; a regulatory boundary can restrict interactions between elements on opposite sides of it.

CTCF is a DNA-binding protein that participates in chromatin organization and can support enhancer-blocking activity at the H19 control region. Its ability to occupy particular recognition sites in this region depends on the methylation state. On the maternal chromosome, the ICR is generally unmethylated in the classical model, so CTCF can bind. This occupancy contributes to an insulated configuration in which the downstream enhancers cannot efficiently activate the more distant Igf2 promoters. The H19 promoter is positioned on the enhancer-accessible side of the boundary and remains capable of responding to them.

The causal sequence matters. Maternal Igf2 is not simply silent because the maternal Igf2 coding region has been chemically destroyed or because the entire maternal chromosome is heterochromatic. Instead, a specific control region permits a protein to bind and restrict a productive regulatory interaction. The enhancer itself can remain active; H19 transcription demonstrates that enhancer function has not been universally abolished. This is a local redistribution of regulatory access.

On the paternal chromosome the H19 ICR is methylated. Methylation interferes with the occupancy of CTCF at its relevant recognition sites. Without the enhancer-blocking configuration, the downstream enhancers can participate in activation of paternal Igf2. Paternal H19 is normally repressed in the classical setting, with additional promoter methylation and local chromatin changes contributing to that state. The two outcomes are related, but loss of CTCF binding is not by itself a universal direct activator of all neighboring promoters.

This arrangement creates an important exception to the slogan that DNA methylation always silences the nearest gene. Methylation of an insulator can indirectly promote expression of a gene that the unmethylated insulator would otherwise separate from its enhancer. The direct effect is reduced binding of a methylation-sensitive factor; the indirect effect is increased productive enhancer access to Igf2. Correctly predicting expression therefore requires identifying the function of the methylated sequence. A methylated activator-binding site and a methylated insulator-binding site may have opposite consequences for downstream transcription.

The genomic location of an enhancer also constrains the interpretation. In the simplified H19 configuration, the insulator lies between the enhancer and Igf2, but not between the enhancer and H19. Moving an enhancer across a boundary in a hypothetical construct could change its sensitivity to CTCF even if the methylation state remained identical. This is why a diagram of the gene cluster is more useful than memorizing two arrows showing methylation and repression. The relative order of elements is part of the mechanism.

Consider a mutation that prevents CTCF from recognizing the maternal ICR while leaving the shared enhancers and Igf2 promoter functional. In the simplified model, the enhancer-blocking function is reduced and maternal Igf2 may become expressed. If paternal Igf2 continues to be expressed, the result is loss of normal monoallelic expression. The effect is cis-acting because the changed control sequence is physically associated with the maternal gene copy. The paternal homologue contains its own intact regulatory configuration. A cell-wide CTCF deficiency, in contrast, can affect many genomic regions and is not a clean locus-specific experiment.

A second perturbation could methylate the normally unmethylated maternal ICR. This can reduce maternal CTCF occupancy and produce a regulatory configuration resembling the paternal state. The DNA sequence remains unchanged. However, full conversion of the entire locus cannot be assumed from one methylation measurement. The H19 promoter, the enhancer configuration and tissue-specific transcription factors must also be considered. A measurement of maternal Igf2 activation is stronger evidence of altered regulatory output than the mere observation that the ICR gained methylation.

In a third hypothetical experiment, an investigator deletes the enhancer shared by the two genes on the paternal chromosome. Even if the paternal ICR is methylated and lacks CTCF, the relevant enhancer-mediated activation of paternal Igf2 can be lost. Removing a block on communication is not equivalent to supplying an activating input. This distinction is a general feature of gene control: derepression establishes permission for expression, whereas activation supplies machinery or contacts that actually increase transcription.

Conversely, introducing an unmethylated CTCF-binding sequence between paternal Igf2 and a required enhancer might reduce paternal transcription, provided the sequence establishes a functional boundary in the relevant context. This prediction is conditional on the position, orientation where relevant, occupancy and local organization of the engineered element. It is inappropriate to treat every CTCF motif as an absolute barrier to every enhancer. The classical model provides a causal framework for an examination problem, not a complete universal description of three-dimensional genome architecture.

The H19 and Igf2 region can be investigated using reciprocal genetic crosses, allele-specific RNA measurements, methylation assays and binding assays for CTCF. For instance, finding methylated paternal DNA and predominantly paternal Igf2 RNA is consistent with the model. Showing that a mutation in the maternal CTCF-binding sites allows maternal expression adds causal evidence. Demonstrating that restoration of a functional boundary restores the original allele-specific output would strengthen the inference further. Each observation tests a different link between the regulatory mark and the expression phenotype.

The expression pattern should be qualified by organism, tissue and developmental stage. The canonical mouse example is valuable for understanding the regulatory logic, but not every Igf2 promoter in every mammalian tissue obeys an identical monoallelic rule. Some imprinted genes show partial or tissue-specific imprinting. An exam question may deliberately state that a particular tissue uses the standard model; the answer should follow that premise. Without such a premise, broader biological claims require appropriate evidence.

7. Reciprocal crosses and the meaning of allele-specific evidence

An allele-specific assay distinguishes transcripts originating from different copies of the same gene. Suppose strain A carries an adenine at a harmless marker position in an exon, while strain B carries a guanine. When these strains are crossed, a heterozygous offspring possesses both markers in genomic DNA. If the RNA retains the marker, sequencing the transcript can reveal the relative contribution of each allele. This is more informative for imprinting than measuring total RNA alone, because normal total RNA does not necessarily imply normal expression from each homologue.

In cross one, the mother comes from strain A and the father from strain B. In cross two, the mother comes from strain B and the father from strain A. If the A allele is preferentially expressed in both crosses, a sequence-associated difference is plausible. It may arise from a cis-regulatory variant linked to the marker rather than the marker itself. If the maternal allele is preferentially expressed in both crosses, the favored marker changes with the direction of the cross. This pattern is consistent with maternal expression determined by parent of origin.

A reciprocal-cross result is strong evidence only when alternative sources of parent-dependent differences are considered. The maternal organism supplies the egg cytoplasm and the prenatal environment, whereas the father does not contribute those factors in the same way. Maternal genotype can therefore influence offspring physiology without allele-specific genomic imprinting. However, observing expression tied to the chromosome of maternal origin within a heterozygous nucleus is more directly relevant than observing a phenotype that differs between cross directions. Experimental context determines how strongly other explanations can be excluded.

A mapping bias can also mimic unequal allelic expression. If transcript reads from strain A align more efficiently to the reference sequence than reads from strain B, the RNA analysis may exaggerate A-derived expression. Genomic DNA from the heterozygote provides a useful control because the two alleles are normally present at equal copy number. A departure from equal representation in genomic measurements warns of technical bias or genuine copy-number differences that must be accounted for. Reciprocal crosses alone cannot repair a badly biased assay.

A total RNA measurement can hide loss of imprinting. Suppose that normal cells produce ten RNA units from the paternal allele and none from the maternal allele. After a perturbation, paternal expression falls to five units while maternal expression rises to five. The total remains ten units, yet the parental expression rule has changed. Conversely, a cell can double the output of the active paternal allele while retaining complete maternal repression. Total output changes but the imprint-specific expression pattern is preserved. Neither a constant nor a changed total RNA value uniquely determines whether imprinting was lost.

Allelic ratios also depend on the cell population being examined. Imagine that a gene is paternally expressed in one tissue but biallelically expressed in another. A mixed sample produces an intermediate paternal fraction even if imprinting is perfect within the tissue that normally displays it. Changes in tissue composition during development can thus alter the measured ratio without changing the imprinting machinery within any cell type. Purified populations and appropriately matched developmental stages improve interpretation.

The term loss of imprinting is sometimes used for inappropriate activation of a normally silent allele, but disruption of parental regulation can also reduce or eliminate expression from the normally active allele. These outcomes have different effects on gene dosage. For a gene that is normally monoallelically expressed, inactivation of the sole expressed copy could produce a stronger functional deficit than would be expected from losing one allele of a biallelically expressed gene. This does not mean the silent allele lacks a normal coding sequence. Its regulatory state makes that sequence unavailable for normal expression.

A simple teaching example illustrates the relation between allele function and parental origin. Suppose a growth-related gene is expressed only from the paternal chromosome and that a loss-of-function coding variant is inherited from the mother. In the tissue where maternal silencing is complete, the paternal wild-type allele can supply normal gene product. If the same coding variant is inherited from the father, the active allele may produce no functional product. The sequence variant is identical, but its phenotype depends on which parental expression program operates. The inherited mutation is genetic; the difference in its expression is epigenetic.

It is tempting to classify this pattern as dominance or recessiveness based on the presence of one mutant allele. That description is insufficient unless the parental origin is specified. In a typical dominance analysis, both alleles are assumed to participate according to the gene's usual expression rules. In an imprinted context, the normally inactive wild-type allele may be unable to compensate for a defective active allele. This is an expression-dependent functional hemizygosity, despite the physical presence of two copies of the gene.

The strongest examination answers therefore separate four levels: physical copy number, DNA sequence identity, epigenetic regulatory state and measured RNA or protein output. A normal copy-number result does not prove that two functional transcripts are made. A normal sequence does not prove that the promoter is active. An appropriate imprinting mark does not prove that a downstream enhancer is functional. And a normal amount of RNA does not prove that its protein product is active. Combining measurements across levels is how one narrows the possible mechanisms.

8. Alternative imprinting mechanisms and biological significance

Although the Igf2/H19 example emphasizes enhancer insulation, other imprinted clusters employ different arrangements. At some loci a long noncoding transcript is produced from one parental chromosome and contributes to the repression of neighboring genes on that chromosome. The imprint may control whether the noncoding RNA promoter is accessible. Its transcription or RNA product can then influence nearby chromatin. This kind of organization shows that the primary parental mark need not be located at each gene whose expression it ultimately controls.

An important experimental distinction concerns the act of transcription and the resulting RNA molecule. Suppose an antisense transcript passes through the promoter of a nearby coding gene. Transcription itself might interfere with assembly of the other gene's transcription machinery, even if the completed RNA molecule has no additional regulatory activity. In another model the RNA molecule interacts with chromatin-associated factors that favor repression. Deleting the RNA promoter disrupts both possibilities simultaneously, so the result alone cannot distinguish them. A conceptual comparison between premature termination of transcription and disruption of RNA activity can help separate these hypotheses.

These mechanisms are often chromosome-local rather than equivalent to the small RNA pathway discussed in the preceding node. A miRNA typically acts through pairing with target transcripts and may affect RNAs made from either homologue. An imprinted long noncoding RNA may act predominantly in cis at the locus where it is transcribed. Its function depends on spatial association and the relevant regulatory complexes rather than on the canonical Dicer and Argonaute mechanism. Noncoding RNA is a broad category; the label does not define a single molecular pathway.

Imprinted expression can contribute to control of growth and development because altering the amount of a regulatory protein can affect many downstream processes. The existence of a growth-associated imprinted gene does not establish that all imprinting evolved for growth control. The parental-conflict hypothesis offers one evolutionary explanation for certain patterns of resource allocation between mother and developing offspring. Under its assumptions, maternally and paternally inherited alleles may differ in the selective interests associated with offspring demand for maternal resources. This can help explain particular expression patterns, but it is not a universal explanation for every imprinted locus.

A hypothesis about evolutionary origin is different from a proximate molecular mechanism. CTCF binding and enhancer insulation explain how maternal Igf2 expression is regulated in the classical locus. A resource-allocation model seeks to explain why a parent-dependent system might have been favored over evolutionary time. One kind of explanation does not automatically prove the other. A molecular perturbation that activates maternal Igf2 can support the local regulatory mechanism without independently testing the historical evolutionary scenario.

Plant imprinting provides a useful limited comparison. In flowering plants, parent-of-origin expression is especially prominent in the endosperm, a nutritive tissue produced through double fertilization. The typical endosperm has two maternal genome contributions and one paternal contribution, unlike a diploid mammalian somatic cell. Accordingly, ordinary biallelic expression per genome copy need not produce equal maternal and paternal read counts in that tissue. The expected baseline must be adjusted for genome dosage. This comparative case illustrates why parental bias cannot be interpreted without understanding ploidy and the developmental system.

For instance, in an idealized triploid endosperm with equally active copies of a nonimprinted gene, two maternal copies and one paternal copy give an expected maternal fraction of two-thirds. A measurement of approximately 67 percent maternal reads is therefore compatible with equal expression per copy. The same fraction in a diploid heterozygous tissue with one copy from each parent would indicate unequal allelic output, subject to technical and biological caveats. The numerical baseline is not a universal fifty percent; it follows from the number and activity of the available copies.

Imprinting is also distinct from the ordinary random selection of an allele for expression. If a population contains equal numbers of cells expressing only maternal and only paternal alleles, its bulk RNA might be balanced even though each cell is monoallelic. A parent-of-origin imprint produces a consistent preference in the relevant cell population, whereas random monoallelic expression does not necessarily favor one parental origin. The same issue becomes central when considering random X-chromosome inactivation, where chromosome-level clonality can produce a patchwork of cells expressing different parental X alleles.

9. The dosage problem and the discovery of X inactivation

The presence of two homologues of an autosome is ordinarily associated with two copies of each gene located on it. Sex chromosomes complicate this relationship. In the typical mammalian XX and XY chromosome complements, the number of X chromosomes differs. Many genes on the X chromosome do not possess a functionally equivalent copy on the Y chromosome. If all such genes were expressed equally per X copy, cells with two X chromosomes could produce approximately twice the amount of their products as cells with only one. The actual regulation of expression makes the comparison considerably more complex.

Dosage compensation is the biological adjustment of gene expression that reduces the effects of unequal sex-chromosome copy number. It is not a requirement that every gene produce exactly the same number of transcripts in every cell of every individual. Rather, it concerns the regulation of expression relative to chromosome dosage and the functional demands of cells. Several animal groups solve this problem using different mechanisms. In placental mammals, the characteristic response in many somatic cells is to silence most genes on all but one X chromosome.

An important historical observation was the presence of a densely staining nuclear body in many female mammalian somatic cells. Barr and Bertram described sex-associated differences in the nuclei of cat neurons in 1949. The structure later became known as the Barr body and is associated with condensed inactive X chromatin. At the time, a staining difference revealed a physical asymmetry between nuclei but did not, by itself, explain the behavior of individual genes. A mechanism linking this structure to inheritance and expression had to account for genetic evidence as well.

Mary Lyon proposed in 1961 that one X chromosome in female mammals becomes inactive early in development and that the inactivation state is maintained in descendant cells. The hypothesis helped explain mosaic phenotypes in female mice heterozygous for X-linked coat-color loci. In a patch of cells descended from a precursor with one parental X active, the phenotype can reflect the allele on that X. Another patch may descend from a precursor with the other X active. Such a mosaic is difficult to explain by a model in which both X-linked alleles are equally expressed in every cell.

A familiar visual example involves the orange coat-color locus in domestic cats. A heterozygous XX animal can have patches reflecting alternative X-linked pigmentation alleles. The basic relationship is between the active allele in a pigment-producing lineage and its cellular phenotype. White areas in a calico coat involve additional genetic influences; they should not be interpreted as patches in which both X chromosomes are necessarily silent. Coat pattern geometry also depends on the migration and proliferation of pigment-cell precursors. Thus the phenotype illustrates clonality but is not a literal map of chromosome inactivation in every tissue.

In modern notation, Xa denotes an active X chromosome and Xi denotes an inactive X chromosome. In a typical differentiated XX cell, one X is Xa and the other Xi. In a typical XY cell, the single X is Xa. The Xi is not removed from the genome: its DNA remains physically present and is replicated as cells proliferate. A DNA copy-number assay therefore distinguishes two X chromosomes in an XX nucleus even if transcription from most genes on one is strongly reduced.

The word inactive can be misleading unless its meaning is specified. Most genes subject to X inactivation have greatly reduced transcription from Xi, but some genes escape inactivation and remain expressed to varying degrees. XIST itself is a notable case of an RNA transcribed from the chromosome undergoing inactivation or maintained in its inactive state. Therefore, silence of Xi is a chromosome-wide tendency with locus-specific exceptions, not the absolute absence of RNA synthesis from every base on the chromosome.

The spatial scale of X inactivation differs from the scale of genomic imprinting at an autosomal control region. An imprinted locus can influence a cluster of nearby genes, while X inactivation coordinates expression across a chromosome containing many unrelated genes. Yet both systems employ chromosome-local regulatory effects, long noncoding RNAs in representative cases and chromatin-based maintenance. This shared logic makes X inactivation an unusually useful model for integrating the mechanisms introduced in the preceding chapters.

It is equally important to distinguish dosage compensation between chromosome complements from possible dosage balance between X-linked and autosomal genes. Silencing one X in XX cells makes the number of active X chromosomes more similar to that in XY cells, but it does not by itself demonstrate that all X-linked genes are expressed at the same level as all autosomal genes. Expression of individual genes depends on their regulatory sequences, cell types and evolutionary histories. A claim about X-to-autosome expression balance therefore requires a different comparison from a claim about XX-to-XY dosage compensation.

10. Initiation of silencing: the X-inactivation center and XIST RNA

In placental mammals, an important region involved in initiating X inactivation is the X-inactivation center. It contains XIST in humans and Xist in mice, together with regulatory elements and additional genes that influence the choice and timing of inactivation. The names XIST and Xist refer to corresponding long noncoding RNA systems in the two organisms; their regulatory details are not identical. The distinction is important because many textbook mechanistic experiments use mice, whereas statements about humans require attention to species differences.

XIST is transcribed by RNA polymerase II but does not function primarily as a protein-coding messenger. Its RNA remains associated with the nucleus and becomes enriched over the chromosome from which it is produced. In a microscopy experiment using RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization, this enrichment can appear as a cloud associated with the Xi territory. Such a signal is very different from the diffuse cytoplasmic distribution expected of an ordinary translated messenger RNA. The RNA's localization is part of its regulatory function.

A key feature of the mechanism is that XIST acts predominantly in cis. If the RNA is produced from one X chromosome, it preferentially associates with and initiates silencing on that chromosome rather than routinely silencing the homologous X elsewhere in the nucleus. This raises the question of how a long RNA can act over a chromosome without needing a separate complementary recognition sequence at every target gene. The answer involves physical association with chromatin, nuclear organization and the recruitment of regulatory proteins, rather than canonical small interfering RNA cleavage of each transcript.

The XIST RNA contains regions with distinct functional contributions. Some sequences are particularly important for initiating repression, while other regions contribute to localization, RNA retention and recruitment of chromatin-associated factors. An exam-level description need not memorize every repeat domain. The crucial structure-function principle is that producing an RNA and localizing it correctly are separable from its ability to recruit a silencing activity. A mutant RNA may remain detectable over chromatin yet fail to initiate normal transcriptional repression.

Among the factors associated with Xist-mediated silencing in mouse experimental systems, SPEN contributes to transcriptional repression, including pathways linked to histone deacetylation. Removing acetyl groups from appropriate histone sites can favor a less transcriptionally permissive chromatin environment. This is not because acetylation chemically converts DNA into a different sequence, but because histone modification alters protein interactions and chromatin accessibility. Xist thereby links an RNA-based signal to the chromatin-based control systems introduced earlier.

Polycomb-associated chromatin modifications also become enriched over the forming inactive X. In a simplified description, Polycomb complexes contribute to repressive chromatin features including H2AK119 ubiquitination and H3K27 trimethylation. These modifications need not represent two independent instructions of equal timing and significance at every gene. They are components of a developing repressive domain. Recruitment and functional effects can differ between early and late stages and among chromosomal regions, so the correct model is a coordinated transition rather than a single simultaneous chemical event.

The distribution of XIST RNA over the Xi is not best visualized as a perfectly uniform paint coating every DNA base. Chromosomes occupy three-dimensional territories in the nucleus. Sites close in physical space can be far apart along the linear DNA sequence. RNA-associated silencing factors can reach chromosomal regions through these spatial relationships, and accessibility and local regulatory environments influence the speed and extent of repression. This helps explain why some genes are silenced earlier than others and why some remain expressed despite the presence of XIST elsewhere on the chromosome.

The initiation process involves more than the existence of two X chromosomes. A developing cell must establish an appropriate number of active X chromosomes, distinguish future Xa from future Xi and couple that choice to silencing. Counting, choice and execution are useful conceptual categories. Counting concerns the relationship between chromosome complement and the number that must be inactivated. Choice concerns which chromosome becomes inactive. Execution concerns the molecular transition from an active chromosome to a predominantly repressed state.

In mouse models the antisense transcript Tsix is one regulator of Xist expression. Its transcription is associated with repression of Xist on the future active X in relevant developmental contexts. The opposing regulatory relationship helps stabilize a distinction between homologues that initially have similar potential. If a perturbation reduces the ability of one chromosome to restrain Xist at the relevant stage, that chromosome may become preferentially inactivated. This is a useful example of asymmetry emerging from regulation rather than from different numbers of X-linked genes.

The mouse Tsix model should not be transferred mechanically to human cells. The details of the homologous region, expression patterns and early developmental X-inactivation differ between species. In particular, the timing and character of XIST expression in early human embryos do not simply duplicate the established mouse sequence. An examination question about a named mouse regulator should be answered within that system. A more general question about mammalian dosage compensation can be addressed through the core roles of the X-inactivation center, XIST localization and chromatin silencing.

XIST expression on an otherwise active chromosome is an instructive perturbation. In a suitable experimental setting, introducing an Xist-expressing regulatory construct into an autosome can cause silencing of genes near the construct and, depending on context, across a larger domain. Such experiments support the idea that an RNA-associated regulatory system can induce cis repression outside its normal X-chromosome setting. They do not prove that every autosome or every differentiated cell type responds identically. Developmental competence and chromosomal context constrain the effect.

Consider a mutant Xist RNA that is expressed at normal abundance but is defective in recruitment of an essential silencing factor. If an RNA-FISH experiment shows a normal-looking localization domain while nascent RNA from X-linked genes remains abundant, the result argues that localization alone is insufficient. In contrast, if the RNA fails to accumulate over the chromosome and transcription remains high, a defect in localization could explain the outcome without needing to assume that the silencing domain itself has lost biochemical activity. Separate measurements are essential to distinguish these mechanisms.

11. Establishment of heterochromatin and maintenance of the inactive X

An X chromosome that has only recently begun expressing XIST is not necessarily equivalent to a mature Xi. Establishment is a progressive transition in which transcription declines across many genes and several repressive chromatin features accumulate. The characteristic mature state is maintained in differentiated cell lineages even when some factors essential at initiation are no longer required to the same degree. This distinction between starting a process and preserving its consequences is a central principle of epigenetic memory.

The inactive chromosome often shows reduced histone acetylation, enrichment of particular repressive histone modifications, altered chromosome organization and methylation at promoters of many silenced genes. Histone variants such as macroH2A are also associated with mature inactive X chromatin. Each feature can contribute to the overall regulatory environment, but none should be treated as an infallible universal diagnostic at every locus. The presence of a histone mark provides a clue about chromatin state; it does not substitute for measurement of expression from the gene of interest.

DNA methylation at promoters helps stabilize repression once an inactive state is established. If a promoter has become inaccessible and is then methylated, ordinary transcription factors may have reduced ability to bind or recruit the appropriate machinery. After replication, maintenance methylation can restore the modified promoter state on newly synthesized strands. At the same time, inherited histones and continuing interactions with chromatin-associated factors support reconstruction of the larger Xi environment. This layered organization helps explain why Xi repression can withstand the transient disturbances of cell division.

One consequence of layering is that a single perturbation may have different effects when performed at different developmental stages. Removal of Xist before initiation can prevent normal establishment of silencing on the affected chromosome. Removal after establishment may leave many genes repressed for a substantial period because other mechanisms preserve their chromatin state. The maintenance response is locus- and context-dependent; in some settings continued XIST contributes to stability, and loss of it can promote reactivation. Thus neither 'XIST is required forever for every gene' nor 'XIST is irrelevant after initiation' is a safe universal statement.

A simple way to test maintenance in a hypothetical system is to compare two groups of cells with a removable Xist allele. In the first group the allele is disabled before differentiation, and in the second it is disabled after a stable Xi has formed. The timing of the perturbation is the independent variable. The response should include allele-specific nascent transcription, local chromatin marks and the fraction of cells retaining the inactive state over several divisions. Comparing only a single final measurement of total XIST RNA would miss the distinction the experiment was designed to address.

If loss of Xist alone causes little reactivation in a mature cell, but simultaneous interference with DNA methylation causes substantial reactivation, the result is compatible with partly overlapping maintenance mechanisms. It does not establish that all inactive genes depend on precisely two pathways. Additional factors may preserve repression at resistant loci. Combined perturbations may also affect cell viability or proliferation, changing the composition of the measured population. To support a specific mechanistic conclusion, investigators must show that reactivation occurs in cells with the expected inactive chromosome rather than being produced solely by selection.

The condensation of Xi can be visualized as a Barr body in many interphase nuclei. This observation connects molecular chromatin organization with a classical cytological marker. However, the visibility of the body depends on the cell type, cell-cycle phase, staining method and imaging conditions. A nucleus in which no clear Barr body is seen does not necessarily contain no inactive X. Conversely, a dense nuclear region cannot be assigned to Xi solely from its appearance; chromosome-specific probes can provide more direct identification.

The inactive X generally replicates later than the active X in many standard cytological descriptions. Replication timing provides another property correlated with its distinctive chromatin state. The important word is correlated: observing late replication alone does not prove that delayed replication is the sole cause of transcriptional silencing. An experimental intervention that changes replication timing without changing other regulatory components would be needed to test that particular causal claim. Chromosome inactivation is not equivalent to a failure to replicate.

The Xi is also reorganized in three-dimensional space. A chromosome with many silenced promoters can occupy a different nuclear compartment from a chromosome engaged in extensive transcription. Physical compaction, local loops and association with nuclear structures may help maintain a repressive environment, although the relationship between compaction and repression is not identical at every scale. An experimentally observed movement of a locus away from a transcription-rich region may be a consequence, a contributor or both. Causal reasoning should not reduce the entire inactive state to a single image of a condensed chromosome.

The stability of X inactivation is balanced by controlled opportunities for reactivation. In the female mouse germline, the formerly inactive X can be reactivated during germ-cell development. Reprogramming in certain experimental cell states can also alter Xi regulation. These examples demonstrate that the chromosome retains information necessary for gene expression and is not irreversibly destroyed. The reactivation event is biologically distinct from the routine inheritance of Xi in a differentiated somatic lineage. The regulatory requirements change when the cell is preparing to establish new developmental states.

12. Random choice, clonal inheritance and tissue mosaicism

For random X inactivation in a typical XX embryonic cell population, either parental X can become the inactive one. Once an individual cell has established a particular choice, its descendants ordinarily retain that choice during subsequent somatic proliferation. Randomness applies primarily to the original decision in each precursor, not to the decision being repeated independently each time a mature cell divides. Confusing those two stages leads to incorrect predictions about tissue organization.

Suppose four precursor cells each produce a large group of descendants. Two precursors retain the maternal X as active and two retain the paternal X. If all four clones grow equally, the resulting tissue has equal contributions from the two classes. Nevertheless, individual cells are not necessarily expressing both parental alleles of a gene subject to inactivation. The population average conceals a mosaic in which each clone has a characteristic active allele. This distinction is similar to, but mechanistically different from, the bulk allele-expression ambiguity encountered in genomic imprinting.

A perfectly balanced tissue is not required by random choice. If a small number of embryonic precursors make independent choices, chance alone can produce unequal numbers of clones with the two active X chromosomes. In a toy model with n equivalent precursors, each choosing the maternal X as active with probability one-half, the number K of maternal-active precursors follows a binomial distribution. The probability of exactly k is the binomial coefficient n choose k multiplied by (1/2) to the power n. The assumptions include independence, equal choice probability and no subsequent difference in clone growth.

For four such precursors, the probability that exactly two retain the maternal X active is six divided by sixteen, or 37.5 percent. The probability that all four retain the same parental X active is two divided by sixteen, or 12.5 percent. A very small founding population can therefore yield strongly skewed proportions without any intrinsic preference for one chromosome. As the number of independent equally contributing precursors increases, the proportional fluctuation expected from chance decreases. This model does not determine the actual number of founding cells in a real tissue.

The expected maternal-active fraction in the binomial model is one-half. Its standard deviation is the square root of p(1-p)/n, where p is the probability of maternal-active choice. At p equal to 0.5 and n equal to 100, the standard deviation of the fraction is 0.05, or five percentage points. If n is four, the corresponding value is 0.25. The biological interpretation is that small effective founder numbers produce greater variation between tissues or individuals, even with an unbiased choice mechanism.

Unequal clone growth can create skew even when the initial choices were balanced. Suppose equal numbers of two classes initially exist, but one class proliferates faster because the active X carries an allele that improves survival in the measured tissue. After several rounds, the class with the favorable allele becomes overrepresented. The original inactivation machinery need not have preferred that chromosome. This is secondary skewing due to selection, and it can be distinguished conceptually from primary skewing at the stage of choice by following the cells over time.

In a cell population where the active maternal allele produces a functional enzyme and the active paternal allele carries a loss-of-function variant, the tissue phenotype depends on both the proportion and spatial distribution of the two cell classes. If the enzyme acts only within the cell that produces it, mutant-active cells may remain deficient even when neighboring cells are normal. If the relevant product is secreted or metabolites can be exchanged, the relationship between clone fraction and organismal function may be less direct. Therefore a measured ratio of active alleles is not automatically a quantitative prediction of phenotype severity.

Random X inactivation also differs from a fixed parent-of-origin rule. In most somatic lineages of placental mammals that undergo random X inactivation, the maternal X is not obligatorily inactive or obligatorily active. At an autosomal paternally expressed imprinted locus, by contrast, the relevant paternal allele is normally active across the population displaying that imprint. A bulk sample can show similar amounts of maternal and paternal X-linked RNA under random X inactivation, whereas a correctly chosen sample from a strictly imprinted locus shows a strong parental preference.

There are developmental and species-specific exceptions. In mice, an imprinted form of X inactivation preferentially affects the paternal X during early development and is retained in certain extraembryonic lineages; embryonic lineages undergo reactivation followed by random X inactivation. Marsupials also show a predominantly paternal form of X inactivation, with mechanisms and completeness differing from those of placental mammals. These exceptions clarify that random choice is not the definition of all X inactivation. A question specifying tissue and species should be interpreted within that context.

A classic count-based rule is that the number of inactive X chromosomes in a typical differentiated mammalian somatic cell is approximately the total number of X chromosomes minus one, provided at least one X is present and the normal counting and inactivation mechanisms operate. Thus XX cells generally have one Xi, XXX cells two, and XXY cells one, whereas a typical XY cell has none. The Y chromosome does not count as an inactive X. These predictions refer to expected chromosome regulation, not necessarily the number of readily visible staining bodies in an arbitrary microscopic image.

The count rule does not mean that individuals with extra X chromosomes must have identical gene dosage to individuals with the typical chromosome complement. Escape genes, pseudoautosomal genes and other regulatory differences can preserve dosage effects even when all but one X are largely silenced. The rule also should not be extended without qualification to early developmental stages, germ cells, abnormal cell lines or unusual chromosome rearrangements. Examination problems often assume ordinary differentiated somatic cells, and the answer should state that assumption when necessary.

13. Escape genes and alternative dosage-compensation systems

A chromosome-wide inactivation system must still accommodate genes whose appropriate dosage is not achieved by silencing every copy. The pseudoautosomal regions of the mammalian sex chromosomes contain homologous sequences on X and Y that participate in pairing and recombination during meiosis. Many genes in these regions escape X inactivation. A typical XX cell can therefore express two X-derived copies of a pseudoautosomal gene, whereas a typical XY cell may express one X-derived and one Y-derived copy. Silencing one pseudoautosomal copy in XX cells would not necessarily improve equivalence between the chromosome complements.

The pseudoautosomal explanation accounts for only a subset of escape. Some genes located outside these regions can be transcribed from Xi. The fraction of expression from Xi varies across genes, tissues and individuals. Inactivation is therefore often described by the relative expression of an Xi allele compared with its Xa counterpart, not merely by a binary annotation. A gene may escape partially, such that transcription from Xi is detectable but lower than that from Xa. The same gene can be more strongly repressed in one cellular context than another.

Local regulatory environment is one possible contributor to escape. A promoter may retain interactions with factors supporting transcription, and chromatin organization can limit the spread of repressive effects from adjacent regions. Boundaries and chromosome topology can help maintain distinct local domains. However, observing a boundary-associated protein near an escaping gene does not by itself establish that this protein is both necessary and sufficient for escape. A targeted perturbation followed by allele-specific expression measurement is necessary to evaluate that hypothesis.

A quantitative model clarifies the biological significance of partial escape. Let a be the expression of one active allele of an X-linked gene under defined conditions. Let e be the fraction of that output supplied by each inactive X copy. In an XX cell the predicted expression is a(1+e), assuming the active allele has the same output as the single X in an otherwise comparable XY cell and the Y has no functional homolog. For a fully inactivated gene e is zero, giving equal predicted expression. For a fully escaping gene e is one, giving twice the predicted expression in the XX cell.

Suppose that a particular active allele produces 80 transcript units and the Xi copy produces 20. The escape fraction is 20/80, or 0.25. Total XX expression is 100 units, compared with 80 units in the idealized XY comparison. The ratio is 1.25, corresponding to 25 percent higher output. This calculation does not establish that all XX tissues actually have 25 percent higher expression of that gene. The assumed equal Xa output, identical cell type and absence of a Y-derived contribution are essential conditions for the comparison.

The model extends to a typical somatic cell with three X chromosomes and one active X. If the two Xi copies each supply 20 units, total expression becomes 120 units and the ratio relative to a single Xa becomes 1.5. Genes subject to complete inactivation remain near the idealized single-copy baseline, whereas escaping genes retain a dosage effect as X copy number increases. This is why counting Barr bodies cannot establish complete functional dosage equivalence across chromosome complements.

Even a balanced bulk maternal-to-paternal transcript ratio cannot establish escape. Imagine a population of XX cells with equal contributions of maternal-active and paternal-active clones. A fully silenced gene can produce a balanced ratio because each class supplies RNA from a different allele. To identify escape, the investigator must know which X is inactive in each cell or clone and then show that the gene is expressed from that chromosome. A clonal population with established skew, or an appropriate single-cell allele-specific measurement, can make this inference possible.

Comparative biology reinforces the idea that a common physiological problem need not have a universal molecular solution. In Drosophila, the male-specific lethal complex participates in increased expression of many genes on the single male X chromosome. Its action involves chromatin modification, including acetylation of histone H4 at lysine 16. This strategy differs from the mammalian system of reducing transcription from most genes on one X chromosome in XX cells. Both involve chromatin-mediated control but adjust expression in different directions.

In the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, XX hermaphrodites reduce transcription from both X chromosomes through a dosage compensation complex with condensin-related components, compared with the single X in XO males. The relevant unit of comparison is again gene output rather than simply the number of active-looking chromosomes. Calling either X a conventional mammalian Xi would be misleading because the system does not silence most genes on just one chromosome.

These comparisons are most useful when organized by three questions. Which chromosome complement is adjusted? Is output increased or decreased? Does regulation act on one chromosome or on both? The mammalian, fly and nematode examples answer these questions differently. Their common purpose does not imply that their proteins are homologous in every respect or that the mammalian XIST pathway is present in all animals. Evolution can recruit different chromatin-associated factors to address similar dosage constraints.

14. Experimental reasoning: identifying the source and persistence of silencing

A robust explanation of epigenetic regulation requires evidence for several different claims. One claim concerns the DNA sequence and chromosome copy number. A second concerns the molecular state of chromatin. A third concerns transcription from a particular allele. A fourth concerns the persistence of the state after the original regulatory input is removed. These claims require different measurements, and none can safely be inferred from another without suitable controls.

For example, a methylation assay based on bisulfite conversion can distinguish many unmethylated cytosines from methylated cytosines through their different conversion behavior followed by sequence analysis. Ordinary bisulfite sequencing does not distinguish 5-methylcytosine from 5-hydroxymethylcytosine in the simplest interpretation; incomplete conversion can also create a false methylation signal. An unmethylated control DNA can help estimate conversion efficiency. If a particular sequence polymorphism distinguishes the maternal and paternal copies, reads covering both the marker and the assayed sites can relate methylation to parent of origin.

A chromatin immunoprecipitation assay can test whether a protein or histone modification is enriched near a region of interest. Chromatin fragments associated with the chosen antigen are recovered using an antibody, and their DNA is measured relative to suitable input and controls. In an imprinting experiment, a CTCF antibody could be used to ask whether the maternal H19 control region is preferentially occupied. Enrichment is evidence of association, not automatically evidence that occupancy is the cause of expression. A site mutation that changes occupancy and has the predicted effect on nearby transcription provides a different kind of evidence.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization can reveal the localization of a particular RNA in the nucleus. In the X-inactivation setting, XIST accumulation over one chromosome is consistent with formation or maintenance of an Xi-associated RNA domain. A chromosome-specific DNA probe can help identify the territory. Detection of nascent transcripts from particular X-linked genes can then test whether transcription occurs within or outside that domain. These observations relate spatial localization to transcriptional activity without assuming that an entire chromosome is uniformly inactive.

Nascent transcription and mature RNA abundance should be distinguished when interpreting an allele-specific perturbation. A mature transcript can remain detectable after its gene stops initiating transcription because the RNA requires time to degrade. Conversely, a newly activated gene may have detectable nascent RNA before mature RNA accumulates substantially. If the research question is whether XIST directly initiates transcriptional repression, early measurements of newly synthesized RNA are particularly informative. If the question concerns functional dosage of a protein, measurements of protein abundance and activity may be needed later.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which an inducible regulatory factor suppresses a reporter for one day and is then removed. The reporter remains silent for several weeks. To test memory, the investigator should verify loss of the inducing factor, monitor the reporter across documented cell divisions and exclude changes in DNA sequence or reporter copy number. An otherwise identical reporter lacking the proposed recruitment site can serve as a control. If both reporters remain silent to the same extent, the particular recruitment site may not explain the phenomenon. If only the intact reporter retains repression, the evidence for site-dependent memory is stronger.

Further discrimination requires testing the mechanism after the state has formed. Removing a writer before the initial treatment asks whether it contributes to establishment. Removing it after the treatment asks whether it contributes to maintenance. A factor may be essential in the first experiment but dispensable in the second if the established state recruits a separate maintenance mechanism. Conversely, a maintenance enzyme can be unnecessary for the initial silencing signal yet necessary for stable inheritance through successive divisions.

In a hypothetical rescue experiment, cells lacking a methylation maintenance factor show progressive loss of a repressive state. Reintroducing an active version of the factor before extensive methylation loss might restore maintenance. Introducing it after all original marks have been erased might fail to restore the original pattern if there is no remaining positional information or de novo targeting mechanism. This difference is predicted by the distinction between restoration of an existing pattern and establishment of a new one. A failed late rescue is therefore not necessarily evidence that the reintroduced enzyme is nonfunctional.

A catalytic mutant and a recruitment mutant provide another useful comparison. Both can reduce methylation at a locus in intact cells, but the catalytic mutant may also fail to methylate an appropriate substrate in a simplified biochemical assay. A recruitment mutant might retain catalytic activity in that assay while failing to localize correctly in cells. This comparison illustrates why an experimental phenotype must be interpreted at the appropriate level of biological organization. Activity measured with purified molecules does not guarantee effective regulation within chromatin.

In many questions the student receives only a few pieces of evidence and must identify the most defensible conclusion rather than construct an entire experimental model. For example, a normal DNA sequence, increased promoter methylation and reduced RNA expression are consistent with epigenetic repression. They do not establish causality, rule out altered transcription factor abundance or demonstrate inheritance through cell division. The best answer may explicitly state what additional experiment would discriminate these possibilities. Scientific rigor involves limiting conclusions to what the observations support.

An informative quantitative measure of parental expression is the maternal RNA fraction M/(M+P), where M and P are allele-specific RNA quantities measured in equivalent units. This expression is meaningful only when total RNA is greater than zero and measurements have been corrected for relevant biases. If M is 30 units and P is 90 units, the maternal fraction is 0.25 and the paternal fraction is 0.75. The ratio P/M is three. These measures describe the same dataset but emphasize different quantities; none alone identifies whether the expression difference is caused by imprinting.

A useful companion measure is total RNA abundance, M+P. Imagine a strictly maternally expressed gene producing 120 maternal units and no paternal units. After treatment, the maternal contribution becomes 30 and the paternal contribution becomes 90. Total expression remains 120 units despite a major change in parental expression pattern. If the treatment instead reduces maternal output to 30 without activating paternal expression, total output declines to 30 but the maternal fraction remains one. Both quantities should be reported when the biological question concerns loss of imprinting and gene dosage together.

15. Integrating regulatory mechanisms and biological outcomes

The three major topics of this chapter can be connected through the distinction between information, interpretation and output. The DNA sequence supplies binding sites, coding instructions and structural context. An epigenetic feature supplies a regulatory cue that can distinguish otherwise similar copies or cells. A protein or RNA-based mechanism interprets that cue. The resulting output is transcription, RNA processing or protein production. Persistence requires that some component of the regulatory state survive or be reconstructed following cell division.

In mitotic memory, the relevant comparison is typically between descendant cell lineages under equivalent conditions. Their genome sequences can be the same, while their transcriptional programs remain distinct. In genomic imprinting, the relevant comparison is between maternal and paternal alleles at a particular locus. In X inactivation, the comparison is between X chromosomes within a cell and between clones that have made different chromosome choices. The biological level at which the asymmetry occurs determines which measurements are most informative.

A methylated DNA region can have different consequences in these systems. At an ordinary silenced promoter, methylation may reduce transcription by interfering with activation or favoring repressive factors. At the H19 control region, methylation reduces insulator occupancy and can indirectly support paternal Igf2 expression. At a mature Xi, methylation of many silenced promoters contributes to stable repression as part of a broader chromosome state. The same chemical modification therefore cannot be assigned one universal transcriptional sign without specifying its regulatory context.

RNA likewise performs diverse functions. In the preceding chapter, miRNA and siRNA are sequence-guided regulators that can reduce RNA stability or translation through Argonaute-containing complexes. At some imprinted loci, a long transcript contributes to local gene repression through transcriptional or chromatin-associated mechanisms. XIST acts over a chromosome in cis and coordinates repressive chromatin changes. All are examples of regulation involving noncoding RNA, but their size, localization, targets and downstream effectors differ. Assigning Dicer to every RNA-based silencing process would confuse the broad category with one particular pathway.

The consequences of a perturbation depend on whether the targeted component creates a state, interprets a state or maintains a state. A mutation destroying a CTCF recognition site can change an allele's response even if its methylation is unchanged. A loss of maintenance methylation may gradually erode a state without immediately changing all affected transcripts. A defect in XIST recruitment of repressors can prevent establishment of Xi even if XIST RNA remains visible. These outcomes follow from identifying the molecular step that is disrupted rather than treating all regulatory proteins as equivalent switches.

A final distinction concerns biological failure and normal reprogramming. Loss of imprint maintenance at the wrong developmental stage may disrupt the dosage of growth-regulatory genes. Erasure of old imprints in the germline is instead necessary for normal establishment of appropriate parental marks. Reactivation of Xi in a developing germ-cell lineage can be normal, whereas unintended reactivation of some Xi genes in a differentiated cell can alter dosage. The same directional change in methylation or expression can have different significance depending on when and where it occurs.

These principles have practical applications in cell biology and biotechnology. When cells are reprogrammed into a more developmentally flexible state, their gene expression must change and some existing regulatory memories may be reset. Examining imprinting and X-linked expression can reveal whether the resulting cells have retained or altered particular epigenetic features. A cell that looks morphologically similar to a reference population may still differ in these regulatory properties. Such measurements complement, rather than replace, assessments of cell identity and function.

Another application is interpretation of allele-specific expression in genetic studies. A coding variant that is rarely expressed because it resides on a silenced parental allele may have a different effect from the same variant on an expressed allele. In an X-linked mosaic, a bulk sample may obscure a subset of cells in which a variant is functionally important. The general lesson is that a genome sequence identifies potential products but not the full pattern of their utilization. Appropriate expression and regulatory-state evidence are required to connect genotype to phenotype.

The chapter also provides a bridge to cell signaling. A signaling pathway can activate a transcription factor, which can then recruit chromatin regulators and change gene expression. Some effects disappear when the signal is removed, whereas others persist because downstream regulatory states are self-maintaining. The next unit considers how cells receive, transmit and interpret signals; the mechanisms studied here explain why an identical current signal can sometimes elicit distinct responses in cells with different regulatory histories. This connection requires no assumption that every signaling response is permanently imprinted.

An advanced examination problem should therefore be approached by establishing the comparison before identifying the mechanism. Is the question contrasting parental alleles, X chromosome complements, earlier and later developmental stages, or genetically identical lineages? Does the observation describe DNA methylation, RNA abundance, protein activity, or a chromosomal structure? Has the experiment measured a cause or a correlated feature? If those questions are answered explicitly, the student can reason through unfamiliar systems even when the named molecular components differ from the examples used in this chapter.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Every epigenetic change is inherited indefinitely

Correction: Persistence must be measured; transient marks may not constitute memory. Why it is confused: The term epigenetic is often used loosely. Exam reasoning: Distinguish short-term induction from mitotic inheritance.

Methylation changes the DNA base sequence

Correction: A methyl group modifies cytosine without substituting another base. Why it is confused: Chemical change is confused with mutation. Exam reasoning: Contrast sequencing and methylation assays.

DNA polymerase copies methylation automatically

Correction: Maintenance methylation requires separate enzymatic activity. Why it is confused: Sequence copying is mistaken for mark copying. Exam reasoning: Predict hemimethylation immediately after replication.

Passive demethylation removes old methyl groups

Correction: It dilutes marked cytosines by producing unmodified DNA. Why it is confused: A falling fraction looks like physical removal. Exam reasoning: Use division-count data and nondividing controls.

All histone methylation represses transcription

Correction: Effect depends on the residue, modification and context. Why it is confused: The methyl group is treated as one universal signal. Exam reasoning: Distinguish H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 from active-associated marks.

A DMR is always the primary ICR

Correction: A DMR can be secondary; functional control must be demonstrated. Why it is confused: Observation and mechanism are conflated. Exam reasoning: Interpret deletion and rescue results.

Imprints preserve grandparental identity forever

Correction: Germline erasure and reestablishment usually reset the parental mark. Why it is confused: Stable somatic inheritance is confused with transmission through gametes. Exam reasoning: Trace a maternal allele through a male germline.

Methylation always reduces the nearest gene's expression

Correction: At an insulator it can indirectly enable another gene's expression. Why it is confused: Promoter examples are generalized to all DNA. Exam reasoning: Predict Igf2 expression after ICR methylation.

A 50% methylation average proves one marked parental allele

Correction: Mixed or partially marked populations can give the same average. Why it is confused: Bulk averages conceal molecular heterogeneity. Exam reasoning: Choose an allele-resolved methylation assay.

A reciprocal-cross phenotype alone proves imprinting

Correction: Maternal environment or cytoplasmic factors may explain a phenotype. Why it is confused: Parentage is mistaken for allele-specific transcription. Exam reasoning: Combine reciprocal crosses with allelic RNA and genomic controls.

X inactivation deletes an X

Correction: Xi DNA remains and replicates. Why it is confused: A silent chromosome is confused with an absent chromosome. Exam reasoning: Compare DNA copy number with transcription.

All Xi genes are off

Correction: XIST and escape genes are exceptions. Why it is confused: The chromosome-wide label is taken literally. Exam reasoning: Compute partial escape and dosage.

Random XCI is repeated at every mitosis

Correction: A precursor's established choice is usually inherited clonally. Why it is confused: Random initial choice is confused with maintenance. Exam reasoning: Predict lineage mosaics.

Random choice guarantees a 50:50 tissue

Correction: Small founder numbers and selection can skew proportions. Why it is confused: Expected value is mistaken for an exact outcome. Exam reasoning: Apply a binomial model.

XIST works like siRNA

Correction: XIST is a long nuclear RNA with chromosome-local regulatory action. Why it is confused: All noncoding RNA is assigned to RISC. Exam reasoning: Compare localization and effector mechanisms.

An XIST cloud proves full silencing

Correction: Localization and repression are separable; expression must be measured. Why it is confused: A visual marker is equated with function. Exam reasoning: Analyze a localization-competent silencing mutant.

Barr body count proves complete dosage compensation

Correction: Escape genes can retain copy-number effects. Why it is confused: Chromosome counts are equated with output. Exam reasoning: Calculate dosage in XXX cells.

Every organism uses mammalian XCI

Correction: Flies and nematodes use different regulatory strategies. Why it is confused: A shared purpose is mistaken for identical mechanism. Exam reasoning: Compare which chromosome complement is adjusted.

Revision and mastery

Stable regulation depends on establishment, interpretation and maintenance of a state. Genomic imprinting uses parent-specific regulation at selected loci; mammalian XCI largely silences one X and preserves chromosome choice across somatic lineages. DNA methylation, chromatin and noncoding RNAs can cooperate but their roles are context dependent.

Essential equations

  • f(n)=f₀[(1+p)/2]^n under the idealized maintenance model.
  • P(K=k)=C(n,k)p^k(1−p)^(n−k) for independent equal founder choices.
  • SD of founder fraction=√[p(1−p)/n].
  • Maternal RNA fraction=M/(M+P), when total is positive.
  • Ideal expression with N X chromosomes and one Xa=a[1+(N−1)e].
