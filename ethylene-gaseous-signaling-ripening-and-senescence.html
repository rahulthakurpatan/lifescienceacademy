1. A hormone that moves through air

A ripening fruit can change the developmental behavior of another fruit that is not touching it. This observation is unusual because the familiar chemical signals of plants are often imagined as dissolved substances moving through sap or passing between neighboring cells. Ethylene, a molecule consisting of two carbon atoms and four hydrogen atoms, provides an alternative mode of communication. The two carbon atoms are linked by a double bond. At ordinary physiological temperatures the substance is a gas, and an ethylene molecule can leave its cell of origin, cross an air-filled space and enter another living tissue. The fact that a chemical travels through the air does not make its effects nonspecific. A receptor distinguishes ethylene from many other small molecules, and regulated changes in the receptor system communicate its presence to the transcriptional machinery of the receiving cell.

The physiological behavior of a signal depends on the rate at which it is produced, the ease with which it leaves the region of production and the responsiveness of the tissue that receives it. Consider a piece of fruit maintained in a ventilated room and another identical fruit maintained in a closed vessel. If their initial production rates are equal, the concentration of ethylene surrounding the enclosed fruit may rise more rapidly. The enclosed fruit has not necessarily acquired a more active biosynthetic enzyme. Instead, its gaseous product is being removed more slowly. If the increased ethylene concentration induces additional ethylene synthesis, the initially physical difference in ventilation may become a difference in metabolic activity. This example separates accumulation from production and introduces the importance of feedback.

A hormone must be distinguished from a general toxic influence. At sufficiently high concentrations or under inappropriate conditions many chemical compounds alter plant growth, but a reproducible response alone does not establish that the compound is a normal endogenous regulator. Evidence for ethylene as a plant hormone includes its synthesis by plant tissues, its physiological effects at low concentrations, the existence of receptors and signal transduction proteins, and the consequences of altering either biosynthesis or sensitivity genetically. Together these forms of evidence explain why exposure to a small molecule in the atmosphere can result in coordinated changes in growth and development rather than simply causing tissue injury.

The same ethylene molecule is involved in several outcomes that appear very different. It can restrict the elongation of a dark-grown seedling, stimulate expansion in certain submerged plants, induce ripening in a mature tomato, and accelerate shedding of particular floral organs. This diversity is not contradictory. The signal changes the activity of downstream regulatory systems; the available transcription factors, target genes, developmental competence and environmental conditions differ among tissues. The response of a dark-grown hypocotyl should therefore not be treated as a universal definition of ethylene action. A major objective of this chapter is to connect a common molecular pathway with distinct physiological outputs without assuming that every plant behaves identically.

2. Building ethylene from methionine

Methionine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that participates in protein synthesis and several metabolic processes. Its role in ethylene biosynthesis begins when methionine adenosyltransferase uses ATP to convert methionine to S-adenosyl-L-methionine, commonly abbreviated SAM. The adenosyl group attached to sulfur changes the chemical reactivity of the molecule. SAM is a metabolic junction: methyltransferases use it as a methyl donor, other pathways consume it in polyamine metabolism, and ethylene biosynthesis draws material from the same cellular pool. It is therefore misleading to regard every molecule of SAM as committed to making ethylene.

Commitment to the conventional ethylene route occurs through ACC synthase, abbreviated ACS. This pyridoxal phosphate-dependent enzyme converts SAM to 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) and 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA). ACC contains a three-membered carbon ring and a carboxyl group. It is not itself a gaseous molecule and behaves differently from ethylene in the aqueous environment of a cell. The production of ACC establishes a useful intermediate pool from which ethylene can subsequently be formed. The amount of ACC accumulated at a given instant reflects the difference between its generation and all the processes that consume, transport or conjugate it.

ACC oxidase (ACO) catalyzes the final conversion. The reaction requires molecular oxygen and a ferrous iron-containing catalytic system, with ascorbate participating as a reductant and bicarbonate supporting activity. Ethylene, carbon dioxide and cyanide are products derived during ACC oxidation. The cyanide byproduct is metabolically important because it can interfere with respiratory enzymes if allowed to accumulate. Plant cells possess cyanide-detoxifying metabolism, including β-cyanoalanine synthase, which combines cyanide with cysteine. The ability to manage a byproduct belongs to the physiological cost of producing a hormone and cannot be inferred merely from the small size of the final product.

Two enzymes control different positions in this route. If ACS activity is impaired, little newly synthesized ACC is available to ACO. Supplying ACC externally can potentially bypass the defect, provided it reaches the appropriate cells and sufficient ACO activity and oxygen are available. In contrast, a severe ACO defect cannot be bypassed by providing more ACC. External ethylene bypasses both biosynthetic steps, but it cannot necessarily rescue a defect in the hormone's receptor or its downstream signaling components. This sequence of chemical rescue treatments offers a powerful way to distinguish biosynthesis and responsiveness without directly observing every enzymatic reaction.

ACS is often a major regulatory point because its activity controls entry into the dedicated pathway. However, it is not always the sole rate-limiting step. A cell with abundant ACC but inadequate oxygen cannot sustain a high ACO reaction rate. A developing fruit may alter the expression of both enzymes. Several ACS isoforms respond differently to developmental and environmental signals, and protein turnover can change enzyme abundance without a corresponding change in messenger RNA. When interpreting an experiment, distinguish transcript abundance, enzyme protein abundance, catalytic activity and pathway flux. The four measurements describe related but nonidentical properties.

An important feature of the pathway is that the sulfur-containing portion of methionine is not discarded as ethylene. MTA enters the methionine salvage pathway, commonly called the Yang cycle, in which the methylthio group is recycled into methionine. This preserves sulfur and supports repeated ethylene production without consuming an entirely new sulfur atom for every ethylene molecule formed. The cycle still requires additional metabolic resources; recycling does not make ethylene biosynthesis energetically free. The regenerated methionine must again be activated to SAM using ATP. It follows that a defect in methionine salvage may be particularly consequential during sustained high production, when demand on the common methionine and SAM pools is high.

3. Precursor storage, transport and turnover

A distinction between ethylene itself and its precursor clarifies the official syllabus terms storage, breakdown and transport. Ethylene is not generally stored as a stable inactive glycoside waiting to be hydrolyzed. Its concentration is influenced by continuous synthesis, escape through intercellular air spaces, exchange with the surrounding atmosphere and, under some conditions, physical retention. By comparison, ACC is chemically suitable for transport in aqueous compartments and can be metabolically diverted into conjugated forms. Malonyl-ACC is an important example; other conjugates occur in plants. These compounds should not all be assumed to release ACC rapidly under every physiological condition. Conjugation may decrease the immediate availability of free precursor, and the subsequent fate of the conjugate depends on species and tissue.

If the amount of free ACC increases after a stress treatment, several mechanisms remain possible. ACS might be producing ACC more quickly, ACO might be consuming it more slowly, import from other tissue might have increased, or conjugation might have decreased. A single concentration measurement does not distinguish these alternatives. Similarly, measurement of ethylene released by a whole leaf does not reveal which cell type supplied the precursor. A rigorous interpretation requires a time series, measurements of precursor pools and enzyme activity, or a carefully chosen perturbation of one step.

Ethylene movement over a short distance can occur by diffusion. The net flux is directed down a concentration gradient, although individual molecules move in random directions. The gas moves more readily through interconnected air spaces than through a water-filled route of comparable length. A waxy surface, submerged tissue or restricted ventilation can change resistance to escape. ACC, by contrast, may move between cells through regulated transport processes and can travel long distances in vascular sap. Thus, the organ in which ethylene is ultimately detected may not be the organ in which the precursor was synthesized.

The signaling lifetime of ethylene is not simply the chemical lifetime of an ethylene molecule. Once the gas concentration falls, receptors can again support repression of the response, but induced messenger RNAs, proteins and physiological changes may persist. A fruit cell wall that has already been extensively modified will not instantly recover its original mechanical properties when ethylene is removed. This difference between the concentration of a signal and the duration of its consequences is useful when interpreting pulse treatments, withdrawal experiments and the response of produce to altered storage atmospheres.

A simple mass balance provides a quantitative model for an enclosed chamber. Let P represent net emission into the headspace in microlitres per hour, V the chamber's gas volume in litres, and C the concentration in microlitres per litre. In a perfectly mixed sealed chamber with constant P and negligible uptake or leakage, dC/dt = P/V. If a sample emits 0.40 microlitres per hour into a 2.0 litre headspace for three hours from a zero starting concentration, the accumulated amount is 1.2 microlitres and C is 0.60 microlitres per litre, approximately 0.60 ppm by volume. This is an illustrative calculation, not a measured biological rate. The headspace must exclude volume occupied by the fruit. The assumption of constant emission may fail if the fruit starts autocatalytic synthesis or if oxygen becomes limiting.

For a ventilated chamber in which fresh air contains negligible ethylene, a first approximation is dC/dt = P/V − QC/V, where Q is the air exchange flow in litres per hour. At a steady concentration the two fluxes balance, giving C = P/Q. Doubling Q halves C if emission is unchanged. This is not a law of ripening: production can respond to concentration and developmental stage. It is a physical model for separating ventilation and biosynthesis. In experimental comparisons, identical chamber volumes, controlled gas flow and measurements within a validated range are essential.

4. The unusual logic of ethylene perception

Ethylene perception in Arabidopsis involves a family of receptors that includes ETR1, ETR2, ERS1, ERS2 and EIN4. The receptors are primarily associated with the endoplasmic reticulum membrane. Their membrane-spanning regions participate in ethylene binding, supported by a copper cofactor. Their cytosolic portions interact with downstream regulatory proteins. Although some ethylene receptor domains resemble bacterial histidine kinases, the essential examination-level logic of the pathway should not be replaced by a conventional positive two-component phosphorelay. The principal Arabidopsis receptor system suppresses ethylene responses when the ligand is absent.

The protein kinase CTR1, named for the constitutive triple response mutant in which its normal activity is lost, associates with the receptors. In the absence of ethylene, receptor-dependent CTR1 activity maintains a repressive state. One important substrate of CTR1 is EIN2, a membrane-associated positive regulator. CTR1 phosphorylates the cytosolic region of EIN2 and prevents activation of the downstream response. Thus an active unoccupied receptor is not evidence of active ethylene-responsive transcription. It is evidence of a maintained brake on transcriptional activation.

Ethylene binding changes the receptor system in a manner that decreases CTR1-mediated repression. The carboxyl-terminal portion of EIN2 can then participate in signal transfer, including nuclear signaling and regulation of translation. Downstream, the transcription factors EIN3 and EIL1 accumulate and regulate responsive genes, including genes encoding additional transcription factors of the ethylene response factor family. The pathway consequently converts a local change in gas concentration into changes in gene expression. Some effects occur more quickly than others because different target systems require different amounts of transcription, translation and cellular remodeling.

EIN3 abundance is regulated in part by the F-box proteins EBF1 and EBF2. These are specificity components of ubiquitin ligase complexes that promote turnover of EIN3-family regulators. When ethylene signaling is active, EIN2-dependent mechanisms reduce the effective turnover pressure on EIN3, allowing its accumulation. Feedback involving EBF proteins and receptor abundance helps shape the duration and sensitivity of responses. The pathway therefore should not be understood as a permanently latched switch. It is a dynamic balance between the synthesis, stabilization and degradation of regulatory molecules.

It is helpful to use two sign changes to remember the canonical pathway. An unliganded receptor supports CTR1 activity, and CTR1 inhibits a positive regulator, EIN2. EIN2 in turn enables increased EIN3/EIL1-dependent transcription. Ethylene inhibits the upstream inhibitory system. Removing inhibition from a positive regulator increases the downstream response. In an examination, following the signs of the arrows is safer than using the ambiguous expression receptor activation, which may refer either to ligand binding or to the activity of the receptor as a negative regulator.

There are five receptors rather than only one. Consequently the phenotype caused by loss of a single receptor is not necessarily equivalent to the phenotype of losing most receptor activity. Other family members can continue to repress signaling. Moreover, a particular dominant receptor allele can preserve a repressive state despite exposure to ethylene. This is a fundamentally different genetic event from deletion of a receptor gene. The distinction between gain-of-function and loss-of-function is essential when interpreting ETR1-associated ethylene insensitivity.

5. The triple response as a genetic assay

A dark-grown Arabidopsis seedling elongates its hypocotyl as it searches for light. Its apical hook helps protect the delicate shoot apex. Exposure to ethylene under these conditions alters the pattern: hypocotyl elongation is inhibited, radial thickening becomes conspicuous, and the apical hook becomes exaggerated. Root elongation is also commonly reduced. Descriptions of the triple response vary slightly in how the root phenotype is counted, so the organism and the measured feature should always be specified. The important point is that ethylene creates a recognizable combination of growth responses rather than merely reducing total seedling size.

The ecological interpretation is that a seedling encountering mechanical resistance may alter its growth so that its vulnerable apex is better protected. This is a useful functional model, not proof that every component of the assay was selected solely for soil emergence. A mutation affecting hook formation can change one part of the visible response without necessarily abolishing all ethylene-dependent transcription. Conversely, a severely unhealthy seedling can be short even without ethylene pathway activation. Measurements of radial growth, hook curvature, reporter expression and untreated controls help distinguish these possibilities.

A classical screen identifies seedlings that fail to change normally in an ethylene-containing atmosphere or that show a response without added ethylene. A dominant insensitive receptor allele such as etr1-1 can maintain the repression of the pathway; added ethylene then has little effect on the characteristic seedling phenotype. Loss of the positive signal transducer EIN2 also causes insensitivity, but for a different reason: removal of upstream repression cannot be conveyed to the downstream regulators. A loss-of-function ctr1 mutant exhibits a constitutive response because an essential repressor is defective.

Roman and colleagues used genetic relationships among mutant classes to help order components of the pathway. If the phenotype of a double mutant resembles that of one single mutant, the corresponding allele is said to be epistatic in that comparison. In the simplified canonical model, loss of CTR1 can bypass a receptor that maintains repression, whereas loss of EIN2 can suppress the constitutive response caused by loss of CTR1. These results place the receptor upstream of CTR1 and the positive EIN2 step downstream. Epistasis does not establish direct physical interaction; it gives functional ordering under the specified genetic and phenotypic conditions. Biochemical tests are required to establish phosphorylation or direct protein binding.

To interpret a gas treatment experiment, compare the response to ACC with the response to ethylene itself. Suppose seedling A fails to respond to ACC but responds to ethylene, while seedling B fails to respond to both. Under conditions in which ACC is absorbed normally, A may have reduced ACO activity and B may have a defect in perception or transduction. However, failure to respond to ACC could also be caused by poor uptake or diversion into conjugates. Direct measurement of ACC and ethylene production would help establish the causal step. If a seedling responds normally to both ACC and ethylene but not to a stimulus that usually increases endogenous ethylene, an upstream biosynthetic or stimulus-sensing defect becomes more plausible.

6. Fruit ripening and positive feedback

Fruit development and ripening are related but distinct processes. A fruit grows through cell division and expansion, accumulates reserves and develops its tissues before reaching a stage in which ripening is possible. Ripening coordinates changes in texture, pigmentation, aroma, acidity and other properties that influence dispersal and consumption. Ethylene is an important coordinator of this process in many species, but developmental competence is necessary. Exposing a fruit that is too immature to ethylene need not produce the same outcome as exposing a mature green fruit.

A climacteric fruit characteristically undergoes a rise in respiration associated with ripening and an increase in ethylene production. Banana, apple and tomato are standard examples. The respiratory rise refers to gas exchange associated with metabolism; it is not the chemical conversion of ethylene into carbon dioxide. Respiratory measurements and ethylene emission measurements therefore require separate analytical channels. A plot containing oxygen uptake or carbon dioxide release and ethylene concentration should not be interpreted as though these quantities were the same variable.

The conventional distinction between system 1 and system 2 ethylene synthesis explains the transition in many climacteric fruits. System 1 describes basal production before ripening, often characterized by negative or limited feedback. System 2 describes the developmental phase in which ethylene promotes additional ethylene synthesis. In tomato, induction of specific ACS and ACO genes contributes to this autocatalytic process. The switch also requires the appropriate developmental regulatory context. Ethylene is not sufficient to transform any arbitrary tissue into a ripening fruit.

Positive feedback explains how a modest initial stimulus can cause a rapid increase in emission. An ethylene-responsive increase in ACS activity produces more ACC. Adequate ACO and oxygen convert more ACC into ethylene. The newly synthesized ethylene can stimulate additional responsive cells, reinforcing the original stimulus. Nevertheless the increase cannot continue indefinitely. Substrate availability, changing tissue physiology, gene regulation and progression through ripening limit the response. A realistic graph has an onset, a period of rapid increase and subsequent changes rather than an endless exponential rise.

The changes produced by ripening are separable. Modification of pectin, hemicellulose and other wall components contributes to fruit softening. Pigment changes may include chlorophyll breakdown and the accumulation of carotenoids or other pigments, depending on species. Altered sugar, organic acid and volatile metabolism changes taste and odor. A treatment may suppress one subset of these outputs more strongly than another. Therefore a fruit that changes color after a treatment has not necessarily undergone normal ripening in all respects. Firmness, color, volatile compounds, respiration and ethylene production supply complementary measurements.

Non-climacteric fruits, such as grape and strawberry, generally lack the classic autocatalytic ethylene and respiratory climacteric that defines the climacteric pattern. They nevertheless possess ethylene perception and biosynthetic machinery, and particular tissues or developmental stages can respond to ethylene. Other hormones, including ABA, play important roles in ripening of some non-climacteric fruits. It is incorrect to state that these fruits contain no ethylene or that ethylene can never influence any property of them. The classification describes characteristic patterns of ripening regulation, not the presence or absence of all ethylene-related biology.

The ethylene perception inhibitor 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) can be used to distinguish perception-dependent changes from changes caused by other aspects of maturation. Because it acts at receptors, inhibition of a response by 1-MCP does not establish direct inhibition of ACS or ACO. The treatment may secondarily alter ethylene production by interfering with autocatalytic feedback. Different fruits and stages may show different durations of inhibition, in part because of receptor turnover and continuing development. These principles support interpretation of postharvest experiments without assuming that one intervention preserves all fruits equally.

7. Senescence and organ separation

Senescence is a regulated transition from an active functional state toward dismantling and nutrient redistribution or loss of organ function. It is not interchangeable with immediate cell death. A senescing leaf may continue to carry out metabolic activities while chloroplast components are dismantled and nitrogen is remobilized. In many flowers, visible wilting or petal deterioration follows a regulated developmental program. Ethylene accelerates senescence in some tissues and species, while in others senescence is comparatively insensitive to ethylene. Developmental age, other hormone signals and environmental conditions influence the outcome.

Abscission, in contrast, is the controlled separation of an organ from the parent plant at a specialized region called an abscission zone. The zone consists of cells that become competent to separate through changes in intercellular adhesion and cell wall properties. Ethylene can promote expression or activity of wall-remodeling enzymes in responsive abscission zones. The resulting loss of adhesion permits a petal, leaf or fruit to detach. Detachment is not simply the mechanical tearing of an entire senescent organ. Adjacent cells may establish a protective boundary that limits damage and water loss.

Auxin arriving from the distal organ often helps maintain reduced sensitivity of the abscission zone to ethylene. A decline in auxin supply, such as after removal of a leaf blade in a classical model, can increase the zone's competence to respond to ethylene. Applying auxin in an appropriate spatial and temporal context may delay separation. This is a model of regulation through sensitivity, not proof that auxin universally suppresses every ethylene response. The effects of auxin depend on where and when it is applied, and auxin can also stimulate ethylene biosynthesis in other developmental settings.

An experimental comparison of intact and debladed petioles helps separate changes in competence from changes in ethylene supply. If both sets receive the same external ethylene concentration but the debladed group separates earlier, the difference cannot be explained simply by the external gas concentration. It is consistent with altered sensitivity or competence, although removal of the blade also changes several physiological processes and wound signals. Local auxin replacement, mock handling and measurement of separation force can strengthen the interpretation. A good experiment measures the response at the abscission zone instead of using general wilting as the only endpoint.

8. Flooding, oxygen limitation and hormonal interactions

Flooding introduces two simultaneous problems for gaseous signaling: oxygen supply to submerged tissue declines and the escape of gases becomes restricted. Water-filled pores in the soil impede gas exchange, so respiration can consume oxygen more rapidly than it is replenished. ACC oxidase requires oxygen, making conversion of ACC to ethylene sensitive to this limitation. At the same time, ethylene that is formed in submerged tissues may accumulate because diffusion through water is much slower than diffusion through air. Whether measured tissue ethylene rises or falls depends on the balance of these processes.

In waterlogged tomato plants, roots can accumulate ACC and export it in xylem sap. The precursor arrives in the shoot, where oxygen is comparatively abundant, and ACO converts it to ethylene. A prominent physiological response is epinasty, a downward curvature caused by unequal growth on opposite sides of the petiole. Bradford and Yang reported experimental evidence for this root-to-shoot precursor movement in 1980. The result demonstrates why measuring only ethylene in the roots would be insufficient to identify the origin of a signal observed in the shoot.

To test the mechanism, compare aerated roots and waterlogged roots while measuring ACC in xylem sap, ethylene emission from shoots and petiole angle. Increased xylem ACC followed by increased shoot emission and curvature is consistent with the precursor transport model. A stronger test reduces ACC production in roots or limits its conversion in shoots and asks which outputs are affected. A rise in ACC with a fall in shoot ethylene after suppression of shoot ACO is consistent with a block at the final step. The precise outcome still depends on transport, residual enzyme activity and compensatory regulation.

Not all flooded plants use the same adaptive strategy. In certain wetland species ethylene contributes to elongation that may help leaves regain contact with air, while in others growth restraint can conserve carbohydrates during temporary submergence. Ethylene can also participate in adventitious root formation and the development of aerenchyma, an arrangement of interconnected gas spaces that facilitates internal gas movement. These outcomes reflect species-specific genetic and hormonal contexts. The common observation of high ethylene under flooding does not by itself tell us whether elongation will increase or decrease.

The interaction with auxin is especially relevant to seedling growth. Ethylene can alter auxin synthesis, transport and spatial distribution, leading to changes in root elongation or apical hook geometry. A root-specific auxin transport defect may therefore modify an ethylene growth phenotype even when other ethylene outputs remain detectable. Jasmonate and ethylene also interact in plant defense; for example, ethylene-regulated transcriptional components can cooperate with jasmonate-dependent regulators in responses to certain necrotrophic pathogens. Other combinations of signals can favor different defense programs. A statement that two hormones always act synergistically or always act antagonistically is too broad without a tissue, dose, developmental stage and response variable.

9. How ethylene became recognized as an endogenous hormone

Early observations of unusual vegetation near sources of illuminating gas provided a starting point, but they did not distinguish the active chemical from the other components of the gas. In 1901, Dimitry Neljubow associated characteristic changes in dark-grown pea seedlings with ethylene. The seedling phenotype offered a biological assay: instead of asking whether an observer thought a plant looked unhealthy, researchers could examine a recognizable growth pattern. The challenge was to identify a specific constituent capable of reproducing the pattern. Comparison with an atmosphere from which the active constituent had been removed and exposure to defined gases helped separate a chemically specific effect from the effects of a complex mixture.

At this stage ethylene could still have been classified simply as an external growth regulator. The demonstration by Richard Gane in 1934 that plant material releases ethylene was therefore a separate conceptual advance. A gas detected outside a fruit might have been produced by a contaminating microorganism, introduced by the environment, or generated by the fruit itself. Reliable identification of ethylene from biological material required chemical evidence rather than relying solely on another plant's response to the collected atmosphere. Once endogenous production and physiological sensitivity were established, investigators could ask whether changes in production at particular developmental stages were functionally important.

The history illustrates an important distinction between sufficiency and necessity. Supplying ethylene to a competent fruit and observing accelerated ripening shows that the treatment can promote that outcome. It does not, by itself, show that normal ripening depends on endogenous ethylene. For the latter question, it is necessary to reduce production or responsiveness while otherwise preserving developmental conditions. A biosynthesis perturbation followed by rescue with ethylene is especially informative because it tests both the necessity of the endogenous supply and the competence of the downstream system. An insensitive receptor mutant provides a complementary test of the requirement for perception.

Modern gas chromatography made it much easier to detect and quantify ethylene in samples of air surrounding tissues. Separation from other volatile compounds is important because ripening fruit emits a mixture rather than pure ethylene. In a chromatography measurement, a calibrated detector produces a response associated with the peak of the separated substance. A retention time consistent with a standard is useful for identification, but coeluting substances remain a possible source of error. Calibration with known gas concentrations, appropriate blanks and consistent sample volumes supports quantitative comparisons. None of these measurements alone establishes biological activity; that requires a physiological or molecular readout.

The discovery of ACC as the immediate precursor created an additional experimental possibility. A researcher could supply a defined water-soluble precursor to a tissue and ask whether ethylene production increased. This moved the discussion from the location of released gas to a specific metabolic pathway. Chemical precursor experiments were eventually integrated with enzyme characterization, isotope-labeling approaches and the isolation of genes encoding biosynthetic enzymes. The observation that a tissue converts an externally supplied precursor is useful, but its normal in vivo flux also depends on precursor availability, competing pathways and regulation.

Genetic analysis then changed the question from how ethylene is formed to how a cell detects it. The small molecular size of ethylene suggested that it might have difficult-to-identify interactions, yet mutant phenotypes demonstrated that a genetically defined signaling system is required for normal responses. Isolation of ethylene-resistant and constitutive-response Arabidopsis mutants provided a route from physiology to molecular components. A strong examination answer integrates these historical stages: exogenous activity, endogenous production, enzymatic synthesis, receptor-dependent signaling and regulated physiological consequences. No single stage substitutes for the others.

10. Pathway flux, precursor pools and metabolic regulation

Consider the amount of free ACC within a defined tissue, measured in nanomoles per gram fresh mass. Its time derivative describes whether the pool is growing or shrinking. For a fixed amount of tissue, the conceptual balance is synthesis by ACS plus import minus consumption by ACO, conjugation and export, with possible return from reversible metabolic forms. This is a bookkeeping relationship rather than an enzyme-specific rate law. It is useful because it permits reasoning when several processes change simultaneously. When a tissue reaches a steady pool size, synthesis has not stopped; inputs and outputs have become approximately equal.

An illustrative example compares two leaf samples in which free ACC remains near 8 nanomoles per gram over an hour. In sample A, formation and total removal are each 2 nanomoles per gram per hour. In sample B, both are 10 nanomoles per gram per hour. The two samples have the same concentration but very different turnover. If all removal occurred through ACO and if the conversion were one mole of ethylene per mole of ACC, sample B would produce five times as much ethylene. If most ACC removal instead occurred by conjugation, the ratio of ethylene emission could be quite different. Pool measurements need to be interpreted in conjunction with fate measurements.

The division of metabolic flux between ACO and competing routes depends on enzyme capacity and on conditions within the tissue. The Michaelis–Menten relationship offers a limited illustration: v = Vmax S/(Km + S), where S denotes substrate concentration, Vmax is the rate approached at high substrate concentration and Km is the concentration giving half of Vmax in the simple model. If the concentration of free ACC is low relative to the apparent Km, increases in ACC can substantially increase ACO-dependent flux. When ACC is already abundant, additional substrate may have little effect because catalytic capacity is close to saturation. The equation should not be applied to a whole plant as though oxygen, ascorbate, enzyme abundance and tissue access were fixed under all conditions.

For a hypothetical preparation with Vmax = 12 nanomoles per minute and Km = 20 micromolar ACC, a concentration of 10 micromolar gives 12 × 10/(20 + 10) = 4 nanomoles per minute. At 40 micromolar the rate becomes 12 × 40/(20 + 40) = 8 nanomoles per minute. Fourfold higher substrate has doubled the rate, not quadrupled it. At 40 micromolar, halving catalytic capacity to 6 nanomoles per minute would yield 4 nanomoles per minute. These illustrative calculations demonstrate why substrate accumulation cannot automatically rescue a severe enzyme deficiency. No empirical kinetic constants are implied.

Regulation of ACS can be understood at several time scales. A signal may increase transcription of a particular ACS gene, increasing the number of its transcripts. Translation subsequently determines the production of new protein. Once the protein exists, phosphorylation or other regulatory events can affect its stability or activity. Some ACS proteins are targeted for degradation through protein quality-control and ubiquitin-dependent mechanisms. The persistence of ACS protein can therefore change before, after or independently of the change in messenger RNA. An experimental report that measures transcript abundance alone cannot demonstrate that the flux into ACC increased proportionally.

One class of Arabidopsis mutants produces more ethylene because regulatory control of ACS turnover is disturbed. The ETO1 protein participates in limiting the abundance of certain ACS isoforms; impairment of this control can increase the amount of functional enzyme. A seedling with increased production may display an exaggerated ethylene response in ordinary air. It is useful to distinguish this type of phenotype from the phenotype of a ctr1 mutant. Both can produce short, thickened hypocotyls in the absence of an experimental ethylene treatment, but the upstream cause differs. An inhibitor of endogenous ethylene synthesis or a measurement of emitted gas can help distinguish an overproducer from a signaling mutant.

Regulation can also involve substrate availability outside the dedicated pathway. SAM serves as a donor in multiple biochemical processes. A broad disruption of SAM synthesis can affect protein metabolism, methylation and other processes in addition to ethylene. A reduction in ethylene output in such a mutant does not necessarily imply a highly specific ethylene biosynthetic defect. If supplying ethylene restores one phenotype but leaves many others abnormal, the result supports a contribution of ethylene without establishing that every effect of the mutation is mediated through ethylene. This is an important distinction between pathway-specific and pleiotropic perturbations.

The methionine salvage cycle is sometimes drawn as a circle with methionine and SAM on one side and MTA returning to methionine on the other. The drawing represents conservation and recycling of the methylthio moiety, not regeneration of the carbon atoms that have escaped as ethylene. Additional cellular metabolites support the transformations. The original ATP-dependent activation of methionine to SAM must also recur. Thus the availability of methionine in a tissue depends on primary synthesis, protein turnover, salvage and consumption. During high ethylene production, the capacity of the salvage pathway can influence the sustainability of precursor supply.

A plant can adjust its ethylene output not only by changing the rate at which it synthesizes precursor but also by changing precursor distribution. Local import of ACC allows one tissue to increase output without inducing ACS in the same cells. Conversely, export or conversion to conjugates may reduce the amount of ACC available locally. An examination may present simultaneous measurements of free ACC and malonyl-ACC. Increased conjugate accumulation while free ACC falls is compatible with increased conjugation, but without time-resolved flux measurements one cannot exclude lower synthesis or higher oxidation. The appropriate interpretation is a supported possibility rather than a uniquely established mechanism.

The distinction between ACC as precursor and ACC as a potential signal also merits care. Some studies have found physiological effects of ACC that cannot be fully explained by its conversion to ethylene. These observations caution against assuming that every ACC treatment is equivalent to a gas treatment. At NET-level depth, the dominant biosynthetic role of ACC remains the central principle. When a response persists under well-controlled conditions in which ethylene production and perception are disrupted, however, a distinct ACC-dependent mechanism becomes a reasonable possibility. Incomplete blockade, alternative signaling and nonspecific treatment effects must be considered before drawing that conclusion.

11. Experimental evidence for the receptor-to-nucleus mechanism

A chain of genetic interactions does not automatically describe the biochemical relationship between its components. For example, the phenotype of a ctr1 mutant placed CTR1 upstream of positive regulators, but many molecular possibilities remained open. CTR1 could have regulated transcription of a gene encoding an intermediate, phosphorylated another enzyme, altered protein degradation or modified a membrane-associated component. The identity of a direct substrate had to be addressed experimentally. Researchers used knowledge of CTR1's kinase activity and the central position of EIN2 to test whether the two could be connected through phosphorylation.

The 2012 work of Ju and colleagues provided evidence that CTR1 directly phosphorylates the cytosolic portion of EIN2. This relationship was investigated with biochemical phosphorylation assays as well as tests of the functional consequences of altering relevant residues. A kinase assay can ask whether a purified or defined kinase transfers phosphate to a candidate substrate under controlled conditions. Appropriate comparison with an inactive kinase or a substrate containing altered phosphorylation sites helps identify the dependence of the reaction. The existence of phosphorylation in vitro is not sufficient by itself to prove that the event regulates a living cell, so additional physiological and genetic evidence was needed.

A related line of work by Qiao and colleagues connected ethylene-dependent changes in EIN2 phosphorylation with processing and localization of its carboxyl-terminal portion. The signal starts near a membrane, but the transcriptional response is nuclear. Detecting a change in the cellular distribution of a tagged protein fragment can help connect these compartments. Such experiments must establish that the tag has not destroyed the activity of the protein. A tagged version that restores the characteristic phenotype of a deficient plant is generally a more reliable reporter than a tagged protein whose function has not been evaluated. Localization should also be compared in untreated and treated plants.

The logic of phosphorylation-site mutants is straightforward if the chemistry of the substitutions is interpreted cautiously. Serine or threonine can accept phosphate on a hydroxyl group. Replacement by alanine removes the usual phosphorylatable side chain at that site. A nonphosphorylatable version of a regulatory protein may mimic aspects of its dephosphorylated state. If the altered EIN2 becomes active even when ethylene is absent, that observation is consistent with inhibitory phosphorylation in the normal protein. However, a substitution may also change protein structure, stability or interaction surfaces. The strongest inference comes from convergence of several types of evidence, not from treating every amino acid replacement as a perfect molecular switch.

The downstream transcriptional regulators introduce another level of experimental reasoning. A large increase in EIN3 protein following hormone exposure might be caused by increased synthesis, reduced degradation or a combination. If EIN3 messenger RNA remains unchanged but protein abundance rises, altered transcription of EIN3 is not required to explain the observation. A decrease in degradation rate can increase protein abundance substantially even with constant synthesis. EBF1 and EBF2 help target EIN3-family proteins for proteasomal degradation. Modulation of the abundance and translation of these F-box proteins is therefore a route for controlling the transcriptional response indirectly.

Consider an illustrative protein turnover model, dN/dt = s − kN, where N is EIN3 abundance in arbitrary units, s is its constant synthesis rate and k is a first-order degradation constant. At steady state, N = s/k. If s is 10 units per hour and k is 2 per hour, steady-state abundance is 5 units. A reduction in k to 0.5 per hour gives a steady-state abundance of 20 units. A fourfold increase in protein therefore need not require any increase in synthesis. The half-life of a protein under a first-order decay assumption is ln(2)/k; in this example it rises from approximately 0.35 hours to 1.39 hours. This is a teaching model for stabilization, not a measurement of EIN3 turnover in Arabidopsis.

One could investigate the proposed change in turnover by comparing protein disappearance after a controlled pulse of expression. Alternatively, protein accumulation can be measured over time while examining transcript abundance. Inhibiting the proteasome can provide supporting evidence for a degradation mechanism, but proteasome inhibition also affects many cellular proteins. Changes induced by that treatment cannot all be assigned specifically to EIN3 or to EBF activity. A comparison of wild-type and appropriate EBF-defective backgrounds helps to isolate the regulatory step.

Once EIN3 or EIL1 has accumulated, transcriptional changes provide a link between the initial signal and physiological behavior. A reporter driven by an ethylene-responsive promoter can indicate that transcriptional regulation has occurred. For a claim of direct binding by a particular transcription factor, additional evidence about occupancy of promoter regulatory sequences is required. Increased expression of a target gene in the presence of EIN3 may arise indirectly through another regulator. The distinction between direct target and downstream responsive gene is particularly important in branched signaling pathways.

The canonical linear pathway is a powerful organizing model but it does not imply that all outputs occur through exactly one transcription factor at exactly the same rate. Several receptors and transcriptional regulators have overlapping and nonoverlapping functions. Some growth responses have rapid components that are not adequately described by a single slow transcriptional cascade. The examination-level model should be used to predict major phenotypes while recognizing that measurements at different time points, tissues or developmental stages may reveal additional layers. The existence of these layers does not reverse the central negative-regulatory logic of the receptors and CTR1.

12. Sensitivity, gas measurement and time-course interpretation

Sensitivity is often confused with hormone concentration. A tissue that exhibits a large response at a low ethylene concentration may be highly sensitive, whereas another tissue may require much more ethylene to show a similar response. A third tissue may exhibit only a small response even at high concentration because its downstream physiological capacity is limited. These patterns describe different dose-response properties. The amount of hormone emitted by the tissue cannot be deduced simply from the position of its dose-response curve when supplied with an external concentration.

A common simplified receptor-binding model is θ = C/(Kd + C). The variable θ is the fraction of independent equivalent binding sites occupied, C is free ligand concentration and Kd is the dissociation constant under the model assumptions. For ethylene, a gas-phase concentration is only an indirect proxy for the concentration accessible at the receptor; partitioning into membranes and aqueous compartments complicates that relationship. If the appropriate ligand concentration equals Kd, the predicted occupancy is one-half. If it equals nine times Kd, occupancy is nine-tenths. The formula is illustrative, not a full receptor-family model. In particular, half occupancy should not be equated automatically with half maximal growth inhibition.

A physiological dose-response curve can instead be described by its EC50, the concentration producing a response halfway between a defined baseline and maximal response. The EC50 depends on the chosen endpoint and measurement interval. For a root elongation assay, one may use percentage inhibition relative to an untreated seedling. For an ethylene-responsive promoter, one may use reporter intensity. It is possible for a plant to show a reduced EC50 in one assay while showing a different maximal response in another. A signaling cascade can amplify receptor occupancy and produce a nonlinear relationship between ligand binding and the final output.

Suppose untreated wild-type roots have an average length of 20 millimeters and roots exposed to a specified ethylene concentration grow to 12 millimeters during the same interval. Percent inhibition relative to control is (20 − 12)/20 × 100 = 40%. If untreated roots of a mutant are only 10 millimeters long and exposed mutant roots are 8 millimeters, their percentage inhibition is 20%. Comparing only final treated lengths would misleadingly suggest the mutant had the stronger response because its roots are shorter. The appropriate denominator for treatment-induced change is the untreated control of the same genotype. Initial length must also be considered if elongation rather than final organ length is the desired variable.

In a time-course experiment, the onset of changes is as informative as their magnitude. An increase in ACC before an increase in ethylene is consistent with the sequential biosynthetic pathway. Yet the timing of a measured increase also depends on the detection threshold and sampling interval. An ethylene increase occurring before a detectable rise in ACC may reflect rapid conversion of a small precursor pool rather than an alternative pathway. Direct enzyme activity measurements, isotope labeling and targeted perturbations make causal inference more robust than temporal order alone.

Gas sampling presents a separate difficulty. A sealed incubation provides an integrated measurement over time. Suppose two fruits are each placed in 1 litre of headspace for two hours. Fruit A yields 2 microlitres per litre and has a mass of 0.1 kilogram; fruit B yields 3 microlitres per litre and has a mass of 0.3 kilogram. If the initial concentration is zero, the estimated net emission rates normalized by mass are 2/(2 × 0.1) = 10 and 3/(2 × 0.3) = 5 microlitres per kilogram per hour. Although fruit B releases more gas in total, fruit A has a higher mass-specific rate. This is a hypothetical example that assumes equal headspace, no gas leakage and approximately constant emission.

Normalization by fresh mass is useful, but it is not always biologically sufficient. Water content, tissue composition and surface-to-volume ratio may differ among samples. When comparing developmental stages, the fraction of living metabolically active tissue may change. Depending on the research question, normalization by dry mass, tissue area, protein content or number of organs may complement fresh-mass measurements. The chosen denominator should be stated clearly because a rate normalized by organ mass and a rate normalized by a particular cell population answer different questions.

Experimental chambers must also avoid confounding ethylene exposure with oxygen depletion or carbon dioxide accumulation. A fruit respires while releasing ethylene, so prolonged enclosure can change several gases simultaneously. A matched chamber that differs only in controlled ethylene concentration is preferable for a direct hormone response test. When estimating production, collecting several early time points and verifying approximate linearity can reveal whether the enclosure is altering the rate being measured. A chamber blank can identify background contamination. Replication across independent fruits is essential because repeated measurements of one fruit do not capture the variation among biological samples.
