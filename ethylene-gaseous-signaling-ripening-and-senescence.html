1. A hormone that moves through air

A ripening fruit can change the developmental behavior of another fruit that is not touching it. This observation is unusual because the familiar chemical signals of plants are often imagined as dissolved substances moving through sap or passing between neighboring cells. Ethylene, a molecule consisting of two carbon atoms and four hydrogen atoms, provides an alternative mode of communication. The two carbon atoms are linked by a double bond. At ordinary physiological temperatures the substance is a gas, and an ethylene molecule can leave its cell of origin, cross an air-filled space and enter another living tissue. The fact that a chemical travels through the air does not make its effects nonspecific. A receptor distinguishes ethylene from many other small molecules, and regulated changes in the receptor system communicate its presence to the transcriptional machinery of the receiving cell.

The physiological behavior of a signal depends on the rate at which it is produced, the ease with which it leaves the region of production and the responsiveness of the tissue that receives it. Consider a piece of fruit maintained in a ventilated room and another identical fruit maintained in a closed vessel. If their initial production rates are equal, the concentration of ethylene surrounding the enclosed fruit may rise more rapidly. The enclosed fruit has not necessarily acquired a more active biosynthetic enzyme. Instead, its gaseous product is being removed more slowly. If the increased ethylene concentration induces additional ethylene synthesis, the initially physical difference in ventilation may become a difference in metabolic activity. This example separates accumulation from production and introduces the importance of feedback.

A hormone must be distinguished from a general toxic influence. At sufficiently high concentrations or under inappropriate conditions many chemical compounds alter plant growth, but a reproducible response alone does not establish that the compound is a normal endogenous regulator. Evidence for ethylene as a plant hormone includes its synthesis by plant tissues, its physiological effects at low concentrations, the existence of receptors and signal transduction proteins, and the consequences of altering either biosynthesis or sensitivity genetically. Together these forms of evidence explain why exposure to a small molecule in the atmosphere can result in coordinated changes in growth and development rather than simply causing tissue injury.

The same ethylene molecule is involved in several outcomes that appear very different. It can restrict the elongation of a dark-grown seedling, stimulate expansion in certain submerged plants, induce ripening in a mature tomato, and accelerate shedding of particular floral organs. This diversity is not contradictory. The signal changes the activity of downstream regulatory systems; the available transcription factors, target genes, developmental competence and environmental conditions differ among tissues. The response of a dark-grown hypocotyl should therefore not be treated as a universal definition of ethylene action. A major objective of this chapter is to connect a common molecular pathway with distinct physiological outputs without assuming that every plant behaves identically.

2. How ethylene became recognized as an endogenous hormone

Early observations of unusual vegetation near sources of illuminating gas provided a starting point, but they did not distinguish the active chemical from the other components of the gas. In 1901, Dimitry Neljubow associated characteristic changes in dark-grown pea seedlings with ethylene. The seedling phenotype offered a biological assay: instead of asking whether an observer thought a plant looked unhealthy, researchers could examine a recognizable growth pattern. The challenge was to identify a specific constituent capable of reproducing the pattern. Comparison with an atmosphere from which the active constituent had been removed and exposure to defined gases helped separate a chemically specific effect from the effects of a complex mixture.

At this stage ethylene could still have been classified simply as an external growth regulator. The demonstration by Richard Gane in 1934 that plant material releases ethylene was therefore a separate conceptual advance. A gas detected outside a fruit might have been produced by a contaminating microorganism, introduced by the environment, or generated by the fruit itself. Reliable identification of ethylene from biological material required chemical evidence rather than relying solely on another plant's response to the collected atmosphere. Once endogenous production and physiological sensitivity were established, investigators could ask whether changes in production at particular developmental stages were functionally important.

The history illustrates an important distinction between sufficiency and necessity. Supplying ethylene to a competent fruit and observing accelerated ripening shows that the treatment can promote that outcome. It does not, by itself, show that normal ripening depends on endogenous ethylene. For the latter question, it is necessary to reduce production or responsiveness while otherwise preserving developmental conditions. A biosynthesis perturbation followed by rescue with ethylene is especially informative because it tests both the necessity of the endogenous supply and the competence of the downstream system. An insensitive receptor mutant provides a complementary test of the requirement for perception.

Modern gas chromatography made it much easier to detect and quantify ethylene in samples of air surrounding tissues. Separation from other volatile compounds is important because ripening fruit emits a mixture rather than pure ethylene. In a chromatography measurement, a calibrated detector produces a response associated with the peak of the separated substance. A retention time consistent with a standard is useful for identification, but coeluting substances remain a possible source of error. Calibration with known gas concentrations, appropriate blanks and consistent sample volumes supports quantitative comparisons. None of these measurements alone establishes biological activity; that requires a physiological or molecular readout.

The discovery of ACC as the immediate precursor created an additional experimental possibility. A researcher could supply a defined water-soluble precursor to a tissue and ask whether ethylene production increased. This moved the discussion from the location of released gas to a specific metabolic pathway. Chemical precursor experiments were eventually integrated with enzyme characterization, isotope-labeling approaches and the isolation of genes encoding biosynthetic enzymes. The observation that a tissue converts an externally supplied precursor is useful, but its normal in vivo flux also depends on precursor availability, competing pathways and regulation.

Genetic analysis then changed the question from how ethylene is formed to how a cell detects it. The small molecular size of ethylene suggested that it might have difficult-to-identify interactions, yet mutant phenotypes demonstrated that a genetically defined signaling system is required for normal responses. Isolation of ethylene-resistant and constitutive-response Arabidopsis mutants provided a route from physiology to molecular components. A strong examination answer integrates these historical stages: exogenous activity, endogenous production, enzymatic synthesis, receptor-dependent signaling and regulated physiological consequences. No single stage substitutes for the others.

3. Building ethylene from methionine

Methionine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that participates in protein synthesis and several metabolic processes. Its role in ethylene biosynthesis begins when methionine adenosyltransferase uses ATP to convert methionine to S-adenosyl-L-methionine, commonly abbreviated SAM. The adenosyl group attached to sulfur changes the chemical reactivity of the molecule. SAM is a metabolic junction: methyltransferases use it as a methyl donor, other pathways consume it in polyamine metabolism, and ethylene biosynthesis draws material from the same cellular pool. It is therefore misleading to regard every molecule of SAM as committed to making ethylene.

Commitment to the conventional ethylene route occurs through ACC synthase, abbreviated ACS. This pyridoxal phosphate-dependent enzyme converts SAM to 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) and 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA). ACC contains a three-membered carbon ring and a carboxyl group. It is not itself a gaseous molecule and behaves differently from ethylene in the aqueous environment of a cell. The production of ACC establishes a useful intermediate pool from which ethylene can subsequently be formed. The amount of ACC accumulated at a given instant reflects the difference between its generation and all the processes that consume, transport or conjugate it.

ACC oxidase (ACO) catalyzes the final conversion. The reaction requires molecular oxygen and a ferrous iron-containing catalytic system, with ascorbate participating as a reductant and bicarbonate supporting activity. Ethylene, carbon dioxide and cyanide are products derived during ACC oxidation. The cyanide byproduct is metabolically important because it can interfere with respiratory enzymes if allowed to accumulate. Plant cells possess cyanide-detoxifying metabolism, including β-cyanoalanine synthase, which combines cyanide with cysteine. The ability to manage a byproduct belongs to the physiological cost of producing a hormone and cannot be inferred merely from the small size of the final product.

Two enzymes control different positions in this route. If ACS activity is impaired, little newly synthesized ACC is available to ACO. Supplying ACC externally can potentially bypass the defect, provided it reaches the appropriate cells and sufficient ACO activity and oxygen are available. In contrast, a severe ACO defect cannot be bypassed by providing more ACC. External ethylene bypasses both biosynthetic steps, but it cannot necessarily rescue a defect in the hormone's receptor or its downstream signaling components. This sequence of chemical rescue treatments offers a powerful way to distinguish biosynthesis and responsiveness without directly observing every enzymatic reaction.

ACS is often a major regulatory point because its activity controls entry into the dedicated pathway. However, it is not always the sole rate-limiting step. A cell with abundant ACC but inadequate oxygen cannot sustain a high ACO reaction rate. A developing fruit may alter the expression of both enzymes. Several ACS isoforms respond differently to developmental and environmental signals, and protein turnover can change enzyme abundance without a corresponding change in messenger RNA. When interpreting an experiment, distinguish transcript abundance, enzyme protein abundance, catalytic activity and pathway flux. The four measurements describe related but nonidentical properties.

An important feature of the pathway is that the sulfur-containing portion of methionine is not discarded as ethylene. MTA enters the methionine salvage pathway, commonly called the Yang cycle, in which the methylthio group is recycled into methionine. This preserves sulfur and supports repeated ethylene production without consuming an entirely new sulfur atom for every ethylene molecule formed. The cycle still requires additional metabolic resources; recycling does not make ethylene biosynthesis energetically free. The regenerated methionine must again be activated to SAM using ATP. It follows that a defect in methionine salvage may be particularly consequential during sustained high production, when demand on the common methionine and SAM pools is high.

4. Pathway flux, precursor pools and metabolic regulation

Consider the amount of free ACC within a defined tissue, measured in nanomoles per gram fresh mass. Its time derivative describes whether the pool is growing or shrinking. For a fixed amount of tissue, the conceptual balance is synthesis by ACS plus import minus consumption by ACO, conjugation and export, with possible return from reversible metabolic forms. This is a bookkeeping relationship rather than an enzyme-specific rate law. It is useful because it permits reasoning when several processes change simultaneously. When a tissue reaches a steady pool size, synthesis has not stopped; inputs and outputs have become approximately equal.

An illustrative example compares two leaf samples in which free ACC remains near 8 nanomoles per gram over an hour. In sample A, formation and total removal are each 2 nanomoles per gram per hour. In sample B, both are 10 nanomoles per gram per hour. The two samples have the same concentration but very different turnover. If all removal occurred through ACO and if the conversion were one mole of ethylene per mole of ACC, sample B would produce five times as much ethylene. If most ACC removal instead occurred by conjugation, the ratio of ethylene emission could be quite different. Pool measurements need to be interpreted in conjunction with fate measurements.

The division of metabolic flux between ACO and competing routes depends on enzyme capacity and on conditions within the tissue. The Michaelis–Menten relationship offers a limited illustration: v = Vmax S/(Km + S), where S denotes substrate concentration, Vmax is the rate approached at high substrate concentration and Km is the concentration giving half of Vmax in the simple model. If the concentration of free ACC is low relative to the apparent Km, increases in ACC can substantially increase ACO-dependent flux. When ACC is already abundant, additional substrate may have little effect because catalytic capacity is close to saturation. The equation should not be applied to a whole plant as though oxygen, ascorbate, enzyme abundance and tissue access were fixed under all conditions.

For a hypothetical preparation with Vmax = 12 nanomoles per minute and Km = 20 micromolar ACC, a concentration of 10 micromolar gives 12 × 10/(20 + 10) = 4 nanomoles per minute. At 40 micromolar the rate becomes 12 × 40/(20 + 40) = 8 nanomoles per minute. Fourfold higher substrate has doubled the rate, not quadrupled it. At 40 micromolar, halving catalytic capacity to 6 nanomoles per minute would yield 4 nanomoles per minute. These illustrative calculations demonstrate why substrate accumulation cannot automatically rescue a severe enzyme deficiency. No empirical kinetic constants are implied.

Regulation of ACS can be understood at several time scales. A signal may increase transcription of a particular ACS gene, increasing the number of its transcripts. Translation subsequently determines the production of new protein. Once the protein exists, phosphorylation or other regulatory events can affect its stability or activity. Some ACS proteins are targeted for degradation through protein quality-control and ubiquitin-dependent mechanisms. The persistence of ACS protein can therefore change before, after or independently of the change in messenger RNA. An experimental report that measures transcript abundance alone cannot demonstrate that the flux into ACC increased proportionally.

One class of Arabidopsis mutants produces more ethylene because regulatory control of ACS turnover is disturbed. The ETO1 protein participates in limiting the abundance of certain ACS isoforms; impairment of this control can increase the amount of functional enzyme. A seedling with increased production may display an exaggerated ethylene response in ordinary air. It is useful to distinguish this type of phenotype from the phenotype of a ctr1 mutant. Both can produce short, thickened hypocotyls in the absence of an experimental ethylene treatment, but the upstream cause differs. An inhibitor of endogenous ethylene synthesis or a measurement of emitted gas can help distinguish an overproducer from a signaling mutant.

Regulation can also involve substrate availability outside the dedicated pathway. SAM serves as a donor in multiple biochemical processes. A broad disruption of SAM synthesis can affect protein metabolism, methylation and other processes in addition to ethylene. A reduction in ethylene output in such a mutant does not necessarily imply a highly specific ethylene biosynthetic defect. If supplying ethylene restores one phenotype but leaves many others abnormal, the result supports a contribution of ethylene without establishing that every effect of the mutation is mediated through ethylene. This is an important distinction between pathway-specific and pleiotropic perturbations.

The methionine salvage cycle is sometimes drawn as a circle with methionine and SAM on one side and MTA returning to methionine on the other. The drawing represents conservation and recycling of the methylthio moiety, not regeneration of the carbon atoms that have escaped as ethylene. Additional cellular metabolites support the transformations. The original ATP-dependent activation of methionine to SAM must also recur. Thus the availability of methionine in a tissue depends on primary synthesis, protein turnover, salvage and consumption. During high ethylene production, the capacity of the salvage pathway can influence the sustainability of precursor supply.

A plant can adjust its ethylene output not only by changing the rate at which it synthesizes precursor but also by changing precursor distribution. Local import of ACC allows one tissue to increase output without inducing ACS in the same cells. Conversely, export or conversion to conjugates may reduce the amount of ACC available locally. An examination may present simultaneous measurements of free ACC and malonyl-ACC. Increased conjugate accumulation while free ACC falls is compatible with increased conjugation, but without time-resolved flux measurements one cannot exclude lower synthesis or higher oxidation. The appropriate interpretation is a supported possibility rather than a uniquely established mechanism.

The distinction between ACC as precursor and ACC as a potential signal also merits care. Some studies have found physiological effects of ACC that cannot be fully explained by its conversion to ethylene. These observations caution against assuming that every ACC treatment is equivalent to a gas treatment. At NET-level depth, the dominant biosynthetic role of ACC remains the central principle. When a response persists under well-controlled conditions in which ethylene production and perception are disrupted, however, a distinct ACC-dependent mechanism becomes a reasonable possibility. Incomplete blockade, alternative signaling and nonspecific treatment effects must be considered before drawing that conclusion.

5. Precursor storage, transport and turnover

A distinction between ethylene itself and its precursor clarifies the official syllabus terms storage, breakdown and transport. Ethylene is not generally stored as a stable inactive glycoside waiting to be hydrolyzed. Its concentration is influenced by continuous synthesis, escape through intercellular air spaces, exchange with the surrounding atmosphere and, under some conditions, physical retention. By comparison, ACC is chemically suitable for transport in aqueous compartments and can be metabolically diverted into conjugated forms. Malonyl-ACC is an important example; other conjugates occur in plants. These compounds should not all be assumed to release ACC rapidly under every physiological condition. Conjugation may decrease the immediate availability of free precursor, and the subsequent fate of the conjugate depends on species and tissue.

If the amount of free ACC increases after a stress treatment, several mechanisms remain possible. ACS might be producing ACC more quickly, ACO might be consuming it more slowly, import from other tissue might have increased, or conjugation might have decreased. A single concentration measurement does not distinguish these alternatives. Similarly, measurement of ethylene released by a whole leaf does not reveal which cell type supplied the precursor. A rigorous interpretation requires a time series, measurements of precursor pools and enzyme activity, or a carefully chosen perturbation of one step.

Ethylene movement over a short distance can occur by diffusion. The net flux is directed down a concentration gradient, although individual molecules move in random directions. The gas moves more readily through interconnected air spaces than through a water-filled route of comparable length. A waxy surface, submerged tissue or restricted ventilation can change resistance to escape. ACC, by contrast, may move between cells through regulated transport processes and can travel long distances in vascular sap. Thus, the organ in which ethylene is ultimately detected may not be the organ in which the precursor was synthesized.

The signaling lifetime of ethylene is not simply the chemical lifetime of an ethylene molecule. Once the gas concentration falls, receptors can again support repression of the response, but induced messenger RNAs, proteins and physiological changes may persist. A fruit cell wall that has already been extensively modified will not instantly recover its original mechanical properties when ethylene is removed. This difference between the concentration of a signal and the duration of its consequences is useful when interpreting pulse treatments, withdrawal experiments and the response of produce to altered storage atmospheres.

A simple mass balance provides a quantitative model for an enclosed chamber. Let P represent net emission into the headspace in microlitres per hour, V the chamber's gas volume in litres, and C the concentration in microlitres per litre. In a perfectly mixed sealed chamber with constant P and negligible uptake or leakage, dC/dt = P/V. If a sample emits 0.40 microlitres per hour into a 2.0 litre headspace for three hours from a zero starting concentration, the accumulated amount is 1.2 microlitres and C is 0.60 microlitres per litre, approximately 0.60 ppm by volume. This is an illustrative calculation, not a measured biological rate. The headspace must exclude volume occupied by the fruit. The assumption of constant emission may fail if the fruit starts autocatalytic synthesis or if oxygen becomes limiting.

For a ventilated chamber in which fresh air contains negligible ethylene, a first approximation is dC/dt = P/V − QC/V, where Q is the air exchange flow in litres per hour. At a steady concentration the two fluxes balance, giving C = P/Q. Doubling Q halves C if emission is unchanged. This is not a law of ripening: production can respond to concentration and developmental stage. It is a physical model for separating ventilation and biosynthesis. In experimental comparisons, identical chamber volumes, controlled gas flow and measurements within a validated range are essential.

6. The unusual logic of ethylene perception

Ethylene perception in Arabidopsis involves a family of receptors that includes ETR1, ETR2, ERS1, ERS2 and EIN4. The receptors are primarily associated with the endoplasmic reticulum membrane. Their membrane-spanning regions participate in ethylene binding, supported by a copper cofactor. Their cytosolic portions interact with downstream regulatory proteins. Although some ethylene receptor domains resemble bacterial histidine kinases, the essential examination-level logic of the pathway should not be replaced by a conventional positive two-component phosphorelay. The principal Arabidopsis receptor system suppresses ethylene responses when the ligand is absent.

The protein kinase CTR1, named for the constitutive triple response mutant in which its normal activity is lost, associates with the receptors. In the absence of ethylene, receptor-dependent CTR1 activity maintains a repressive state. One important substrate of CTR1 is EIN2, a membrane-associated positive regulator. CTR1 phosphorylates the cytosolic region of EIN2 and prevents activation of the downstream response. Thus an active unoccupied receptor is not evidence of active ethylene-responsive transcription. It is evidence of a maintained brake on transcriptional activation.

Ethylene binding changes the receptor system in a manner that decreases CTR1-mediated repression. The carboxyl-terminal portion of EIN2 can then participate in signal transfer, including nuclear signaling and regulation of translation. Downstream, the transcription factors EIN3 and EIL1 accumulate and regulate responsive genes, including genes encoding additional transcription factors of the ethylene response factor family. The pathway consequently converts a local change in gas concentration into changes in gene expression. Some effects occur more quickly than others because different target systems require different amounts of transcription, translation and cellular remodeling.

EIN3 abundance is regulated in part by the F-box proteins EBF1 and EBF2. These are specificity components of ubiquitin ligase complexes that promote turnover of EIN3-family regulators. When ethylene signaling is active, EIN2-dependent mechanisms reduce the effective turnover pressure on EIN3, allowing its accumulation. Feedback involving EBF proteins and receptor abundance helps shape the duration and sensitivity of responses. The pathway therefore should not be understood as a permanently latched switch. It is a dynamic balance between the synthesis, stabilization and degradation of regulatory molecules.

It is helpful to use two sign changes to remember the canonical pathway. An unliganded receptor supports CTR1 activity, and CTR1 inhibits a positive regulator, EIN2. EIN2 in turn enables increased EIN3/EIL1-dependent transcription. Ethylene inhibits the upstream inhibitory system. Removing inhibition from a positive regulator increases the downstream response. In an examination, following the signs of the arrows is safer than using the ambiguous expression receptor activation, which may refer either to ligand binding or to the activity of the receptor as a negative regulator.

There are five receptors rather than only one. Consequently the phenotype caused by loss of a single receptor is not necessarily equivalent to the phenotype of losing most receptor activity. Other family members can continue to repress signaling. Moreover, a particular dominant receptor allele can preserve a repressive state despite exposure to ethylene. This is a fundamentally different genetic event from deletion of a receptor gene. The distinction between gain-of-function and loss-of-function is essential when interpreting ETR1-associated ethylene insensitivity.

7. Experimental evidence for the receptor-to-nucleus mechanism

A chain of genetic interactions does not automatically describe the biochemical relationship between its components. For example, the phenotype of a ctr1 mutant placed CTR1 upstream of positive regulators, but many molecular possibilities remained open. CTR1 could have regulated transcription of a gene encoding an intermediate, phosphorylated another enzyme, altered protein degradation or modified a membrane-associated component. The identity of a direct substrate had to be addressed experimentally. Researchers used knowledge of CTR1's kinase activity and the central position of EIN2 to test whether the two could be connected through phosphorylation.

The 2012 work of Ju and colleagues provided evidence that CTR1 directly phosphorylates the cytosolic portion of EIN2. This relationship was investigated with biochemical phosphorylation assays as well as tests of the functional consequences of altering relevant residues. A kinase assay can ask whether a purified or defined kinase transfers phosphate to a candidate substrate under controlled conditions. Appropriate comparison with an inactive kinase or a substrate containing altered phosphorylation sites helps identify the dependence of the reaction. The existence of phosphorylation in vitro is not sufficient by itself to prove that the event regulates a living cell, so additional physiological and genetic evidence was needed.

A related line of work by Qiao and colleagues connected ethylene-dependent changes in EIN2 phosphorylation with processing and localization of its carboxyl-terminal portion. The signal starts near a membrane, but the transcriptional response is nuclear. Detecting a change in the cellular distribution of a tagged protein fragment can help connect these compartments. Such experiments must establish that the tag has not destroyed the activity of the protein. A tagged version that restores the characteristic phenotype of a deficient plant is generally a more reliable reporter than a tagged protein whose function has not been evaluated. Localization should also be compared in untreated and treated plants.

The logic of phosphorylation-site mutants is straightforward if the chemistry of the substitutions is interpreted cautiously. Serine or threonine can accept phosphate on a hydroxyl group. Replacement by alanine removes the usual phosphorylatable side chain at that site. A nonphosphorylatable version of a regulatory protein may mimic aspects of its dephosphorylated state. If the altered EIN2 becomes active even when ethylene is absent, that observation is consistent with inhibitory phosphorylation in the normal protein. However, a substitution may also change protein structure, stability or interaction surfaces. The strongest inference comes from convergence of several types of evidence, not from treating every amino acid replacement as a perfect molecular switch.

The downstream transcriptional regulators introduce another level of experimental reasoning. A large increase in EIN3 protein following hormone exposure might be caused by increased synthesis, reduced degradation or a combination. If EIN3 messenger RNA remains unchanged but protein abundance rises, altered transcription of EIN3 is not required to explain the observation. A decrease in degradation rate can increase protein abundance substantially even with constant synthesis. EBF1 and EBF2 help target EIN3-family proteins for proteasomal degradation. Modulation of the abundance and translation of these F-box proteins is therefore a route for controlling the transcriptional response indirectly.

Consider an illustrative protein turnover model, dN/dt = s − kN, where N is EIN3 abundance in arbitrary units, s is its constant synthesis rate and k is a first-order degradation constant. At steady state, N = s/k. If s is 10 units per hour and k is 2 per hour, steady-state abundance is 5 units. A reduction in k to 0.5 per hour gives a steady-state abundance of 20 units. A fourfold increase in protein therefore need not require any increase in synthesis. The half-life of a protein under a first-order decay assumption is ln(2)/k; in this example it rises from approximately 0.35 hours to 1.39 hours. This is a teaching model for stabilization, not a measurement of EIN3 turnover in Arabidopsis.

One could investigate the proposed change in turnover by comparing protein disappearance after a controlled pulse of expression. Alternatively, protein accumulation can be measured over time while examining transcript abundance. Inhibiting the proteasome can provide supporting evidence for a degradation mechanism, but proteasome inhibition also affects many cellular proteins. Changes induced by that treatment cannot all be assigned specifically to EIN3 or to EBF activity. A comparison of wild-type and appropriate EBF-defective backgrounds helps to isolate the regulatory step.

Once EIN3 or EIL1 has accumulated, transcriptional changes provide a link between the initial signal and physiological behavior. A reporter driven by an ethylene-responsive promoter can indicate that transcriptional regulation has occurred. For a claim of direct binding by a particular transcription factor, additional evidence about occupancy of promoter regulatory sequences is required. Increased expression of a target gene in the presence of EIN3 may arise indirectly through another regulator. The distinction between direct target and downstream responsive gene is particularly important in branched signaling pathways.

The canonical linear pathway is a powerful organizing model but it does not imply that all outputs occur through exactly one transcription factor at exactly the same rate. Several receptors and transcriptional regulators have overlapping and nonoverlapping functions. Some growth responses have rapid components that are not adequately described by a single slow transcriptional cascade. The examination-level model should be used to predict major phenotypes while recognizing that measurements at different time points, tissues or developmental stages may reveal additional layers. The existence of these layers does not reverse the central negative-regulatory logic of the receptors and CTR1.

8. Sensitivity, gas measurement and time-course interpretation

Sensitivity is often confused with hormone concentration. A tissue that exhibits a large response at a low ethylene concentration may be highly sensitive, whereas another tissue may require much more ethylene to show a similar response. A third tissue may exhibit only a small response even at high concentration because its downstream physiological capacity is limited. These patterns describe different dose-response properties. The amount of hormone emitted by the tissue cannot be deduced simply from the position of its dose-response curve when supplied with an external concentration.

A common simplified receptor-binding model is θ = C/(Kd + C). The variable θ is the fraction of independent equivalent binding sites occupied, C is free ligand concentration and Kd is the dissociation constant under the model assumptions. For ethylene, a gas-phase concentration is only an indirect proxy for the concentration accessible at the receptor; partitioning into membranes and aqueous compartments complicates that relationship. If the appropriate ligand concentration equals Kd, the predicted occupancy is one-half. If it equals nine times Kd, occupancy is nine-tenths. The formula is illustrative, not a full receptor-family model. In particular, half occupancy should not be equated automatically with half maximal growth inhibition.

A physiological dose-response curve can instead be described by its EC50, the concentration producing a response halfway between a defined baseline and maximal response. The EC50 depends on the chosen endpoint and measurement interval. For a root elongation assay, one may use percentage inhibition relative to an untreated seedling. For an ethylene-responsive promoter, one may use reporter intensity. It is possible for a plant to show a reduced EC50 in one assay while showing a different maximal response in another. A signaling cascade can amplify receptor occupancy and produce a nonlinear relationship between ligand binding and the final output.

Suppose untreated wild-type roots have an average length of 20 millimeters and roots exposed to a specified ethylene concentration grow to 12 millimeters during the same interval. Percent inhibition relative to control is (20 − 12)/20 × 100 = 40%. If untreated roots of a mutant are only 10 millimeters long and exposed mutant roots are 8 millimeters, their percentage inhibition is 20%. Comparing only final treated lengths would misleadingly suggest the mutant had the stronger response because its roots are shorter. The appropriate denominator for treatment-induced change is the untreated control of the same genotype. Initial length must also be considered if elongation rather than final organ length is the desired variable.

In a time-course experiment, the onset of changes is as informative as their magnitude. An increase in ACC before an increase in ethylene is consistent with the sequential biosynthetic pathway. Yet the timing of a measured increase also depends on the detection threshold and sampling interval. An ethylene increase occurring before a detectable rise in ACC may reflect rapid conversion of a small precursor pool rather than an alternative pathway. Direct enzyme activity measurements, isotope labeling and targeted perturbations make causal inference more robust than temporal order alone.

Gas sampling presents a separate difficulty. A sealed incubation provides an integrated measurement over time. Suppose two fruits are each placed in 1 litre of headspace for two hours. Fruit A yields 2 microlitres per litre and has a mass of 0.1 kilogram; fruit B yields 3 microlitres per litre and has a mass of 0.3 kilogram. If the initial concentration is zero, the estimated net emission rates normalized by mass are 2/(2 × 0.1) = 10 and 3/(2 × 0.3) = 5 microlitres per kilogram per hour. Although fruit B releases more gas in total, fruit A has a higher mass-specific rate. This is a hypothetical example that assumes equal headspace, no gas leakage and approximately constant emission.

Normalization by fresh mass is useful, but it is not always biologically sufficient. Water content, tissue composition and surface-to-volume ratio may differ among samples. When comparing developmental stages, the fraction of living metabolically active tissue may change. Depending on the research question, normalization by dry mass, tissue area, protein content or number of organs may complement fresh-mass measurements. The chosen denominator should be stated clearly because a rate normalized by organ mass and a rate normalized by a particular cell population answer different questions.

Experimental chambers must also avoid confounding ethylene exposure with oxygen depletion or carbon dioxide accumulation. A fruit respires while releasing ethylene, so prolonged enclosure can change several gases simultaneously. A matched chamber that differs only in controlled ethylene concentration is preferable for a direct hormone response test. When estimating production, collecting several early time points and verifying approximate linearity can reveal whether the enclosure is altering the rate being measured. A chamber blank can identify background contamination. Replication across independent fruits is essential because repeated measurements of one fruit do not capture the variation among biological samples.

9. The triple response as a genetic assay

A dark-grown Arabidopsis seedling elongates its hypocotyl as it searches for light. Its apical hook helps protect the delicate shoot apex. Exposure to ethylene under these conditions alters the pattern: hypocotyl elongation is inhibited, radial thickening becomes conspicuous, and the apical hook becomes exaggerated. Root elongation is also commonly reduced. Descriptions of the triple response vary slightly in how the root phenotype is counted, so the organism and the measured feature should always be specified. The important point is that ethylene creates a recognizable combination of growth responses rather than merely reducing total seedling size.

The ecological interpretation is that a seedling encountering mechanical resistance may alter its growth so that its vulnerable apex is better protected. This is a useful functional model, not proof that every component of the assay was selected solely for soil emergence. A mutation affecting hook formation can change one part of the visible response without necessarily abolishing all ethylene-dependent transcription. Conversely, a severely unhealthy seedling can be short even without ethylene pathway activation. Measurements of radial growth, hook curvature, reporter expression and untreated controls help distinguish these possibilities.

A classical screen identifies seedlings that fail to change normally in an ethylene-containing atmosphere or that show a response without added ethylene. A dominant insensitive receptor allele such as etr1-1 can maintain the repression of the pathway; added ethylene then has little effect on the characteristic seedling phenotype. Loss of the positive signal transducer EIN2 also causes insensitivity, but for a different reason: removal of upstream repression cannot be conveyed to the downstream regulators. A loss-of-function ctr1 mutant exhibits a constitutive response because an essential repressor is defective.

Roman and colleagues used genetic relationships among mutant classes to help order components of the pathway. If the phenotype of a double mutant resembles that of one single mutant, the corresponding allele is said to be epistatic in that comparison. In the simplified canonical model, loss of CTR1 can bypass a receptor that maintains repression, whereas loss of EIN2 can suppress the constitutive response caused by loss of CTR1. These results place the receptor upstream of CTR1 and the positive EIN2 step downstream. Epistasis does not establish direct physical interaction; it gives functional ordering under the specified genetic and phenotypic conditions. Biochemical tests are required to establish phosphorylation or direct protein binding.

To interpret a gas treatment experiment, compare the response to ACC with the response to ethylene itself. Suppose seedling A fails to respond to ACC but responds to ethylene, while seedling B fails to respond to both. Under conditions in which ACC is absorbed normally, A may have reduced ACO activity and B may have a defect in perception or transduction. However, failure to respond to ACC could also be caused by poor uptake or diversion into conjugates. Direct measurement of ACC and ethylene production would help establish the causal step. If a seedling responds normally to both ACC and ethylene but not to a stimulus that usually increases endogenous ethylene, an upstream biosynthetic or stimulus-sensing defect becomes more plausible.

10. Fruit ripening and positive feedback

Fruit development and ripening are related but distinct processes. A fruit grows through cell division and expansion, accumulates reserves and develops its tissues before reaching a stage in which ripening is possible. Ripening coordinates changes in texture, pigmentation, aroma, acidity and other properties that influence dispersal and consumption. Ethylene is an important coordinator of this process in many species, but developmental competence is necessary. Exposing a fruit that is too immature to ethylene need not produce the same outcome as exposing a mature green fruit.

A climacteric fruit characteristically undergoes a rise in respiration associated with ripening and an increase in ethylene production. Banana, apple and tomato are standard examples. The respiratory rise refers to gas exchange associated with metabolism; it is not the chemical conversion of ethylene into carbon dioxide. Respiratory measurements and ethylene emission measurements therefore require separate analytical channels. A plot containing oxygen uptake or carbon dioxide release and ethylene concentration should not be interpreted as though these quantities were the same variable.

The conventional distinction between system 1 and system 2 ethylene synthesis explains the transition in many climacteric fruits. System 1 describes basal production before ripening, often characterized by negative or limited feedback. System 2 describes the developmental phase in which ethylene promotes additional ethylene synthesis. In tomato, induction of specific ACS and ACO genes contributes to this autocatalytic process. The switch also requires the appropriate developmental regulatory context. Ethylene is not sufficient to transform any arbitrary tissue into a ripening fruit.

Positive feedback explains how a modest initial stimulus can cause a rapid increase in emission. An ethylene-responsive increase in ACS activity produces more ACC. Adequate ACO and oxygen convert more ACC into ethylene. The newly synthesized ethylene can stimulate additional responsive cells, reinforcing the original stimulus. Nevertheless the increase cannot continue indefinitely. Substrate availability, changing tissue physiology, gene regulation and progression through ripening limit the response. A realistic graph has an onset, a period of rapid increase and subsequent changes rather than an endless exponential rise.

The changes produced by ripening are separable. Modification of pectin, hemicellulose and other wall components contributes to fruit softening. Pigment changes may include chlorophyll breakdown and the accumulation of carotenoids or other pigments, depending on species. Altered sugar, organic acid and volatile metabolism changes taste and odor. A treatment may suppress one subset of these outputs more strongly than another. Therefore a fruit that changes color after a treatment has not necessarily undergone normal ripening in all respects. Firmness, color, volatile compounds, respiration and ethylene production supply complementary measurements.

Non-climacteric fruits, such as grape and strawberry, generally lack the classic autocatalytic ethylene and respiratory climacteric that defines the climacteric pattern. They nevertheless possess ethylene perception and biosynthetic machinery, and particular tissues or developmental stages can respond to ethylene. Other hormones, including ABA, play important roles in ripening of some non-climacteric fruits. It is incorrect to state that these fruits contain no ethylene or that ethylene can never influence any property of them. The classification describes characteristic patterns of ripening regulation, not the presence or absence of all ethylene-related biology.

The ethylene perception inhibitor 1-methylcyclopropene (1-MCP) can be used to distinguish perception-dependent changes from changes caused by other aspects of maturation. Because it acts at receptors, inhibition of a response by 1-MCP does not establish direct inhibition of ACS or ACO. The treatment may secondarily alter ethylene production by interfering with autocatalytic feedback. Different fruits and stages may show different durations of inhibition, in part because of receptor turnover and continuing development. These principles support interpretation of postharvest experiments without assuming that one intervention preserves all fruits equally.

11. Developmental competence and experimental dissection of ripening

The ripening process has to be situated within the life history of a fruit. A developing ovary acquires tissues with different future functions, and their growth changes the physical and biochemical environment of the seed. Before a fruit reaches physiological maturity, its developmental priorities may include cell proliferation, cell enlargement and accumulation of metabolites. Ripening imposes a different pattern of gene expression. The fruit may become more accessible to dispersing animals, or its protective structures may change. Ethylene responsiveness develops as part of this context, rather than being a feature that can be predicted from gas concentration alone.

The term mature green has a particular experimental value in tomato. It describes a stage in which the fruit is still visually green but is sufficiently developed to undertake ripening. Two fruits with similar external coloration can nevertheless differ in maturity. If the fruits are not matched by developmental stage, a comparison of their response to ethylene may confuse sensitivity with differences in developmental competence. The strongest experiment randomly assigns comparable fruits to treatment groups and uses independent fruits, rather than different pieces of the same fruit alone, to estimate biological variation.

A ripening time course can measure the interval between treatment and the first detectable change, the rate of change during the response, and the ultimate value of each physiological parameter. A treatment that delays the start of softening by two days without altering the subsequent rate affects a different feature of the curve from a treatment that leaves onset unchanged but reduces the rate of softening. Recording only the firmness on one arbitrary day cannot distinguish these cases. Similarly, a smaller peak in ethylene emission need not mean that total ethylene released throughout ripening was smaller. The integrated emission depends on both the height and the duration of the peak.

It is useful to distinguish the respiratory climacteric from the autocatalytic control of ethylene at the molecular level. Respiration describes the oxidation of metabolic fuels to support cellular energy metabolism. A ripening tissue undertakes extensive metabolic reorganization, including synthesis and turnover of cellular components. Changes in respiratory demand accompany these processes. ACS and ACO, in contrast, define the dedicated biochemical route to ethylene. A fruit can alter respiration because of changes in temperature, tissue damage, microbial contamination or oxygen availability independently of the developmental ethylene switch. A respiratory peak alone does not demonstrate an autocatalytic hormone response.

Consider a hypothetical set of mature tomato fruits measured daily for five days. Ethylene emission in arbitrary units per kilogram per hour is 1, 2, 8, 14 and 7. The rate of carbon dioxide evolution in separately defined units is 10, 12, 20, 28 and 18. These measurements display associated peaks, but temporal association does not establish that ethylene is the immediate biochemical cause of all additional carbon dioxide. To test dependence on perception, matched fruit could receive 1-MCP and the same variables could be followed. To test dependence on biosynthesis, a genetic reduction in the activity of a dedicated enzyme is preferable to an intervention that also directly inhibits respiration. The correct causal interpretation comes from comparing several perturbations.

The autocatalytic mechanism can also be considered in terms of an input that initiates a response and an internal process that sustains it. If external ethylene is supplied transiently, and the fruit continues to increase its own ethylene release after transfer to clean air, the response is consistent with activation of system 2 synthesis. Residual gas trapped in tissue or chambers could imitate sustained release during the first part of the measurement, so an appropriate washout interval and a chamber blank are necessary. Sustained expression of ripening-associated ACS genes and rising endogenous production after the external stimulus has been removed would strengthen the case.

A biosynthetic perturbation can be combined with this pulse experiment. In a fruit with sufficiently reduced ACS capacity, external ethylene can stimulate perception-dependent transcription but might not produce normal endogenous amplification. One should distinguish rescue of physiological outputs during continuous gas exposure from restoration of the fruit's ability to sustain its own signal after withdrawal. If color change and softening proceed under continued exposure but are delayed after withdrawal, the results can be consistent with impaired feedback amplification rather than defective receptor function. They need not be identical for all outputs.

Firmness is often interpreted as an index of cell wall breakdown, but the measured mechanical properties of fruit also depend on cellular water content, turgor, geometry and tissue organization. The middle lamella, which contains pectic substances that contribute to adhesion between neighboring plant cells, changes during ripening. Enzymes associated with pectin modification, together with other wall-remodeling processes, can alter how tissues deform and separate. A treatment that reduces firmness without changing a chosen pectin-modifying enzyme transcript might still act through other wall components or through water relations. Conversely, an increase in the transcript of a wall enzyme does not guarantee that this enzyme alone determines the mechanical response.

Pigment production supplies a different kind of endpoint. In tomato, the transition from green to red involves changes in plastid organization and pigment composition. Chlorophyll loss and carotenoid accumulation are not the same chemical reaction. A decline in green coloration may make another pigment more visible even without a proportional increase in its synthesis. Measuring individual pigments and the expression of relevant biosynthetic genes gives a more specific interpretation than scoring color alone. Ethylene can coordinate these changes without directly catalyzing the reactions responsible for the pigments.

Sugar and acid measurements should likewise be interpreted relative to the species. A banana contains substantial starch before ripening, and the conversion of starch to soluble sugars contributes to its characteristic change in sweetness. Other fruits begin ripening with different storage compounds, and concentration can change when water is lost. A higher soluble-solids reading is therefore not always evidence that the amount of sugar per fruit increased. Comparing total content per organ with concentration per unit mass can distinguish metabolic conversion from a concentration effect caused by dehydration. Ethylene-dependent regulation of metabolism must not be equated with a universal chemical transformation across all fruits.

An experiment comparing grape and tomato illustrates why the classification of fruits is not an all-or-none test for hormone action. Suppose a mature tomato develops a sustained increase in endogenous ethylene and respiration after a defined stimulus, whereas a grape shows no comparable respiratory rise but does change expression of a subset of ethylene-responsive genes. These observations are compatible with climacteric and non-climacteric patterns respectively. The grape still has a molecular response to ethylene. The evidence for classification depends on the characteristic ripening pattern; it does not require that one class lacks functional receptors.

The term non-climacteric is also not a synonym for a fruit that cannot change after harvest. Detached fruits can continue metabolism, lose water, alter pigmentation and undergo deterioration. Some ripening-related processes may continue depending on the species and maturity at harvest. A fruit's capacity to complete normal ripening after detachment depends on developmental state and available resources. A comparison between an attached unripe fruit and a detached fruit treated with ethylene involves more variables than hormone concentration because removal from the plant changes water and assimilate supply.

Postharvest storage provides an application of these distinctions. Limiting exposure to external ethylene can reduce unintended signaling between neighboring organs. Altering temperature can change many enzyme rates as well as gas movement, and controlled atmospheres alter respiratory conditions. Blocking ethylene perception can delay certain responses in susceptible fruits. The physiological principle is not that all changes can be prevented by a single treatment, but that a reduction in signaling may alter particular components of the ripening program. Quality depends on both delayed deterioration and the capacity to develop desirable eating characteristics. A treatment that preserves firmness but prevents desirable aroma development may not yield the intended biological or commercial result.

12. Senescence and organ separation

Senescence is a regulated transition from an active functional state toward dismantling and nutrient redistribution or loss of organ function. It is not interchangeable with immediate cell death. A senescing leaf may continue to carry out metabolic activities while chloroplast components are dismantled and nitrogen is remobilized. In many flowers, visible wilting or petal deterioration follows a regulated developmental program. Ethylene accelerates senescence in some tissues and species, while in others senescence is comparatively insensitive to ethylene. Developmental age, other hormone signals and environmental conditions influence the outcome.

Abscission, in contrast, is the controlled separation of an organ from the parent plant at a specialized region called an abscission zone. The zone consists of cells that become competent to separate through changes in intercellular adhesion and cell wall properties. Ethylene can promote expression or activity of wall-remodeling enzymes in responsive abscission zones. The resulting loss of adhesion permits a petal, leaf or fruit to detach. Detachment is not simply the mechanical tearing of an entire senescent organ. Adjacent cells may establish a protective boundary that limits damage and water loss.

Auxin arriving from the distal organ often helps maintain reduced sensitivity of the abscission zone to ethylene. A decline in auxin supply, such as after removal of a leaf blade in a classical model, can increase the zone's competence to respond to ethylene. Applying auxin in an appropriate spatial and temporal context may delay separation. This is a model of regulation through sensitivity, not proof that auxin universally suppresses every ethylene response. The effects of auxin depend on where and when it is applied, and auxin can also stimulate ethylene biosynthesis in other developmental settings.

An experimental comparison of intact and debladed petioles helps separate changes in competence from changes in ethylene supply. If both sets receive the same external ethylene concentration but the debladed group separates earlier, the difference cannot be explained simply by the external gas concentration. It is consistent with altered sensitivity or competence, although removal of the blade also changes several physiological processes and wound signals. Local auxin replacement, mock handling and measurement of separation force can strengthen the interpretation. A good experiment measures the response at the abscission zone instead of using general wilting as the only endpoint.

13. Flower longevity, leaf aging and the abscission zone

The lifespan of a flower is closely related to its reproductive function. An open flower may invest carbohydrates and water in structures that attract pollinators or facilitate fertilization. After successful pollination, continuation of the same expenditure may no longer be necessary. In ethylene-sensitive species, a signal originating in reproductive tissues can contribute to the coordinated termination of petal function. This is a useful context in which to distinguish the source of a hormonal signal from the location of the visible response. A petal can begin to change because of a signal produced elsewhere within the flower.

Carnation and petunia are widely used examples of ethylene-sensitive flower senescence. In some such systems, pollination or developmental aging increases ethylene production and may induce additional production in petals. Positive feedback makes it possible for a change initiated in a restricted region to become a coordinated organ-level response. Other flowers, including examples such as iris or daylily, do not show the same strong dependence of petal senescence on ethylene. A statement that removal of ethylene always prevents petal senescence ignores these differences. It also overlooks the fact that aging programs may continue through other regulatory mechanisms even in a flower whose ethylene response has been delayed.

The sequence after pollination can be investigated by monitoring reproductive structures and petals separately. An increase in ethylene production in a stigma or style before an increase in petal emission suggests an interorgan signal. It does not establish that ethylene gas alone travelled between them, because ACC or other signals might contribute to communication. Local perturbations, isolated tissue experiments and measurements of precursor availability can test more specific explanations. The student should avoid inferring the path of a molecule solely from the temporal sequence of visible wilting.

Wilting is a mechanical description, not a complete explanation of senescence. Petals can wilt because of water deficit, altered cell wall properties or changes in metabolism that accompany developmental aging. Supplying water may temporarily restore turgor in some circumstances without reversing a developmental senescence program. Conversely, an ethylene-insensitive flower can wilt if its water supply is interrupted. Experiments on cut flowers need to control water availability and handling so that a change in longevity is not automatically attributed to hormonal regulation.

Leaf senescence has an additional physiological importance because mature leaves are major photosynthetic organs and stores of nutrients. During regulated aging, chloroplast function declines, proteins may be degraded and nutrients may be exported to developing or storage organs. Ethylene can influence the rate or timing of these changes, but the leaf's developmental age and metabolic state matter. A young leaf may not respond to the same ethylene stimulus in the same way as a mature leaf approaching senescence. This illustrates an interaction between a developmental program and a signaling pathway, rather than an invariant command that all leaves must obey.

To test whether ethylene accelerates aging, one can compare leaves of equal initial developmental stage maintained under matched light, humidity and nutrient conditions. A useful set of readouts includes chlorophyll content, photosynthetic performance and expression of genes associated with senescence. The appearance of yellowing is not enough to establish complete loss of viability or to quantify nutrient export. If the leaves differ in chlorophyll before treatment, the rate of change from their own baselines is more meaningful than a single absolute endpoint. Measuring export to a sink organ requires a separate approach.

Abscission has a distinct anatomical prerequisite. In an organ with a recognizable separation zone, the cells at that region acquire the capacity to detach in a coordinated fashion. This capacity is established developmentally, often long before the actual separation event. A hormone can increase or decrease the likelihood that the zone will activate without necessarily establishing the entire zone from the beginning. The distinction between formation of a competent abscission zone and activation of separation is comparable to the distinction between fruit maturation and ripening. A treatment applied to an organ lacking a properly specified zone may fail even if ethylene perception is intact.

Auxin supplied by a distal organ can influence the sensitivity of its attachment region. In a simplified classical leaf-abscission model, auxin movement from the blade toward the base contributes to maintaining a state less responsive to ethylene. Removing the blade reduces that source. If ethylene becomes more effective at promoting separation after the removal, a change in sensitivity is plausible. Yet excision is also a wound, and wounding can alter local ethylene production. Comparing intact, sham-treated and debladed organs under the same defined gaseous exposure helps separate the two effects.

The event of separation can be quantified as a decrease in the force required to detach the organ. Suppose four matched groups of petioles have median separation forces of 1.2 newtons for an untreated intact group, 0.8 newtons for an ethylene-treated intact group, 0.6 newtons for a debladed group and 0.2 newtons for a debladed plus ethylene group. These invented numbers illustrate an interaction but do not establish its mechanism. Blade removal reduces the median separation force by 0.6 newtons in both contexts. The ethylene-associated reduction is likewise 0.4 newtons in both blade conditions. Thus these particular values are compatible with additive effects on the force scale. They are not evidence of synergism merely because the combined group has the lowest force.

An alternative measure might be the proportion of organs that detach by a fixed time. If separation occurs when force falls below a threshold, additive changes in the force variable can produce a dramatic nonadditive change in the proportion of organs crossing that threshold. This is a general lesson about measurement scales. The same biological process can look approximately additive on one scale and strongly interacting on another. When a question asks whether two hormones act synergistically, the student should first ask which model of independent effects is being used and what response variable is measured.

Cell wall hydrolases contribute to separation by modifying the material that holds cells together. Polygalacturonase activity can participate in pectin degradation, while other enzymes modify additional wall components. Their functions are spatially regulated; widespread uncoordinated degradation throughout the organ would not constitute normal abscission. Microscopic evidence of separation at the expected cell layer and localized gene expression strengthen the inference that an observed decrease in attachment force reflects abscission-zone activation. A whole-organ assay of enzyme activity may miss the relevant change because the zone represents a small fraction of the tissue.

Finally, the completion of separation is not the end of the plant's physiological problem. The retained tissue must maintain its integrity at the site where the organ was lost. Formation of a protective boundary, including changes in cell walls or other barrier properties, helps limit water loss and exposure of vulnerable tissue. Ethylene participates in regulatory networks associated with organ shedding but does not by itself explain every aspect of wound sealing or tissue differentiation. These developmental details connect hormone physiology with the study of organ senescence and programmed cell death elsewhere in the curriculum.

14. Flooding, oxygen limitation and hormonal interactions

Flooding introduces two simultaneous problems for gaseous signaling: oxygen supply to submerged tissue declines and the escape of gases becomes restricted. Water-filled pores in the soil impede gas exchange, so respiration can consume oxygen more rapidly than it is replenished. ACC oxidase requires oxygen, making conversion of ACC to ethylene sensitive to this limitation. At the same time, ethylene that is formed in submerged tissues may accumulate because diffusion through water is much slower than diffusion through air. Whether measured tissue ethylene rises or falls depends on the balance of these processes.

In waterlogged tomato plants, roots can accumulate ACC and export it in xylem sap. The precursor arrives in the shoot, where oxygen is comparatively abundant, and ACO converts it to ethylene. A prominent physiological response is epinasty, a downward curvature caused by unequal growth on opposite sides of the petiole. Bradford and Yang reported experimental evidence for this root-to-shoot precursor movement in 1980. The result demonstrates why measuring only ethylene in the roots would be insufficient to identify the origin of a signal observed in the shoot.

To test the mechanism, compare aerated roots and waterlogged roots while measuring ACC in xylem sap, ethylene emission from shoots and petiole angle. Increased xylem ACC followed by increased shoot emission and curvature is consistent with the precursor transport model. A stronger test reduces ACC production in roots or limits its conversion in shoots and asks which outputs are affected. A rise in ACC with a fall in shoot ethylene after suppression of shoot ACO is consistent with a block at the final step. The precise outcome still depends on transport, residual enzyme activity and compensatory regulation.

Not all flooded plants use the same adaptive strategy. In certain wetland species ethylene contributes to elongation that may help leaves regain contact with air, while in others growth restraint can conserve carbohydrates during temporary submergence. Ethylene can also participate in adventitious root formation and the development of aerenchyma, an arrangement of interconnected gas spaces that facilitates internal gas movement. These outcomes reflect species-specific genetic and hormonal contexts. The common observation of high ethylene under flooding does not by itself tell us whether elongation will increase or decrease.

The interaction with auxin is especially relevant to seedling growth. Ethylene can alter auxin synthesis, transport and spatial distribution, leading to changes in root elongation or apical hook geometry. A root-specific auxin transport defect may therefore modify an ethylene growth phenotype even when other ethylene outputs remain detectable. Jasmonate and ethylene also interact in plant defense; for example, ethylene-regulated transcriptional components can cooperate with jasmonate-dependent regulators in responses to certain necrotrophic pathogens. Other combinations of signals can favor different defense programs. A statement that two hormones always act synergistically or always act antagonistically is too broad without a tissue, dose, developmental stage and response variable.

15. Flood duration, gas retention and contrasting growth strategies

Waterlogging of a root system and complete submergence of a plant create related but not identical environments. In waterlogging, shoots may remain above the water surface and retain access to atmospheric oxygen, while roots experience restricted gas exchange. In complete submergence, gas exchange of aerial tissues is also altered. The two situations differ in photosynthetic opportunity, the routes through which oxygen can reach tissues, and the possible routes of ethylene escape. An explanation developed for the waterlogged tomato root should not be transferred automatically to a fully submerged rice shoot.

Ethylene can accumulate rapidly in a submerged tissue because its outward movement encounters high resistance. A useful conceptual model is to represent gas loss as the product of a conductance and a concentration difference between tissue and its environment. Lower conductance reduces outward flux at the same concentration gradient. The tissue concentration can rise until the gradient is sufficient to support removal of the gas being produced. This model helps explain why physical trapping may precede severe oxygen depletion, depending on the tissue and conditions. If oxygen falls so far that production becomes negligible, continued accumulation is not guaranteed.

The requirement for oxygen by ACO creates a particularly instructive spatial arrangement. In the waterlogged tomato model, the precursor formed in roots can enter the transpiration stream. When transported to a more oxygenated region it becomes available for conversion. The shoot response therefore integrates root physiology, vascular flow and local enzyme activity. Interruption of xylem transport would be expected to alter this communication, but such an intervention also disrupts water relations and mineral delivery. A specific experimental conclusion requires controls for these accompanying changes.

A conceptual labeling experiment could distinguish root-derived precursor from locally generated ethylene. If a labeled carbon atom is introduced into ACC available to the root, detection of labeled ethylene in the shoot could demonstrate that at least some precursor-derived material reached the shoot and entered the ethylene pathway. The choice of label position matters because only two of the carbons in ACC enter ethylene; other carbon atoms appear in different products. If the label is lost as carbon dioxide or cyanide during conversion, the absence of labeled ethylene would not demonstrate failure of transport. This is a general principle of tracing metabolism: the atom followed must be expected to remain in the measured product.

In rice, the presence of internal air spaces supports oxygen delivery into otherwise poorly aerated organs. Aerenchyma is a tissue arrangement containing enlarged interconnected gas spaces. These spaces can reduce the diffusion resistance between aerial and submerged parts, provided there is access to a source of oxygen. Some aerenchyma is developmentally present even under nonflooded conditions, and additional formation can occur in response to flooding. Ethylene can contribute to induced lysigenous aerenchyma formation, involving controlled removal of certain cells. The adaptive significance depends on spatial organization: indiscriminate loss of tissue would not necessarily establish an effective aeration route.

Different flood environments favor different growth strategies. Deepwater rice can escape gradually rising water by rapid internode elongation. Ethylene accumulation helps initiate a regulatory response involving ethylene response factor genes such as SNORKEL1 and SNORKEL2 and gibberellin-dependent growth. Elongation can restore communication between submerged tissues and the atmosphere if leaves reach the water surface. The gain comes with a cost: cell expansion and supporting metabolism consume reserves, and elongation is useful only if emergence is achievable before those reserves are exhausted.

A contrasting strategy in rice carrying an effective SUB1A allele is to limit elongation during temporary complete submergence. Ethylene-induced SUB1A regulation restricts excessive gibberellin-dependent growth and helps conserve carbohydrate reserves, allowing more effective recovery when the water recedes. This is not a simple lack of ethylene perception. Ethylene participates in both responses, but different downstream regulatory systems direct different outcomes. The same gaseous signal therefore conveys information about the environment while the genotype helps determine how that information is acted upon.

An illustrative ecological comparison makes the tradeoff explicit. Consider a plant with 100 arbitrary carbohydrate units at the start of flooding. If rapid elongation consumes 18 units per day and modest maintenance consumes 7 units per day, an escape strategy has a high cost if emergence takes longer than the available reserves permit. In this simplified constant-cost model, the reserves last about 5.6 days at the higher rate or 14.3 days at the lower rate. These values are hypothetical and ignore photosynthesis and changes in consumption over time. If the rapid-growth plant reaches the air after three days and restores carbon assimilation, the cost may be worthwhile. If the water remains too deep for emergence, conservation is more advantageous. The physiological effect of ethylene cannot be classified as beneficial or harmful without the environmental scenario.

The reoxygenation period after flooding can introduce additional stress. Metabolic pathways that functioned under oxygen limitation must adjust to renewed oxygen availability, and reactive oxygen species can change during recovery. A plant that remains alive during submergence is not necessarily able to recover growth after drainage. Survival experiments should therefore follow plants after the water recedes, not only measure their height under water. A tall plant may have escaped effectively or may simply have exhausted its resources, depending on whether its new shoot position permitted renewed gas exchange.

Ethylene-dependent changes in roots can interact with auxin-dependent growth processes. Adventitious roots may develop from stem regions under certain flooded conditions, changing the distribution of roots relative to oxygen and nutrient availability. Hormonal regulation influences the competence of tissues to initiate and emerge as new roots. The mere appearance of roots under flooding does not establish that ethylene alone is sufficient for their development. Comparisons with auxin signaling or transport perturbations can test whether the two systems contribute to different stages of the process.

A broader stress signal should not be confused with direct oxygen sensing. Ethylene accumulation can supply early information about the restriction of gas exchange because it is a consequence of reduced diffusion. Cells also possess mechanisms that respond directly to oxygen availability and regulate metabolism under low oxygen. These mechanisms interact with hormonal pathways, but they are not identical to ethylene receptors. A plant can therefore show both a response to gaseous hormone accumulation and a response to oxygen depletion. An experimental design that varies oxygen independently of ethylene is required to distinguish the two inputs.

16. How ethylene changes other hormone-dependent outputs

Plant cells rarely receive one hormonal signal in isolation. The concentration of ethylene can change in response to auxin, and the pattern of auxin action can change in response to ethylene. It is useful to identify the particular direction of causation rather than describe the relationship simply as an interaction. If auxin stimulates ACS expression or activity, an effect of auxin may be mediated partly through increased ethylene production. If ethylene changes auxin synthesis or transport, the downstream auxin distribution may contribute to an ethylene-induced phenotype. Both directions can exist within one plant, but the contribution of each depends on the tissue and the response being studied.

The root is a particularly useful system for distinguishing local growth responses from whole-organ hormonal concentration. Root elongation occurs largely through the expansion of cells that have passed through the proliferative region near the apex. Auxin helps regulate root growth, and the local concentration and distribution of auxin influence how rapidly cells expand. In Arabidopsis, ethylene can increase auxin production and modify its movement into regions involved in elongation. Consequently, a defect in auxin transport may reduce ethylene-induced inhibition of root growth even if the upstream ethylene signaling apparatus is functional. A simple measurement of root length alone does not identify the affected hormone receptor.

An appropriate comparison would examine wild type, an ethylene signaling mutant, and a mutant with altered auxin transport. Each genotype would receive either clean air or a specified ethylene exposure under matched growth conditions. Root elongation would be measured together with a reporter of ethylene-responsive transcription and a reporter of auxin-dependent transcription. If the transport mutant retains an ethylene-dependent transcriptional response but fails to redistribute the auxin reporter normally and displays less root growth inhibition, the observations support a role of auxin downstream of the ethylene signal in that particular endpoint. This interpretation does not require all ethylene effects to pass through auxin.

Apical hook development provides a second example. The hook is produced by differential growth on opposite sides of the developing shoot. An asymmetry in growth requires either an asymmetry in a regulator or a difference in responsiveness to it, or both. Ethylene influences the developmental program that establishes and maintains exaggerated curvature, including auxin-dependent mechanisms involving HOOKLESS1. A mutation affecting a local organizer of hook development can remove hook exaggeration without eliminating all effects of ethylene on hypocotyl length. Thus a developmental target can be epistatic for one measured phenotype while being irrelevant to another branch of the response.

A useful caution concerns the meaning of a transcriptional reporter. Many auxin reporters report the activity of a response pathway rather than directly measuring the local free concentration of auxin. Their expression may be influenced by the amount of hormone, receptor activity, the abundance of regulatory proteins and time since stimulation. A spatial change in reporter output supports a change in local auxin response; it is not automatically a direct chemical map. Combining reporters with transport mutations, tissue-specific experiments and direct measurements of hormone abundance can distinguish alternative mechanisms.

The relation between ethylene and jasmonate is often introduced through plant defense. A pathogen or damaged tissue can activate multiple chemical signaling systems at once. Jasmonate and ethylene can cooperate in the expression of defense genes in response to particular necrotrophic pathogens, which obtain nutrients from dead or damaged host tissue. Ethylene-associated transcription factors such as ERF1 and ORA59 participate in this regulatory context. Jasmonate signaling changes the availability of its own transcriptional regulators through a distinct receptor and repressor system. Cooperation at the output does not mean that the two hormones share the same receptor or that one is an obligatory biosynthetic intermediate of the other.

Consider two genes, one induced mainly by ethylene in a given tissue and another strongly induced only when both jasmonate and ethylene signaling are active. An ethylene-insensitive mutant might fail to induce both genes after a combined treatment, while a jasmonate-insensitive mutant might retain induction of the first gene but lose most induction of the second. These patterns would suggest different logic at the regulatory outputs. They would not prove a physical interaction between the two receptors. Measurement of additional targets or promoter activity would help determine whether the effects are mediated through shared transcriptional regulators or other changes in the state of the tissue.

Hormonal crosstalk is often described with terms such as synergism and antagonism. These terms should be applied to a defined response under defined conditions. Two hormones may cooperate to increase defense-associated transcription while showing opposing effects on a separate developmental process. Even at one target, the apparent interaction can change with dose, timing or the baseline activity of the tissue. A factorial experiment, in which each hormone is tested alone and both are tested together along with an untreated control, is a better starting point than comparing only the combined treatment with the untreated group.

As an illustrative example, assume normalized reporter outputs of 1, 3, 4 and 10 for untreated, ethylene, jasmonate and combined treatments respectively. An additive model for changes above the untreated baseline predicts 1 + (3 − 1) + (4 − 1) = 6 for the combined group. The observed value of 10 exceeds this particular expectation. A multiplicative model for fold changes instead predicts 3 × 4 = 12. The same observed combined output is smaller than that expectation. Neither comparison should be interpreted as proof of an interaction without estimates of variation and a justified model of independent effects. This quantitative reasoning is especially useful when an examination supplies a small table of treatment responses.

A second form of crosstalk arises because hormonal responses change a tissue's resource demands. Growth, defense and recovery all require metabolic inputs. A reduction in root elongation after exposure to ethylene and jasmonate might represent regulation of growth rather than nonspecific toxicity. But toxicity is still an alternative possibility if concentrations are excessive. Assessing viability, recovery after signal withdrawal and pathway-specific transcription can help differentiate regulated growth adjustment from damage. Responses in one experimental environment should not be extrapolated to every plant species or every kind of stress.

17. Distinguishing production, perception and physiological competence

A complete explanation of ethylene physiology requires a distinction among three classes of experimental failure. First, the tissue may fail to generate the signal. Second, it may generate the signal but fail to perceive or transmit it. Third, it may perceive the signal but lack the developmental or physiological machinery needed to perform the selected response. These classes can produce superficially similar phenotypes. A fruit that remains firm and green may have a biosynthetic defect, a receptor defect, an immature developmental state or an alteration of the ripening program itself. The appropriate next experiment differs in each case.

A rescue matrix provides a structured way to test the alternatives. One compares the response to endogenous induction, the immediate precursor ACC and exogenous ethylene. A specific deficiency of ACS should lower the capacity to produce ACC following endogenous induction. Provided downstream components function, ACC and ethylene can bypass that deficiency. A sufficiently severe ACO defect prevents ACC rescue because the precursor cannot efficiently be oxidized, whereas ethylene can bypass the defect. A perception defect blocks the normal response to both precursor and final hormone. A downstream developmental defect may produce the same treatment pattern for one endpoint but retain a normal upstream ethylene-responsive reporter. These predictions assume that the treatments reach their intended sites and the pathway is otherwise functional.

Experimental design becomes especially important when a mutant retains partial activity. A plant with reduced, rather than abolished, ACO activity might respond to a very high external ACC concentration because the remaining enzyme converts enough precursor to ethylene. Similarly, a receptor mutant may retain responses at sufficiently high gas concentrations if sensitivity is reduced rather than absent. The classification of a phenotype as insensitive should therefore be based on a dose-response curve or on a defined assay concentration, not on one negative result without controls. Describing an allele as weak or strong can be useful when comparing different results in the same pathway.

A direct measure of ethylene emission can narrow the alternatives further. An overproducing mutant in clean air may have high emission and a constitutive-looking phenotype. A signaling mutant lacking CTR1 activity may display a similar phenotype without a corresponding increase in emission being required. Feedback can nevertheless alter ethylene synthesis even in a signaling mutant, so normal or elevated emission alone is not a definitive diagnostic. The most informative combination compares emission, sensitivity and the effect of reducing endogenous synthesis. A phenotype that persists when ethylene production is effectively blocked is consistent with constitutive downstream signaling, assuming other growth abnormalities have been excluded.

In an experiment that uses a chemical inhibitor, specificity is rarely absolute. A compound interfering with a pyridoxal phosphate-dependent enzyme might affect other enzymes that use the same cofactor. A chemical that changes respiratory activity may alter ACO indirectly by changing oxygen availability or cellular redox state. Appropriate controls include an untreated sample, a solvent control when applicable, tests of viability and an independent way to perturb the same pathway. Rescue with the downstream product supports the proposed position of the inhibitor, but incomplete rescue can reflect residual off-target effects. Genetics and biochemistry complement pharmacological evidence rather than simply replacing one another.

Perception blockers present a different interpretation problem from biosynthesis inhibitors. A receptor antagonist can delay ethylene-dependent outputs even if a fruit continues to release gas. In climacteric fruit, receptor blockade may also reduce later gas production because the positive feedback loop has been interrupted. Therefore, decreased emission after a perception blocker is not in itself proof that the blocker acts directly on ACS or ACO. To distinguish direct enzyme inhibition from indirect feedback, one could compare enzyme activity in an isolated biochemical preparation with the behavior of intact tissues. The two experimental systems answer different questions.

Temporal resolution can help distinguish an immediate signaling defect from altered developmental competence. In a fruit with a receptor insensitive to ethylene, a rapid molecular marker of ethylene perception may be absent after gas treatment. In a fruit unable to complete a particular color change, early transcriptional markers may respond normally while the later pigment endpoint remains abnormal. A comparison of several readouts over time allows the investigator to place the functional block more precisely. It is not always possible to order the affected processes uniquely, because pathways can branch and feed back on each other. However, the observed pattern can eliminate some simple explanations.

The interpretation of genetic complementation follows a related logic. If a plant has a recessive mutation affecting a particular signaling gene, introduction of a functional copy of that gene may restore the response. This is evidence that the altered gene contributed to the phenotype. It does not establish that the gene product is sufficient to produce ethylene responses in a cell lacking every other component of the pathway. Overexpression may produce an abnormal phenotype that differs from the normal gene's physiological role. The expression level and tissue distribution of the introduced copy matter.

A set of double mutants can establish functional order under controlled conditions. For the canonical pathway, removal of a negative regulator can produce constitutive downstream activity, but this requires downstream positive regulators. A double mutant combining a defective negative regulator with loss of an essential positive regulator may resemble the positive-regulator mutant. A different response in another organ might reflect branch-specific signaling or residual allele activity rather than invalidating the entire pathway. Examination problems usually specify simplified alleles and a particular phenotype. In actual experiments, interpretation depends on the strength of each mutation and the developmental context.

Finally, the distinction between correlation and causal contribution extends to environmental responses. Wounding, flooding or aging may increase ethylene emission. If a treatment that reduces ethylene signaling also reduces the physiological response, this supports a causal role of the hormone. Yet environmental stresses activate other pathways in parallel. A response may be partly dependent on ethylene and partly independent of it. Comparing the residual response in signaling-defective and normal plants helps estimate the contribution under the chosen conditions. The meaningful result is not necessarily complete absence of the phenotype. Partial dependence can be biologically important.

18. From a gaseous molecule to a coordinated biological decision

The preceding examples reveal why ethylene cannot be understood by memorizing a list of effects. The central mechanism consists of a regulated source of precursor, an oxygen-dependent conversion step, a diffusible product, a negative-regulatory receptor system and a set of tissue-specific outputs. Each part determines a different feature of the final response. A leaf under water, a germinating seedling and a mature fruit may all use the same basic pathway while experiencing entirely different constraints on gas escape, precursor availability and competence.

It is useful to reconstruct a case in causal order. Imagine a mature fruit in an atmosphere from which ethylene is continuously removed. The fruit may produce low basal amounts of the gas, but the local concentration and receptor occupancy depend on how rapidly it leaves the tissue. When developmental regulation initiates a sufficiently strong biosynthetic increase, local signaling can stimulate expression of additional biosynthetic enzymes. The gas can then accumulate in the tissue or reach neighboring organs, and physiological targets respond according to their developmental state. If ventilation is increased, the local concentration may decrease even though ACS activity is initially unchanged. If receptors are blocked, the downstream response may be reduced even though gas remains present. If a wall-modifying gene is defective, the fruit may remain firmer despite normal gas and signaling measurements. These are distinct points of intervention in one integrated system.

The same reconstruction in a waterlogged tomato begins at a different point. Oxygen restriction in the roots can limit oxidation of ACC while increasing the precursor pool through regulatory changes. Vascular movement transfers part of that pool into shoots, where oxygen permits ethylene formation. The resulting signal influences petiole growth. At no step does the hypothesis require ethylene to be carried as a stable glycoside through the xylem. A question that describes high ACC in the root, increased ethylene in the shoot and epinasty can thus be answered by following both the chemical identity and the anatomical location of the moving signal.

In a seedling, by comparison, the key experimental issue may be the sign of a genetic component rather than the source of the signal. Active CTR1 maintains repression, so loss of CTR1 produces an apparent response in the absence of a hormone treatment. Loss of EIN2 removes an essential positive link and prevents the response. A dominant receptor that fails to relinquish repression also blocks the response. All three phenotypes become understandable when the signs and ordering of the pathway are drawn explicitly. Memorizing that all receptor mutations are insensitive would obscure the negative-regulatory role and give incorrect predictions for loss of receptor function.

This style of reasoning is transferable to other hormone systems, but the molecular details should remain distinct. The auxin receptor pathway, gibberellin signaling and abscisic acid signaling each involve their own ligand recognition and regulatory mechanisms. Some hormones act by promoting degradation of a repressor; ethylene relieves upstream inhibition and stabilizes positive transcriptional regulators in the canonical pathway. A shared principle is that the response can emerge from removal of a brake, not just from activation of an enzyme by a ligand. The specific proteins, cellular compartments and physiological outcomes determine how that principle is implemented.

Ethylene also illustrates why a plant hormone need not have a single dedicated long-distance transport mechanism. Diffusion is suitable for communication across nearby air spaces, while precursor transport can couple organs over greater distances in aqueous vascular streams. Regulation of local precursor generation and conjugation provides ways to control where ethylene becomes available. Changes in environmental resistance to gas movement can alter concentration without a direct change in enzyme expression. These factors should be considered together whenever an experimental problem reports a gas concentration instead of a production flux.

A final comparison concerns timing. The release of gas from an enclosed volume can change relatively quickly when ventilation is altered. Receptor-associated regulatory events and protein turnover take their own characteristic times. Transcriptional responses and physiological changes may occur later, and some developmental outcomes are difficult to reverse once completed. Removing ethylene may stop further signaling without restoring the original structure of a detached organ or a softened fruit. This is a general feature of biological control: the lifetime of a signal need not equal the lifetime of a cellular decision. Exam questions involving pulses, washout treatments and delayed readouts often test this difference.

Examination traps and misconceptions

  1. All gas accumulation is increased synthesis

    Reduced ventilation alone can raise concentration; examine emission rate and gas loss.

    Why this trap works: The stem may describe a sealed vessel without changing enzyme activity. Use the mass balance: production minus loss.

  2. SAM is dedicated to ethylene

    SAM also supports methyl transfer and other pathways, so a SAM defect can be pleiotropic.

    Why this trap works: A precursor arrow can look like a dedicated pathway. Ask whether the metabolite also supplies other reactions.

  3. ACC is ethylene

    ACC is a nongaseous precursor; the ACO reaction requires oxygen.

    Why this trap works: Similar pathway labels invite treating precursor and product alike. A rescue by ACC requires active ACO; ethylene bypasses it.

  4. ACO functions normally in complete anoxia

    Molecular oxygen is required, so ACC may accumulate when oxidation is restricted.

    Why this trap works: Hypoxia can increase precursor supply or retention, but oxygen is a substrate. Predict ACC and gas separately.

  5. The Yang cycle recycles emitted ethylene

    It salvages the MTA methylthio group into methionine, not emitted ethylene carbon.

    Why this trap works: The circular drawing may suggest recycling the final product. Track MTA and methionine, not the emitted gas.

  6. Ethylene is stored as malonyl-ethylene

    Malonyl-ACC is a conjugated precursor, not a conjugated gaseous hormone.

    Why this trap works: The storage vocabulary used for auxin may be transferred incorrectly. Identify the chemical species before assigning storage.

  7. All ACC conjugates are readily reversible stores

    Conjugate fates differ; free ACC availability is not inferred solely from total conjugates.

    Why this trap works: A large conjugate pool looks like available precursor. Ask whether release has actually been demonstrated.

  8. Ethylene turns CTR1 on

    In the canonical pathway ligand reduces receptor-supported CTR1 repression.

    Why this trap works: Ligand binding often activates receptors in other pathways. Here the key step is relief of a negative regulator.

  9. Every etr1 mutation causes insensitivity

    Dominant insensitive receptor alleles differ from receptor loss-of-function and redundancy.

    Why this trap works: Mutant names alone conceal allele behavior. Ask whether the receptor remains inhibitory or loses function.

  10. ctr1 loss prevents all responses

    Loss of the brake produces a constitutive response if downstream positives function.

    Why this trap works: Kinase loss often seems to imply loss of response. Follow the inhibitory sign and check downstream EIN2.

  11. EIN2 is a transcription factor

    EIN2 is a membrane-associated signal transducer; EIN3/EIL1 are transcriptional regulators.

    Why this trap works: The EIN label is not a protein class. Distinguish membrane signal relay from sequence-specific transcription.

  12. Higher EIN3 protein proves higher EIN3 transcription

    Reduced degradation can stabilize EIN3 with unchanged transcript.

    Why this trap works: A protein blot is easily read as a transcript assay. Compare protein turnover with RNA measurement.

  13. Short roots alone define the triple response

    The characteristic phenotype includes shoot thickening and exaggerated hook; controls exclude poor growth.

    Why this trap works: A single growth defect can mimic one response component. Use all three morphological traits and matched controls.

  14. Non-climacteric fruits lack ethylene

    They lack the classic ripening-associated climacteric pattern, not all hormone sensitivity.

    Why this trap works: Climacteric is a ripening classification rather than a biosynthesis knockout. Distinguish basal emission and autocatalytic rise.

  15. 1-MCP directly inhibits ACC oxidase

    It interferes with perception; biosynthesis may change secondarily via feedback.

    Why this trap works: An inhibitor that lowers gas later may appear enzymatic. Test perception and direct ACO activity separately.

  16. All petal senescence requires ethylene

    Flower species differ substantially in ethylene dependence.

    Why this trap works: A carnation example may be generalized to every flower. Identify species and test ethylene sensitivity.

  17. Abscission and senescence are synonyms

    Senescence is functional aging; abscission is localized controlled detachment.

    Why this trap works: Both may occur near organ death. Look for a specialized separation zone versus gradual metabolic decline.

  18. Waterlogging requires ethylene transport in xylem

    In the classic tomato example, ACC moves in xylem and is converted in shoots.

    Why this trap works: The transported signal is often named after the final hormone. Trace labeled ACC in sap and local ACO activity.

  19. Ethylene always inhibits elongation

    Rice escape growth provides a context-dependent counterexample.

    Why this trap works: The seedling assay is easily generalized to adult organs. Specify tissue, developmental stage and oxygen regime.

  20. A combined treatment larger than either alone proves synergy

    Compare an explicit additive or multiplicative null and account for variation.

    Why this trap works: A larger combined response is not a statistical interaction. Define the null model before comparing treatments.

Original CSIR-NET and SET practice

Part B — conceptual foundations

1. Which immediate precursor is oxidized to ethylene?

  1. SAM
  2. ACC
  3. MTA
  4. Methionine
Answer and explanation

B. ACO converts ACC to ethylene; SAM is the ACS substrate, MTA is salvaged, and methionine is upstream.

2. Which enzyme requires oxygen for the terminal step?

  1. ACC oxidase
  2. Methyltransferase
  3. ACC synthase
  4. Methionine adenosyltransferase
Answer and explanation

A. ACO uses oxygen during oxidation of ACC; ACS uses PLP and SAM, while methionine activation uses ATP.

3. Which cofactor is characteristic of ACC synthase?

  1. FAD
  2. Heme
  3. Pyridoxal phosphate
  4. Biotin
Answer and explanation

C. ACS is PLP-dependent; the other cofactors are not its characteristic requirement.

4. What is the principal role of the Yang cycle here?

  1. Phosphorylate EIN2
  2. Recycle the methylthio moiety into methionine
  3. Convert ethylene into ACC
  4. Transport gas in xylem
Answer and explanation

B. MTA produced by ACS is salvaged into methionine. The cycle does not recapture gas or control receptor phosphorylation directly.

5. Which is a conjugated ethylene precursor?

  1. Ethylene glycoside
  2. CTR1
  3. EIN2
  4. Malonyl-ACC
Answer and explanation

D. Malonyl-ACC is a metabolic derivative of ACC; the gas is not normally stored as a glycoside. EIN2 and CTR1 are signaling proteins.

6. Where are the canonical Arabidopsis ethylene receptors mainly located?

  1. Chloroplast thylakoid
  2. Nuclear matrix
  3. Endoplasmic reticulum membrane
  4. Mitochondrial matrix
Answer and explanation

C. The receptor family is primarily ER-associated; its signaling domains interact with CTR1 on the cytosolic side.

7. In air without ethylene, CTR1 normally does what?

  1. Phosphorylates and represses EIN2
  2. Activates EIN3 directly
  3. Degrades ACC
  4. Exports ethylene
Answer and explanation

A. Receptor-supported CTR1 activity inhibits EIN2. It is not a biosynthetic enzyme or the direct activator of EIN3.

8. Which proteins are transcriptional regulators downstream of EIN2?

  1. ACS/ACO
  2. ETR1/ERS1
  3. MTA/SAM
  4. EIN3/EIL1
Answer and explanation

D. EIN3 and EIL1 regulate transcription; ACS and ACO are enzymes, ETR1 and ERS1 are receptors, MTA and SAM metabolites.

9. Which genotype has a constitutive triple-response phenotype in the simplified pathway?

  1. ctr1 loss
  2. Dominant etr1-1
  3. Strong ACO loss
  4. Strong ein2 loss
Answer and explanation

A. CTR1 is a repressor. EIN2 loss and dominant insensitive receptor alleles reduce signaling; ACO loss reduces biosynthesis.

10. What is the classical triple response of a dark-grown seedling?

  1. Longer hypocotyl, thinner stem, absent hook
  2. Increased flowering, root hair loss, ripening
  3. Stomatal opening, leaf senescence, germination
  4. Shortened/thickened hypocotyl and exaggerated hook
Answer and explanation

D. The characteristic shoot phenotype is shortened, thickened growth and exaggerated hook; root elongation is also often reduced.

11. Which pair is conventionally climacteric?

  1. Grape and strawberry
  2. Grape and cherry
  3. Strawberry and citrus
  4. Tomato and banana
Answer and explanation

D. Tomato and banana undergo a typical ripening-associated ethylene and respiration rise; the other examples are conventionally non-climacteric.

12. System 2 ethylene production is best described as:

  1. Ethylene-independent respiration only
  2. Conjugation of gaseous ethylene
  3. Autocatalytic production during ripening of many climacteric fruits
  4. Complete absence of ACS
Answer and explanation

C. System 2 includes positive feedback promoting endogenous synthesis; basal system 1 is distinct.

13. 1-MCP primarily interferes with:

  1. Xylem water uptake
  2. Methionine synthesis
  3. Ethylene perception
  4. All respiratory electron transport
Answer and explanation

C. 1-MCP acts at ethylene receptors; indirect effects on production can follow feedback interruption.

14. Which statement about flower senescence is correct?

  1. All flowers are insensitive
  2. Dependence varies among species and stage
  3. Senescence means immediate organ abscission
  4. All flowers need ethylene for senescence
Answer and explanation

B. Carnation and petunia can be ethylene-sensitive, while iris and daylily provide comparatively insensitive examples.

15. The abscission zone primarily performs:

  1. Pollen meiosis
  2. Localized organ separation
  3. Carbon fixation
  4. Nitrogen fixation
Answer and explanation

B. Specialized cells at the attachment region alter adhesion and wall properties to permit detachment.

16. In waterlogged tomato, the major classical root-to-shoot precursor is:

  1. ACC
  2. EIN3 protein
  3. Malonyl-ethylene gas
  4. CTR1 kinase
Answer and explanation

A. ACC travels in xylem to oxygenated shoot tissue; the final gas is synthesized there by ACO.

17. Aerenchyma is important because it:

  1. Prevents every hormonal response
  2. Is identical to xylem sap
  3. Stores ethylene covalently
  4. Provides connected gas spaces aiding aeration
Answer and explanation

D. Gas spaces reduce internal diffusion resistance, provided an oxygen source exists.

18. Which comparison correctly separates a loss-of-function from a dominant insensitive receptor allele?

  1. Receptor genotype cannot affect sensitivity
  2. Both necessarily abolish all response
  3. Both necessarily cause excess synthesis
  4. Loss of receptor repression can enhance response; a persistent-repression allele can block it
Answer and explanation

D. Receptor redundancy and negative regulation explain why loss and persistent repression have distinct outcomes.

19. Which is a positive feedback example?

  1. ACO becoming oxygen-limited
  2. EIN3 degradation in air
  3. Ethylene inducing ACS and more ethylene in ripening fruit
  4. Ethylene diffusing away
Answer and explanation

C. The product promotes increased production. Diffusion and degradation are removal processes, and oxygen limitation restricts production.

20. What distinguishes ethylene from ACC in transport?

  1. Ethylene diffuses through gas spaces; ACC can travel in vascular sap
  2. Both require identical polar carriers
  3. ACC is gaseous; ethylene is an amino acid
  4. Neither can move between organs
Answer and explanation

A. The compounds differ physically; local gas diffusion and long-distance precursor movement can both contribute to communication.

Part C — analytical reasoning

21. A specific ACS-deficient seedling has normal receptors and ACO. Which treatment pattern restores response?

  1. Only gas
  2. Neither ACC nor gas
  3. ACC and gas
  4. Only ACC
Answer and explanation

C. ACC bypasses ACS and can be oxidized; gas bypasses both enzymes. Only-gas rescue is more consistent with a severe ACO block or unavailable ACC.

22. A seedling responds to gas but not ACC despite confirmed ACC uptake and oxygen. The most direct hypothesis is:

  1. Reduced ACO capacity
  2. No receptor binding
  3. Constitutively active CTR1
  4. Loss of EIN3
Answer and explanation

A. The gas bypasses ACO; intact gas responsiveness argues against a severe perception or downstream signaling defect.

23. A strong ctr1 loss is combined with strong ein2 loss. What is expected for the canonical triple response?

  1. More gas synthesis necessarily
  2. Wild-type response
  3. Constitutive triple response
  4. Insensitivity resembling ein2
Answer and explanation

D. EIN2 is required downstream of CTR1, so releasing the brake cannot restore a missing positive transducer.

24. A dominant insensitive receptor allele is combined with ctr1 loss. In the simple pathway the double phenotype is:

  1. Insensitive because receptor remains active
  2. Normal only after ACC
  3. No viable prediction despite specified simple pathway
  4. Constitutive response
Answer and explanation

D. Removal of downstream CTR1 bypasses persistent receptor-supported repression, assuming essential positive components function.

25. Headspace is 2 L; 0.40 μL/h is emitted for 3 h, initially zero, no loss. Concentration?

  1. 1.2 ppm
  2. 2.4 ppm
  3. 0.2 ppm
  4. 0.6 ppm
Answer and explanation

D. Amount = 0.40 × 3 = 1.2 μL; divide by 2 L = 0.60 μL/L, approximately 0.60 ppm by volume.

26. A chamber at steady state has P = 6 μL/h and Q = 3 L/h. Doubling Q with fixed P changes concentration to:

  1. 2 μL/L
  2. 1 μL/L
  3. 0.5 μL/L
  4. 4 μL/L
Answer and explanation

B. C=P/Q. Initially 2 μL/L; at 6 L/h the concentration is 1 μL/L. A fixed production assumption is specified.

27. With Vmax=12 and Km=20 μM, v at 40 μM ACC is:

  1. 8
  2. 2
  3. 4
  4. 6
Answer and explanation

A. v=12×40/(20+40)=8 in the units of Vmax. Substrate saturation makes the response nonlinear.

28. EIN3 synthesis stays 10 units/h; degradation k drops from 2 to 0.5 h⁻¹. New steady abundance?

  1. 20 units
  2. 5 units
  3. 10 units
  4. 40 units
Answer and explanation

A. At steady state N=s/k. It rises from 5 to 20 without requiring increased transcription.

29. Two genotypes have control/treated root lengths 20/12 mm and 10/8 mm. Which has larger relative inhibition?

  1. Second, 80%
  2. First, 40%
  3. Both 20%
  4. Second, because treated roots are shorter
Answer and explanation

B. First inhibition is (20−12)/20=40%; second (10−8)/10=20%. Baselines must be genotype matched.

30. Two 1-L chambers accumulate 2 and 3 μL/L in 2 h; fruit masses 0.1 and 0.3 kg. Which mass-specific rate is greater?

  1. Second, 3 versus 2
  2. Second, 15 μL/kg/h
  3. Equal
  4. First, 10 versus 5 μL/kg/h
Answer and explanation

D. Divide net amount by elapsed hours and fruit mass: 2/0.2=10; 3/0.6=5. Total emission and normalized emission differ.

31. 1-MCP reduces softening and later gas emission. What conclusion is justified?

  1. It proves respiration produces ethylene
  2. Perception blockade can reduce feedback-driven synthesis
  3. It directly inhibits ACO
  4. It destroys methionine
Answer and explanation

B. Blocking signal perception can interrupt autocatalysis. Direct ACO inhibition requires separate enzyme evidence.

32. A fruit retains an early ethylene reporter response but does not soften. Which hypothesis is strongest?

  1. No functional ethylene receptor
  2. Complete lack of gas binding
  3. No ACS activity necessarily
  4. Output-specific developmental defect
Answer and explanation

D. Early reporter activation shows perception and at least one signaling branch work. The missing endpoint could involve wall regulation or competence.

33. After flooding, root ACC and shoot ethylene rise. Shoot ACO is suppressed. Which result supports precursor movement?

  1. Reduced shoot ethylene despite available ACC
  2. No root ACC can exist
  3. Xylem gas becomes a glycoside
  4. Shoot ethylene must rise further
Answer and explanation

A. A local conversion block can leave imported precursor available but reduce gas output and epinasty.

34. A submerged tissue produces 1 unit/h while its gas loss falls from 1 to 0.2 unit/h initially. What happens immediately?

  1. Production becomes zero
  2. Concentration immediately falls
  3. Net accumulation initially increases by 0.8 unit/h
  4. Gas concentration stays constant
Answer and explanation

C. Net balance is production minus loss, initially 1−0.2=0.8. Loss may later change with the concentration gradient.

35. Rapid flood escape costs 18 carbohydrate units/day; quiescence costs 7. From 100 units with no assimilation, what is the consequence at 8 days?

  1. Only quiescence retains reserves under this model
  2. Both have exhausted reserves
  3. Escape leaves 44 units
  4. Quiescence must have exhausted reserves
Answer and explanation

A. Escape reserves last 100/18≈5.6 days; quiescence lasts 100/7≈14.3 days. The conclusion assumes no restored assimilation.

36. Reporter values control 1, ethylene 3, JA 4, combined 10. What is the additive expectation above baseline?

  1. 12
  2. 10
  3. 6
  4. 7
Answer and explanation

C. Add changes 2 and 3 to baseline 1, giving 6. The multiplicative expectation is 12; interaction depends on the justified model and variance.

37. A dominant receptor allele remains insensitive even with high ethylene. Why is deletion of several receptor genes not equivalent?

  1. Receptors synthesize oxygen
  2. Deletion turns ethylene into auxin
  3. Deletion must preserve repression
  4. Receptors normally repress the response, so deletion can release that brake
Answer and explanation

D. Persistent repression and loss of repression are opposite functional changes, especially when receptor redundancy is removed.

38. EIN3 protein increases while its mRNA is unchanged. Which additional experiment most directly tests stabilization?

  1. Measure xylem pressure
  2. Compare protein decay after controlled expression pulse
  3. Count stomata only
  4. Measure leaf color only
Answer and explanation

B. A decay time course tests turnover. Transcript abundance alone cannot distinguish changes in translation from altered degradation.

39. Root auxin-transport mutant retains an ethylene reporter but loses ethylene-induced root inhibition. Best interpretation?

  1. Ethylene is not perceived at all
  2. ACC cannot enter the plant
  3. All ethylene responses use auxin
  4. Auxin distribution contributes to this growth output downstream or in parallel
Answer and explanation

D. The upstream reporter shows signaling persists. The defective output implicates auxin-dependent growth regulation, without generalizing to all responses.

40. Separation force (N) control 1.2, ethylene 0.8, debladed 0.6, combined 0.2. Which statement is supported?

  1. Blade removal has no effect
  2. Effects are additive on this force scale
  3. Synergy is proven because 0.2 is smallest
  4. Ethylene has no effect

B. Ethylene reduces force by 0.4 in either blade condition, and blade removal reduces by 0.6 in either gas condition. Additivity does not establish mechanism.

Revision and mastery

Ethylene is a gaseous regulator made from methionine through SAM and ACC. ACS controls entry to the dedicated route; oxygen-dependent ACO releases ethylene. The Yang cycle salvages sulfur-containing material, while ACC conjugation and transport adjust precursor availability. Receptors and CTR1 repress the pathway in air. Ethylene reduces that repression, permitting EIN2-dependent accumulation of EIN3/EIL1 and gene regulation. Tissue competence, feedback and other hormones determine the physiological output.

Equations and assumptions

Sealed, well-mixed chamber: ΔC = Pt/V; ventilated steady state: C = P/Q for ethylene-free inflow and fixed emission. First-order protein turnover: N = s/k at steady state. Simple substrate saturation: v = Vmax S/(Km + S). Relative inhibition = (control − treated)/control × 100%, using matched baselines.
