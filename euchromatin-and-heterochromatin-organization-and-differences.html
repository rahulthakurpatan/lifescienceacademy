1. One genome, different physical environments

In a typical nucleated human somatic cell, the DNA molecules together contain billions of base pairs. Yet a cell does not read all of them at once. A liver cell and a skin cell generally possess the same inherited DNA sequences but make different sets of proteins. Even within one chromosome, neighbouring stretches of DNA may behave differently: one is readily contacted by regulatory proteins, another is packed into a dense domain. How can a molecule with the same chemical backbone support such different physical environments?

The answer begins with chromatin, the combination of nuclear DNA, histones and associated proteins. A chromosome is a continuous DNA molecule with its associated chromatin before replication, or two sister DNA molecules after replication. Euchromatin and heterochromatin are not alternative kinds of DNA double helix, and they are not separate chromosomes. They describe regional states of chromatin. A single chromosome can contain both states. In interphase, when most chromosomes are not individually resolved as metaphase structures, the variation in local packing becomes particularly conspicuous.

A cell needs both access and protection. Transcription requires proteins to recognize regulatory sequences and RNA polymerase to move along a DNA template. Replication requires copying machinery to gain access to every region before division. At the same time, unrestricted access to repeated sequences and mobile DNA can produce unwanted transcription or destabilize the genome. Chromatin therefore has to be organized selectively rather than uniformly compressed or uniformly exposed. The distinction between euchromatin and heterochromatin describes one major solution to this problem.

Three related properties must be kept separate from the beginning. Compaction describes how DNA and proteins occupy three-dimensional space. Accessibility describes how readily a particular molecule can physically reach and interact with a DNA segment. Transcription describes actual synthesis of RNA. These properties influence one another, but they are not identical measurements. A gene may be accessible without being transcribed because its activating factor is absent; a small region within a dense domain may be actively transcribed. The chapter develops these distinctions before applying them to experimental data.

2. How staining revealed two chromatin states

Long before investigators could sequence DNA or identify histone modifications, they could observe stained nuclei. In 1928 Emil Heitz introduced the distinction between regions that remained conspicuously condensed and regions that lost their obvious condensed appearance after mitosis. The name heterochromatin reflects the different staining behaviour of these regions, whereas euchromatin refers to the more typical, dispersed chromatin of the interphase nucleus. The original distinction was cytological: it was based on physical appearance, not on a direct assay of gene expression.

Consider a dividing cell followed from metaphase through telophase into interphase. During metaphase, chromosomes are generally condensed enough to be individually distinguished. As the daughter nuclei reform, most chromatin becomes less compact. Some regions retain a much denser appearance and can be seen as dark bodies or patches. This persistence is the crucial observation. The cell still contains the same chromosomal DNA after decondensation; the loss of a distinct dark image is not evidence that DNA has been degraded or removed. It reflects how the material is distributed.

The optical distinction is relative. If two regions contain the same number of DNA molecules but one occupies a smaller volume, that region may have more DNA per unit volume and appear more intensely stained under comparable conditions. However, actual staining also depends on dye chemistry, base composition, thickness, fixation, accessibility to the stain and the imaging method. DAPI, a commonly used fluorescent DNA stain, preferentially binds AT-rich sequences. A bright DAPI focus can therefore reflect both DNA concentration and sequence composition. A dark region in a bright-field image may correspond to a bright focus in a fluorescence image; the sign of the image contrast is not the definition of heterochromatin.

Classical chromosome banding provides another view. C-banding preferentially reveals constitutive heterochromatic regions in many chromosomes, often near centromeres. G-bands are a different pattern: dark G-bands often correspond to relatively AT-rich, gene-poor, late-replicating regions, but not every dark G-band is constitutive heterochromatin. The methods cannot be interchanged merely because each produces bands. A meaningful comparison requires knowing which preparation was used and what property the signal reports.

A condensed nucleolus or an apoptotic nucleus must not automatically be classified as heterochromatin simply because it is conspicuous. The nucleolus is a specialized nuclear compartment associated with ribosome production; condensed chromatin in a dying cell is part of a different biological process. A cytological description is useful as a first observation, but classification becomes more reliable when independent structural, molecular and functional evidence is available.

A related classical term is heteropycnosis, meaning a difference in the degree of chromosomal condensation or staining relative to surrounding material. Positive heteropycnosis describes a region that appears more condensed or more intensely stained than its neighbours at a particular stage. It is an observation of contrast, not an independent molecular explanation. A region may appear different at different stages, so a single image is less informative than a comparison across the cell cycle. In an examination, distinguish the observation that a chromosome segment is heteropycnotic from the inference that it forms a stable heterochromatic domain.

3. A common nucleosome foundation, different packing

The basic structural unit of most eukaryotic nuclear chromatin is the nucleosome. About 147 base pairs of DNA wrap around a core containing two molecules each of histones H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Linker DNA joins neighbouring core particles, and histone H1 can associate with the DNA near entry and exit sites. Both euchromatin and heterochromatin contain nucleosomes. The difference is not that one possesses histones while the other consists of naked DNA. Instead, their nucleosome arrangements, associated proteins and interactions between chromatin segments differ.

To see why this matters, imagine a transcription factor whose binding site is on a short DNA segment. When that segment lies on the outward-facing surface of a nucleosome or in exposed linker DNA, the factor may have a better opportunity to bind than when the site faces the histone core. DNA can also transiently unwrap from a nucleosome. Consequently, even DNA associated with histones is not completely inaccessible. The probability and duration of exposure depend on nucleosome position, DNA sequence, local protein interactions and other factors.

At the next scale, neighbouring nucleosomes can interact through histone tails and other chromatin proteins. Chromatin-associated proteins can also connect segments that are distant along the DNA sequence. If many such contacts stabilize a compact region, the average space available for large molecules to penetrate or bind particular sites may decrease. This does not require the DNA sequence to change. Conversely, reduced attractive contacts, repositioning of nucleosomes, or local nucleosome removal can expose sites. ATP-dependent chromatin remodelling proteins use energy to alter nucleosome arrangement; the detailed regulation of these proteins is studied with gene expression.

The familiar textbook sequence from a 10-nm nucleosome array to a regular 30-nm fibre and progressively larger coils is a useful historical model, but it must not be interpreted as a universal series of fixed structures in living nuclei. Chromatin in cells often forms irregular and variable assemblies. Euchromatin can be locally compact, and heterochromatin need not have one universal geometric arrangement. The useful comparison is relative organization and probability of access, not a rigid identification of one fibre diameter with one state.

The sequence of DNA and the state of its packaging are related but separable. Satellite-rich DNA often provides an environment favourable to heterochromatin; a region containing a functional gene may acquire a repressive state without changing its nucleotide sequence. An appropriate explanation therefore considers both the underlying sequence and the proteins and structural interactions that act on that sequence.

4. Euchromatin: accessible is not the same as active

Euchromatin is generally less densely organized during interphase than classical heterochromatin. Many gene-rich chromosomal arms contain extensive euchromatic regions. Regulatory sites in such regions are often more accessible to transcription factors, and active genes frequently occur in this environment. The physical organization makes transcription possible but does not guarantee it.

A gene may possess an accessible promoter, the region at which transcription machinery can assemble, yet produce little or no RNA. For example, a promoter may lack a necessary tissue-specific activator. A transcriptional repressor may be bound, or the cell may lack the physiological signal that activates a relevant factor. Such a gene can be silent without being embedded in a large heterochromatic domain. The distinction becomes important when comparing tissues: the absence of a particular RNA does not establish that its gene has entered heterochromatin.

Within a transcriptionally active euchromatic gene, nucleosomes still exist. Transcription machinery encounters them and their organization may change as RNA is synthesized. A promoter can show a region of low nucleosome occupancy while the body of the gene contains many nucleosomes. In this way, accessibility varies along a single gene. An average measurement across a large chromosome segment may conceal such small local differences.

Histone acetylation, particularly at active regulatory regions, is often associated with euchromatin. Acetylation of a lysine side chain reduces its positive charge and can change interactions between histones and negatively charged DNA or neighbouring nucleosomes. Acetylated residues can also recruit proteins that recognize them. This provides a physical connection between a chemical modification and local organization. Nevertheless, the presence of acetylation alone does not establish that a gene is transcribed, and a cell does not classify every acetylated nucleosome as a whole euchromatic domain.

Euchromatic regions in many organisms are relatively rich in unique sequences and protein-coding genes, but they also contain introns, noncoding regulatory DNA and repeated sequences. Gene density is a statistical feature of many such regions, not their defining chemical composition. A region can contain repeats yet remain accessible. Likewise, a gene-rich stretch can become repressed in certain cell types. The central principle is that the state reflects the current chromosomal environment rather than a permanent label derived exclusively from the sequence.

5. Heterochromatin: organized restriction and genome stability

Heterochromatin is a chromatin state commonly characterized by relatively dense organization, reduced access for many DNA-binding molecules and a lower average level of transcription than active euchromatin. It is not inactive waste. A chromosome contains many sequences whose function is structural or whose transcription and mobility need to be restrained. The organization of heterochromatin helps a cell achieve that restraint.

Pericentromeric heterochromatin provides an important example. A centromere is the chromosomal region where the kinetochore assembles, enabling chromosome attachment to spindle microtubules. The surrounding pericentromeric regions often contain substantial tandemly repeated DNA. Specialized chromatin in these flanking regions contributes to the chromosome's structural behaviour, including sister-chromatid cohesion in many organisms. The functional centromere itself has specialized nucleosomes containing a centromere-associated histone H3 variant, CENP-A in humans. It should not be equated with the neighbouring heterochromatin simply because the regions lie close together.

Repetitive DNA gives rise to a special challenge. If numerous similar sequences become freely available as recombination substrates, incorrect alignment between copies may lead to unwanted rearrangements. Many repetitive regions also contain fragments or copies of transposable elements. Restraining their transcription and mobility helps limit genome instability. Heterochromatin provides an environment that can reduce these risks while retaining the DNA as a permanent part of the chromosome. The actual consequences depend on the organism, sequence and chromatin machinery; packaging is one contributor, not a complete guarantee against recombination or transposition.

A telomere consists of specialized terminal DNA and associated proteins that protect a natural chromosome end. Chromatin adjacent to telomeres can exhibit repressive or heterochromatin-like properties. But telomere protection is not just a consequence of compaction: specific end-binding proteins and the chromosome end structure are essential. The extent of subtelomeric heterochromatin also differs between organisms and loci. A location near a chromosome end is therefore useful supporting evidence, not sufficient classification.

Heterochromatin can still contain genes. In Drosophila melanogaster, for instance, the light and rolled genes reside within or near constitutive heterochromatic regions and require an appropriate chromosomal context for normal expression. The proteins needed for these genes to function can gain access locally even when the broader domain is compact. This is not a contradiction: the average property of a large region does not dictate the exact state of every base pair. It also explains why globally disrupting heterochromatin can impair expression of some genes rather than uniformly activate every gene.

6. Constitutive and facultative heterochromatin

The distinction between these forms concerns the persistence and biological context of the state, not two different chemical backbones. Constitutive heterochromatin is characteristically maintained at particular chromosomal regions in many cell types of an organism. It is commonly rich in repeated DNA, especially pericentromeric satellite arrays. Its organization is usually restored after DNA replication and cell division. Constitutive does not mean that its nucleosomes never move or that all its DNA is permanently unreachable. Rather, its regional identity is comparatively stable.

Facultative heterochromatin forms when chromosomal regions adopt a repressed, often compact state in a particular cellular or developmental context. Those same regions may be relatively accessible in other cells, or at a different developmental stage. The underlying DNA can contain ordinary functional genes. One example is the inactive X chromosome in most somatic cells of a typical XX placental mammal. The two X chromosomes have homologous regions, yet one becomes largely repressed. The distinction is primarily one of state, not deletion of genes.

Imagine three nuclei from the same organism. A satellite-rich pericentromeric region appears condensed in all three. A developmental gene cluster is comparatively compact in two tissues but accessible and active in the third. The first observation suggests constitutive heterochromatin. The second is consistent with facultative heterochromatin, provided other evidence supports formation of a regional repressive state. A single untranscribed gene in the first two tissues, without evidence of altered chromatin organization, would not establish facultative heterochromatin.

The distinction also explains how chromatin can be stable and flexible at the same time. A region may retain its organization through many rounds of cell division once its state has been established. Yet a developmental change can alter the environment that maintains the state, permitting a region to become accessible. Facultative does not mean that chromatin changes randomly every minute or that each cell must switch the region between active and inactive states. Some facultative states, including X inactivation, are remarkably stable within a differentiated cell lineage.

Constitutive and facultative domains often differ in characteristic histone modifications. H3K9me3, meaning trimethylation of lysine 9 on histone H3, is commonly enriched in constitutive heterochromatin in many animals. H3K27me3, trimethylation of lysine 27 on H3, is often associated with facultative repressive domains. These are useful exam-level associations, not universal signatures: organisms differ, and domains may carry several marks or show different organization. Histone methylation is not always repressive; its consequence depends on the modified residue and molecular context.

7. Establishing and maintaining a regional state

Why does a dense chromatin patch remain a patch rather than spread indefinitely throughout a chromosome? The answer requires distinguishing initial recruitment, reinforcement, and limits. Particular DNA sequences, RNA molecules or sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins can favour recruitment of chromatin-associated factors at a site. Once a site has been modified or bound by a protein, that local environment can recruit additional factors. Such interactions can stabilize neighbouring nucleosomes and create a region with properties different from its surroundings.

A classical example of this general principle involves methylated H3K9 and heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1). A histone methyltransferase can add methyl groups to lysine 9 of histone H3. Certain HP1 proteins recognize this modified residue using a binding domain called a chromodomain. HP1 can interact with additional chromatin proteins and can help establish contacts among chromatin components. When the factors recruited in this environment include proteins that generate or maintain compatible histone modifications, the initial state can be reinforced. The causal sequence is recognition of a local feature, recruitment of compatible proteins, and stabilization of a larger domain. This is a simplified structural explanation, not a claim that one HP1 mechanism describes every heterochromatic region.

Spread is not inevitable. Some neighbouring sequences recruit factors associated with more accessible chromatin; particular boundaries can restrict propagation, and the balance of modification, removal and protein binding may differ across a chromosome. A boundary is therefore a functional transition where the ability to sustain a state changes. It need not be a rigid physical wall. The same initial site may produce different extents of repressive chromatin in different cells. This principle becomes visible experimentally in position-effect variegation.

Replication introduces another challenge. A DNA polymerase cannot copy the chromosomal template through an unchanged, permanently closed nucleosome arrangement. During replication, nucleosomes are disrupted and histones are redistributed or newly deposited on the daughter DNA. Parental histones and other local features can contribute to rebuilding the chromatin environment. Proteins that recognize existing marks can help recruit machinery that restores characteristic properties near them. DNA sequence and continued recruitment also help restore regional identity. This is one way a physical organization may persist through cell generations without being encoded as a change in DNA sequence.

It is tempting to conclude that a single histone mark is both necessary and sufficient to create every heterochromatin state. That conclusion would exceed the evidence. Marks are useful indicators and may participate in formation, but a modification can appear in a region without reproducing all properties of a mature domain. Chromatin organization results from several interacting processes. The detailed establishment of epigenetic marks and silencing pathways belongs to Unit 3; here the important structural idea is that recruitment, local reinforcement, inheritance and boundaries explain why regional states are persistent yet potentially changeable.

It is also important to distinguish DNA methylation from histone methylation. DNA methylation changes a chemical group on a DNA base, commonly cytosine in many vertebrate contexts. Histone methylation modifies an amino acid in a protein. Neither changes the base sequence itself. In many mammalian constitutive heterochromatic regions, DNA methylation and repressive histone marks can coexist and help maintain a restrictive environment, but they are not interchangeable. Some organisms maintain heterochromatin with little or no conventional DNA methylation. An experiment measuring DNA methylation cannot therefore be interpreted as a direct measurement of H3K9me3. Likewise, not every methylated cytosine is a marker of heterochromatin; methylation can occur in expressed gene bodies. What matters is the site, the molecular context and the other properties of the region.

8. The Barr body: a visible facultative state

In 1949 Murray Barr and Ewart Bertram described a strongly staining nuclear body in neurons of female cats that was usually absent in male cells. Later work connected this structure with an inactive X chromosome. Mary Lyon proposed in 1961 that one X chromosome in female mammals becomes inactivated early in development and that the choice is maintained in descendant cells. This explained why genetically heterozygous animals could display patches of different coat colour instead of a uniform intermediate appearance.

In the usual XX mammalian cell, two X chromosomes carry many genes for which excessive expression from both copies would disturb the balance of gene products relative to typical XY cells. X chromosome inactivation provides a major dosage-compensation mechanism in placental mammals. In typical embryonic somatic lineages, one X becomes largely inactive while the other remains active. XIST, a long noncoding RNA, associates predominantly with the chromosome that produces it. The RNA and recruited factors contribute to a repressive chromatin environment on that same chromosome. The structural result is a largely compact inactive X, often identifiable as a Barr body in interphase nuclei.

The inactive X is still a chromosome. It retains its DNA, must be replicated, and is inherited by daughter cells. The condensation is not the loss of an X chromosome, and it does not mean that the two X chromosomes have fused. Many genes are repressed, but some escape inactivation, including many genes in the pseudoautosomal regions that have corresponding copies on X and Y. Escape varies among genes, tissues and individuals. Thus the inactive X is a compelling example of a regional repressive state that nevertheless has local exceptions.

When the maternal and paternal X carry different alleles of an X-linked gene that is subject to inactivation, different cells may express different copies depending on the chromosome that remains active. Descendants of a cell tend to maintain its choice, producing patches of related cells. This is cellular mosaicism caused by different expression states, not necessarily mosaicism in chromosome number or DNA sequence. The distribution can be skewed by chance or selection; an equal number of cells with each active X is not guaranteed.

In conventional exam problems, a typical differentiated human somatic cell with N X chromosomes and one active X has N − 1 inactive X chromosomes. Therefore an XX cell has one, an XY cell has zero, and an XXX cell has two. A typical XXY cell also has one, while a typical 45,X cell has zero. The arithmetic is about X number, not the presence of a Y chromosome. It assumes one active X and usual inactivation; early embryonic cells, germ cells, atypical inactivation and technical visibility can invalidate a literal microscopic count. Not every inactive X will necessarily be separately visible in every preparation.

The Barr body is called facultative heterochromatin because a homologous X can be active in other cells and the inactive state is developmentally established. Its persistence in adult tissues does not convert it into constitutive heterochromatin. The decisive distinction is the capacity of essentially similar chromosomal material to occupy different functional and organizational states depending on developmental context.

The identity of the Barr body can be investigated independently of staining. In a conceptual chromosome-identification experiment, a fluorescent DNA probe complementary to an X-specific sequence can locate X-chromosomal material in an interphase nucleus. Comparing the position of the X signals with the condensed nuclear body helps establish its chromosomal origin. A matched XX cell provides two X signals; an XY control usually provides one. A separate RNA probe for XIST can identify the chromosome associated with that RNA in a suitable somatic cell. If a dense focus colocalizes with one X signal and XIST RNA, the combined evidence supports its identification as the inactive X. DNA hybridization identifies a chromosome, however, whereas RNA localization and expression measurements provide evidence about state. No single probe establishes that all genes across that chromosome are silent.

9. Replication timing and the cell-cycle distinction

DNA replication occurs during S phase. In eukaryotic chromosomes replication does not begin at all sites simultaneously. Instead, different chromosomal domains tend to be copied at different times. In many mammalian cells active, relatively accessible domains replicate early in S phase, while much constitutive heterochromatin and the inactive X replicate later. This pattern is one of the traditional contrasts between the two chromatin states.

To understand the distinction, imagine two DNA regions, A and B, and two groups of synchronized cells. Group one receives a short pulse of a labelled nucleotide analogue near the beginning of S phase; group two receives the same pulse later. DNA synthesized during each pulse incorporates the label. If A is preferentially labelled early and B late, the data suggest that A and B tend to replicate at different times. The result does not show that B is absent during early S phase. B was present throughout, but synthesis of new DNA there occurred predominantly later.

This experiment measures timing, not the speed of a single replication fork. A late signal can reflect late initiation at available origins or the arrival of forks from elsewhere. To conclude that a particular region replicates slowly, one would need additional information about the distance copied and duration of fork movement. It is equally incorrect to assume that all late-replicating DNA finishes after mitosis: a normal cell must complete genome duplication before division. A late domain is still replicated within S phase.

Chromatin organization and replication timing are associated through multiple features, including access to initiation machinery and the broader organization of domains. Neither property is an unconditional predictor of the other. The replication timing of some regions changes with cell type and differentiation. Not all euchromatic domains replicate early, and not every heterochromatic domain replicates late; specific examples differ between organisms and cell types. A single timing observation is not sufficient to classify chromatin.

During mitosis the distinction based on interphase compaction is harder to recognize because both euchromatic and heterochromatic regions undergo chromosome-scale condensation. A gene that was active in interphase does not become constitutive heterochromatin merely because its chromosome is condensed at metaphase. Global changes in transcription and condensation during mitosis should be distinguished from the regional identity of chromatin. On return to interphase, euchromatic domains generally regain a relatively dispersed organization while many constitutive heterochromatic domains remain or become relatively compact again.

10. Position-effect variegation: sequence is not the whole explanation

In a classical genetic experiment, a gene can be moved to a new chromosomal environment by a rearrangement. If the sequence of the gene remains intact but its expression changes, the result is evidence of a position effect. One particularly instructive form is position-effect variegation, or PEV, investigated in Drosophila. The normal white gene participates in the production of eye pigment. A functional white allele in its usual euchromatic environment contributes to normal red pigmentation. A defective allele can produce white eyes.

In the classical white-mottled rearrangement, an inversion brings the white gene near heterochromatin. Instead of producing one uniform colour, the eye may show red and white patches. A simple mutation that destroyed the gene in all cells would not readily account for red patches in the same eye. The gene is potentially functional, but in some cell lineages its expression is reduced or silenced by its new chromosomal environment. In lineages where the gene remains active, pigmentation develops. The result is a visible mosaic.

An explanatory model is that heterochromatin can extend into neighbouring DNA to different extents in different cells. If the local repressive environment reaches the relocated gene, expression is reduced. If it does not, the gene may remain active. The state can persist during subsequent divisions, so neighbouring descendant cells produce similarly coloured patches. Variation across the tissue can therefore emerge from the behaviour of chromatin in genetically similar cells rather than recurrent mutations of the pigment gene.

Investigators can test this interpretation by comparing the rearranged line to a normal-position control with a functional white gene and to a loss-of-function white control. Sequencing or genetic analysis can check that the functional coding sequence was not destroyed. Mutations affecting heterochromatin proteins can then be tested for their effects on the variegated phenotype. A mutation weakening repressive chromatin often suppresses PEV, increasing red pigmentation in this model. A mutation favouring repression can enhance PEV, increasing white areas. The direction depends on the precise gene and perturbation; the labels suppressor and enhancer refer to variegation, not directly to the amount of pigment.

The controls matter because eye pigmentation depends on more than white-gene expression. A mutation affecting a separate pigment pathway could alter colour without changing chromatin. Direct examination of the gene's expression and chromatin state strengthens the interpretation. An experimental result in which a relocated reporter acquires repressive marks and becomes less accessible is more informative than a change in colour alone. The classic work and later studies of PEV establish that chromosomal environment matters, but a specific rearrangement by itself does not distinguish every mechanism that might affect the relocated locus.

The reverse situation is equally instructive. Studies of the Drosophila light and rolled genes found that displacement from their normal heterochromatic environment can interfere with normal expression. If heterochromatin were simply an inert mass of completely inactive DNA, this result would be difficult to explain. The regional environment is adapted to the genes that normally occupy it. For examination reasoning, ask which gene is being moved, where it originated, and what environment it needs. Do not assume that moving any gene into euchromatin necessarily increases its expression.

11. Chromatin within the three-dimensional nucleus

Interphase chromosomes occupy spatially organized territories rather than floating as perfectly mixed DNA molecules. Different parts of a chromosome can occupy different nuclear environments. In many differentiated animal cells, dense heterochromatic regions are frequently observed near the inner nuclear membrane or around the nucleolus. Regions associated with the nuclear lamina, a supporting network beneath the inner nuclear membrane, are often relatively gene-poor and transcriptionally repressed.

Position within the nucleus is related to chromatin state but is not a definition of it. An interior domain can be heterochromatic, and a peripheral gene can be accessible or active. Nuclear architecture also differs between cell types. Some cells display arrangements quite different from the familiar peripheral heterochromatin pattern. The appropriate conclusion from a peripheral location is that it is consistent with a common arrangement, not proof of a particular functional state.

The distinction between linear position and spatial proximity is particularly important. Two sequences can be far apart along a chromosome but close together in the folded nucleus. Conversely, adjacent domains along the same chromosome can occupy distinct local environments. Chromatin folding and contacts help bring similar regions together, potentially reinforcing differences between domains. No universal radial rule can describe all chromosomal material.

A biological example is cellular differentiation. As a cell develops a specialized function, its pattern of regulatory proteins and chromatin organization changes. Some previously accessible regions become repressed, while others become available. The rearrangement may be accompanied by changes in nuclear position, replication timing or histone marks. A comparison of two cell types at the same DNA locus can thus reveal how different cellular environments support different states without requiring a DNA mutation. Establishing which change causes the others requires perturbation experiments, not merely a comparison of two images.

12. Testing accessibility, compaction and expression independently

A powerful strategy for studying chromatin is to measure several distinct properties rather than rely on a single indicator. Microscopy can reveal dense domains and their nuclear location. A nuclease, an enzyme that cuts nucleic acid, can test how easily DNA is contacted. An antibody against a modified histone can reveal whether a marker is enriched. RNA measurements can test whether particular genes are expressed. Each assay asks a different question, and together they can provide a much stronger interpretation.

Consider a simplified DNase I accessibility assay. Equivalent amounts of nuclei from two cell populations are exposed to the same amount of enzyme for the same time. DNase I can cut exposed DNA more readily than DNA that is shielded by chromatin proteins. After digestion, investigators examine how much intact DNA remains at a chosen locus. A locus that is digested more extensively under comparable conditions is generally more accessible to the nuclease. In a well-designed experiment, undigested samples establish starting DNA amounts, known accessible and protected sites provide controls, and a naked-DNA comparison can help identify intrinsic differences in cleavage susceptibility. Excessive digestion would erase useful differences by cutting even relatively protected material.

Reduced nuclease sensitivity does not necessarily mean large-scale heterochromatin. A single DNA-bound protein or a positioned nucleosome can protect a short segment. Conversely, an accessible site can exist within a mostly compact domain. Accessibility is a local property measured with a particular probe under particular conditions. When a larger region is being classified, measurements at multiple representative sites are more informative than a single protected sequence.

A complementary approach is immunodetection of histone marks. An antibody specific for H3K9me3 can identify regions where that modification is enriched, provided specificity and background are adequately controlled. Chromatin immunoprecipitation, at the conceptual level, isolates DNA associated with a chosen protein or histone modification using an antibody. Comparing the recovered DNA with input DNA and appropriate negative controls estimates relative enrichment. Strong enrichment of H3K9me3 at a repeat-rich region supports classification as constitutive heterochromatin in a suitable system. It does not directly measure three-dimensional density or prove that each gene in the region is silent.

RNA abundance provides functional evidence, but it too requires care. The amount of RNA detected depends on synthesis and degradation. Low steady-state RNA can arise because a transcript is rapidly degraded even if its gene is transcribed. A measurement of newly synthesized RNA is closer to a direct estimate of transcription. For a gene with little RNA but high accessibility, the appropriate conclusion is that accessibility alone has not ensured accumulation of RNA. It would be premature to call the gene heterochromatic without structural evidence.

An integrated comparison might examine a repeat-rich domain that is dense in microscopy, enriched for H3K9me3, relatively nuclease-resistant and copied late in S phase. These observations reinforce one another. A different domain might be accessible, contain an inactive gene, and replicate early. Its lack of RNA alone is outweighed by the evidence of an accessible environment. In a third case, a compact region might produce a particular RNA. Rather than discard the data as contradictory, test whether the RNA originates from a locally accessible gene embedded within the dense region. The scale and method of measurement govern the meaning of the result.

A useful perturbation experiment separates the consequences of removing a chromatin-associated protein from the effects of changing the DNA sequence. Imagine comparing a control cell population with one in which a protein involved in maintaining a repeat-rich domain has been depleted. Measure the same DNA loci in both populations, normalize for DNA input, and check whether cells have similar cell-cycle distributions. If the region loses a characteristic mark, becomes more nuclease-sensitive and produces more repeat-derived RNA, the combined observations support a role for that protein in maintaining a restrictive state. Restoring the protein and observing recovery of these features would strengthen the inference. If the cells instead enter a different phase of the cell cycle or lose chromosomes, those changes could affect the measurements independently. The experiment does not demonstrate that the protein alone is sufficient to form heterochromatin, because other cellular factors remain present.

13. Quantitative reasoning with biological measurements

There is no single mathematical equation defining euchromatin or heterochromatin. Nevertheless, simple quantitative relationships help interpret real types of observations. In all the following worked examples, the numbers are hypothetical teaching data, not published experimental measurements.

First consider a physical density model. Let N be the number of base pairs in a region and V its effective occupied volume, measured in cubic micrometres (µm³). The average DNA density ρ equals N/V, in base pairs per µm³. Suppose two equal-sized regions each contain 600,000 bp. If A occupies 0.12 µm³ and B occupies 0.04 µm³, their densities are 600,000/0.12 = 5,000,000 bp/µm³ and 600,000/0.04 = 15,000,000 bp/µm³. B has three times the average DNA density of A. Under a simplified linear, equally efficient DNA stain, average fluorescence per unit volume could be threefold higher in B, while total integrated fluorescence would be equal. This conclusion assumes equal DNA content and comparable staining chemistry; intensity alone cannot be used as a universal density measurement.

Next consider nuclease digestion. If I₀ is a normalized intact-DNA signal before digestion and I is the signal after digestion, the fraction digested is (I₀ − I)/I₀. Multiplication by 100 converts the fraction to a percentage. For region A, an initial signal of 100 and final signal of 25 gives (100 − 25)/100 × 100 = 75% digested. For B, 100 and 80 give 20% digested. A is more sensitive under these assay conditions. The ratio of digested fractions is 0.75/0.20 = 3.75; it is not a 3.75-fold measure of transcription, compaction or intrinsic enzymatic rate. The assay is an endpoint comparison.

In a third example, suppose comparable early and late S-phase pulses yield 72 and 18 normalized label units at locus A, and 16 and 64 units at B. One simple early-label fraction is E/(E + L), where E and L are the normalized early and late signals. For A the fraction is 72/90 = 0.80, while for B it is 16/80 = 0.20. Under the stated pulse assumptions A is preferentially labelled early and B late. The total signals differ, so comparing raw early signals alone could be misleading. These fractions are not exact percentages of the DNA replicated across the entire S phase unless the experimental design samples the whole relevant interval with appropriate normalization.

Finally, suppose a variegated eye has 150 scored red cells and 50 white cells in a simplified binary model. The proportion classified as red is 150/(150 + 50) = 0.75. In a matched modifier line, 180 of 200 scored cells are red, giving 0.90. The red fraction has increased by 15 percentage points, or by (0.90 − 0.75)/0.75 × 100 = 20% relative to its original value. Assuming pigment faithfully reports expression and sampling is comparable, the result is consistent with reduced silencing. It does not show that transcription increased by 20% in each active cell. It is a change in the fraction of cells classified as active. Real PEV patches may contain related cells and therefore cannot automatically be treated as statistically independent samples.

14. Putting the evidence together and avoiding false rules

Euchromatin and heterochromatin are best understood as contrasting regional organizations of a shared chromatin material. They represent useful biological categories because the differences have consequences for accessibility, structural stability and the use of genomic information. Neither term should be reduced to one characteristic. A stain reports a physical or chemical signal; a histone mark reports enrichment of a molecular feature; an RNA assay reports an aspect of gene activity; replication labelling reports the time at which new DNA is synthesized. The properties often correlate, but their agreement is not guaranteed.

When deciding whether a region is constitutive or facultative heterochromatin, compare equivalent loci across relevant cell types or developmental states. Stable pericentromeric repeat-rich chromatin in many tissues is a strong example of the constitutive form. A chromosome region that becomes repressed during differentiation yet is accessible in another cell type is consistent with the facultative form. The inactive X exemplifies this second category even when its state is faithfully inherited for many cell generations.

A region cannot be assumed to lack genes simply because it is dense. Heterochromatin may contain genes whose expression depends on the surrounding environment, and some sequences within otherwise repressed domains may be locally accessible. Similarly, euchromatic DNA is not uniformly expressed. An accessible but inactive promoter can lack its required activator. Methylated histones need not always repress; the residue that is modified is crucial. Late replication is a tendency, not a mandatory definition. Chromosomes condense during mitosis without becoming uniformly constitutive heterochromatin.

A useful examination strategy is to identify what has been measured, the scale of that measurement and the assumptions linking it to a chromatin state. Then ask whether an alternative explanation remains possible. A change in DNA sequence is one explanation of reduced gene expression, but position-effect experiments demonstrate a different possibility: the same potentially functional gene may behave differently when its local chromosomal environment changes. A chromatin measurement alone does not establish causal direction, but changing a chromatin-associated factor while holding other relevant variables constant can help test causation.

The next conceptual question follows naturally from the role of repeated DNA in heterochromatin. Some genomic repeats arise from mobile genetic elements. Understanding the structural differences between DNA transposons and retrotransposons will explain why their movement can change the organization of a chromosome and why restraining their activity matters. The mechanisms of transcriptional and epigenetic control will be developed separately in Unit 3. The foundation here is the ability to distinguish physical organization, molecular access and actual gene activity, and to reason about how a stable genome supports different functional states.

15. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original examination-style questions. Choose the best answer before opening the explanation.

1. Which statement best defines the distinction?

  1. Different DNA backbones
  2. Regional chromatin organization
  3. Histones only in heterochromatin
  4. Different chromosome numbers
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: B. Both states contain DNA and histones; their regional organization differs. A confuses DNA chemistry, C incorrectly removes histones from euchromatin, and D confuses packaging with chromosome count.

2. A gene is accessible but no transcript is detected. What follows?

  1. It must be heterochromatic
  2. Its DNA was deleted
  3. Accessibility need not ensure transcription
  4. All nucleosomes were removed
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: C. An activator may be absent or RNA may be degraded. A is unsupported, B requires sequence evidence, and D is not implied by accessibility.

3. Which is a standard example of facultative heterochromatin?

  1. Inactive mammalian X
  2. All centromeres
  3. Bacterial nucleoid
  4. Every metaphase chromosome
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. The X is developmentally inactivated but an equivalent chromosome can remain active. B conflates specialized centromeric chromatin with a state, C lacks the relevant eukaryotic nucleosome organization, and D confuses mitotic compaction with regional identity.

4. Which histone mark is commonly enriched in animal constitutive heterochromatin?

  1. H3K9me3
  2. Every histone acetylation
  3. H3K4me3 at active promoters
  4. None of the histones can be modified
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. H3K9me3 is a common association, not a universal criterion. B and C are often linked to active chromatin; D contradicts known histone modifications.

5. What does a late S-phase label primarily indicate?

  1. DNA was absent in G1
  2. DNA synthesis occurred later
  3. The chromosome never replicates
  4. The fork moved more slowly
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: B. The pulse reports incorporation time. A and C contradict DNA retention and genome replication. D requires independent distance and time measurements.

6. Assertion: heterochromatic domains can contain expressed genes. Reason: accessibility can vary locally within a dense region.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. Local differences allow access to individual loci within a dense domain. B misses this direct explanation; C and D incorrectly deny one of the statements.

7. A bright DAPI focus necessarily proves which property?

  1. High transcription
  2. No genes
  3. H3K27 methylation
  4. None of these
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: D. DAPI signal depends on DNA concentration and AT preference. A, B and C require independent functional or molecular evidence.

8. What is the usual Barr body count in a typical XXY somatic cell?

  1. Zero
  2. One
  3. Two
  4. Three
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: B. With one active X, two X chromosomes leave one inactive X. A treats Y as preventing inactivation; C and D count extra or all chromosomes incorrectly.

9. Which statement correctly distinguishes DNA methylation from H3K9 methylation?

  1. Both modify DNA bases
  2. Both alter DNA base order
  3. The first modifies DNA; the second modifies a histone residue
  4. They are identical measures of chromatin density
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: C. DNA methylation modifies a base chemically; H3K9 methylation modifies a lysine on a protein. A and B confuse substrates and sequence, while D confuses chemical marks with physical density.

10. Which description of the inactive X is most accurate?

  1. Its DNA is absent
  2. All genes on it are invariably silent
  3. It can contain expressed escape genes
  4. It cannot replicate
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: C. Escape genes are a well-established exception. A and D are incompatible with chromosome inheritance, and B incorrectly universalizes repression.

16. Part C — Experimental and quantitative reasoning

Original examination-style questions. Choose the best answer before opening the explanation.

1. A reporter moves from euchromatin to near pericentromeric heterochromatin. Its coding sequence remains intact. Some cells express it and others do not. Which is the best model?

  1. All cells lost the gene
  2. Variable regional repression
  3. Every cell has the same transcription rate
  4. DNA replication is permanently blocked
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: B. Local state and its extent can differ between lineages. A is ruled out by intact DNA, C contradicts the observed expression pattern, and D would threaten cell division and is not demonstrated.

2. A modifier increases red cells in a white-mottled fly from 40% to 70%. What is the strongest conclusion under a binary reporter model?

  1. Fraction expressing white rises by 30 percentage points
  2. Each cell transcribes 30% more
  3. The white locus has been deleted
  4. Repression has increased
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. 70 − 40 = 30 percentage points; relative increase is 75%. B confuses cell fraction and expression per cell, C contradicts restored pigment, and D predicts more silencing, not less.

3. Two equal DNA regions occupy 0.2 and 0.05 µm³. Assuming equivalent staining, what density ratio follows?

  1. 1:1
  2. Second is twice as dense
  3. Second is four times as dense
  4. Second has four times the total DNA
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: C. Density is N/V. The ratio is 0.2/0.05 = 4. A ignores different volumes; B is an arithmetic error; D confuses total DNA with density.

4. Equal starting DNA signals are 120 for each region. After digestion A is 30 and B is 90. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. A is 75% digested, B 25%
  2. A is 25%, B 75%
  3. B is threefold more transcribed
  4. A has no nucleosomes
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. A loses 90/120 = 75%, B loses 30/120 = 25%. B reverses intact and lost fractions. C substitutes transcription for sensitivity. D is not established by enzyme cleavage.

5. Early and late normalized label signals are (30, 70) for locus P and (60, 40) for Q. What can be inferred?

  1. P tends later than Q
  2. P necessarily has a slower fork
  3. Q is certainly euchromatic
  4. P cannot be expressed
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. Early fractions are 0.30 and 0.60. B requires fork measurements; C and D are unjustified absolute classifications based on timing alone.

6. A developmental locus is H3K27me3-enriched and compact in one tissue, but accessible and expressed in another. Its DNA sequence is unchanged. Best explanation?

  1. Constitutive deletion
  2. Facultative chromatin state
  3. Different DNA bases
  4. All euchromatin is transcriptionally active
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: B. The state varies with cellular context. A and C contradict the sequence evidence; D is a false generalization.

7. An experimental mutation reduces H3K9 methylation, activates a nearby reporter but reduces expression of a native heterochromatic gene. How should this be interpreted?

  1. The native gene cannot exist
  2. Data prove all marks are irrelevant
  3. Different loci may depend differently on chromosomal environment
  4. All genes should become active equally
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: C. A displaced euchromatic reporter can benefit from loss of repression while a native gene can lose a supportive environment. A contradicts the observation, B overgeneralizes, and D assumes uniform responses.

8. A dense nuclear region is H3K9me3-enriched and late-replicating but contains an expressed gene. Which additional observation resolves the apparent conflict?

  1. Local accessibility at the expressed locus
  2. Loss of all DNA from the region
  3. Uniform silence at every site
  4. Absence of replication in the entire nucleus
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. An accessible local site may coexist with a broadly dense environment. B and D contradict normal genome function, and C contradicts the measured expression.

9. An XX heterozygote has two different alleles of an X-linked gene subject to inactivation. Adjacent cell patches express distinct alleles. Best explanation?

  1. Stable lineage-specific active X choice
  2. Alternation of DNA sequence in every cell cycle
  3. Two Barr bodies in each cell
  4. Loss of every paternal X
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. Different cells can retain different active X choices through divisions. B requires unnecessary mutations, C would imply both Xs inactive under the usual model, and D confuses inactivation with chromosome loss.

10. An accessible locus has low steady-state RNA. Which additional measurement most directly distinguishes reduced synthesis from rapid RNA loss?

  1. DAPI brightness
  2. Newly synthesized RNA at the locus
  3. Chromosome count
  4. Centromere position
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: B. Nascent RNA measures synthesis more directly than accumulated RNA. A measures stain signal, C chromosome number and D morphology; none directly reports synthesis.

11. A protein depletion makes pericentromeric DNA more nuclease-sensitive and increases repeat RNA. Restoring the protein reverses both effects. What is supported?

  1. The protein contributes to maintenance of a restrictive state
  2. The protein alone is sufficient for all heterochromatin
  3. The repeat DNA must have changed sequence
  4. All cells must have entered mitosis
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: A. A reversible perturbation supports a functional maintenance role. B is stronger than the evidence because other factors are present; C is not required; D is not shown and would need cell-cycle data.

12. A compact nuclear body colocalizes with an X-specific DNA probe and XIST RNA. A gene on that X produces RNA. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. The body cannot be an inactive X
  2. The gene may escape X inactivation
  3. The DNA probe measures protein abundance
  4. The whole X must be fully active
Show answer and reasoning

Answer and reasoning: B. DNA and RNA localization support identification of the inactive X; local expression is compatible with escape. A and D turn a local exception into an unjustified chromosome-wide conclusion. C confuses the probe target.
