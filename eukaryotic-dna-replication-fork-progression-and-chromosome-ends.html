1. The copying problem in a eukaryotic chromosome

A eukaryotic cell preparing to divide has to achieve more than an increase in the amount of DNA within its nucleus. It must make a complete second copy of every chromosome, maintain the order of sequences within each DNA molecule, assemble both copies into chromatin and leave the chromosomes in a condition in which they can be separated. Failure at any one of these stages can produce a cell that has nearly the expected quantity of DNA but does not possess two functional copies of its genome. The distinction between chemical synthesis and chromosome completion is central to understanding eukaryotic DNA replication.

Consider an ordinary diploid somatic cell in G1. Each chromosome is represented by a single chromatid containing one continuous double-stranded nuclear DNA molecule. At the end of a normal S phase, each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids. The DNA content doubles from 2C to 4C, whereas the chromosome number, when counted by undivided centromeres, does not double. The sisters remain associated until their segregation. A tetraploid cell at G1 may also contain 4C DNA if C refers to the haploid complement. Consequently, a measurement of DNA mass alone is insufficient to determine chromosome number or the success of a replication cycle.

The chemical basis of replication is shared with bacteria. Each original DNA strand carries sequence information for the synthesis of a complementary strand. Newly formed duplexes each normally contain one parental strand and one new strand. This is semiconservative replication. Eukaryotic cells do not need an alternative principle of base pairing merely because their chromosomes are larger or because the DNA is located in the nucleus. What has changed during evolution is the organization and coordination of the molecular machinery that carries out the same chemical operation on a different physical substrate.

Three features of a typical nuclear chromosome make this organization particularly important. First, the chromosome can be so long that a single pair of replication forks would take far longer than the available S phase. Many initiation sites therefore distribute the copying work among many simultaneously active machines. Second, nuclear DNA is wrapped around nucleosomes and associated with numerous other proteins. The replication machinery must obtain access to DNA while ensuring that functional chromatin is rebuilt on the product molecules. Third, the molecule is linear. A conventional polymerase needs a suitable primer terminus and cannot solve every geometric problem encountered at an exposed chromosome end.

These three issues are distinct. An increased number of replication origins can shorten the time needed to duplicate a long chromosome, but does not itself provide a primer beyond a physical end. Telomerase can maintain terminal repeats in a cell that expresses it, but cannot duplicate the interior megabases of a chromosome. A histone chaperone can assemble nucleosomes after replication, but cannot substitute for a DNA polymerase. By separating the biological tasks, it becomes possible to predict which measurable defects should follow particular molecular interventions rather than describing every affected cell simply as defective in replication.

Throughout this chapter a normal, unperturbed nuclear replication fork in a well-studied eukaryotic system provides the reference case. Yeast and vertebrate cells offer complementary experimental evidence. Their proteins and regulatory strategies are broadly related, but the exact sequence requirements of origins, replication timing patterns and telomere structures need not be identical. Organelle genomes are not included in this nuclear model; they have their own replication machinery and are considered with extrachromosomal replicons.

2. Polarity, primer chemistry and the fork as a moving boundary

A DNA strand has direction because the deoxyribose sugars in successive nucleotides are connected through asymmetric phosphodiester bonds. The free ends are conventionally described by the positions of their sugar carbons: 5′ and 3′. Two strands of a DNA duplex run antiparallel. When one strand is read from its 3′ end toward its 5′ end, the complementary strand runs from 5′ toward 3′. That geometry, rather than any special property of the bases adenine or guanine, gives rise to leading and lagging strand synthesis.

A replicative DNA polymerase extends an existing primer. At its catalytic site the hydroxyl group attached to the 3′ carbon of the primer attacks the alpha phosphate of an incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate, abbreviated dNTP. The reaction creates a new phosphodiester linkage and releases pyrophosphate. Metal ions, typically magnesium in the physiological catalytic system, facilitate the chemistry and help position the reacting groups. The incoming nucleotide supplies both the building block and chemical energy for incorporation; subsequent pyrophosphate hydrolysis helps favor the overall synthetic process. The newly incorporated sugar presents the next 3′ hydroxyl group, so the operation can be repeated.

The essential bookkeeping is therefore that a new strand grows 5′ to 3′ while the template is read 3′ to 5′. A polymerase does not reverse this chemical direction when it encounters the opposite parental strand. Instead, replication solves the spatial problem by changing how often synthesis is started. Suppose a fork moves to the right, and the upper parental strand runs 3′ to 5′ from left to right. Its new complement can grow continuously toward the moving fork. The lower parental strand runs 5′ to 3′ from left to right. Its new complement must grow toward the left, away from the advancing boundary. Newly exposed regions of that lower template repeatedly acquire new primers, producing short sections that subsequently join.

The words leading and lagging describe synthesis relative to a particular fork. They are not permanent labels for the two strands of a chromosome. If a second fork moves leftward from an origin, the polarity relationship is reversed. The upper parental strand from the previous example becomes the lagging-strand template at the leftward fork. On a linear chromosome with several bidirectional origins, a single continuous parental strand can be used for leading synthesis in one interval and lagging synthesis in another. This is why any examination diagram must be solved locally using both fork direction and strand-end labels.

A replication fork is a structural and operational boundary, not a particular protein. Ahead of the fork the parental DNA is still double stranded. At the junction the two parental strands are separated; behind it, new duplex DNA is produced. Helicase displacement and polymerase extension are separable processes. If nucleotide availability is suddenly reduced, the chemical rate of polymerization can fall even though a helicase remains capable of hydrolyzing ATP. If unwinding temporarily outruns synthesis, exposed single-stranded DNA can accumulate. Conversely, blocking unwinding deprives a polymerase of freshly accessible template even if its own active site is intact.

An internal lagging-strand fragment is not an isolated final DNA product. It is a temporary piece of a future continuous daughter strand. The piece initially has a composite RNA–DNA region at its 5′ end and a growing DNA 3′ terminus. Its neighbor provides a 3′ end from which synthesis can proceed into the region containing the old primer. Subsequent displacement, nuclease cleavage and ligation remove the discontinuity. This arrangement works precisely because the next fragment is initiated farther along the advancing fork. At the end of a linear chromosome the necessary extra template may no longer exist, which is a different problem from joining two internal fragments.

The chemical reaction should also be distinguished from proofreading. Some DNA polymerases possess an exonuclease activity that removes an incorrectly placed nucleotide from the growing 3′ end. Such editing occurs in the 3′ to 5′ direction and does not imply that DNA was ever synthesized in that direction. A polymerase lacking its own proofreading site may still make a useful primer or participate in a replication system where errors can be corrected by other processes. Fidelity is developed further in node 3.A.07; here it matters because the primer-making polymerase and the elongating polymerases have different tasks.

3. Why multiple origins are needed

The length of the human haploid nuclear genome is on the order of three billion base pairs. A replicating diploid nucleus contains approximately twice that amount of parental sequence before S phase begins. A single replication fork in an animal cell may advance at a rate on the order of one to a few kilobases per minute, with substantial variation among cell types and genomic regions. Even if a chromosome were freely accessible naked DNA, relying on one central origin would be an inefficient strategy for very long chromosomes.

For a simple model, consider an isolated chromosome segment of length L base pairs that has an origin exactly at its center. Two forks move away from the origin at equal constant speed v base pairs per minute. The left fork must traverse L/2 base pairs and the right fork must do the same. Both reach the boundaries after time T = L/(2v). This expression describes the time to replicate the length of the parental segment under the stated assumptions. It does not require an extra factor of two for the two new complementary strands; each progressing fork duplicates the duplex region that it traverses.

For example, consider a hypothetical 120 million base-pair chromosome and fork speed of 2,000 base pairs per minute. One central origin would complete copying after 120,000,000/(2 × 2,000) = 30,000 minutes, or 500 hours. That is clearly incompatible with an ordinary mammalian S phase. If 600 evenly spaced origins fire simultaneously and their forks advance at the same speed, each origin is responsible for an average interval of 200,000 base pairs. The corresponding ideal completion time is 200,000/4,000 = 50 minutes. The dramatic difference follows from parallelism, not from faster polymerase chemistry.

Real replication is not this perfectly synchronized. Origins fire at different times, forks vary in speed, and some potential origins are never activated because they are copied by forks from neighboring origins. Origin density and observed replication time are related, but one cannot calculate the number of actual initiation events solely by measuring the number of sites where licensing proteins are bound in G1. Nor is the average spacing sufficient to predict completion time: the last region to finish may be a long gap between late active origins. The mean is a useful approximation to total work, while the maximum remaining interval often sets the critical delay.

The historical demonstration of multiple units in mammalian chromosomes depended on observing the distribution of newly synthesized DNA rather than merely measuring an increase in nuclear DNA. Huberman and Riggs in 1968 analyzed pulse-labeled mammalian DNA fibers. Spatially separated incorporation tracks supported replication at multiple sites along a chromosome. The key insight is that a short pulse marks regions actively synthesized while the label is available. If a single origin alone were responsible for a long unbroken chromosome, the possible spatial patterns of incorporation would differ from those of many concurrently operating forks. Tandem labeled regions and the patterns generated by labeling over different time intervals provided evidence for distributed copying.

The experiment also illustrates the difference between localization and mechanism. Incorporation of labeled thymidine indicates DNA synthesis in the relevant experimental context. It does not identify the responsible polymerase or reveal the precise order in which licensing factors were recruited. Establishing these later details required biochemical purification, genetics and additional physical measurements. A good scientific conclusion states what the observation demonstrates while recognizing the questions that it cannot answer.

Modern pulse-labeling methods can distinguish two consecutive nucleotide-analog exposures with different fluorescent detection reagents. A fork moving steadily across a stretch of DNA generates consecutive colored tracks, whereas an origin that initiates during the first pulse can produce outward-moving pairs of tracks. A termination event between active origins gives convergent tracks. Because DNA is stretched for measurement, a length calibration in kilobases per micrometre is needed before estimating speed. Broken fibers, uneven stretching and lack of signal at the edges can make a pattern ambiguous; one should analyze many appropriately selected molecules and compare matched samples.

4. Licensing, activation and the transition to a progressing fork

Origin licensing and activation were introduced in node 3.A.02. Their relevance here is that a progressing fork has a molecular composition different from a licensed but inactive origin. In G1, origin recognition proteins and loading factors place the MCM2–7 helicase core onto double-stranded DNA, typically in the form of an inactive double hexamer. Loading makes the site capable of initiating later. The cell does not obtain two moving helicases simply by loading MCM; activation requires further regulated steps.

In S phase the action of S-phase cyclin-dependent kinases and Dbf4-dependent kinase promotes initiation. Association of Cdc45 and GINS with the MCM2–7 core forms the active CMG helicase. The two helicase complexes become associated with opposite strands at the origin and progress in opposite directions along the parental DNA. An elongating fork contains CMG along with DNA polymerases, primer-making machinery and accessory factors. The change from a loaded MCM double hexamer on duplex DNA to two active helicases associated with single-stranded templates is a structural reorganization, not simply an increase in enzyme concentration.

The usual mechanism prevents licensing and firing from occurring indiscriminately at the same time. Low cyclin-dependent kinase activity in the appropriate cell-cycle period allows loading; the environment that promotes initiation during S phase also suppresses new licensing through several organism-dependent regulatory mechanisms. This arrangement makes it possible for forks to pass through previously licensed sites without those sites repeatedly launching new rounds of synthesis. Once an inactive origin is passively replicated by an incoming fork, it has lost its opportunity to initiate in that S phase.

The abundance of licensed sites has a beneficial consequence. If a neighboring fork is delayed, a licensed site that would normally be copied passively may have time to fire, supplying an additional pair of forks. Such dormant origins are potential reserve capacity. They are not a license for uncontrolled rereplication. Their use remains subject to cellular conditions and checkpoint regulation, and the number and distribution of additional active origins are limited.

Imagine two cells with similar levels of MCM protein but different levels of activated CMG. One might have normal licensing in G1 but fail at origin firing during S phase. The other might have formed active forks but fail to extend them because a polymerase is inhibited. In the first case there should be fewer new initiation structures, while in the second case initiation structures can form but fail to produce normal-length newly synthesized DNA. A DNA-content histogram alone might show a similar delay in both cases. Combining origin-associated protein measurements with short pulse labeling and fork-track analysis would help distinguish the mechanisms.

A further distinction is that the replication schedule is not simply the expression schedule of all genes. Many accessible genomic regions replicate early, whereas compact chromatin often replicates later, but these are broad relationships rather than universal rules. An actively transcribed locus and an early replication origin are not equivalent entities. Replication duplicates DNA whether or not the DNA is being transcribed; the timing of its duplication reflects a regulated chromosomal program. A locus replicated late is not thereby excluded from the genome of either daughter cell.

The growing fork now faces the need to maintain the exposure of both templates, recruit the correct primer-making and elongating proteins, and couple their activities with chromatin remodeling. The following sections examine these components in the order in which their chemical and physical contributions become necessary.

5. CMG helicase, RPA and the physical environment of the replisome

The name CMG is constructed from three components: Cdc45, the MCM2–7 complex and GINS. The MCM ring contains six different but related subunits, and its motor activity depends on ATP. Cdc45 and GINS contribute to the formation and functioning of the active helicase rather than serving as alternative DNA polymerases. The ring encloses predominantly the leading-strand template at a normal fork and translocates along that strand in its 3′ to 5′ direction. The other parental strand is excluded from the central channel during the usual strand-separation model. The result of movement is the continued availability of two single-stranded templates.

This molecular architecture is useful for understanding fork polarity. At the rightward fork drawn in the preceding section, the upper strand has the 3′ to 5′ orientation required for movement of CMG toward the right. The same strand is read by the leading-strand DNA polymerase. The helicase is ahead of the polymerase in the direction of overall fork progression, although both are associated with the replisome. There is no contradiction between the direction of helicase movement on the template and the direction of new DNA synthesis: they are directions measured on different molecular strands.

The energy used by CMG is not simply the energy stored in DNA base pairs. ATP binding and hydrolysis drive conformational changes in the helicase motor that support directed movement. Base pairing and base stacking stabilize the duplex; the motor progressively overcomes this stability while moving along the parental template. An ATPase-defective helicase can therefore bind DNA yet fail to unwind it productively. Conversely, thermal denaturation of purified DNA can expose a single-stranded template without ATP, but it does not reproduce the controlled, local movement of a replication fork in a living cell.

Once the parental strands separate, they are vulnerable to intramolecular folding and reannealing. Replication protein A, abbreviated RPA, binds exposed single-stranded DNA in eukaryotic nuclei. It is a heterotrimeric protein complex rather than a DNA polymerase. Its binding helps organize and protect single-stranded regions, reducing inappropriate interactions with complementary sequences and helping coordinate enzymes that need access to the template. Bacterial SSB performs an analogous general function, but the two names should not be interchanged as though they refer to identical protein complexes.

The lagging-strand template is particularly likely to expose extended single-stranded intervals because its new DNA is made in successive fragments. RPA cannot simply form an impenetrable shell around the template: primase and polymerase need local access. Protein binding therefore has to be dynamic, involving exchange and displacement as synthesis proceeds. A cell can contain abundant RPA yet have an abnormal fork if that protein is unable to interact properly with newly exposed DNA or if its normal turnover at the fork is impaired.

A distinction of experimental importance is that a large RPA signal can indicate an accumulation of single-stranded DNA without specifying its cause. It can arise when helicase activity becomes uncoupled from polymerase activity, during repair processes, or when DNA ends are processed. To infer an elongation defect, the signal should be localized to replicating DNA and interpreted alongside nucleotide incorporation and appropriate cell-cycle controls. Conversely, a failure to detect RPA might reflect reduced exposure of single strands rather than proof that replication is proceeding normally.

The mechanical problem does not end at the fork. DNA in a chromosome has constrained rotational freedom because it is bound to proteins and organized into loops and domains. Removing helical turns at one site creates a tendency toward torsional strain elsewhere. In a simplified topological picture, unwinding produces excess positive supercoiling ahead of the progressing fork if the DNA cannot freely rotate. Topoisomerases provide controlled passage or rotation of DNA strands to relax such strain. Topoisomerase I can relax supercoiling by transiently breaking one DNA strand. Topoisomerase II can pass one duplex through a transient break in another duplex and is particularly important for resolving interlinked daughter molecules.

If a fork progresses through a topologically constrained interval of 10,500 base pairs and the relaxed helical repeat is approximately 10.5 base pairs per turn, the initial DNA contains about 1,000 helical turns over that interval. Complete strand separation must accommodate changes associated with these turns through relaxation, rotation, or interwinding of daughter molecules. This estimate is not a prediction of exactly one thousand enzymatic turnovers: topoisomerase classes alter topology differently, and DNA can distribute torsion among multiple structures. It establishes the scale of the physical problem that accompanies copying even a modest stretch of chromosomal DNA.

6. The three principal nuclear replicative DNA polymerases

The eukaryotic fork does not employ a single replicative polymerase for every stage of synthesis. The central division of labor involves DNA polymerase alpha (Pol α), DNA polymerase delta (Pol δ) and DNA polymerase epsilon (Pol ε). Their properties complement one another. Pol α is associated with the primase responsible for beginning new chains. Pol δ and Pol ε are much better adapted to extensive DNA synthesis and possess intrinsic proofreading exonuclease activities. The typical nuclear replication system uses Pol ε predominantly for leading-strand elongation and Pol δ predominantly for lagging-strand elongation.

The assignment is useful because it describes the main traffic of nucleotide incorporation in normal chromosomal replication. It must not be interpreted as an absolute rule that every leading-strand nucleotide in every situation is synthesized by Pol ε or that Pol δ can never extend a leading-strand primer. In particular, Pol δ can contribute to establishment of leading-strand synthesis and can take over synthesis under some circumstances. The cell's dependence on the structural functions of a protein can also differ from its dependence on the catalytic activity of that protein. The phrase predominantly distinguishes a normal physiological allocation from a chemically obligatory exclusion.

Pol α has a polymerase catalytic site that extends DNA but works as part of a complex containing primase activity. Primase makes RNA directly on a single-stranded DNA template, without the pre-existing DNA 3′ terminus required by the DNA polymerase active sites. The RNA primer is transferred within the complex to the DNA polymerase component, which adds a limited stretch of DNA. The resulting starter provides a DNA 3′ end that can be recognized by an elongating DNA polymerase. Typical primers have approximately ten RNA nucleotides and a few tens of DNA nucleotides, but their exact lengths depend on the biological system and experimental conditions.

Why should a cell use a relatively low-processivity polymerase to start chains rather than allow the most processive enzyme to begin at once? The issue is not that Pol δ or Pol ε cannot bind an RNA–DNA hybrid under any circumstance. The issue is that their DNA polymerase reactions need a pre-existing, appropriately positioned primer terminus. Primase can establish that starting geometry. Pol α extends it to generate a useful intermediate, after which a more processive polymerase takes over. Polymerase switching is thus a coordinated handoff between different stages of DNA synthesis rather than redundant competition among equivalent enzymes.

Pol α lacks the intrinsic 3′ to 5′ proofreading exonuclease characteristic of Pol δ and Pol ε. If Pol α contributes a short DNA stretch, it is reasonable to ask how the cell limits the mutational burden associated with this less accurate synthesis. One important feature of lagging-strand maturation is that Pol δ can displace DNA at the beginning of a previous fragment together with the RNA primer, and the resulting displaced material can be cleaved. Not every nucleotide initially made by Pol α is necessarily removed under all conditions; retained primer-associated DNA and subsequent correction processes must be considered. The small proportion synthesized by Pol α and the action of other enzymes help limit the consequences of its lack of intrinsic proofreading.

Pol δ is strongly stimulated by its association with PCNA, the eukaryotic sliding clamp. Its catalytic activities include extending a primer and proofreading at the growing terminus. Its ability to synthesize DNA while displacing a downstream strand is central to the maturation of lagging-strand fragments. A mutation that selectively compromises strand displacement could therefore cause a defect in primer removal and fragment joining even if the mutant enzyme is able to extend an isolated primer on a simple template. A test that measures only incorporation of labeled dNTP into such an isolated primer would miss the distinction.

Pol ε associates with the CMG-centered replisome and is positioned to synthesize the leading strand. In addition to its catalytic region, it contains structural features involved in replisome organization. In some experimental yeast contexts, a catalytically impaired form of the leading-strand machinery may permit DNA synthesis through compensating activities while disrupting the normal division of labor. This is a useful illustration of the difference between removal of an entire protein and selective mutation of an active site. Removing the whole complex may eliminate both catalytic and structural contributions, producing a more extensive phenotype than inactivating one activity.

Other DNA polymerases are present in a eukaryotic cell but are not normally responsible for the bulk of nuclear chromosome duplication. For example, Pol β is associated principally with repair synthesis, whereas Pol γ is the principal mitochondrial replicative DNA polymerase in animals. The presence of a DNA polymerase in a nuclear extract does not establish that it is a major replicative enzyme. Function must be assigned from biochemical properties, interactions, genomic evidence and the consequences of selective perturbation. A polymerase name is therefore a useful clue, but not a substitute for mechanistic reasoning.

The evolution of eukaryotic replication proteins illustrates that analogous functions can be performed by related or different molecular structures. Eukaryotic Pol α, δ and ε belong to the B family of DNA polymerases; the principal chromosomal Pol III of E. coli belongs to the C family. The shared tasks of priming, processivity, proofreading and coordinated elongation do not require the enzymes to have identical names or evolutionary origins. This distinction will help when comparing nuclear replication with bacterial and archaeal systems.

7. Evidence for polymerase division of labor

Assigning a particular polymerase to a specific strand is experimentally harder than demonstrating that an enzyme can synthesize DNA in a test tube. Both Pol δ and Pol ε can extend suitable primers. Detecting either enzyme at replicating DNA indicates an association with replication but does not reveal which of two neighboring parental strands it is copying. The spatial separation between leading and lagging templates is small, and both are being processed as part of an organized molecular complex. An experiment that distinguishes their catalytic contributions must exploit a feature that differs between the two synthesis modes.

One informative feature is the direction of a replication fork relative to a sequence whose orientation is known. Consider a reporter positioned on one side of a well-characterized bidirectional origin. If that origin fires efficiently, the usual direction of replication through the reporter is predictable. The two orientations of the reporter place complementary sequences into different template contexts. Reversing its orientation can exchange whether a particular diagnostic base belongs to the leading or lagging template without altering the general biochemical identity of the polymerase being examined.

Certain engineered polymerase variants produce characteristic biases in the errors they make. Such a variant can be used as a genetic marker for the synthesis performed by the affected polymerase. In a simplified example, suppose a polymerase is unusually prone to insert G opposite template T compared with inserting C opposite template A. Because complementary sequences reverse the identity of these potential mismatches when analyzed in a defined reporter, mutation spectra can supply information about which parental strand was copied by the error-prone enzyme. The evidence concerns differences between classes of mutations, not merely an increase in their total number.

Pursell and colleagues reported evidence in 2007 supporting a major role for Pol ε in leading-strand replication in budding yeast. Nick McElhinny and colleagues in 2008 used a diagnostic Pol δ mutator allele to examine replication orientation and obtained results supporting a primary lagging-strand role for Pol δ. Together the studies support the familiar model of strand specialization in normally functioning yeast replication forks. These studies are especially instructive because they ask about the cellular context of the polymerases, instead of equating activity on a purified template with the normal physiological assignment.

For conceptual interpretation, consider a hypothetical pair of orientation-dependent reporter experiments. In a Pol ε variant the diagnostic error appears eighty times in the orientation predicted to reveal leading synthesis and ten times in the opposite orientation. In a Pol δ variant the corresponding numbers are twelve and seventy-two. These invented values illustrate a pattern of complementary strand preferences. They are not measurements from the historical studies. Under comparable conditions, the ratio of diagnostic events in the preferred and alternative orientations would be eight for the first variant and six for the second, consistent with substantial but nonexclusive contributions.

The ratios cannot automatically be interpreted as exact proportions of DNA synthesized by the two enzymes. The probability of a diagnostic mutation depends on intrinsic polymerase bias, sequence context, proofreading, mismatch correction and the survival and detection of mutant cells. A mutation may be erased by a different enzyme before becoming a heritable substitution. A reporter can also be replicated from a different origin in a subset of cells. Controls should establish fork orientation, reporter comparability and a well-characterized diagnostic error bias. The correct conclusion is a predominant association supported by the pattern, not an exact census of nucleotides inferred from uncorrected mutation counts.

Biochemical approaches provide a complementary test. Reconstituted fork systems allow investigators to alter the availability of specific proteins and observe the structures and quantities of DNA products. They can reveal that a protein interaction stimulates processive synthesis or that a particular enzyme is needed to complete a defined intermediate. Such systems deliberately simplify the cell, so a finding that a particular polymerase is sufficient in an artificial reaction does not prove it is the exclusive enzyme in intact chromosomes. The strongest mechanistic account combines experimental approaches with different limitations.

The classic simian virus 40 replication system also illustrates this point. It was invaluable for dissecting eukaryotic DNA synthesis using host cell proteins, but the viral initiator-helicase and the set of requirements in that system are not identical to those of a normal cellular chromosome. Historical conclusions drawn from viral replication must be interpreted in the context of the substrate used. Modern models of nuclear replication incorporate cellular genetic evidence and the architecture of the CMG replisome.

8. PCNA and RFC: maintaining a processive DNA polymerase

A polymerase can add many nucleotides during a single encounter with its template or dissociate after adding only a few. The average number of nucleotides incorporated per binding event is called processivity. It is not the same as catalytic speed. A polymerase that incorporates fifty nucleotides per second while bound may nevertheless make little DNA overall if it dissociates repeatedly and rebinds slowly. Increasing the time spent attached to DNA can improve the effective output of a replication system without accelerating the active-site chemical reaction.

PCNA, proliferating cell nuclear antigen, forms a ring built from three related positions occupied by identical subunits in the usual eukaryotic homotrimer. When the ring is around duplex DNA, it can move along the DNA rather than simply remaining at a fixed sequence. A polymerase associated with the ring is less likely to escape into solution while it is extending a primer. The advantage is especially striking for Pol δ. PCNA is not an enzyme that synthesizes a phosphodiester bond; it changes the physical relationship between the polymerase and its DNA substrate.

A closed protein ring cannot ordinarily be placed around an internal segment of a very long DNA molecule simply by threading it from one end. Replication factor C, RFC, is the ATP-dependent clamp loader. It recognizes a suitable primer-template junction, interacts with PCNA and opens the clamp. ATP-dependent structural transitions permit loading of the ring around DNA. Subsequent ATP hydrolysis and changes in RFC allow release of the loaded clamp, which can then associate with the elongating polymerase. Loading requires recognition of the appropriate DNA structure rather than any randomly available double-stranded region.

The directionality of synthesis is relevant even to a clamp-loading experiment. The productive junction contains a recessed 3′ primer end adjacent to template available for extension. An intact blunt duplex with no exposed template beyond the primer terminus does not provide the same initiation substrate. Nor does adding PCNA solve the problem of a missing 3′ hydroxyl. The clamp supports an already feasible polymerase reaction; it does not change the chemical requirement for a primer.

PCNA has another important function as a platform for binding several proteins that act on newly synthesized DNA. Among these are factors involved in primer removal, ligation and chromatin assembly. Their interactions are coordinated in time and space. It is misleading to imagine that all these proteins must occupy the same PCNA surface simultaneously in a fixed permanent complex. Binding sites may be used sequentially, and more than one clamp may be present in a replicated region. PCNA's physical persistence on DNA helps organize the transition from copying to processing.

Suppose an isolated polymerase incorporates an average of twenty nucleotides before dissociating. In a simplified model with an effective clamp it incorporates one thousand nucleotides before dissociating, while the intrinsic incorporation speed remains unchanged at forty nucleotides per second. Copying a hypothetical ten-kilobase template would then require approximately five hundred binding events without the clamp and ten events with it. If each renewed productive association costs two seconds and the first association is counted, the model gives 250 seconds of incorporation time plus 1,000 seconds of association time without the clamp, versus 250 plus 20 seconds with it. The large difference arises without changing the speed of chemistry.

In an actual cell there are more variables, including clamp retention, loader kinetics, protein concentration and the distribution rather than the mean of binding times. The model nevertheless identifies the essential point: a clamp defect may manifest as short synthesis products, reduced fork progression or excessive polymerase dissociation even when the active-site chemistry is normal. A useful biochemical test compares extension on a short substrate that requires little processivity with synthesis on a much longer substrate. If the mutant extends short primers nearly normally but produces truncated long products, a processivity problem becomes a plausible explanation.

An important parallel with bacterial replication is the β sliding clamp and its loader. Both systems use a ring around DNA and an ATP-dependent loading mechanism, but their proteins and subunit arrangements differ. E. coli's β clamp is a homodimer, whereas typical eukaryotic PCNA is a homotrimer. Their similar ring-like architecture is a response to the same physical requirement: maintaining a mobile attachment to a long polymeric substrate without obstructing the movement needed for synthesis.

9. Leading-strand establishment and coordinated elongation

The origin-to-fork transition is not complete until a primer has been positioned so that an elongating polymerase can follow the moving helicase. At a newly fired origin, Pol α–primase produces an initial RNA–DNA starter on an exposed template. RFC loads PCNA at suitable primer-template junctions. Pol δ can extend the initial primer during establishment of the leading strand, and normal leading elongation is then carried predominantly by the Pol ε associated with CMG. The exact handoff need not be identical at every origin or in every reconstituted system. The important idea is that the leading strand also requires priming even though the subsequent synthesis of a long uninterrupted interval may proceed continuously.

Once a leading strand has been established, the rate at which it grows is influenced by helicase progression, polymerase incorporation, the supply of dNTPs and the physical accessibility of chromatin. These steps are not necessarily limited by the same factor at all times. An enzyme that is responsible for a rapid chemical reaction in isolation can participate in a slowly moving fork because another component is limiting. In a simplified series of events that must all be completed before further progress, the slowest recurring step can exert a disproportionate effect on total throughput. Consequently, a cell that produces more Pol ε need not have faster replication if the rate is limited by template access or nucleotide production.

The leading and lagging synthesis modes are coordinated within a common replisome, but their organization is not perfectly symmetric. The leading polymerase advances in the same general direction as the fork. A lagging-strand polymerase moves along its local template toward the preceding fragment while the fork continues forward. Various arrangements and flexibilities of DNA and protein allow the two synthetic processes to proceed in parallel despite this difference in chemical polarity. A looped lagging-strand template is a familiar illustration of a possible organizational solution; one should not require a stable bacterial-style trombone loop at every eukaryotic fork. The essential constraint is directional synthesis and repeated primer formation, not a single universal diagram of the three-dimensional arrangement.

Pol α–primase is recruited repeatedly to lagging-strand templates through interactions within the replisome. In a yeast-centered description, Ctf4 can help link the priming machinery to the helicase complex; related organizing factors occur in other eukaryotes. The point of such a linkage is to ensure that the primer-making machinery is in the neighborhood of newly exposed DNA rather than having to locate each newly available segment through unrestricted diffusion. These interactions do not turn primase into a helicase or eliminate the need for the clamp loader.

A normal leading-strand track may contain occasional interruptions under perturbation. A polymerase encountering an obstacle can stall while unwinding continues to a limited extent. Cells may also initiate synthesis again downstream under certain conditions, leaving a gap that must be dealt with later. Such circumstances do not make ordinary leading-strand synthesis discontinuous in the same sense as the regularly repeated Okazaki fragments of the lagging strand. They establish a useful distinction between the normal mode of synthesis and deviations caused by obstacles or damage. Detailed repair mechanisms are treated in later nodes.

There is also no requirement that the two strands begin exactly simultaneously at the level of an individual nucleotide. Short differences in the timing of priming, displacement and synthesis are inherent to the organization of a fork. What must remain coordinated over a longer interval is the production of two daughter duplexes without leaving an indefinitely growing tract of unfinished DNA. A moving fork is therefore better understood as a dynamic assembly that maintains an organized flow of intermediate structures than as a static cluster of enzymes positioned identically on every chromosome.

10. The cycle of an eukaryotic Okazaki fragment

An Okazaki fragment is a short, transiently discontinuous segment of new DNA produced on a lagging-strand template. In nuclear eukaryotes typical fragment lengths are on the order of one to two hundred nucleotides, although the distribution varies. These are much shorter than the approximately kilobase-scale fragments often discussed for E. coli. The difference is related to the physical organization of eukaryotic DNA and the coordinated activities of nucleosome assembly, priming and fragment processing. It is not evidence that eukaryotic DNA has a different direction of phosphodiester bond formation.

Imagine a rightward-moving fork in which the lagging template runs 5′ to 3′ from left to right. An earlier fragment begins at a position left of the most recently exposed DNA. As the helicase advances, a new site farther to the right becomes accessible. Primase synthesizes a short RNA complementary to the newly available DNA, and Pol α extends that RNA. A suitable primer-template junction is then available for RFC to load PCNA. Pol δ takes over and elongates the fragment toward the left, in the direction of the 5′ end of the previously synthesized fragment.

The new fragment may reach the earlier fragment before that earlier fragment's initial RNA–DNA starter has been removed. This generates a structure in which the elongating polymerase approaches the 5′ end of a downstream strand. Pol δ can perform limited strand displacement, continuing synthesis while pushing the old 5′ strand out of the duplex. It thereby creates a single-stranded flap. The flap contains material from the old primer and may include DNA. A nuclease can cleave this displaced portion, exposing a new downstream 5′ terminus. Repeated cycles of short displacement and cleavage allow the junction to move through the primer region while replacing it with DNA.

The resulting new DNA products are contiguous in their base-pairing interactions before they become covalently continuous. This distinction is important. When the upstream new strand ends with a 3′ hydroxyl and the downstream new strand begins with a 5′ phosphate, with no missing nucleotide between them, the structure is a nick. Base pairing to the continuous parental template can hold the two adjacent pieces in place even though the phosphodiester linkage between them has not been made. DNA ligase I can seal an appropriately configured nick. It cannot replace a missing run of nucleotides; a genuine gap must first be filled by a polymerase.

A mature continuous daughter strand is therefore the result of at least four different operations: establishing a primer, extending it, removing the old primer material while replacing it with DNA, and forming the final backbone bond. Failure at each stage leaves a different characteristic intermediate. Inhibition of primase should reduce the number of newly initiated fragments. Inhibition of elongation should leave many short incomplete products if priming still occurs. Failure of flap cleavage can leave displaced primer material or abnormal junctions, whereas failure of ligase can allow many otherwise mature fragments to accumulate as unsealed nicks. A generic observation of reduced DNA integrity cannot distinguish these cases without examining the molecular structures.

The distinction between the production of a fragment and the disappearance of the fragment as a detectable independent strand is also useful for pulse-chase reasoning. A short pulse of labeled nucleotide can mark newly synthesized DNA that includes small lagging-strand products. During a chase in which new labeled nucleotide is no longer supplied, these labeled segments can become incorporated into higher molecular weight DNA as maturation proceeds. Under denaturing analysis, unsealed nicks interrupt the covalent strand, so small pieces can remain detectable even though the duplex may appear largely intact under native conditions.

A complication is that labeling of newly synthesized DNA does not exclusively label lagging-strand products. Leading-strand synthesis also incorporates the tracer. Moreover, breaks introduced during sample preparation can produce short labeled pieces that resemble unfinished replication intermediates. A convincing interpretation includes a comparison with untreated cells, gentle preparation conditions, a time course and an analysis that distinguishes pre-existing DNA degradation from the appearance of new fragments. If inhibition of ligase selectively prevents the labeled short pieces from entering long covalent strands during a chase, while a matched control shows efficient conversion, the causal explanation becomes stronger.

At an illustrative fork speed of 1.8 kilobases per minute and an average fragment length of 180 nucleotides, the lagging-strand machinery would initiate approximately ten fragments each minute. The calculation uses 1,800 template positions exposed per minute divided by 180 positions per fragment. Thus the average interval between initiations is about six seconds. The calculation does not imply that each fragment takes exactly six seconds to mature. Several stages may overlap, and a fragment initiated in one interval may be processed while a later fragment is being made. Distinguishing initiation frequency from maturation time prevents a common error in interpreting a pipeline of molecular events.

11. Primer removal: RNase H, FEN1 and DNA2

The maturation process must remove RNA from a region that eventually belongs to a DNA chromosome. RNase H enzymes recognize RNA associated with DNA in hybrid structures. Their activity contributes to processing certain RNA-containing intermediates and embedded ribonucleotides, with differences between RNase H1 and RNase H2 and between experimental substrates. A single general statement that RNase H removes every part of every eukaryotic replication primer would be inaccurate. In the common flap-dependent model of lagging-strand maturation, Pol δ and FEN1 are central participants, and the final junction is resolved through coordinated processing rather than by the indiscriminate degradation of RNA alone.

FEN1, or flap endonuclease 1, is a structure-selective nuclease that recognizes displaced DNA or RNA-containing flap structures at appropriate junctions. It cleaves the displaced segment to create an end suitable for subsequent processing or ligation. The geometry of the substrate is a key part of specificity. A nuclease that recognized only a particular base sequence would not be sufficient to process the diverse primer junctions distributed across the genome. Structure-selective recognition allows many different sequences to be handled by the same machinery because they present related physical arrangements.

In the predominant short-flap pathway, Pol δ performs limited displacement and FEN1 cuts the resulting flap. The polymerase can then extend further, and another cleavage can occur. The old primer region is progressively exchanged for newly synthesized DNA while the length of the displaced strand is kept short. Pol δ proofreading activity can help regulate the balance between extension and displacement through changes at the growing terminus. The products of repeated processing are therefore shaped by interactions between polymerase and nuclease rather than the activities of independent enzymes acting on unrelated substrates.

Long flaps can occasionally form. A long single-stranded flap may bind RPA, which changes its accessibility to FEN1. The nuclease/helicase DNA2 can help shorten such flaps, after which further FEN1 processing produces a suitable junction for ligation. The common exam-level model distinguishes a predominant short-flap route from a route involving longer flaps and additional processing. It does not require DNA2 to cut every primer, nor does it assign an exclusive universal pathway to all organisms and all possible chromatin contexts.

An inhibitor of FEN1 is expected to alter the population of flap intermediates and impede maturation. If DNA2 can shorten a subset of problematic longer flaps, that activity is not equivalent to final nick formation and need not rescue loss of FEN1. Similarly, adding purified DNA ligase to a mixture containing a flap or a gap does not guarantee a restored continuous backbone. A ligase requires appropriate DNA termini brought into adjacency by the template structure. The upstream enzymatic activities prepare this chemical substrate.

It is helpful to visualize a hypothetical in vitro substrate with an upstream primer ending at position 100, a downstream fragment starting at position 101, and an intact parental template extending through both. Initially there is a nick if the two ends have suitable chemistry. If the downstream fragment instead begins at position 105, positions 101 through 104 are missing from the new strand and the structure contains a four-nucleotide gap. Pol δ could fill this gap using the parental template. If it continues beyond position 104 into the downstream region, the beginning of the downstream fragment may be displaced as a flap. FEN1 cleavage can restore a nick, after which ligase seals it. The names of the intermediates are defined by their physical geometry, not by the enzyme that happens to bind them.

RNase H activity can be investigated using a defined RNA–DNA hybrid substrate, while FEN1 activity is more directly revealed using a defined flap. However, simple substrate assays measure a component of the complete system. Demonstrating that an enzyme can cut a purified structure does not prove that the identical reaction is always the major route in vivo. In a more informative comparison, one varies enzymes individually in a reconstituted maturation reaction and examines whether RNA-containing intermediates, flaps, nicks or gaps accumulate. Including an active add-back of the missing protein helps test whether the observed defect is causally related to its loss.

RNase H2 also participates in removing ribonucleotides that have been incorporated into genomic DNA. That function should not be confused with the programmed formation of an RNA primer by Pol α–primase. A polymerase can occasionally incorporate an individual ribonucleotide into a DNA chain, whereas a primer consists of intentionally synthesized RNA at a chain start. Both create RNA-containing nucleic acid structures, but the biological origin and exact processing requirements differ. Comprehensive treatment of repair pathways belongs to the subsequent fidelity and DNA repair nodes.

12. Ligation, product quality and the energy budget

DNA ligase I is the principal nuclear ligase associated with ordinary replication-fragment maturation. It catalyzes the formation of a phosphodiester bond at a nick where an adjacent 3′ hydroxyl and 5′ phosphate are aligned in a duplex. Eukaryotic DNA ligases of this type use ATP. During the catalytic cycle the ligase becomes adenylated, transfers the activating AMP group to the DNA 5′ phosphate, and promotes attack by the neighboring 3′ hydroxyl. AMP is released as the backbone linkage is formed. The activation mechanism makes ligation chemically distinct from the addition of a new dNTP by DNA polymerase.

The difference between elongation and ligation can be traced to their inputs. Polymerase incorporates a new nucleotide carrying a triphosphate, and pyrophosphate is released. Ligase acts on two nucleotides already present in the DNA pieces, activating one of their termini using an external energy source. Ligation does not insert an extra base at the junction and therefore does not increase the number of nucleotides in a mature strand. It increases covalent continuity. This is why a cell can have nearly normal nucleotide incorporation but extensive strand interruptions when ligase function is defective.

Bacterial comparisons often feature NAD-dependent DNA ligase in E. coli. Many bacteria possess such enzymes, while ATP-dependent ligases occur in several biological contexts. It would be incorrect to use energy cofactor identity alone as a universal taxonomic criterion. The important comparison for the representative systems is that the ATP-dependent nuclear ligase I and the NAD-dependent E. coli replicative ligase solve the same backbone-sealing problem using different adenylation sources.

Consider an idealized newly synthesized lagging strand of exactly 18,000 nucleotides produced as 100 mature pieces of 180 nucleotides each. If the pieces are arranged consecutively along an uninterrupted strand and there are no further connections at the segment boundaries, 99 ligation events are necessary to convert those pieces into a single covalent chain. This is a connectivity calculation: joining two chains reduces the number of independent chains by one. In a continuous chromosome segment already connected to preceding and following products, additional boundary ligations may be required. The number of primer starts and the number of ligation events are thus similar for a long region but not mathematically identical in a finite isolated segment.

The polymerase energy requirement can also be estimated separately. Copying a parental duplex of 18,000 base pairs into two daughter duplexes requires incorporation of approximately 36,000 deoxyribonucleotides into the retained new DNA, ignoring endpoint overhangs and the extra nucleotides consumed during primer replacement, proofreading and other processing. Each incorporated dNTP provides the activated chemical substrate for the synthetic step. Primase also consumes ribonucleoside triphosphates, helicase and clamp loading consume ATP, ligase consumes ATP, and additional DNA processing may require energy. A count of retained nucleotides is therefore a lower-bound description of synthetic output rather than a complete cellular energetic budget.

In a pulse-chase experiment, one may measure a high fraction of incorporated label in acid-insoluble DNA even in a sample whose newly made DNA is incompletely joined. Incorporation and chain continuity are different readouts. For example, an untreated sample and a ligase-impaired sample could both incorporate a comparable amount of labeled nucleotide during a brief interval. If the newly made strands in the second sample are much shorter during denaturing electrophoresis and fail to mature during the chase, ligation or precursor processing is implicated. Examining the chemical character of the junctions distinguishes a true ligase defect from a defect upstream.

To test that distinction conceptually, take two sets of DNA intermediates from a defined reconstituted synthesis reaction. The first has directly adjacent termini and the second has gaps or residual flaps. Addition of active ligase to the first set should restore continuity, whereas addition to the second should not do so until the substrate has been appropriately processed. An inactive ligase variant provides a useful negative control. If active ligase cannot rescue either sample, one should also verify ATP availability and the chemistry of the DNA termini before concluding that all substrates contain gaps.

The role of ligase is not restricted to replication; DNA ligases also participate in repair. A cell phenotype resulting from loss of a ligase can therefore contain both direct replication consequences and secondary defects in repair. Using a short perturbation during S phase, examining labeled nascent strands and comparing with nonreplicating controls helps identify the immediate contribution to replication. This principle of assigning a phenotype to a particular molecular stage will recur in the analysis of chromatin assembly and chromosome ends.

13. Replication timing and the mathematics of converging forks

When several origins initiate along a chromosome, a region between neighboring active origins is usually copied from both sides. The forks that face one another need not travel at the same speed, and they need not start at the same time. In a simple model let the left origin be at position zero and the right origin at position D along a coordinate increasing toward the right. Let their firing times be tL and tR, respectively, and let the rightward and leftward fork speeds be vL and vR. Once both have started, the coordinate of the rightward fork is xL = vL(t − tL), while the coordinate of the leftward fork is xR = D − vR(t − tR).

The moment of encounter is obtained by setting these coordinates equal. Rearranging the equation gives t = (D + vL tL + vR tR)/(vL + vR). The numerator has units of length, because each product of a speed and a time is a length. The denominator has units of length per time. The result is a time measured from the same reference point as the firing times. The meeting coordinate follows by substituting the result into either position equation. This derivation is preferable to automatically taking the midpoint because asymmetry in either speed or firing time shifts the encounter.

Take a hypothetical interval of 180 kilobases between two origins. The left origin fires at minute zero and its rightward fork advances at 2 kilobases per minute. The right origin fires at minute twenty and its leftward fork moves at 1 kilobase per minute. After the first twenty minutes the left fork has copied 40 kilobases; the remaining interval is 140 kilobases. Once both forks move toward each other, the gap closes at the combined rate of 3 kilobases per minute, taking a further 46.67 minutes. The encounter occurs at about minute 66.67, 133.33 kilobases to the right of the first origin. From the right origin the distance traveled is 46.67 kilobases. The two distances sum to 180 kilobases, a useful independent check.

The formula has an important domain restriction: both origins must actually fire before an incoming fork reaches their positions. If the right origin in that example were scheduled to fire at minute one hundred, the left fork would reach its position after ninety minutes. The right origin would be copied passively before its scheduled activation, and the two-fork encounter formula would not describe a real event. For an examination problem the first check is therefore whether the stated activation times are compatible with the assumed origin activity. A negative time of travel for either fork is a warning that the algebra has been applied outside its domain.

Origin-to-origin intervals are also different from chromosome-end intervals. A stretch between two active origins can be copied by two converging forks; a stretch between the final active origin and a chromosome end ordinarily has only the outward-moving fork available to copy it. If the terminal distance is 90 kilobases and the fork moves at 1.5 kilobases per minute, the time to reach the end is 60 minutes after origin firing, not 30 minutes. Although another fork may be moving in the opposite direction from the same origin, it does not contribute to copying that terminal stretch. The spatial arrangement matters more than the total number of forks on the chromosome.

To estimate the total active fork requirement for a genome, denote by G the number of parental base pairs that must be copied and by T the duration of the copying period. At constant mean fork speed v, the required mean number of simultaneously active forks is approximately G/(vT), assuming forks productively traverse nonoverlapping parental regions. More generally the total copied length equals the integral of the sum of active fork speeds over the duration of synthesis. A cell with a diploid parental DNA content of 6 × 10^9 base pairs, an eight-hour S phase and a mean productive fork speed of 1,500 base pairs per minute would require an average of approximately 8,333 active forks. The calculation divides six billion by 1,500 times 480 minutes. The two daughter strands made by each fork are already implicit in the definition of copied parental length.

This number is not an estimate of the total number of distinct licensed origins. Some forks terminate while others begin, so the population of active forks changes with time. A single initiation event normally produces two forks, but those forks may not remain active for equal lengths of time. Even the total number of initiation events is not necessarily one half of the mean active fork number: many rounds of initiation and termination can contribute to the time average within one S phase. The model is useful for estimating concurrent workload, not for counting licensing events from genome size alone.

Replication timing can be investigated by sorting or identifying cells in different portions of S phase and measuring newly incorporated label across the genome. A region overrepresented among early-labeled DNA relative to late-labeled DNA is considered early replicating under the conditions studied. The measurement can be made at the population level, so the result often represents a probability distribution or average timing rather than an invariant firing schedule for every individual cell. A locus that is early in one cell type can have different timing in another cell type because chromatin organization and origin activation patterns can vary.

14. Fork convergence, replisome removal and decatenation

A replication fork does not have to reach a dedicated terminal sequence to finish copying an internal chromosomal interval. In a multi-origin chromosome, most internal termination events occur where forks meet. The unreplicated region between them becomes progressively smaller until the last stretch of parental duplex is separated and copied. There may still be immature lagging-strand products at or near the site, and they require the same fundamental primer-processing and ligation reactions as other internal fragments.

It is tempting to imagine two helicases colliding head-on on the same template strand. In the conventional CMG model, the two helicases encircle opposite parental strands, because each tracks along its own leading-strand template. This arrangement permits a different kind of encounter than that of two rings attempting to move in opposite directions along exactly the same molecular track. Studies of vertebrate replication termination using experimentally controlled DNA substrates support a model in which converging helicases can pass one another as synthesis finishes, and CMG unloading follows completion of local DNA synthesis. The detailed geometries of protein disassembly need not be memorized to understand the distinction between convergence and removal.

Termination involves the regulated disassembly of the replisome. In representative eukaryotic systems the CMG complex is marked for unloading through ubiquitylation of a component, and ATP-dependent extraction machinery helps remove the complex from chromatin. The relevant ubiquitin ligases differ among organisms; a learner should distinguish the broadly conserved logic of removing the completed machine from the particular protein names used in budding yeast or vertebrates. This unloading does not simply happen because all nuclear DNA has disappeared as a substrate. Much double-stranded DNA remains in the nucleus, but the termination machinery must selectively dismantle completed replication complexes.

The molecular order of events has been investigated using cell-free systems that support replication of defined DNA molecules. By controlling the convergence of forks and following the association of replication proteins with DNA, one can compare the timing of synthesis completion and helicase disappearance. Dewar, Budzowska and Walter reported a vertebrate system in which CMG removal followed final ligation rather than being a prerequisite for completion of DNA synthesis. The experimental interpretation is that a helicase can remain associated with a completed region until a subsequent removal step. A broader review by Dewar and Walter places synthesis completion, replisome disassembly and topological resolution among the principal aspects of termination.

This result provides a useful model for distinguishing two types of experimentally induced defects. A perturbation that prevents fork convergence would leave a parental interval not yet copied. A perturbation that selectively blocks removal of a completed helicase could leave CMG associated with DNA even after local synthesis and joining have been accomplished. Simply detecting high levels of chromatin-bound helicase in both cases would not identify which defect occurred. Measurements of unreplicated DNA, nascent-strand continuity and helicase association would be needed to separate the alternatives.

Another obstacle at termination is the topological relationship between the newly copied DNA molecules. While two daughter duplexes are being produced, the parental strands have to be separated. Rotation of the fork or nearby DNA can transfer some torsional strain into intertwining of newly produced sister duplexes, sometimes called precatenation. After synthesis is complete, persistent topological links between daughter molecules or constrained chromosomal domains must be resolved for normal segregation. Type II topoisomerases are especially suited to this task because they can transiently open one duplex and pass another duplex through the opening.

In a circular-DNA model, two interlinked daughter rings are a simple and visible illustration of catenation. Linear chromosomes have physical ends, so unconstrained linear DNA does not have the same invariant linking topology as a pair of closed rings. However, chromosomal DNA is highly organized and its large constrained domains can remain intertwined over biologically relevant times. Topoisomerase II contributes to removing these entanglements and supports segregation. Sister chromatids are also physically held together by cohesin, which is not a catenane. Removal of topological interlinks and regulated release of cohesion are separate requirements for chromosome separation.

A cell treated with a perturbation that impairs topoisomerase II can display chromosome-segregation defects even if bulk DNA synthesis appears largely complete. That observation does not automatically establish that every defect arises solely from persistent catenanes, since topoisomerases also contribute to chromosome structure and replication progression. It does suggest a useful experiment: measure DNA synthesis and assess the physical separation of completed DNA products independently. If incorporation and DNA content are near normal but sister separation is impaired, a topological or cohesion defect is more plausible than a complete failure of polymerization.

The contrast with E. coli is educational. In that bacterium, Tus–Ter sites can help constrain fork movement in a termination region. A typical eukaryotic internal chromosomal termination event is instead governed largely by the locations and progression of converging forks. Some eukaryotic loci contain specific fork barriers, so it would also be wrong to claim that no sequence or protein can influence termination in eukaryotes. The general lesson is to avoid treating one model organism's specialized termination feature as a universal condition of DNA replication.

15. Reconstructing nucleosomes while DNA is copied

A nuclear replication fork moves through chromatin rather than an indefinitely long naked DNA helix. Nucleosomes contain DNA wrapped around a histone octamer, with an approximately 147-base-pair core DNA segment in the canonical nucleosome. They stabilize and compact the genome while also influencing access to the DNA. A polymerase cannot simply read through a fully intact nucleosome core without changes in the contacts between histones and DNA. Replication therefore involves both disruption or rearrangement of parental nucleosomes in advance of synthesis and reassembly of nucleosomes behind the fork.

The amount of DNA available for histone binding doubles during S phase. Imagine a parental interval containing 100 nucleosomes. If both daughter intervals are to recover the same average nucleosome density as the parental interval, the two daughters together require about 200 nucleosomes. The original histone material can provide only about half of the required core components, even if it is perfectly recycled. New histones must be supplied to complement the old material. This simple conservation argument explains why replication-coupled histone synthesis and deposition are important, independently of the precise arrangement of individual nucleosomes.

Parental histones and newly synthesized histones follow related but distinguishable routes. Histone chaperones bind histones and limit their inappropriate aggregation or nonspecific binding to DNA. In the vicinity of the replication fork, parental histone components can be transferred onto newly replicated DNA, while new H3–H4 histones are supplied and deposited by replication-coupled assembly factors. ASF1 participates in histone handling and transfer, and chromatin assembly factor 1 (CAF-1) promotes deposition of newly synthesized H3–H4 in replication-associated contexts. Further addition and organization of H2A–H2B helps form the complete nucleosome core.

These processes are closely linked to DNA synthesis. CAF-1 interacts with PCNA, which provides a useful physical connection between replication products and assembly machinery. A sliding clamp left in the neighborhood of a newly synthesized region can help recruit a factor needed to package that region. Such a connection solves a spatial targeting problem: the histone-deposition machinery is directed toward DNA that has just been synthesized rather than deposited in an indiscriminate fashion across the whole genome. The binding interaction does not mean that CAF-1 itself extends DNA or that PCNA itself supplies histones.

Histone distribution during replication is not semiconservative in the same precise sense as the DNA strands. In ordinary semiconservative DNA copying each daughter duplex receives one old DNA strand and one new DNA strand. Histones are protein complexes that can be transferred, separated, exchanged and supplemented. In many systems parental H3–H4 tetramers tend to be recycled as units, and newly synthesized H3–H4 provides additional nucleosome components, but the details of segregation and assembly can depend on histone type and context. It would be misleading to claim that each individual nucleosome is split exactly into equal old and new halves by the fork.

The length distribution of eukaryotic Okazaki fragments is related to the size and arrangement of chromatin structures. Newly assembled nucleosomes influence how far Pol δ can continue displacement synthesis during maturation. Patterns in fragment ends can therefore reflect nucleosome organization. However, the typical fragment length is not a fixed integer multiple of 147 base pairs, because linker DNA, priming positions, nucleosome occupancy and processing differ among regions. The canonical nucleosome core length is a structural measurement, whereas an Okazaki fragment length is a distribution of transient synthesis products. Their similar scale is biologically informative, not evidence of exact equivalence.

Smith and Stillman's 1989 study identified human CAF-1 through its capacity to promote chromatin assembly on DNA replicated in a cell-free system involving SV40. The experimental advance was the separation of DNA synthesis from efficient nucleosome assembly and the identification of a factor that coupled them. Chromatin assembly can be assessed through structural readouts, including nuclease protection patterns and changes in DNA topology in appropriate circular substrates. The key conclusion is that a replicated DNA product is a target for organized histone deposition, and that deposition can require factors not needed simply to form phosphodiester bonds.

For an illustrative experimental comparison, suppose two extract samples synthesize similar amounts of new DNA, but only one efficiently generates nuclease-protected nucleosomal fragments. Addition of functional CAF-1 to the second sample restores the protection pattern. An inactive or interaction-deficient control fails to restore it. Such a result would support a defect in replication-coupled assembly. It would still be necessary to verify that the nuclease conditions and histone availability are comparable; DNA with unusual protein associations could also be protected from cleavage. This educational scenario demonstrates the value of combining a synthesis assay with a distinct chromatin-assembly readout.

Histone supply is coordinated with cell-cycle progression in many proliferating cells. If histone production is inadequate, newly made DNA may remain temporarily less well packaged, and cellular stress can follow. In contrast, excessive free histones can bind inappropriate targets, so simply accumulating surplus histones is not an unlimited solution. Chaperones and regulation help keep the available supply matched to the demand imposed by DNA synthesis. This requirement is a notable difference from the chromosomal replication system of bacteria such as E. coli, whose DNA organization does not use the canonical eukaryotic nucleosome repeat.

16. Replication and the inheritance of chromatin states

DNA sequence is not the only feature that influences gene expression. Chromosomal regions can differ in histone modifications, DNA methylation and association with regulatory proteins. When replication disrupts parental chromatin, information associated with these features must be re-established if daughter cells are to retain their appropriate regulatory states. The copied sequence supplies binding sites and structural context, while recycled histones and maintenance mechanisms can contribute to the restoration of chromatin properties.

A simple dilution model clarifies the challenge. Suppose a parental domain has 100 nucleosomes and every parental nucleosome carries a particular histone modification. After DNA replication there are approximately 200 nucleosomes distributed across the two daughters. If all modified parental histones are retained and all newly deposited histones initially lack that modification, the immediate aggregate fraction of modified nucleosomes would be approximately one half. This estimate assumes that every original marked unit is retained, that density is restored and that the relevant marks can be counted at the nucleosome level. It is not a claim that exactly half of every individual daughter domain is marked.

Regulatory proteins can recognize existing chromatin features and contribute to re-establishing similar features on neighboring nucleosomes. DNA sequence-specific factors, transcription and local chromatin environment also influence restoration. The biological mechanism is therefore more complicated than copying a complementary DNA sequence. Histone modification patterns are not encoded by a straightforward one-to-one base-pairing rule. Re-establishment can involve feedback and local recruitment, and some marks may change when cells differentiate or respond to environmental signals.

DNA methylation gives another example of maintenance after semiconservative replication. At a symmetrically methylated CpG site in a typical mammalian cell, the parental strand retains its methylated cytosine while the newly synthesized complementary strand initially lacks the corresponding methylation. Maintenance DNA methyltransferase activity, principally DNMT1 in this model, helps restore methylation patterns, with assistance from factors that recognize the postreplicative context. This is a different chemical process from adding the DNA nucleotide itself. The polymerase first builds the complementary DNA strand; the methyltransferase then modifies appropriate cytosine residues within that DNA.

An exam question might distinguish a cell that duplicates the correct DNA sequence but fails to restore methylation from a cell with a mutational error in DNA synthesis. In the first case, a methylation-sensitive assay or appropriate methylation sequencing method might reveal altered methylation while ordinary sequencing reports the same base sequence. In the second case, a sequence substitution could persist even when the methylation machinery is intact. Both defects can affect gene regulation, but they should not be classified as the same primary molecular event.

Chromatin reassembly and modification inheritance demonstrate why completion of S phase is not equivalent to merely measuring the expected DNA mass. A cell must restore the structural and regulatory context of its DNA as well as duplicate base sequences. Detailed gene-silencing mechanisms and epigenetic regulatory networks are reserved for subunit 3.D. Here the essential conclusion is that the processes of DNA synthesis and chromatin restoration are coordinated but experimentally separable, which is why defects in the two processes can be distinguished by carefully selected readouts.

17. Why linear chromosomes create an end-replication problem

At an internal position on the lagging strand, primer removal is possible because a neighboring fragment provides a new DNA 3′ hydroxyl from which the missing region can be replaced. At a physical chromosome end this arrangement breaks down. To see why, imagine an outward-moving fork that approaches the right end of a linear chromosome. The parental strand serving as the lagging template extends in the 5′ to 3′ direction toward the end. The newest complementary fragment starts near that end and grows inward, toward the preceding fragments. Its outermost region initially contains an RNA–DNA primer.

After the terminal primer is removed, the newly synthesized strand may have a missing segment at its 5′ end. There is no neighboring fragment beyond the end of the parental template that can supply the required upstream 3′ hydroxyl. The internal primer-replacement strategy therefore cannot simply be repeated at the terminus. Increasing the concentration of DNA polymerase or ligase does not remove this geometric constraint. More enzyme cannot produce a suitable primer terminus on a piece of DNA that is not present.

The simplest textbook model begins with a blunt-ended double-stranded DNA molecule and supposes that the final lagging primer occupies a short terminal region. Its removal generates a shortened new strand and leaves a corresponding extension of the parental strand at its 3′ end. In subsequent rounds of copying, shortened sequences can become templates for new products. In the absence of an effective maintenance system, terminal sequences would progressively be lost. If the missing material were taken directly from essential genes at chromosome ends, repeated cell division would threaten the integrity of the genetic information.

Natural eukaryotic chromosome ends usually have a more complex geometry than the initial blunt-ended model. Vertebrate telomeres contain repetitive DNA and typically end in a single-stranded G-rich 3′ overhang. The conventional leading-strand product of replication cannot automatically reproduce the full overhang architecture merely by extending to the last nucleotide available on its parental template. The corresponding newly synthesized end and its complementary strand must be processed to establish an appropriate overhang. Thus the two sister chromosome ends that arise from leading and lagging synthesis need not initially have identical lengths or structures.

In a typical human telomere, the G-rich strand is oriented 5′ to 3′ toward the chromosome end and includes repeated TTAGGG sequences. The complementary C-rich strand is oriented in the opposite direction and terminates before the end of the G-rich strand. At the opposite end of the same chromosome, the G-rich terminal strand runs in the opposite spatial direction on a drawing of the chromosome. Consequently, a diagram showing the G-rich strand on top at both ends without changing orientation is not a general representation of a complete linear chromosome. The repeated sequence and outward 3′ polarity are the defining features for the telomere model.

The repeated DNA provides a substrate for mechanisms that maintain terminal length, while telomere-associated proteins contribute to end protection. The two functions must not be conflated. A chromosome with long telomeric repeats can nevertheless exhibit end-protection defects if protective proteins are absent or dysfunctional. Conversely, a short telomere may still be protected for a period before progressive shortening makes its condition critical. The ability to distinguish terminal DNA maintenance from the response to an unprotected chromosome end is essential for interpreting chromosome fusions and senescence.

The end-replication problem is particularly relevant to linear nuclear chromosomes, not a general consequence of the 5′ to 3′ synthetic direction on any DNA molecule. A circular chromosome does not possess a physical terminal 5′ segment that lacks a neighboring region beyond the end. It still requires primer removal and ligation, as well as topological resolution, but it does not have the same end-maintenance geometry. Some organisms with linear DNA use specialized solutions other than canonical telomerase, showing that the replication constraint is general for the substrate while its biological solution has evolved in multiple forms.

18. Telomerase as an RNA-guided DNA polymerase

Telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein complex with an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity. Its catalytic protein is called telomerase reverse transcriptase, or TERT. Its RNA component includes a short template region that specifies the sequence of DNA repeats to be added to a chromosome end. The human RNA component is commonly designated TERC or hTR. Other components contribute to maturation, stability, localization and regulation of the complex. The essential mechanistic distinction is that telomerase uses an RNA template contained within its own complex to extend an existing DNA strand.

The enzyme requires an accessible DNA 3′ terminus. The terminal DNA sequence aligns with a complementary segment of the internal RNA template. TERT adds deoxyribonucleotides to the DNA 3′ hydroxyl, using the RNA as the template for base selection. As a reverse transcriptase, it follows the ordinary direction of DNA synthesis, 5′ to 3′. It does not perform DNA synthesis in the 3′ to 5′ direction, and it does not use ribonucleotides as the main building material for the extended DNA product.

The arrangement of the template places a boundary on how far the polymerase can copy during one repeat-addition event. Following synthesis of an appropriate segment, the newly extended DNA can realign relative to the RNA template. The enzyme can then use the same template region to specify another repeat. Repeated cycles of alignment, extension and translocation allow telomerase to add multiple copies of a short sequence without requiring an RNA molecule containing a complementary copy of the entire telomere. Repeat addition processivity describes the ability to add multiple repeats during one association with a DNA end, whereas nucleotide addition within one repeat is a related but distinct aspect of enzyme behavior.

For an idealized human repeat example, write a segment of RNA template in the 3′ to 5′ direction as AAUCCC. Its DNA complement written 5′ to 3′ is TTAGGG. A real telomerase RNA template contains additional positioning information and the initial alignment can occur at different phases of the repeat. The short illustration therefore demonstrates base complementarity and synthetic polarity rather than the complete sequence or architecture of the human RNA component. It explains why changing the RNA template sequence can change the sequence of the DNA repeat that is produced.

If a primer of length 18 nucleotides is extended by four complete human telomeric repeats, and no partial repeat or primer degradation occurs, the product has length 18 + 4 × 6 = 42 nucleotides. Products extended by different numbers of complete repeats would differ by increments of six nucleotides. For an enzyme specifying a different repeat unit, the step size can differ. A ladder of product sizes can therefore reveal periodic repeat addition when interpreted together with sequence and appropriate controls, although the real pattern can include shorter intermediates and differences in initiation position.

The discovery of telomerase supplied evidence for an enzyme that could overcome the terminal-template limitation in a way not available to a conventional DNA-dependent DNA polymerase. Greider and Blackburn in 1985 identified a specific telomere terminal transferase activity in extracts from Tetrahymena. They examined the elongation of telomeric DNA substrates and found a characteristic repeat-addition activity. The importance of the result was not simply that an extract contained DNA polymerase activity, but that telomere-related sequences were added in an organized pattern consistent with a mechanism of chromosome-end maintenance.

Subsequent work by Greider and Blackburn in 1987 established the dependence of the enzyme on RNA and protein components. A particularly strong test of the role of the RNA came from the 1990 study by Yu and colleagues. Changes introduced into the RNA template sequence of Tetrahymena telomerase led to corresponding altered telomeric sequences in living cells. This sequence-specific prediction discriminates between an RNA that is merely a structural cofactor and an RNA that provides information copied into the DNA product. If the RNA had no templating role, changing a short RNA sequence would not be expected to predictably change the synthesized repeat in this way.

A direct extension assay can conceptually distinguish catalytic activity from telomere abundance. A telomere-containing DNA primer is incubated with the relevant enzyme preparation and nucleotide substrates, and extension products are examined. A control lacking active enzyme tests for background extension, and RNA dependence can be evaluated in a suitable controlled system. A template mutant producing predictably changed repeats provides especially informative evidence of templating. An amplification-based assay such as TRAP can also detect telomerase-dependent products, but it includes an amplification stage and therefore requires controls for inhibition and amplification efficiency. The presence of long telomeres in a sample is not itself a direct measurement of current enzyme activity.

19. Completing the complementary strand and restoring the telomere

Telomerase adds sequence to the G-rich 3′ end, not directly to the shortened C-rich 5′ end. Its reaction therefore supplies additional terminal template rather than filling every missing position on both strands. The extended G-rich DNA can support subsequent complementary-strand synthesis by conventional replication machinery. In vertebrate cells, the CST complex, consisting of CTC1, STN1 and TEN1, cooperates with Pol α–primase to promote C-strand fill-in. Primase establishes an RNA primer, and the DNA polymerase activity adds DNA in the complementary orientation.

At a right-hand chromosome end the G-rich strand runs toward the end in the 5′ to 3′ direction. After telomerase extends this strand farther toward the right, complementary-strand synthesis proceeds in the opposite spatial direction. It begins on an appropriately positioned primer on the extended template and proceeds inward in its own 5′ to 3′ direction. This direction is not a reversal of normal polymerase chemistry. It follows directly from the antiparallel relationship between the template and its complement.

Complementary-strand synthesis is regulated so that the natural 3′ overhang remains available. A perfectly blunt terminus is not the universal desired product. Telomere-associated proteins recognize the duplex and overhang, contributing to a protective configuration and regulation of the maintenance machinery. In vertebrates, shelterin is the major telomere-associated protective complex. Some components recognize duplex telomeric repeats, whereas others recognize single-stranded telomeric DNA or organize the interactions between different components. The DNA repeat, the extension enzyme and the protective protein assembly are distinct elements of chromosome-end biology.

The simple primer-removal model is often sufficient for explaining the classical lagging-strand end problem in an examination. However, actual telomeres already possess a G-rich overhang before they are replicated, and leading- and lagging-derived ends require distinct maturation steps. Work on vertebrate telomeres has demonstrated the importance of CST–Pol α–primase-mediated fill-in for maintaining C-strand length, including the consequences of incomplete lagging-strand synthesis and 5′ resection at leading-derived ends. An accurate general account recognizes telomerase extension of the G strand and complementary-strand completion as separate required contributions to maintaining terminal structure. It does not claim that telomerase alone completely reconstructs a double-stranded telomere.

This separation has a clear experimental consequence. Suppose telomerase activity is unchanged in an engineered cell, but the interaction between CST and Pol α–primase is impaired. Extension of the G-rich strand may still occur while complementary-strand synthesis is defective. A measurement that detects only telomerase extension activity can therefore appear normal despite abnormal telomeric overhangs or C-strand shortening. Measuring strand-specific terminal structures as well as enzyme activity distinguishes this phenotype from a simple absence of telomerase.

Conversely, if telomerase activity is lost, Pol α–primase may remain capable of carrying out complementary-strand synthesis on whatever template is available. It cannot continually add extra sequence to the G-rich 3′ end using an internal RNA template, because it does not possess telomerase's mechanism. Thus neither complex is a complete substitute for the other. The biological system solves both the provision of additional terminal template and the construction of the corresponding strand.

20. Telomere dynamics, cell proliferation and comparative biology

In a simplified model of telomere length across successive population doublings, let L0 denote an initial telomeric length, s the mean sequence loss per division and a the mean compensating addition per division. If these quantities are treated as constants, the expected length after n divisions is L(n) = L0 − n(s − a). The model is useful for distinguishing net shortening from maintenance and elongation. It is not a complete physiological description because loss and addition can differ among chromosome ends, cells and divisions. The shortest dysfunctional telomere can be more important than the mean length of all telomeres.

Consider a hypothetical cell lineage with a representative telomere length of 8,000 base pairs, a loss of 80 base pairs per division, and no compensating addition. If a simplified critical threshold is 4,000 base pairs, the model predicts that the threshold is reached after (8,000 − 4,000)/80 = 50 divisions. If an average of 50 base pairs is restored per division, net loss is 30 base pairs and the first whole division at which length is at or below the threshold is division 134. These values are invented for quantitative teaching; they are not universal human telomere lengths, loss rates or division limits.

If average addition equals average loss, the idealized mean length remains stable. That condition does not prove every telomere is held at a fixed length. One end can shorten while another lengthens, and individual events may produce large changes. Furthermore, telomere damage and protection can change even at a constant average length. A population-level mean therefore cannot establish the functional status of every end. An assay sensitive to the distribution of lengths or to individual short ends supplies information that a mean alone may obscure.

Many differentiated human somatic cell types have little or insufficient telomerase activity to maintain telomeres indefinitely through repeated division. In proliferating germ-line cells, many stem-cell contexts and many cancer cells, telomerase activity can contribute to maintenance. The exact expression and activity depend on cell type and physiological state. Some cancer cells maintain telomeres through recombination-associated mechanisms collectively described as alternative lengthening of telomeres, or ALT. The presence of a cancer does not imply that its maintenance mechanism must be telomerase, and telomerase expression alone does not imply that a cell is malignant.

Loss of adequate telomere maintenance can ultimately cause a proliferating cell to encounter critically dysfunctional chromosome ends. Protective architecture can fail, damage-response signaling can be activated, and proliferation can cease through senescence or cells may undergo other responses. If cell-cycle safeguards are bypassed, dysfunctional ends can contribute to chromosome fusions and genome instability. Telomere shortening and senescence are related phenomena, but neither is the single universal explanation for organismal aging or all forms of senescence. Senescence can also result from stress unrelated to telomere length.

Comparative biology shows that nuclear chromosome end-maintenance is not identical across eukaryotes. Vertebrate repeats are typically TTAGGG, many plants use TTTAGGG, and budding yeast has more irregular telomeric repeats. There are exceptions within these groups. Drosophila maintains its chromosome ends through specialized retrotransposon-associated mechanisms rather than a conventional telomerase system. These examples demonstrate that the underlying need to maintain and protect linear ends can be addressed by evolutionarily distinct strategies. It would therefore be inaccurate to describe the human repeat or the human telomerase complex as a universal property of all eukaryotes.

Telomerase and chromosome replication are relevant to cell culture, developmental biology and cancer research because proliferating cell populations must maintain usable chromosome ends over time. In interpreting such applications, distinguish the evidence of an enzyme's activity from the inference that it determines a whole-cell phenotype. A cell may cease dividing for many reasons, and an intervention that changes telomerase activity may have delayed consequences that depend on the pre-existing distribution of telomere lengths. Short-term DNA incorporation can remain normal even when a long-term telomere maintenance defect is already present.

This completes the causal sequence from origin activation to the reconstruction of the chromosomal product. The nuclear replication machine provides new DNA, chromatin assembly provides packaging, topological processing allows daughter separation, and terminal maintenance provides the specialized solution required at linear chromosome ends. These linked tasks allow a student to interpret replication phenotypes by identifying the molecular intermediate or product that is abnormal, rather than relying on a single undifferentiated concept of replication failure.

22. Part B — Conceptual examination practice

Original examination-style questions, not actual previous-year questions. Attempt each before opening the explanation.

  1. MCQ. Which enzyme complex directly establishes a new RNA–DNA primer at a normal nuclear replication fork?

    A. Pol ε; B. Pol α–primase; C. DNA ligase I; D. PCNA

    Answer and explanation

    B. Primase makes RNA without an existing primer, then Pol α adds DNA. Pol ε extends a primer; ligase seals nicks; PCNA is a clamp.

  2. MCQ. At an unperturbed eukaryotic fork the main elongating polymerases are usually:

    A. α leading, β lagging; B. δ leading, γ lagging; C. ε leading, δ lagging; D. γ leading, ε lagging

    Answer and explanation

    C. This is the predominant nuclear allocation, not an absolute rule. Pol α starts chains, Pol β is associated with repair and animal Pol γ mainly replicates mitochondrial DNA.

  3. MCQ. Which component is a clamp loader rather than a clamp?

    A. RPA; B. RFC; C. PCNA; D. FEN1

    Answer and explanation

    B. RFC uses ATP-dependent conformational changes to load PCNA. RPA binds ssDNA and FEN1 processes flaps.

  4. MCQ. Which property directly defines processivity?

    A. Nucleotides added per binding event; B. Nucleotides added per second while bound; C. ATP hydrolyzed per second; D. Origins fired per chromosome

    Answer and explanation

    A. Processivity describes retention and product length per encounter. B describes catalytic rate; C and D are different properties.

  5. MCQ. The CMG helicase in a typical active fork tracks primarily along which template?

    A. Both strands within the ring; B. Nascent leading strand; C. Parental leading-strand template; D. RNA primer

    Answer and explanation

    C. CMG moves 3′→5′ along the parental leading template in the standard model. The excluded strand provides the lagging template.

  6. MCQ. Which protein binds exposed single-stranded DNA in the usual eukaryotic nuclear replisome?

    A. RPA; B. CAF-1; C. DNA ligase I; D. ORC

    Answer and explanation

    A. RPA stabilizes single-stranded DNA. CAF-1 deposits histones, ligase joins nicks, ORC participates in origin recognition.

  7. MCQ. Which substrate is best suited to DNA ligase I?

    A. A four-base gap; B. A nick between adjacent 3′-OH and 5′-phosphate DNA ends; C. An unprocessed long flap; D. A bare ssDNA template

    Answer and explanation

    B. Ligase seals a prepared nick without adding nucleotides. A gap needs synthesis; a flap needs processing; bare ssDNA lacks the aligned junction.

  8. MCQ. Which factor directly cleaves the displaced short flap during typical lagging-strand maturation?

    A. TERT; B. Topoisomerase II; C. FEN1; D. MCM

    Answer and explanation

    C. FEN1 recognizes the flap junction. TERT maintains terminal repeats, topoisomerase II changes topology and MCM is the helicase core.

  9. MCQ. What does RNase H recognize?

    A. RNA in RNA–DNA hybrids, depending on enzyme and substrate; B. All double-stranded DNA; C. Histone tails; D. An ATP-loaded clamp

    Answer and explanation

    A. RNA-associated structures are its substrates; RNase H is not a general DNA nuclease or clamp regulator.

  10. MCQ. Why are additional histones needed during S phase?

    A. Every old histone is destroyed; B. DNA mass decreases; C. Two daughter DNA intervals require roughly twice the histone occupancy of one parent; D. DNA is replaced by RNA

    Answer and explanation

    C. Old histones can be recycled, but they cannot by themselves package twice the DNA at the same density.

  11. MCQ. Which factor directly links histone deposition to replication through PCNA association?

    A. CAF-1; B. Primase; C. TERT; D. RPA only

    Answer and explanation

    A. CAF-1 promotes deposition of new H3–H4 on newly synthesized DNA. The other factors carry out distinct replication tasks.

  12. MCQ. The primary action of telomerase is to extend:

    A. The C-rich 5′ end; B. The G-rich DNA 3′ end using an internal RNA template; C. Both strands simultaneously; D. The RNA template with DNA-independent RNA synthesis

    Answer and explanation

    B. TERT adds DNA to a 3′ end. Complementary-strand synthesis is a separate step.

  13. MCQ. Which is a typical vertebrate telomeric repeat written 5′→3′ on the G-rich strand?

    A. CCCTAA; B. TTTAGGG; C. TTAGGG; D. UUA GGG

    Answer and explanation

    C. CCCTAA is a complementary sequence written 5′→3′; TTTAGGG is common in plants; U is an RNA base.

  14. MCQ. A G1 diploid cell completes S phase. What changes?

    A. 2C to 4C with the same chromosome number before segregation; B. 2n to 4n necessarily; C. 4C to 2C; D. The chromosome number halves

    Answer and explanation

    A. Sister chromatids form without immediate separation of centromeres. DNA content and chromosome count are different measurements.

  15. MCQ. Which is a major function of topoisomerase II near chromosome completion?

    A. Synthesize primer RNA; B. Resolve duplex interlinks by strand passage; C. Add telomere repeats; D. Restore CpG methylation

    Answer and explanation

    B. Duplex passage contributes to topological separation; the other tasks belong to primase, telomerase and methyltransferases.

  16. MCQ. Which mechanism maintains chromosome ends in Drosophila rather than conventional telomerase?

    A. Specialized retrotransposon-associated maintenance; B. Universal bacterial Tus–Ter; C. DNA ligase alone; D. No end maintenance

    Answer and explanation

    A. Drosophila illustrates an evolutionarily distinct solution to linear ends. The other alternatives cannot explain persistent terminal maintenance.

  17. Assertion–Reason. Assertion: Pol α lacks intrinsic 3′→5′ proofreading. Reason: Pol α–primase specializes in making short starters rather than bulk processive synthesis.

    Choose: both true with reason explaining; both true without direct explanation; assertion only; reason only.

    Answer and explanation

    Both statements true, without a sufficient direct causal explanation. Division of labor helps limit exposure to Pol α errors but specialization itself does not mechanistically explain absence of an exonuclease domain.

  18. Assertion–Reason. Assertion: Internal lagging primers can be replaced with DNA. Reason: Adjacent fragments supply 3′ termini for synthesis across the primer-containing region.

    Use the four alternatives above.

    Answer and explanation

    Both true, reason explains. Strand displacement and nuclease processing use the neighboring fragment; the terminal case lacks an equivalent distal primer source.

  19. Assertion–Reason. Assertion: Multiple origins reduce the time required for a long chromosome to replicate. Reason: Polymerases at distinct origins necessarily have a faster intrinsic active-site rate.

    Use the four alternatives above.

    Answer and explanation

    Assertion true, reason false. Parallel copying rather than an obligatory increase in chemistry speed reduces completion time.

  20. Assertion–Reason. Assertion: A cell may possess active telomerase and still have telomere defects. Reason: Chromosome-end protection and C-strand fill-in are separable functions.

    Use the four alternatives above.

    Answer and explanation

    Both true, reason explains. G-strand extension alone does not guarantee complementary synthesis or protective architecture.

    A. I, III; B. II, IV; C. I, II, III; D. all

    A. RFC loads the clamp, not the other way around. Ligase joins existing DNA termini, not missing nucleotide positions.

  22. Multiple statements. Select all correct statements: I. CMG includes Cdc45 and GINS. II. MCM licensing equals active unwinding. III. Passive replication can remove an unfired origin's opportunity to fire. IV. Every licensed origin fires.

    A. I and III; B. II and IV; C. I, II, IV; D. III only

    A. Inactive loaded MCM requires activation, and licensed origins may be passively copied instead of firing.

  23. Matching. Match I. RPA, II. FEN1, III. RFC, IV. CAF-1 to P. histone deposition, Q. flap cleavage, R. ssDNA binding, S. clamp loading.

    A. I-R, II-Q, III-S, IV-P; B. I-S, II-P, III-R, IV-Q; C. I-Q, II-R, III-P, IV-S; D. I-P, II-S, III-Q, IV-R

    A. Each assignment follows the substrate and molecular task. The other options confuse binding, cleavage, loading and packaging.

  24. Matching. Match I. Pol γ, II. ligase I, III. TERT, IV. Pol ε to P. bulk leading synthesis, Q. animal mitochondrial replication, R. RNA-guided terminal extension, S. nick sealing.

    A. I-Q, II-S, III-R, IV-P; B. I-P, II-R, III-S, IV-Q; C. I-R, II-Q, III-P, IV-S; D. I-S, II-P, III-Q, IV-R

    Answer and explanation

    A. The location, substrate and reaction distinguish these enzymes; TERT is not a mitochondrial polymerase and ligase is not an elongating replicase.

23. Part C — Quantitative and analytical practice

Original questions. The numerical and experimental scenarios are hypothetical.

  1. Numerical. A chromosome is 96 Mb long, with a single central origin and two forks of 1.6 kb/min each. How long does copying take in this ideal model?
    Answer and reasoning

    96,000 kb/(2 × 1.6 kb/min) = 30,000 min = 500 h. Do not count the two daughter strands as extra parental distance. This illustrates the need for distributed origins.

  2. Numerical. The same chromosome has 480 equally spaced, simultaneously fired origins centered within equal domains. Estimate completion time.
    Answer and reasoning

    Each domain is 96,000/480 = 200 kb. Two forks copy 200 kb at 3.2 kb/min: 62.5 min. This assumes no pauses and equally spaced active origins.

  3. Numerical. A 150 kb interval separates two origins firing together. Forks move toward one another at 2 and 1 kb/min. Find the encounter time and coordinate from the faster origin.
    Answer and reasoning

    150/(2+1) = 50 min. The faster fork traverses 100 kb; the other traverses 50 kb. The midpoint would be incorrect because rates differ.

  4. Numerical. A 240 kb interval has a left origin firing at t=0 with v=3 kb/min and a right origin firing at t=20 min with v=2 kb/min. Where and when do the forks meet?
    Answer and reasoning

    The first 20 min closes 60 kb, leaving 180 kb. At 5 kb/min the gap closes in 36 min. Encounter at t=56 min and x=168 kb from the left; 72 kb from the right.

  5. Numerical. A leftward fork from an active origin is 75 kb from a dormant licensed origin and moves at 1.5 kb/min. The dormant origin is scheduled to fire in 65 min. Is a two-fork encounter expected?
    Answer and reasoning

    The existing fork reaches the site at 75/1.5=50 min, before the scheduled firing. Under the assumptions the site is passively replicated; the two-fork model does not apply.

  6. Numerical. A terminal interval extends 72 kb beyond the outermost active origin. The outward fork travels at 1.2 kb/min. How long until it reaches the end?
    Answer and reasoning

    72/1.2 = 60 min. Only one fork copies this interval; dividing by two would incorrectly include the fork traveling away from the end.

  7. Numerical. A diploid genome contains 4.8 × 10^9 parental bp. S phase lasts 10 h, with mean active fork speed 2 kb/min. Estimate the mean active forks.
    Answer and reasoning

    4.8 × 10^9/(2,000 × 600) = 4,000. This is a time-average concurrent fork count, not the cumulative number of origins that fire.

  8. Numerical. A fork travels at 2.4 kb/min. Mean lagging fragment size is 160 nt. Estimate primer starts per minute and mean time between starts.
    Answer and reasoning

    2,400/160 = 15 per min; 60/15 = 4 s per start. This is not the total maturation duration of each fragment.

  9. Numerical. A contiguous lagging-strand segment consists of 125 fully processed fragments, with no external junctions. How many ligations make one continuous strand?
    Answer and reasoning

    124. Each ligation reduces the number of separate chains by one. No new bases are required at these prepared nicks.

  10. Numerical. A 40 kb duplex is copied completely. Ignoring primer replacement and losses, how many new deoxyribonucleotides are retained?
    Answer and reasoning

    80,000, since each parental strand templates a complementary strand of 40,000 nt. The fork traverses 40,000 parental bp, not 80,000.

  11. Numerical. A DNA fiber has a 12 µm track during a 15-min pulse. Calibration is 2 kb/µm. Estimate the fork speed.
    Answer and reasoning

    12 × 2 = 24 kb; 24/15 = 1.6 kb/min. Calibration is necessary; micrometres per minute alone is not a genomic copying rate.

  12. Numerical. An elongating polymerase has an average processivity of 50 nt without clamp and 2,000 nt with clamp. For 20 kb of synthesis, estimate binding events in the mean-length approximation.
    Answer and reasoning

    20,000/50 = 400, versus 20,000/2,000 = 10. The expected reduction is 40-fold. It does not necessarily imply a 40-fold increase in chemical incorporation speed.

  13. Numerical. A constrained relaxed DNA region is 21 kb long. Using 10.5 bp/turn, estimate the initial helical turns relevant to unwinding.
    Answer and reasoning

    21,000/10.5 = 2,000 turns. This is a geometrical estimate, not the number of topoisomerase turnovers; rotation, strand passage and topology redistribution matter.

  14. Numerical. A chromatin domain contains 180 nucleosomes before S phase. Assume complete histone recycling and restored density. How many additional nucleosome equivalents are needed across both daughters?
    Answer and reasoning

    360 total − 180 parental equivalents = 180 new equivalents. This is a material-balance estimate, not a claim about exact partitioning at each nucleosome.

  15. Numerical. Initially 60 of 100 nucleosomes have a mark. The two daughters contain 200 nucleosomes; all old marks survive and new histones initially lack it. What is the immediate aggregate marked fraction?
    Answer and reasoning

    60/200 = 30%, half the initial 60%. It says nothing about the exact fraction in either individual daughter or subsequent restoration.

  16. Numerical. An 18-nt telomeric DNA primer receives seven complete vertebrate-type repeats. What is the product length?
    Answer and reasoning

    18 + 7 × 6 = 60 nt. A six-nucleotide ladder represents repeat increments rather than a universal DNA polymerase stepping distance.

  17. Numerical. A hypothetical lineage begins with 9 kb terminal repeat DNA. Net loss is 55 bp/division and a threshold is 5 kb. Find the first whole division at or below the threshold.
    Answer and reasoning

    (9,000−5,000)/55 ≈ 72.73, so 73 divisions. At 72 divisions 5,040 bp remains. The model ignores heterogeneity and is not a clinical estimate.

  18. Numerical. A telomere loses on average 75 bp per division and gains 45 bp. Starting from 7,500 bp, predict the mean after 40 divisions.
    Answer and reasoning

    Net loss 30 bp/division; total 1,200 bp, yielding 6,300 bp. Extension is not absent, but does not compensate fully for loss.

  19. Experimental. An extract incorporates normal amounts of tracer during a pulse but retains abundant short nascent DNA after a chase. Purified ligase I restores long chains without new dNTPs. What is the likely immediate defect?
    Answer and reasoning

    A nick-sealing defect is strongly supported: recovery without synthesis suggests adjacent termini rather than gaps. Incorporation alone would not have diagnosed this. Verify that active ligase, not buffer, gives rescue.

  20. Experimental. An extract forms abundant persistent flap intermediates. Adding ligase fails to restore long chains; FEN1 followed by ligase restores them. Interpret.
    Answer and reasoning

    The upstream flap processing step is deficient. Ligase cannot act on the unprocessed flap; FEN1 produces an appropriate junction. A ligase-only rescue would instead favor an isolated sealing defect.

  21. Experimental. A mutation produces nearly normal extension on a 20-nt substrate but short products on a 10-kb template. Addition of normal PCNA and RFC rescues the long products. Which property is implicated?
    Answer and reasoning

    Processivity or clamp engagement. A short-substrate assay can show chemistry is intact while long products reveal repeated dissociation. Further experiments must distinguish clamp loading from polymerase–clamp association.

  22. Experimental. Cell A has normal G1 MCM loading but very few new S-phase initiation tracks. Cell B has many initiation tracks but very short elongation tracks. Assign the most likely primary defect class.
    Answer and reasoning

    A: activation/firing, assuming licensing and detection are valid. B: fork progression, potentially unwinding, polymerization or nucleotide supply. Bulk S-phase delay alone does not separate them.

  23. Experimental. Two pulse labels give a hypothetical fiber pattern outward from a central point: later color farther from the center on both sides. What event does it suggest, and what is the reverse arrangement?
    Answer and reasoning

    Bidirectional origin firing during or before the first pulse is consistent with outward progression. Inward movement of two forks toward a gap gives later label nearer the convergence zone. Broken fibers and labeling timing can obscure inference.

  24. Experimental. A Pol ε mutator yields diagnostic errors mostly in one reporter orientation, while a Pol δ mutator yields the complementary orientation bias. Can one infer exact strand percentages directly?
    Answer and reasoning

    No. The pattern supports distinct predominant strand contributions if orientation is controlled. Error probabilities, proofreading, mismatch repair and variable origin usage must be calibrated before quantitative fractions are inferred.

  25. Experimental. A sample contains chromatin-bound CMG after DNA is fully copied and ligated. A second sample also retains CMG but has an unreplicated interval. Is the same defect established?
    Answer and reasoning

    No. The first is compatible with failure of post-synthesis helicase unloading; the second could have a progression or convergence failure. Confirm synthesis completion and measure disassembly independently.

  26. Experimental. A CAF-1-impaired extract copies DNA efficiently but lacks regular nucleosome protection. Functional CAF-1 add-back restores protection. Which direct process is defective?
    Answer and reasoning

    Replication-coupled chromatin assembly. Incorporation is not a measure of packaging. Verify histone availability and nuclease conditions; inappropriate protein binding can otherwise mimic protection.

  27. Experimental. In a methylation assay, DNA sequence and copy number are normal after S phase but formerly symmetric CpG sites are often hemimethylated. What is a plausible defect?
    Answer and reasoning

    Maintenance methylation, for example deficient DNMT1-dependent restoration. This is distinct from replicative sequence copying; a methylation-sensitive assay is required.

  28. Experimental. A telomerase RNA template mutation generates correspondingly altered telomeric repeat sequence in an assay and in cells. Which hypothesis is supported?
    Answer and reasoning

    The RNA supplies sequence information as a template, not merely a structural component. Compare matched wild-type RNA and inactive catalytic controls; repeat sequencing strengthens the interpretation.

  29. Experimental. Cells have normal telomerase direct-extension activity but reduced C-strand fill-in and unusually long G-rich overhangs. Which class of defect is consistent?
    Answer and reasoning

    CST–Pol α–primase-mediated complementary synthesis or its regulation. Telomerase acts on the G-rich 3′ end; normal enzyme activity cannot exclude a complementary-strand defect.

  30. Integrative. Two cells both have 4C DNA, one with severely impaired ligation and another with blocked sister separation due to topology. What measurements distinguish the conditions?
    Answer and reasoning

    Nascent-strand continuity under denaturing conditions can reveal nicks in the first; chromosome separation or defined topological substrate assays support the second. DNA mass alone is insufficient. Cohesion must also be considered in segregation assays.

  31. Integrative. A telomerase-negative proliferating cell line maintains stable mean telomere length. Must the telomerase test be false?
    Answer and reasoning

    No. An ALT-like recombination-associated mechanism can maintain length without detectable telomerase. Verify enzyme assay controls and examine terminal heterogeneity or other independent evidence. Stability alone is not proof of ALT.

  32. Integrative. A rightward fork uses a top parental strand 3′→5′ left to right. An adjacent origin to the right fires a leftward fork. Identify the role of that same top parental strand at each fork.
    Answer and reasoning

    At the rightward fork it is the leading template; at the leftward fork it is the lagging template because 3′→5′ reading runs rightward, opposite fork movement. The leading/lagging labels are local, not intrinsic to a strand.
