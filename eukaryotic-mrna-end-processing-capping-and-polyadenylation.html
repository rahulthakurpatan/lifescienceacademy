1. Why a newly transcribed messenger is not necessarily a mature message

A molecule of DNA and a molecule of RNA can carry corresponding sequences without having identical biological roles. A gene in a eukaryotic chromosome is a persistent source of information, whereas a messenger RNA is a temporary working copy that must be delivered to ribosomes and eventually removed. The cell therefore needs a means of recognizing which RNA molecules are sufficiently mature to be used, protecting them while they are transported, and controlling how long they can contribute to protein synthesis. The two ends of an mRNA provide important sites at which these requirements are addressed.

To see why the ends matter, imagine an RNA molecule containing an intact protein-coding sequence but lacking the normal molecular structures at its termini. The sequence can still specify an amino acid chain in principle. However, if the molecule is rapidly destroyed or fails to recruit the proteins needed for translation, the coded information will have little opportunity to be expressed. The sequence specifying a protein and the molecular features permitting that sequence to be used are distinct aspects of gene expression. The mature messenger is consequently not merely a string of codons.

An ordinary nuclear protein-coding gene has a transcription start site, a transcribed region containing sequences that may be retained or removed, and sequences that guide formation of the mature RNA end. The translation start and stop codons delimit the coding region, not the entire mRNA. The retained sequence preceding the start codon forms the five-prime untranslated region, and the sequence between the stop codon and the mature three-prime end forms the three-prime untranslated region. These regions are transcribed but generally do not specify the amino acid sequence of the major protein product. Their presence enables regulation without altering that product.

The cap is attached at the extreme five-prime end of the RNA rather than at the translation start codon. The polyadenosine tail is appended at a processed three-prime end rather than directly after the stop codon in most genes. Thus a messenger can contain several hundred nucleotides after its stop codon before reaching the cleavage site. A mutation in that region can impair protein production while leaving every codon intact. Such a mutation tests our understanding of the whole gene-expression pathway instead of just the genetic code.

In nuclear eukaryotic gene expression, RNA polymerase II generates the primary transcript. Processing machinery normally begins to act before polymerase II has finished its journey along the gene. The term pre-mRNA refers to the RNA before completion of maturation, not necessarily to a freely floating, full-length molecule that waits in the nucleus for every processing event. Capping occurs near the beginning of synthesis. The three-prime end can only be formed after the relevant sequence signals have been transcribed. Splicing often overlaps these events in time. The sequence of events is constrained by the order in which the information emerges from the polymerase, but it is not a universal rigid assembly line.

This temporal organization is important in experimental reasoning. A short, newly made transcript at an early point in elongation can already be capped and still lack the sequences required for cleavage and polyadenylation. Its absence of a poly(A) tail is not in itself evidence of a defective capping reaction. Conversely, a transcript that has reached the downstream processing region but has not been cleaved may have a perfectly normal cap. Each processing event needs its own molecular assay.

2. The chemical asymmetry of an RNA molecule

RNA is assembled from ribonucleotides connected by phosphodiester bonds. The ribose sugar gives each nucleotide a five-prime carbon and a three-prime carbon. In the interior of an RNA chain, a phosphate links the three-prime oxygen of one sugar to the five-prime oxygen of the next. This orientation gives the polymer a polarity that is retained regardless of whether it folds into a helix or remains single stranded. The notation five-prime to three-prime describes the chemical direction of the backbone, not the position of the molecule in a drawing.

When RNA polymerase initiates a transcript without an external primer, the initiating nucleoside triphosphate is the first residue of the new chain. Subsequent nucleotides become incorporated by attack of the growing chain's terminal three-prime hydroxyl group on the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphate. The beta and gamma phosphates of that incoming nucleotide leave as pyrophosphate. The first nucleotide is unusual because its own five-prime triphosphate can remain attached to the beginning of the nascent RNA. The RNA therefore begins with a triphosphate group and ends with an available three-prime hydroxyl group, provided that it has not undergone any other modification.

The cell uses this chemical distinction to modify the two ends differently. At the five-prime end, an enzyme recognizes a terminal phosphate configuration and converts it into a substrate for attaching a guanosine in an unusual orientation. At the three-prime end, the canonical pathway first generates a new end by cutting the RNA and then uses the hydroxyl on the retained portion as the site at which additional adenosines are polymerized.

Neither reaction should be confused with ordinary elongation of the RNA by polymerase II. Polymerase II selects each incoming nucleotide using base pairing with DNA. Capping instead attaches a particular nucleotide through a linkage not normally present within the RNA backbone. Polyadenylation repeatedly incorporates the same nucleotide without using a DNA template to specify each successive addition. Although all three pathways involve nucleotide chemistry, their information sources and products are not interchangeable.

The meaning of the term terminal modification is also worth clarifying. When a cap is added, the original first transcribed nucleotide remains in the RNA; the cap guanosine is a new chemical attachment on its five-prime side. When a poly(A) tail is added, the newly cleaved RNA retains the genomic sequence up to the chosen cleavage position and acquires an additional sequence of adenosines after that position. The newly synthesized tail is part of the RNA, but its individual residues need not correspond to a long series of thymidines in the DNA template.

3. The structure and nomenclature of the five-prime cap

The characteristic cap of most nuclear eukaryotic mRNAs contains a guanine base methylated at its nitrogen at position seven. Its common shorthand is m7GpppN, where N represents the first transcribed nucleotide, ppp represents the three phosphates connecting the two nucleosides, and m7G denotes seven-methylguanosine. In this notation the base modification should not be confused with methylation of the ribose sugar. The cap guanosine is attached through its own five-prime position to the five-prime position of the original first nucleotide.

The five-prime to five-prime triphosphate bridge is chemically distinct from the three-prime to five-prime phosphodiester connections found in the remainder of the RNA. This unusual linkage has two consequences. It creates a molecular pattern recognized by cap-binding proteins, and it removes the ordinary exposed end configuration that certain exonucleases need for efficient degradation. Methylation of the guanine enhances recognition by specialized proteins. It is not merely a decorative addition to a guanosine that already functions identically.

Number the first two transcribed residues one and two. A cap containing methylated guanine but no additional two-prime ribose methylation at these residues is called cap zero. If the ribose of residue one is methylated at its two-prime oxygen, the product is called cap one. If the corresponding ribose at residue two is also methylated, the product is called cap two. These distinctions describe the chemical modifications, not the number of cap guanosines. All three structures have one terminal cap guanosine. Cap one does not contain two guanosines and cap two does not contain three.

The two-prime oxygen is part of the ribose hydroxyl group, and methylation replaces the hydrogen of that hydroxyl with a methyl group. It does not replace the nucleotide's base, remove its three-prime hydroxyl, or alter the order of codons in the downstream reading frame. The cap pattern varies across organisms and RNA classes; cap one is common among mammalian cellular mRNAs, but a universal requirement for cap two would be an incorrect generalization.

An exam diagram may depict the guanosine at the left of the RNA using a conventional line that resembles the phosphodiester backbone. The labels on the linkage, rather than the visual position of the symbols, establish the identity of the cap. In an ordinary RNA chain, a residue has a different orientation relative to its preceding neighbor. The cap is often called inverted because its attachment joins equivalent five-prime ends. This does not mean the RNA polymerase has switched its direction of synthesis or that a stretch of the RNA sequence has been reversed.

The cap guanine is not counted as the first genomically encoded residue when the transcription start site is identified. If the coding DNA strand at positions plus one through plus six reads ACGTTA, the RNA sequence starts ACGUUA, before accounting for its cap. The modified guanosine attached to the first A should be described separately. Writing GACGUUA as though the first G were transcribed from DNA obscures the distinction between transcription initiation and enzymatic capping.

4. Capping as a sequence of three chemical reactions

The canonical pathway begins with a newly synthesized RNA bearing a five-prime triphosphate, which can be written pppN-RNA. It is useful to distinguish the three phosphate groups by position relative to the first ribose: alpha is closest to the ribose, beta is intermediate, and gamma is farthest away. This naming convention is the same one used for free nucleoside triphosphates. An RNA triphosphatase removes the terminal gamma phosphate by hydrolysis. The remaining RNA has a diphosphate at its beginning and the removed phosphate is released as inorganic phosphate. No guanosine has yet been attached.

The next reaction is catalyzed by RNA guanylyltransferase. Its donor substrate is GTP. The enzyme forms a covalent intermediate with the guanosine monophosphate portion of GTP, releasing pyrophosphate. A nucleophilic lysine side chain in the canonical guanylyltransferase participates in this temporary covalent bond. The activated GMP is subsequently transferred to the diphosphate-bearing RNA. The donor's alpha phosphate and the two remaining RNA phosphates make the three-phosphate bridge. Counting the phosphates from their respective substrates helps explain why the product is not simply guanosine joined to RNA by a normal phosphodiester bond.

A simplified reaction ledger writes the first step as pppRNA plus water yields ppRNA plus inorganic phosphate. The next overall step is GTP plus ppRNA yields GpppRNA plus pyrophosphate. The intermediate enzyme-GMP species is absent from the overall equation because the enzyme is regenerated. The product at this stage is a guanosine-capped RNA that still lacks the characteristic N7 methyl group. The distinction becomes particularly useful when a student is asked to predict the product in a reaction mixture that contains only the first two enzymatic activities.

The third step uses a guanine-N7 methyltransferase and S-adenosylmethionine, abbreviated SAM. SAM provides the transferable methyl group. The product is methylated guanosine attached to RNA, together with S-adenosylhomocysteine. SAM is not the donor of the guanosine, GTP does not supply the methyl group, and ATP is not the direct nucleotide donor for formation of the cap. Although cellular metabolism is needed to replenish all these substrates, the stoichiometric distinction is essential when individual reactions are examined in vitro.

The order of the reactions follows from their substrates. Guanylyltransferase normally acts on the diphosphate RNA produced by the triphosphatase. The methyltransferase can recognize the guanosine-containing product of guanylyl transfer. A loss of the triphosphatase should therefore interfere with formation of the normal cap even if both downstream enzymes are present. A defect restricted to N7 methylation can leave a GpppN structure present without the mature methylated cap. Measuring total attached guanosine and measuring mature methylated guanosine are accordingly different tests.

In mammals the triphosphatase and guanylyltransferase activities are contained in a bifunctional capping enzyme, while N7 methylation is performed by another enzyme. Budding yeast organizes the three activities differently, using separate proteins. These differences in protein organization should not be mistaken for differences in the fundamental chemical transformations. The term capping enzyme can designate a particular multifunctional protein or the collection of enzymes involved in the pathway, so a question describing an enzyme mutant should state which activity has been disrupted.

The subsequent two-prime ribose methylations use additional methyltransferases and methyl donors. They create cap variants rather than the basic five-prime to five-prime linkage itself. If an assay detects N7 methylation but not ribose methylation, it can still detect cap zero. Conversely, a methyl group detected on RNA is not automatically evidence of a cap because other RNA bases and riboses can be methylated during distinct processing reactions. An assay must distinguish the chemical identity and location of its product.

5. Why capping is coordinated with early elongation

The first end of a growing RNA emerges from the RNA exit path of polymerase II while transcription is still in progress. The cap can be made once enough RNA is accessible to the enzymes, commonly when the transcript is only a few tens of nucleotides long. This early timing limits the interval during which an uncapped end is exposed and provides a platform for RNA-associated factors while the transcript is being extended.

The largest subunit of RNA polymerase II contains a carboxy-terminal domain, often abbreviated CTD, with repeated heptapeptide motifs. The phosphorylation pattern of this domain changes during the transcription cycle. During initiation and early elongation, phosphorylation at serine five in the repeats helps recruit the capping machinery. This is a protein interaction and recruitment mechanism. Phosphorylation of the polymerase does not itself convert an RNA triphosphate into a cap, transfer guanosine, or methylate guanine.

The usefulness of recruitment is easiest to understand in terms of local concentration. If polymerase II carries a factor near its emerging RNA, encounters between the factor and its appropriate substrate become more probable than if the factor had to locate every transcript independently throughout the nucleus. Recruitment also establishes temporal order. A processing factor that is most useful near the beginning of transcription can be enriched during that stage, while proteins needed for the three-prime end become effective later. The actual cell contains overlapping interactions rather than an absolute switch between isolated sets of proteins.

An early CTD phosphorylation defect can cause an apparent capping phenotype without changing the catalytic site of any capping enzyme. If the enzyme retains activity against purified diphosphate RNA in a test tube but produces less capped RNA inside the cell, defective recruitment is one possible explanation. Another is that the perturbation also impairs transcription initiation or elongation, reducing the number of RNA substrates. These alternatives can be distinguished only by measuring the amount of synthesized RNA and the fraction that acquires a cap, alongside direct assessment of enzyme activity or localization.

The existence of early recruitment also explains why capping is associated with polymerase II rather than simply being applied to all RNA molecules containing a terminal triphosphate. A ribosomal RNA precursor synthesized by polymerase I may start with a triphosphate, but it normally enters a different maturation pathway. Its initial chemistry is not a sufficient predictor of its ultimate terminal structures. Polymerase identity, associated factors and RNA sequence or structure all contribute to substrate selection.

There are important boundaries to this comparison. Several polymerase II-derived noncoding RNAs receive caps, so capping does not prove that a molecule encodes a protein. Some small nuclear RNAs undergo further cap modifications in their particular maturation pathways. Conversely, various organellar and viral RNAs use distinct mechanisms. The canonical nuclear mRNA cap is therefore best treated as a widespread feature of a particular gene-expression system, not as a defining property of every RNA in eukaryotic cells.

6. Cap binding, protection and communication with the translational machinery

Formation of a chemical cap creates a binding site for proteins that interact selectively with it. In the nucleus, the cap-binding complex contains CBP80 and CBP20, also known as NCBP1 and NCBP2. Recognition depends on the intact cap, and the bound complex contributes to the assembly of a messenger ribonucleoprotein particle. An mRNA in the cell is normally associated with proteins rather than behaving as a permanently naked polymer.

Protection against five-prime-directed decay is partly chemical and partly a consequence of protein association. Many five-prime to three-prime exonucleases, including the familiar cytoplasmic XRN1 enzyme, prefer RNA bearing a five-prime monophosphate. The cap's triphosphate bridge and attached methylated guanosine do not provide that ordinary substrate. A decapping reaction can remove the cap and generate a suitable monophosphorylated end, allowing rapid exonucleolytic decay. The cap is thus a reversible protection rather than a guarantee of permanent stability.

The cap-binding complex helps coordinate subsequent maturation and export. It is incorrect, however, to imagine that a cap alone functions as a universal nuclear export ticket. The RNA must also assemble an appropriate complement of proteins and pass quality-control processes. Unprocessed or incorrectly processed RNA can be retained or degraded despite the presence of an intact cap. The export of mature RNA is developed more fully in Node 3.B.10.

In canonical cytoplasmic cap-dependent translation initiation, eIF4E recognizes the methylated cap. It associates with eIF4G, a scaffold that organizes other factors, and eIF4A, an RNA helicase participating in the preparation of structured RNA for ribosome recruitment. This cap-associated machinery facilitates recruitment of the small ribosomal subunit with its associated initiation factors. The ribosome does not generally begin translation by interpreting the cap guanosine as the first codon. It locates the translation initiation site within the RNA. The cap supports access to the message without changing the meaning of the coding sequence.

A cap-binding mutation can therefore decrease protein output even when the amount of messenger RNA is initially unchanged. Conversely, an uncapped transcript may disappear rapidly, causing reduced protein expression indirectly because less message remains. The two mechanisms can produce a similar endpoint in a reporter assay. Measuring RNA amount and protein output separately is necessary to establish whether the translation efficiency per RNA molecule has changed. A biochemical assay showing reduced eIF4E affinity supports a direct recognition defect but does not, on its own, quantify RNA degradation.

Cap-independent translation strategies also exist, including internal ribosome entry in appropriate contexts. Such examples demonstrate that the cap-dependent pathway is not the only means of recruiting ribosomes. They do not mean that the cap is dispensable for ordinary nuclear mRNA expression. A useful exam answer specifies the assumed pathway before predicting the consequence of removing a cap.

7. The problem of forming a precise three-prime end

The opposite end of the messenger presents a different problem from the one solved by capping. When polymerase II has synthesized the stop codon, it has not necessarily reached the end of the functional transcript. It must continue to transcribe the downstream untranslated region and the signals that direct processing. Nor is the last nucleotide synthesized by polymerase necessarily the last genomically encoded nucleotide retained in the mature messenger. The cell can define a mature RNA boundary by cutting the RNA while polymerase II is still engaged with the DNA template.

Suppose the coding DNA strand contains the sequence AATAAA in a region after the stop codon. The corresponding RNA sequence is AAUAAA. This is a common polyadenylation signal in animal nuclear transcripts, but the signal does not represent the site at which the RNA is physically cleaved. The processing machinery usually recognizes additional surrounding sequence features and makes the cut farther downstream. The six bases in the signal typically remain part of the retained three-prime untranslated region. Beyond the cut, a different region of the nascent transcript is released from the retained product and does not become part of that mature mRNA.

It is helpful to assign separate names to three landmarks: the processing signal, the cleavage site, and the transcription termination region. The processing signal recruits or stabilizes RNA-processing proteins. The cleavage site is the position where a phosphodiester bond is hydrolyzed. The termination region is where polymerase II ceases transcription and disengages from the DNA. These landmarks can occur in that order along a gene, yet their exact separation is variable. A mutation in the processing signal may cause an increase in polymerase readthrough as a secondary consequence, but the signal itself is not simply a DNA instruction for instantaneous polymerase release.

This arrangement contrasts with a hypothetical system in which polymerase stops at the very last nucleotide of every mature messenger. In that system, the precision of mature RNA length would depend entirely on the precision of transcriptional arrest. Cleavage allows processing factors to identify a suitable RNA position separately from the machinery that terminates polymerase movement. It also generates the particular end needed by poly(A) polymerase. In some genes the use of alternative cleavage sites permits production of more than one functional messenger from the same transcription unit.

The cell must discriminate among many occurrences of short sequences. A specified six-nucleotide sequence would be expected approximately once per 4,096 positions under a simplified equal-frequency, independent-base model. Real genes have unequal nucleotide frequencies and local correlations, so that expectation is not a genomic measurement. Nevertheless, the calculation illustrates why a six-base match alone is an inadequate explanation of processing specificity. Many RNA molecules can contain an AAUAAA-like sequence that is not used efficiently as a cleavage signal. Its relationship to neighboring RNA elements and the available processing factors helps determine which sites actually function.

An important distinction follows for gene annotation. The genomic interval assigned to a gene can extend downstream of the most frequently detected mature RNA end. A sequencing read that terminates at a poly(A) tail identifies a processed RNA boundary if the read is interpreted correctly. A nascent RNA read beyond the site identifies continuing transcription. The two observations need not contradict one another because they measure different molecules or different stages in the lifetime of the same transcription event.

8. Cis-acting signals and their arrangement in pre-mRNA

A cis-acting element is a sequence whose principal effect is exerted on the nucleic acid molecule on which it is located. In canonical mammalian polyadenylation, a major element is the polyadenylation signal, frequently the sequence AAUAAA. It is often situated roughly ten to thirty nucleotides upstream of the site of endonucleolytic cleavage. The closely related AUUAAA variant can also function, and additional variants are known. Consequently, AAUAAA is a useful canonical model but not a universal invariant.

The cleavage site itself frequently lies near a CA dinucleotide. The exact preference and location are influenced by the surrounding sequence and the geometry of the assembled processing complex. In simplified diagrams the site is represented by a vertical mark. This mark should be read as a location between two nucleotides, not necessarily as the destruction of one base. Hydrolytic cleavage separates the two chains while leaving the nucleotide sequence on either side otherwise intact.

A downstream sequence element, often enriched in uridine or guanosine and uridine, can support processing at the upstream site. This sequence is important even though it is not retained in the mature messenger when cleavage occurs before it. A regulatory sequence therefore need not appear in the final RNA to influence its production. If a downstream element is removed by a genomic deletion, the mature sequence upstream of the cut could in principle be unchanged, yet fewer molecules may acquire the intended three-prime end.

Additional elements upstream of the principal signal can recruit factors that influence site recognition. A UGUA motif is a familiar example in mammalian transcripts, associated with cleavage factor I. These elements do not occur in every functional site. Their contributions depend on the combination of signals and on cellular conditions. The arrangement is best understood as a distributed recognition system in which multiple protein-RNA contacts cooperate to achieve processing.

One can reason about signal spacing without memorizing an absolute nucleotide number. If the distance between the major signal and the usual cleavage site is greatly altered, proteins that bind the two regions may no longer assemble into the productive configuration used by the original transcript. A new cut may appear at a more suitable distance, or processing may be less efficient. The outcome depends on the sequence of possible nearby sites and is not uniquely determined by the fact that an insertion occurred. A question that expects a definite new cleavage coordinate must provide sufficient additional assumptions or data.

A point mutation in a signal should be analyzed at several levels. First ask whether it changes a sequence that proteins recognize. Then ask whether binding at that location is reduced or abolished, whether another nearby signal remains available, and whether a change in RNA length follows. Finally consider whether the resulting RNA has the same coding region, altered untranslated sequence, or an altered terminal coding exon. These distinctions avoid the common error of treating every polyadenylation-site mutation as a change in the encoded protein.

There is a useful analogy to promoter recognition, but it has limits. A promoter is a DNA sequence involved in initiation of transcription. A polyadenylation signal is recognized in the transcribed RNA during processing. Both can involve cooperative recognition of several sequence elements, but their molecular substrates and effects differ. The DNA sequence encoding a polyadenylation signal can be mutated before transcription; the resulting RNA is then a different substrate for processing. It is not necessary to imagine the cleavage factor binding the untranscribed promoter or acting as the DNA-directed polymerase.

The signal vocabulary also varies among eukaryotic groups. Budding yeast and plants employ combinations of sequence elements that differ from the simple canonical mammalian description. An examination question may present a typical mammalian map and expect AAUAAA, downstream U or GU-rich elements, and the cut site. The same answer should not be extended without qualification to every gene from every organism. The conserved principle is recognition of appropriate RNA features by a processing apparatus that generates a defined end.

9. Assembly of the mammalian cleavage machinery

The principal mammalian factors can be understood by following the tasks that need to be accomplished, rather than by learning a long inventory of subunits. A productive complex must recognize the RNA signals, bring the chosen site into a catalytic environment, carry out hydrolysis of a backbone bond, and coordinate the addition of adenosines to the retained RNA. No single isolated recognition event is sufficient for all of these functions.

Cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor, abbreviated CPSF, performs central roles in this process. Within mammalian CPSF, the proteins WDR33 and CPSF30 contribute to recognition of the canonical AAUAAA motif. CPSF also contains a catalytic endonuclease, CPSF73. The latter is responsible for the cleavage chemistry in the canonical system. Although both recognition and catalysis are assigned to the larger CPSF machinery, it is important to distinguish their molecular functions. A mutation that interferes with AAUAAA recognition is not the same as a mutation that inactivates the endonuclease active site.

Cleavage stimulation factor, abbreviated CstF, interacts with the downstream U-rich or GU-rich sequence region. Its CstF64 component participates in RNA binding. Productive interactions between upstream and downstream factors help define the region in which the endonuclease should cut. Other cleavage factors, including CFIm and CFIIm, make contributions to recognition or assembly, depending on the context. The scaffold protein symplekin helps organize the larger processing system. These names identify functional components of a cooperative machinery, not a strictly universal order in which one protein must attach and depart before the next arrives.

Poly(A) polymerase is associated with the machinery because the end produced by cleavage is the substrate needed for the next reaction. The recruitment of polymerase does not mean that it carries out the cut itself. One can have a polyadenylation-defective extract in which cleavage is still detected if the defect is restricted to tail synthesis and does not compromise complex assembly. In contrast, inactivating CPSF73's catalytic function can eliminate cleavage and thereby deprive poly(A) polymerase of the normal upstream product. Because these factors also interact physically, predictions about a particular protein deletion are more complicated than predictions about selective loss of its catalytic activity.

A student can organize the machinery into recognition, cutting and extension modules. Recognition is primarily associated with RNA-binding factors and sequence elements. Cutting is associated with the endonuclease activity. Extension is associated with poly(A) polymerase and accessory factors. This organization explains why the presence of all proteins does not necessarily mean the reaction will occur: recognition sites may be mutated, catalytic centers may be inactive, or the proper end substrate may not exist.

A reconstitution experiment can examine these roles. Imagine a defined RNA substrate containing the main signal, the intended cut region and downstream binding elements. When an active processing extract is incubated with this substrate, shorter RNA products may appear. A matched RNA carrying a defective signal can test sequence dependence. An extract missing one factor may show reduced processing, and adding purified functional protein may restore it. The restoration is stronger evidence for a specific factor's contribution than the observation that the factor can merely associate with RNA.

However, a failed rescue need not prove that the factor is irrelevant. The purified protein may be inactive, other components may also be lost, or the experimental conditions may not support assembly. A positive rescue can also reflect an indirect structural contribution. To distinguish catalytic from structural roles, compare rescue by wild-type protein with rescue by a catalytically inactive but assembly-competent variant. If only the wild type restores cleavage, the result supports the need for the catalytic function, provided that both forms are present and folded appropriately.

The cooperative behavior of the machinery is a reason to avoid absolute claims about a single consensus sequence. Protein binding to a weak signal may be stabilized by neighboring contacts. Strong signal recognition may not result in cleavage if the catalytic apparatus is disrupted. Efficient formation of an end is therefore an outcome of the entire assembled system. A binding assay, a cleavage assay and a tail-extension assay interrogate different parts of that system and should not be interpreted as interchangeable measurements.

10. Hydrolysis, the two RNA products and the fate of the downstream segment

Endonucleolytic cleavage acts within an RNA molecule. Unlike an exonuclease, which removes residues successively from an existing end, an endonuclease can generate new ends by breaking an internal bond. In canonical messenger three-prime processing, CPSF73 hydrolyzes a phosphodiester bond at the selected region. The upstream product contains the original capped end and the retained sequence of the eventual messenger. It has a new three-prime hydroxyl group that can be extended. The downstream product has a new five-prime monophosphate and contains sequence extending toward the transcribing polymerase.

The direction labels are crucial. The upstream product is often referred to as the five-prime cleavage product because it retains the beginning of the transcript. Nevertheless, the end relevant for polyadenylation is its newly formed three-prime end. The downstream product is the three-prime cleavage product because it contains the more downstream segment, yet its newly exposed end at the cut is a five-prime end. Confusing the name of a fragment with the polarity of its new end can lead to the erroneous conclusion that poly(A) polymerase extends the downstream fragment.

The upstream end is suitable for nucleotide addition because its terminal hydroxyl can participate in the chemistry of RNA extension. The downstream end is a substrate for a different class of enzyme. In the torpedo model of polymerase II termination, a five-prime to three-prime exonuclease such as XRN2 in mammals degrades the downstream RNA and contributes to release of the transcription complex. The separation of the upstream messenger and the continuing downstream transcript thus links processing to termination without making them the same chemical reaction.

Consider a mutation that prevents endonucleolytic cleavage. Polymerase may transcribe beyond the ordinary processing region because cleavage-dependent termination is compromised. One might observe abundant RNA spanning the normal cut site. That result is consistent with the failure to create both the upstream end used for polyadenylation and the downstream end used by the termination-associated exonuclease. In a different perturbation where cleavage is normal but the downstream exonuclease is defective, the upstream messenger can acquire its normal end while transcription persists farther downstream. The two perturbations should therefore be distinguishable by measuring cleavage and downstream transcription separately.

A diagram of a mature transcript often ends immediately after the poly(A) tail. If the drawing is used as a map of polymerase II behavior, it can be misleading. The transcribed downstream sequence is absent from the mature molecule precisely because the transcript was processed, not necessarily because the DNA was never transcribed. To establish where polymerase actually releases the template, examine an assay of engaged polymerase or nascent transcription rather than relying solely on the sequence of mature RNA.

Hydrolysis itself does not require incorporation of ATP into the cleaved product. This is distinct from the energetic requirements of the larger transcription and processing machinery. The catalytic endonuclease uses metal-dependent phosphodiester hydrolysis; surrounding processes can involve nucleotide-dependent enzymes or ATP-consuming regulatory steps. When an examination asks about the direct substrate needed for the cleavage reaction, it is important not to substitute the substrate of poly(A) polymerase merely because both processes are grouped together under three-prime end formation.

Sequence-directed cleavage is also not equivalent to removal of an intron. A spliceosome joins the flanking exons across an intron through a distinct reaction sequence, preserving continuity of the mature RNA. Canonical terminal cleavage separates the retained upstream portion from a discarded downstream portion, and it is followed by nucleotide addition rather than exon ligation at the cut. Both processes break or rearrange RNA backbone bonds, but the biological products differ.

11. Poly(A) polymerase and template-independent extension

Poly(A) polymerase adds adenosine residues to the three-prime hydroxyl of the retained RNA. Its nucleotide substrate is ATP. In the reaction, the oxygen of the terminal hydroxyl attacks the alpha phosphate of ATP; the beta and gamma phosphates depart together as pyrophosphate. The newly added residue becomes the new terminus and presents a hydroxyl that can participate in the next round of addition. Repetition of the reaction generates a chain of adenosine monophosphate residues.

The ordinary three-prime to five-prime phosphodiester linkage is formed during this extension. Thus the chemical orientation of the poly(A) tail is continuous with that of the retained transcript, unlike the inverted linkage of the cap. It is reasonable to describe the tail as an extension of the RNA backbone, whereas the cap is an attachment at the opposite end through an exceptional connection.

The polymerase is called template independent because each added adenosine is not specified through base pairing with a corresponding thymidine in DNA. The enzyme's nucleotide preference and the available ATP determine the characteristic homopolymeric product. This does not mean that site selection is independent of the original transcript sequence. The location at which the extension starts is established by upstream processing events that recognize RNA sequences and generate the substrate. Template independence applies to the repeated incorporation reaction, not to the entire pathway's recognition logic.

For every adenosine incorporated into the tail, one ATP molecule supplies an AMP residue and releases one pyrophosphate. A tail of 150 newly added residues therefore requires 150 direct ATP incorporation events, and generates 150 pyrophosphate molecules at this reaction level. Subsequent hydrolysis of pyrophosphate into two inorganic phosphate ions can favor the forward direction. It would be incorrect to describe the tail as the product of 150 DNA-directed transcription steps simply because the fundamental phosphodiester formation chemistry resembles that of RNA polymerase II.

One must be precise when discussing energy consumption. The ATP used by poly(A) polymerase is both the chemical substrate and the source of an activated phosphate group for extension. The reaction is not simply hydrolysis of ATP to ADP. Each incorporated nucleotide loses its beta and gamma phosphates as pyrophosphate and retains the alpha phosphate in the RNA backbone. Cellular regeneration of ATP and metabolism of pyrophosphate involve additional energetic considerations, but they are separate from counting the direct nucleotide substrates required for the tail.

Poly(A) polymerase must act in the correct location. An isolated polymerase may extend appropriate RNA ends under suitable in vitro conditions without reproducing normal cellular processing specificity. In cells the cleavage and polyadenylation machinery recruits and regulates the polymerase. The enzymatic ability to make an adenosine chain is distinct from the biological ability to put that chain onto the intended RNA at its intended position.

The terms processive and distributive describe how an enzyme interacts with a growing polymer. A processive enzyme can carry out multiple rounds of addition before dissociating from a substrate. A distributive enzyme frequently dissociates after one or a few additions and must bind again for continued extension. Tail extension is supported by interactions involving nuclear poly(A)-binding proteins and the processing machinery, which enhance productive repeated additions. It is not necessary for a completely new transcript to bind a separate poly(A) polymerase for every added nucleotide.

A change in the rate of incorporation can alter the length distribution observed after a fixed incubation time. However, a shorter tail in a cellular sample may also arise because addition stops earlier, because tails are shortened after synthesis, or because the sample contains a different distribution of RNA ages. To distinguish these alternatives, it is useful to compare newly made nuclear RNA with later cytoplasmic RNA and to examine a time course rather than interpreting a single average length as a direct measurement of polymerase speed.

12. Nuclear poly(A)-binding protein and tail length

The production of a tail introduces a new molecular surface on the messenger. A poly(A)-binding protein can associate with the growing adenosine chain. In mammalian nuclei, PABPN1 is an important factor involved in efficient synthesis and length control. Its cooperation with poly(A) polymerase and CPSF helps support repeated incorporation of adenosines as the tail grows. Nuclear tails often reach lengths on the order of a few hundred residues in mammals, but tail lengths are not fixed at a universal integer.

One reason to distinguish length control from length measurement is that an average can conceal substantial variation. Different transcripts and cells can have different tail-length distributions. Measurements made immediately after nuclear processing can differ from measurements made later because shortening and sometimes further extension have occurred. Yeast nuclear tails are generally shorter than typical newly synthesized mammalian nuclear tails. The exact value used in an examination problem should therefore be treated as a supplied biological condition or an approximate example, not as an invariant definition of polyadenylation.

PABPN1 is distinct from the major cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding proteins, commonly called PABPC proteins. During maturation and export, the complement of proteins associated with the transcript changes. The nuclear protein's contribution to formation of the tail should not be attributed automatically to the cytoplasmic protein simply because both bind adenosine-rich RNA. A question specifying nuclear tail synthesis is likely to require the nuclear protein's role; a question specifying cytoplasmic interaction with initiation factors refers to a different molecular context.

A reduction of PABPN1 activity may impair normal tail formation or length control and influence the subsequent handling of the RNA. The precise phenotype depends on how the perturbation is made and the transcripts examined. A simple loss of all protein can produce broader defects than a mutation selectively altering its interaction with poly(A) polymerase. The appropriate prediction is that tail synthesis and length distribution can change, followed by analysis of the actual direction and magnitude from the supplied experimental observations.

Consider two reactions using a fixed amount of cleaved RNA substrate. One contains polymerase alone, whereas the other includes the appropriate accessory factors. If the second generates a larger proportion of long products within the same time interval, this supports stimulation of tail extension under the tested conditions. It does not by itself establish whether individual polymerase molecules are more processive, whether substrate binding is improved, or whether their catalytic rate has increased. Distinguishing these possibilities requires an assay capable of separating repeated addition from substrate association and dissociation.

The physical length of the tail also has implications for measurements. Each appended residue increases RNA length by one nucleotide. An RNA molecule with 1,200 genomically encoded residues retained after splicing and cleavage and 180 appended adenosines contains 1,380 residues before counting its additional cap guanosine. It is incorrect to add the downstream discarded RNA or the introns to this mature length. The result is a count of nucleotides, not a molecular mass, and should not be reported in base pairs because the messenger is not a double-stranded DNA segment.

13. The mature tail as a protein-binding surface

The adenosines at the three-prime end of a messenger are not primarily intended to become part of the protein product. A ribosome normally encounters a translation stop signal before entering the tail. Instead, the tail constitutes a region where proteins involved in the lifetime and use of the messenger can bind. Cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding proteins recognize stretches of adenosines and establish interactions with additional RNA-associated factors. It is these protein interactions as well as the RNA sequence itself that give the tail its biological significance.

An RNA molecule without a tail can sometimes be translated. This is especially clear when one considers the normal existence of particular nonpolyadenylated mRNAs. The correct claim is that a poly(A) tail commonly contributes to the expression of canonical polyadenylated messengers in eukaryotes. Its effect depends on the transcript, the organism, the associated proteins, and the stage of development. Treating every RNA without a long poly(A) tail as a nonfunctional molecule would erase important biological exceptions.

The terminal location of the tail is useful because it allows a cell to change the state of an RNA without necessarily modifying its coding sequence. During gradual removal of adenosines by deadenylases, the sequence specifying amino acids can remain intact for a considerable period. A shortening tail can nevertheless change the RNA's complement of bound proteins and its susceptibility to subsequent decay. This separates modification of an RNA's regulatory state from destruction of the information-bearing part of the molecule.

Consider the protective effect of the tail in terms of substrates for enzymes. Three-prime-directed nucleases remove nucleotides from exposed or appropriately presented three-prime ends. A tail can provide an expendable region before the unique sequence of the messenger is reached, but this is not the whole explanation of protection. Proteins bound to the tail influence access of other enzymes and communicate with regulatory machinery. The poly(A) tail itself can also be a substrate for dedicated enzymes that actively shorten it. It is therefore more accurate to describe tail-associated protection as regulated than to imagine that the adenosines form an impenetrable physical shield.

A reduction in tail length may precede removal of the cap and rapid five-prime-directed degradation, or it may be followed by decay from the opposite end through different pathways. The details of RNA turnover are developed elsewhere in the curriculum. At this stage the important insight is that both ends of the messenger are integrated into its lifetime. The cap and tail do not operate as two independent permanent seals. Their associated proteins can participate in coordinated transitions from a messenger that is available for protein synthesis to one committed to removal.

A transcript population can exhibit a mixture of states. Some molecules are newly made and have recently acquired their nuclear tails. Others have spent time in the cytoplasm and carry shorter tails. Still others may be undergoing active changes in tail length that accompany regulation. An average tail length measured from such a population is not automatically the initial length specified during nuclear processing. This distinction becomes particularly important when comparing two tissues with different transcription rates or different distributions of messenger ages.

14. Functional cooperation between cap and tail

The five-prime cap and three-prime tail are physically separated along the linear sequence, but the RNA is flexible. Proteins interacting with the two termini can bring them into functional proximity. In a widely used model of cap-dependent translation, eIF4E binds the cap and eIF4G provides interaction surfaces for other initiation factors. Cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein can bind eIF4G while it associates with the tail. This arrangement provides a plausible molecular basis for cooperation between the two ends in recruitment of translation machinery.

The term closed-loop model describes a messenger whose ends are brought close through such protein interactions. It does not mean that an additional covalent phosphodiester bond is formed to connect the two RNA ends. A closed-loop messenger can still have chemically distinct five-prime and three-prime termini. If the proteins are removed, the covalent structure of the RNA is still linear. This is different from a truly circular RNA in which the RNA backbone itself forms a continuous circle.

The model explains why combining the two normal terminal features can have a stronger effect on translation than either feature alone in suitable experimental systems. Binding at one end can stabilize or promote the recruitment of machinery associated with the other. A messenger bearing a cap and a tail is therefore not just the sum of two unrelated chemical modifications. The functional state depends on interactions among the proteins recruited by those modifications.

Nevertheless, a model of a protein-mediated loop should not be taken as proof that every actively translated messenger forms a stable loop at every instant. The conformation of an RNA changes, and different translation states need not have identical spatial arrangements. A reduction in translation after interfering with the PABP-eIF4G interaction supports functional cooperation in that system, but it does not by itself measure the geometric shape of individual messenger molecules.

A useful experimental approach is to construct four otherwise identical reporter RNAs. The first has neither terminal feature, the second has a cap only, the third has a tail only, and the fourth has both. If the doubly modified RNA produces far more reporter activity than the others, the result is consistent with cooperative effects. But the comparison becomes more informative when the investigator measures the concentration of each reporter RNA at the time of protein measurement. An increase in reporter output can result from longer survival, better translation per molecule, or a combination of the two.

Suppose two reporters have equal RNA concentrations at the end of a short translation assay, but one produces twice as much protein during a controlled measurement interval. If protein removal and the prior histories of RNA concentration are negligible or matched, the difference supports more efficient production of protein from that reporter. If the RNA concentrations are unequal, protein output alone cannot identify the contribution of translation efficiency. Measuring the rate of protein synthesis rather than only accumulated protein helps to resolve this issue.

The principle of end cooperation also explains the utility of perturbing the recognition proteins rather than altering the RNA itself. A reporter can possess a chemically correct cap yet fail to exploit it efficiently if eIF4E recognition is disrupted. A normal poly(A) tail can coexist with impaired functional cooperation if an interaction surface on eIF4G is altered. Such experiments distinguish the structural presence of a modification from its downstream biological interpretation. They are relevant whenever an examination asks whether a normal molecular structure is sufficient for normal activity.

15. Multiple cleavage sites and transcript diversity

A gene need not always use a single three-prime processing site. If two or more functional processing signals occur in the same transcription unit, the processing machinery can generate messenger populations with different ends. This phenomenon is known as alternative cleavage and polyadenylation. The difference is not merely variation in the number of adenosines attached to a common end. The position of the retained genomic sequence itself can change.

Imagine an exon containing a stop codon followed by a long untranslated region. There is an upstream polyadenylation site within that untranslated region and a second site farther downstream. If cleavage occurs at the first site, the mature messenger contains the shorter portion of the untranslated region. If cleavage occurs at the second, it contains the additional downstream sequence. Both molecules can specify the same amino acid chain because the stop codon and all preceding coding residues are unchanged. Their regulatory behavior can differ because the longer untranslated region provides additional binding sites for RNA-associated proteins and other regulators.

The downstream sequence can contain an element that reduces RNA stability in a particular cell type. A transcript using the upstream site would lack this element, potentially increasing its stability relative to the longer isoform. However, this is a conditional prediction, not a universal rule that shorter untranslated regions always make more protein. Some downstream sequences promote stability, localization or efficient translation. Removing such a sequence could decrease expression or change the place in the cell where the message is used. The biological consequence depends on the identity and activity of the regulatory information retained.

Alternative end formation can also affect protein structure in certain genes. If the selected sites occur in different terminal exons or are coupled to alternative splicing in a way that changes the coding region, the resulting messages can specify proteins with different carboxy-terminal sequences. These cases should be separated from alternative site selection within a shared terminal untranslated region. Without a diagram of the coding and untranslated portions, one cannot infer from the mere existence of alternative polyadenylation whether the proteins differ.

The order of transcription introduces an additional constraint. Polymerase encounters a proximal site before a distal one. Efficient processing at the proximal site can remove the upstream RNA from the continuing transcript before the distal site becomes available for that same messenger. If proximal processing is inefficient, more transcripts can continue far enough to make the distal option accessible. This provides a simple framework for understanding competition between sites. Actual usage also depends on the strength of each site, the rate at which RNA is transcribed and the availability of processing factors.

One possible outcome of weakening a proximal signal is an increase in distal isoform formation. The increase is not guaranteed if the distal site is itself ineffective, if uncleaved transcripts are degraded, or if transcription fails to reach the distal region. A reduction in the proportion of proximal isoforms may therefore be accompanied by an increase, a decrease or little change in the total amount of mature RNA. Relative proportions and absolute abundances must be distinguished.

Consider a sample with 80 molecules using the proximal site and 20 using the distal site. The proximal fraction is eighty percent. Following a perturbation, there may be 20 proximal and 20 distal molecules. The distal fraction has increased from twenty to fifty percent, although the absolute distal abundance has not increased at all. A graph of percentages alone could mislead a student into claiming increased distal production. The two isoforms may also have different decay rates, so steady-state abundance is not always equivalent to the frequency of their formation.

These distinctions are particularly useful in analytical questions. To infer which processing site is selected during synthesis, a measurement of newly formed ends is more direct than a count of accumulated RNA molecules. To infer the functional consequences of site usage, protein output, stability and localization may be relevant. A single measurement rarely answers every question about production and function. The molecular explanation should begin by identifying what is being measured.

16. A regulated tail after the RNA leaves the nucleus

Although the canonical tail is first synthesized in the nucleus, tail length need not remain unchanged in the cytoplasm. Deadenylation can shorten existing tails, and cytoplasmic polyadenylation can lengthen selected tails. These reactions provide a way to modify the behavior of RNA molecules that are already present without requiring another round of transcription from DNA.

The biological value of this possibility is apparent during early animal development. Some maternal messenger RNAs are stored in oocytes and used at later developmental stages. The amount of a particular protein may need to rise at a time when new transcription is limited or when the cell must respond quickly to developmental signals. Regulation of pre-existing messenger RNA can meet that requirement. Lengthening the tails of selected maternal RNAs can be associated with increased translation in such contexts.

Cytoplasmic polyadenylation should not be confused with the standard endonucleolytic processing event in the nucleus. An existing cytoplasmic messenger already possesses its mature genomic end. Additional adenosines can be appended to its tail without repeating the original cleavage event. The distinction matters when an experimental RNA shifts toward a longer apparent size after a developmental signal. If the genomic sequence of the three-prime untranslated region remains unchanged and the added length is due to adenosines, extension of an existing tail is a plausible explanation. If the genomic boundary changes, alternative cleavage must instead be considered.

Cytoplasmic polyadenylation can be controlled by sequences in the untranslated region and proteins recognizing those sequences. Cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding proteins provide a familiar example of a system through which selected messages can be regulated during development. The essential lesson here is the capacity to alter the terminal state of a message selectively. The full signaling networks that direct developmental gene expression belong to the relevant developmental and gene-regulation chapters.

A longer tail is often associated with a more active translation state in oocytes and early embryos, but a direct positive relationship between tail length and translation efficiency is not equally strong in all cell types. In many mature somatic cell contexts, substantial differences in tail length do not produce a simple proportional change in translation. Several additional factors determine how effectively a particular messenger engages ribosomes. A question supplying data from a defined cell type should be interpreted using those data rather than a presumed universal proportionality.

Deadenylation and cytoplasmic extension may also complicate a pulse-chase experiment. A labeled RNA population might exhibit a changing tail length without a change in the amount of unique RNA sequence. If the investigator measures only total RNA size, the experiment may appear to show disappearance or production of particular RNA species. Separate identification of the genomic portion and the terminal tail makes it possible to recognize that a pre-existing population has undergone terminal remodeling.

17. The exceptional three-prime ends of replication-dependent histone mRNAs

One of the most important counterexamples to a simple textbook description of eukaryotic messenger RNA is provided by canonical replication-dependent histone transcripts in animals. Histones needed for packaging newly replicated DNA are produced in coordination with the cell cycle. Many of the corresponding messengers do not receive the conventional long poly(A) tail. Instead, their mature three-prime ends include a conserved stem-loop structure and nearby sequence features involved in specialized processing.

The stem-loop is an RNA secondary structure formed when complementary sequences within the same molecule pair to produce a short helix joined by a loop. The stem-loop binding protein, abbreviated SLBP, recognizes this structure. A downstream histone element participates in recognition by U7 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein through RNA base pairing. Together with processing factors, these interactions support cleavage at the appropriate histone RNA end. CPSF73, the endonuclease used in canonical messenger cleavage, also participates in histone end formation in the specialized machinery.

Notice both the similarity and the difference. In the two pathways a nuclear transcript is cleaved to generate a defined mature boundary, and some catalytic machinery is shared. The signals that guide recognition differ, and the replication-dependent histone messenger does not normally proceed through the typical long-tail addition step. A lack of poly(A) in this context therefore reflects an alternative normal processing system rather than an incomplete ordinary messenger.

SLBP also contributes to the proper expression and regulated turnover of the histone messages. The cell can thereby coordinate histone availability with DNA synthesis. If production of histone mRNA continued unchecked after DNA replication ended, inappropriate histone amounts could be problematic. The distinct end structure provides an opportunity for regulatory control adapted to the biological needs of this group of messages. Detailed cell-cycle signaling and histone metabolism are beyond the scope of the present discussion.

The qualifier replication dependent is essential. Histone genes and histone variants are not all processed identically. Many replication-independent histone variant messages are polyadenylated, and there are organism-specific differences. A question that simply asks whether all histone mRNAs lack tails is therefore asking an overbroad question. The well-established examination exception refers particularly to the canonical replication-dependent histone mRNAs in animals.

The exception has direct methodological implications. Oligo(dT) selection enriches RNAs containing accessible stretches of adenosine. A normal nonpolyadenylated histone messenger can be underrepresented or absent in the selected fraction while present in total RNA. Failure to recover it from that fraction does not demonstrate that its gene is not transcribed. An investigator studying this RNA should choose a method that does not assume every messenger has a long terminal adenosine chain.

The same reasoning applies to other RNA classes. Ribosomal and transfer RNAs have their own normal maturation pathways and do not require the canonical mRNA tail to perform their respective functions. Their depletion from poly(A)-selected samples is useful for some gene-expression measurements but should not be interpreted as evidence that the depleted molecules are biologically unimportant.

18. Distinguishing eukaryotic tails from bacterial and organellar RNA tails

It is tempting to treat a polyadenosine sequence as a universal indicator that an RNA has been stabilized. Comparative biology shows why this is an unsafe rule. Bacterial RNA processing and degradation differ from the nuclear messenger maturation pathway discussed so far. Bacterial transcripts typically do not acquire the characteristic eukaryotic N7-methylguanosine cap and long stabilizing tail as a standard coupled maturation program. Nevertheless, adenosine-containing tails can be added to bacterial RNAs.

In bacteria such as Escherichia coli, polyadenylation can facilitate degradation of certain RNA molecules. Structured RNA ends may resist attack by some three-prime-directed exonucleases because the enzyme needs accessible single-stranded RNA to engage the substrate. An added tail provides such an accessible extension. It can allow an enzyme to begin degradation and approach the structured region. This role differs from the familiar protection and translation-associated functions of the canonical nuclear eukaryotic messenger tail.

The comparison illustrates an important principle of molecular biology: the effect of a chemical feature depends on the machinery that recognizes it. The same class of nucleotides at an RNA terminus can recruit stabilizing proteins in one context and facilitate access by degradative machinery in another. The function cannot be inferred from the base composition of the tail alone. One must identify the organism, RNA class, subcellular compartment and participating proteins.

Many bacterial transcripts also undergo transcription and translation in the same cellular compartment, allowing ribosomes to interact with RNA before transcription is completed. Nuclear eukaryotic messages generally pass through additional maturation and export steps before they encounter cytoplasmic ribosomes. These contrasting organizational arrangements contribute to differences in RNA handling. They should not be reduced to the simplistic claim that bacterial RNA never undergoes processing or that bacterial messages are always unmodified.

Organellar RNAs provide further examples of context-dependent terminal modification. In chloroplasts, addition of adenosine-rich tails can participate in RNA decay, whereas in mammalian mitochondria polyadenylation can help complete stop codons of certain transcripts whose encoded sequence ends before a complete stop triplet. Mitochondrial messenger tail functions vary by transcript and organism. These examples are sufficient to establish the distinction between a canonical nuclear messenger pathway and other RNA systems without importing unrelated details of organellar gene expression.

For an examination, first specify the system. A question about a typical mammalian nuclear messenger expects cleavage-directed addition by poly(A) polymerase and the roles of nuclear and cytoplasmic binding proteins. A question about bacterial RNA turnover may expect the opposite association between tail addition and degradation. A question about mitochondrial stop-codon completion concerns a separate biological context in which added adenosines can contribute to the stop codon itself. The apparent contradictions vanish when the particular molecular pathway is identified.

19. From biochemical observation to a model of RNA maturation

The concept of messenger RNA processing was established through experiments that distinguished newly synthesized nuclear RNA from the molecules associated with cytoplasmic ribosomes. A model in which every messenger is produced in its final form directly from DNA would predict that the essential molecular properties of the message are determined entirely by transcription. The discovery of unusual terminal structures required that model to be enlarged. It became necessary to explain how cellular enzymes alter a transcript after its first residues are made and before it is fully used.

In 1971, Mary Edmonds, M. H. Vaughan Jr. and Hiroshi Nakazato reported covalently attached polyadenylate sequences in heterogeneous nuclear RNA and in rapidly labeled polyribosome-associated RNA from HeLa cells. The study provided evidence compatible with a precursor relationship between nuclear RNA and cytoplasmic messenger RNA. It did not establish that every large nuclear RNA molecule becomes a mature messenger, nor did it establish that every RNA has a polyadenosine tail. The importance of the work was that a distinctive feature could be used to investigate relationships between RNA populations rather than treating every molecule in a heterogeneous population as identical.

Imagine following a biochemical label through two cellular compartments. A short labeling interval marks newly synthesized molecules, and a later period allows their fates to be followed. If labeled RNA bearing a particular terminal feature is first detected in the nucleus and subsequently in the cytoplasmic ribosome-associated population, the time order is consistent with maturation and movement of RNA. It does not by itself prove that every cytoplasmic molecule came from the particular nuclear population being measured. Distinct RNA populations could acquire the same feature, and contamination during fractionation could introduce a misleading signal. The quality of the precursor argument depends on controls for fraction purity and on additional molecular evidence that relates the retained sequences.

A biochemical challenge in studying polyadenylate is to demonstrate that it forms a covalently attached part of an RNA molecule. If free poly(A) chains merely associate noncovalently with larger RNAs, a separation technique could recover them together without establishing that they are connected. Conditions that disrupt noncovalent associations while preserving the RNA backbone help test this alternative. Finding adenosine-rich segments associated with the larger RNA under such conditions supports covalent attachment. Mapping the position of the segment at the three-prime end requires additional evidence.

An independent historical line of inquiry concerned the five-prime structure of viral messenger RNAs. During the 1970s, studies of RNA synthesized by enzymes associated with viruses such as reovirus helped reveal an unusual methylated terminal guanosine linked through a triphosphate bridge. These systems permitted the reactions producing different terminal structures to be studied under controlled biochemical conditions. The viral experiments were not evidence that every cellular messenger is generated by a viral polymerase. Rather, they revealed a chemical modification whose biological significance could be examined in relation to eukaryotic translation and cellular RNA processing.

In a 1975 study, Both, Furuichi, Muthukrishnan and Shatkin examined association of reovirus mRNA with ribosomal subunits using a wheat germ translation system. Their results showed a requirement for the methylated terminal structure under the conditions examined. An especially informative aspect was the distinction between a full messenger bearing the appropriate cap and a short cap-containing oligonucleotide, which did not reproduce the behavior of the full message. The cap was necessary for the tested mode of initiation, but its mere presence on an isolated small RNA fragment did not make that fragment equivalent to a functional messenger.

The general logic of these experiments is the same logic needed in a Part C question. A biochemical modification should be identified by direct chemical analysis. Its necessity for a biological process is investigated by removing or preventing it while keeping other important variables matched. Its sufficiency is a different question, tested by asking whether supplying it alone can produce the outcome. A feature can be necessary in a specified system without being sufficient, and it can be important in one system without being universally required in every system.

Today, experiments on terminal processing can connect chemical identity, protein recognition, RNA fate and cellular phenotype. These are different layers of evidence. An enzyme that transfers a radiolabeled group onto RNA has a demonstrated chemical activity under the assay conditions. Its deletion in cells may also reduce the abundance of mature RNA. Combining those findings makes a stronger mechanistic case than either observation alone, provided alternative explanations involving transcription or degradation have been considered. The historical development of mRNA processing is therefore also a lesson in constructing a causal explanation from several complementary kinds of measurements.

20. Chemical tracing and selective perturbation of capping

A cap contains material derived from two different sources: the guanosine donor and the original RNA end. It is possible to predict the distribution of an isotope label according to which substrate and phosphate position is labeled. The first RNA nucleotide is distinct from the incoming nucleotides that extend the chain because the first nucleotide initially retains all three of its phosphates. If its gamma phosphate is labeled, the canonical triphosphatase reaction should release that label as inorganic phosphate. The alpha and beta phosphates that remain attached to the first RNA nucleotide contribute to the final cap bridge.

A different labeling arrangement uses GTP with a label in the alpha phosphate. During guanylyl transfer, the donor alpha phosphate remains attached to GMP and is incorporated into the new bridge. If the label instead occupies the gamma phosphate of the donor GTP, it is released with pyrophosphate rather than incorporated as part of the cap guanosine. These predictions follow from the reaction chemistry and do not require the student to memorize a particular historical radioisotope experiment.

Labeling the transferable methyl group of SAM provides a different means of following modification. If methylated RNA products appear after addition of an appropriate methyltransferase, the observation supports transfer of a methyl group. However, cells have many methyltransferases, and incorporation of methyl label into total RNA does not establish that the guanine cap has been methylated. A cap-specific chemical separation or structural analysis is required to identify the modification and its location. This is a recurring lesson: a substrate label can show transfer of material without uniquely establishing the identity of the reaction product.

To reconstruct the capping pathway experimentally, one could compare a defined triphosphate-ended RNA with a diphosphate-ended RNA in separate reaction mixtures. A mixture with an active guanylyltransferase and the diphosphate substrate should support the transfer step if the conditions are suitable. Failure to process the triphosphate-ended substrate in the absence of triphosphatase would support the need for the preceding reaction. Adding the missing activity should restore the normal sequence of reactions. A catalytically inactive preparation of that activity is a useful negative control because it tests whether the relevant effect depends on catalysis rather than simply adding another protein to the mixture.

A useful way to reason about such an experiment is to distinguish substrate accumulation from product loss. When a pathway contains three consecutive reactions and the middle step is blocked, the product of the first step may accumulate if it is still being produced and is not rapidly degraded. The final product will decrease because the third step receives less substrate. In a purified reaction mixture this prediction can be relatively direct. Inside a cell, however, uncapped RNA may be removed or transcription may be altered, so accumulation of the predicted intermediate is not guaranteed. A decrease in final product is often easier to observe than accumulation of an unstable precursor.

Consider a hypothetical mixture in which the fraction of RNA bearing a guanosine attachment is eighty percent but the fraction containing methylated guanosine caps is only twenty percent. If the assays have identical calibration and refer to the same total RNA population, sixty percent of the population bears an unmethylated guanosine attachment. The conditional fraction of attached guanosines that are methylated is twenty divided by eighty, or twenty-five percent. It would be incorrect to say that methylation efficiency is twenty percent among attached guanosines because the denominator of that calculation is all RNA. The proper denominator depends on the scientific question.

This distinction can be applied to a mutant capping enzyme. Suppose transcription remains unchanged, guanosine attachment is nearly normal, and methylated caps become rare. The most direct interpretation is impairment of the methylation stage or of the availability of its substrate. A triphosphatase defect or a guanylyltransferase defect alone would be less compatible with preserved guanosine attachment. Nevertheless, a downstream methyltransferase defect could arise from a change in enzyme recruitment, abundance or catalytic function. The biochemical pattern localizes the failed stage, while additional tests establish the precise molecular cause.

21. Observing cleavage and tail synthesis separately

A transcript can be analyzed by denaturing gel electrophoresis, which reduces the influence of RNA secondary structure on migration. Smaller RNA molecules generally migrate more rapidly than larger molecules under appropriately chosen conditions. This permits the investigator to ask whether an RNA substrate remains full length, has been cut into fragments, or has acquired additional terminal nucleotides. The interpretation requires a suitable size marker and knowledge of which parts of the RNA are detected by the chosen label or probe.

Imagine a 1,000-nucleotide synthetic RNA containing a functional processing region. The intended cut leaves a 700-nucleotide upstream product and a 300-nucleotide downstream product. A probe binding near the original five-prime end detects the intact molecule and the 700-nucleotide upstream fragment, but not the downstream fragment. A probe binding only near the original three-prime end detects the intact RNA and the 300-nucleotide fragment. Thus the absence of a band at 300 nucleotides in an experiment using only the upstream probe does not demonstrate that cleavage has failed. It may merely indicate that the product lacks the probe-binding sequence.

Now suppose 180 adenosines are added to the upstream product. The extended molecule has a length of 880 nucleotides, ignoring any special terminal cap contribution to the size estimate. A gel could show the intact precursor at 1,000 nucleotides, the cleaved unextended product near 700 nucleotides and the polyadenylated product near 880 nucleotides. If the tails differ in length among molecules, the extended products may form a broad band or smear. The distribution can be biologically informative because it indicates a population of terminal lengths rather than one precise product.

A catalytic defect in the cleavage reaction can leave more intact precursor and reduce both expected cleavage products. A defect in tail extension can instead permit accumulation of the 700-nucleotide product while reducing the extended species. This distinction assumes that the cleaved product is stable enough to be detected and that the perturbation does not prevent assembly of the whole complex. If it is rapidly degraded, the absence of an upstream band is not definitive evidence of failed cleavage. An early time point or independent assay of cut-end formation can help discriminate these explanations.

Polyadenosine can also be detected through hybridization with a complementary oligodeoxythymidine sequence. This property permits enrichment of polyadenylated RNAs and can provide a way to investigate tail-associated differences. Hybridization alone does not identify the location of the adenosine stretch; an internal A-rich region could also bind the complementary oligonucleotide. A convincing analysis of a terminal tail should establish that the stretch lies after the retained genomic sequence and should distinguish it from any genomically encoded run of adenosines.

One useful conceptual experiment employs RNase H, an enzyme that cleaves RNA in an RNA-DNA hybrid. If an oligodeoxythymidine molecule hybridizes to an accessible poly(A) region, RNase H action can remove or shorten that region. Comparison of the RNA size before and after the treatment can support an inference about the contribution of the adenosine tail to its length. A matched treatment without the complementary DNA provides a control for nonspecific degradation. The experiment has limitations: incomplete hybridization, internal A-rich regions and incomplete digestion can make the apparent size difference an imperfect measure of actual tail length.

A reporter carrying two processing signals can be analyzed in much the same way. If the proximal and distal cleavage sites differ by 400 nucleotides of retained genomic sequence, the two RNA isoforms differ in length even when their tails are equal. If tail lengths also vary, the apparent separation may be broadened. An assay designed to identify the exact cleavage boundary, rather than size alone, is needed to prove alternative site selection. Combining terminal sequence information with length measurements can distinguish altered cleavage position from altered tail extension.

22. Measuring abundance, synthesis and stability with simple models

The amount of a mature messenger at a particular moment depends on how rapidly molecules enter that population and how rapidly they leave it. The formation rate is not necessarily the same as the rate of transcription initiation. If a fraction of newly transcribed molecules fails to complete capping or three-prime processing, fewer molecules may enter the functional mature population even when initiation remains unchanged.

A simple model represents the number of mature molecules by M and their rate of formation by s, in molecules per minute. If every mature molecule has the same constant probability of removal per unit time, the rate of disappearance is kM, where k has units of reciprocal minutes. The balance equation is dM/dt = s − kM. At steady state, formation and disappearance rates are equal, giving M = s/k. This result shows why a reduction in messenger abundance cannot be attributed automatically to reduced transcription. A reduction in the formation of properly processed molecules or an increase in the removal rate can lower the steady-state abundance.

For example, if 60 mature molecules enter the population every minute and the removal constant is 0.03 per minute, the steady-state population in the model is 2,000 molecules. If a processing mutation halves mature RNA formation but leaves the removal constant unchanged, the new steady-state population is 1,000 molecules. If instead the formation rate is unchanged and the removal constant doubles, the same population of 1,000 molecules is predicted. Steady-state RNA abundance alone cannot distinguish these mechanistically distinct outcomes.

If new synthesis is stopped in an idealized experiment, the formation term becomes zero. The equation is then dM/dt = −kM. Dividing by M and integrating gives ln(M/M0) = −kt, where M0 is the amount at the start of the observation and t is elapsed time. Exponentiation gives M = M0 exp(−kt). The half-life is the time at which the remaining amount is one-half the initial amount. Substituting that condition yields t_half = ln(2)/k, approximately 0.693/k.

An RNA population that falls from 800 molecules to 200 molecules in 40 minutes has undergone two halvings if the single-exponential model holds. Its inferred half-life is therefore 20 minutes, and k is approximately 0.0347 per minute. If a capped control population has a half-life of 40 minutes under the same conditions, the removal constant of the shorter-lived population is twice that of the control. The conclusion concerns the net disappearance rate, not the identity of the nuclease. Establishing which biochemical step has changed requires independent evidence.

The model makes assumptions that are especially important for terminal processing. All molecules are treated as if they are equivalent, whereas a real population can contain messages with different tail lengths, different bound proteins or different subcellular locations. Some molecules may need to pass through several steps before they are degraded. A population can then show a lag, or a mixture of fast and slow disappearance, rather than a single exponential curve. It would be inappropriate to force such a dataset into a simple model without checking whether its predictions fit.

The same care is needed when using an inhibitor to stop transcription. An inhibitor may affect more than RNA production, and a change in cellular physiology can itself modify RNA stability. A pulse-chase or controlled reporter strategy can provide alternative approaches to following a defined RNA population. Whatever the method, the scientific purpose is to distinguish changes in the formation of mature messages from changes in their survival, not simply to obtain a number labeled half-life.

A related calculation concerns processing yield. Suppose polymerase produces 120 completed primary transcripts per minute and eighty percent enter a properly cleaved population. If only seventy-five percent of that cleaved population acquire a suitable tail, the formation rate of fully processed three-prime ends is 120 × 0.80 × 0.75, or 72 per minute, under the stipulated sequential conditional probabilities. The percentages should not be added, and independence should not be assumed without justification. The second percentage is already conditional on the first event. A separate cap-efficiency factor can be multiplied only if it is expressed with the appropriate conditional denominator or if independence is explicitly assumed.

23. Applications in gene expression assays and biotechnology

A processing signal is an essential consideration when designing a eukaryotic gene-expression construct. Introducing a protein-coding sequence under a suitable promoter may allow the sequence to be transcribed, yet the resulting RNA can be poorly expressed if its downstream processing information is defective. A functional expression unit therefore includes more than the promoter and open reading frame. Appropriate untranslated regions and terminal processing features support production of an RNA that can participate in the normal cellular expression pathway.

The distinction between a processing signal and a tail is particularly important for interpreting DNA constructs. A plasmid designed to express a nuclear messenger can contain a sequence that will be transcribed into a polyadenylation signal without containing a DNA tract that directly encodes the entire mature tail. The cellular machinery uses the signal and neighboring elements to define a cleavage site, and the polymerase then adds the adenosines. In a different setting, in vitro RNA production may deliberately include a template-encoded stretch of adenosines or use enzymatic addition after transcription. These are alternative ways of generating an adenosine-rich terminal region and should not be mistaken for identical mechanisms.

For synthetic messenger RNA, the cap is also a practical design feature because it influences how the molecule is recognized and used in a eukaryotic cell. RNA synthesized outside cells does not automatically receive the same processing marks as a nuclear transcript in its normal cellular context. Appropriate enzymatic or chemical strategies can supply a cap. The relevant conceptual distinction is that a correctly capped RNA may have been capped during its synthesis or after synthesis; the mature structure alone does not establish the route used to make it.

This provides a simple application of the reaction chemistry learned earlier. If a synthetic RNA has a five-prime triphosphate, it is not structurally equivalent to a capped RNA merely because both termini contain several phosphate groups. The guanosine attachment and appropriate methylation are defining features of the canonical cap. A synthetic molecule with a terminal adenosine chain may be chemically similar to an ordinary tailed messenger at that end even if its adenosines were generated in a different way. To infer how an RNA was produced, the investigator needs knowledge of the preparation or evidence comparing the genomic and terminal sequences.

In molecular genetics, a change in a processing signal can influence phenotype without altering the encoded protein sequence. A mutation near a gene's three-prime processing region may reduce the amount of functional messenger, and consequently reduce the amount of protein. This is an example of how noncoding sequence variants can have biologically important effects. For an exam problem, the first task is to determine whether the variant changes a regulatory RNA signal, whether mature RNA formation is impaired, and whether an alternative site is used. Detailed diagnosis of a human disease requires separate evidence and cannot be inferred solely from the presence of a sequence change.

The same reasoning applies to a reporter-based experiment. If a mutant processing signal reduces reporter activity, the observer has established a difference in the combined output of transcription, RNA maturation, RNA stability and translation under the tested conditions. Additional assays are needed before assigning the whole difference to one stage. This is why a well-designed experiment includes measurements of precursor RNA, mature RNA end identity and protein output rather than relying on a single reporter signal.

RNA selection methods also exploit terminal features. Enriching polyadenylated RNA can make many messenger transcripts easier to study in a sample dominated by ribosomal RNA. But enrichment creates a selection bias. Messenger RNAs that have short or absent tails can be underrepresented, and changes in tail length can change recovery efficiency without an equivalent change in total molecule number. The method is suitable for particular questions, not an unbiased census of all RNA classes. A comparison of total RNA and selected RNA can reveal when this limitation affects interpretation.

Part C · Original analytical practice

All numerical and constructed experimental scenarios below are hypothetical educational problems, not previous-year questions or published datasets.

C1. Isotope reasoning

The initiating RNA nucleotide has a labeled γ phosphate. GTP donor is unlabeled. Where should the label go during canonical capping?

Answer and reasoning

Inorganic phosphate

Triphosphatase hydrolyzes the terminal γ phosphate before guanylyl transfer. The two retained phosphates originate from the RNA end; the third bridge phosphate originates from donor GTP.

C2. Isotope reasoning

A donor GTP carries label only in its α phosphate. Predict label distribution after cap formation.

Answer and reasoning

The cap bridge retains the label.

Guanylyltransferase transfers GMP, including its α phosphate. β and γ leave as PPi; the methyl donor has a separate chemical role.

C3. Numerical

An RNA population of 400 contains 300 guanosine-attached ends, of which 180 have N7 methylation. Find the conditional methylation yield and the mature methylated fraction of all RNA.

Answer and reasoning

60%; 45%.

180/300 = 0.60; 180/400 = 0.45. The denominators differ because the first asks about already attached guanosine and the second about all transcripts.

C4. Mutation

A mutant shows 90% diphosphate RNA, very little GMP attachment and low N7 cap signal. Identify the most likely failed stage.

Answer and reasoning

Guanylyl transfer.

Diphosphate formation indicates triphosphatase activity. Low attachment locates the failure before methylation; a methyltransferase-only defect would not directly abolish attachment.

C5. Mutation

Purified capping enzyme from a mutant has normal catalytic activity, but capping of nascent RNA in cells is reduced. What two explanations need to be distinguished?

Answer and reasoning

Defective recruitment versus altered availability of nascent RNA.

Measure cap fraction relative to newly synthesized RNA and assay polymerase-associated enzyme. A total RNA decrease alone does not localize a catalytic defect.

C6. Assertion–reason

Assertion: A 5′ cap helps protect typical eukaryotic mRNA from XRN1. Reason: XRN1 generally prefers a 5′ monophosphate substrate. Are both true, and does the reason explain the assertion?

Answer and reasoning

Both true; reason explains the assertion.

The cap blocks the ordinary substrate configuration. Decapping can create a monophosphate; protection is not absolute against every nuclease.

C7. Numerical

A transcript has 1,850 retained encoded residues, a tail of 220 residues, and one cap guanosine. Count all nucleotide-derived residues including cap.

Answer and reasoning

2,071.

1,850 + 220 + 1. The phosphate bridge does not contribute an additional nucleotide residue. Introns or downstream discarded sequences must not be included.

C8. Experimental interpretation

A 900-nt precursor is cut at 620 nt. An upstream probe detects a band at 620 but not 280 nt. Is the absence of 280 evidence that cleavage did not occur?

Answer and reasoning

No.

The downstream fragment is 280 nt, but lacks the upstream probe-binding region. Detect it with a downstream probe or an appropriate uniform label.

C9. Numerical

A retained upstream product is 620 nt. Its tailed product migrates near 810 nt under an ideal size assay. Estimate the number of added adenosines.

Answer and reasoning

190 residues.

810 − 620 = 190, assuming other length changes and migration effects are absent. This is a length difference, not a DNA base-pair count.

C10. Mutation

A catalytic CPSF73 mutant produces readthrough RNA and loss of correctly cut RNA. Why would supplying more PAP alone generally not rescue the intended end?

Answer and reasoning

PAP needs the correctly generated 3′ hydroxyl.

Increasing extension capacity cannot replace the missing endonucleolytic positioning reaction. Nonspecific tailing is not equivalent to accurate cleavage.

C11. Experimental interpretation

Cleavage products are normal; the downstream RNA persists and engaged polymerase accumulates beyond the site. Which defect is more consistent: selective loss of XRN2 or catalytic loss of CPSF73?

Answer and reasoning

Selective loss of XRN2.

Cleavage still occurs, whereas downstream RNA removal and termination are affected. CPSF73 loss would directly compromise the cut itself.

C12. Multiple statements

Evaluate: I AAUAAA is normally retained upstream of the cut. II The downstream element can affect maturation without being retained. III The entire tail must be encoded as a genomic A tract.

Answer and reasoning

I and II only.

III confuses sequence-directed cleavage with template-independent addition. I and II follow from the typical ordering of signal, cut and downstream element.

C13. Experimental design

A 3′ processing signal mutant reduces mature RNA by 70%. Design a comparison distinguishing reduced initiation from failure of end formation.

Answer and reasoning

Compare matched nascent transcription and correctly cleaved mature RNA, with appropriate normalization.

If nascent transcription is similar but correct mature ends are depleted and readthrough increases, a processing defect is supported. A reporter protein measurement alone conflates stages.

C14. Numerical

A reaction adds 240 adenosines to each of 50 molecules. Count ATP incorporation events and PPi released.

Answer and reasoning

12,000 each.

240 × 50 = 12,000 ATP molecules incorporated as AMP and 12,000 PPi released. ATP-to-ADP hydrolysis is not the direct chemistry.

C15. Matching

Match A CPSF73, B PABPN1, C eIF4E, D CstF64 to 1 downstream RNA recognition, 2 nuclear tail elongation, 3 cap recognition, 4 RNA cleavage.

Answer and reasoning

A–4, B–2, C–3, D–1.

The roles represent distinct steps and molecular contexts. CstF64 is not the nuclease; eIF4E is not the nuclear tail regulator.

C16. Quantitative reasoning

Under equal independent nucleotide frequencies, estimate the expected occurrences of AAUAAA among 8,192 possible start positions.

Answer and reasoning

2.

Probability = (1/4)^6 = 1/4096; expectation = 8192/4096. Real sequences violate the simplifying model, and a motif occurrence is not proof of functional cleavage.

C17. Experimental interpretation

Proximal and distal isoforms are 90 and 10 molecules in control; mutant has 30 and 10. Has distal abundance increased?

Answer and reasoning

No; only its relative fraction increased from 10% to 25%.

The absolute distal count remains 10. A percentage shift can result solely from reduction of the other population.

C18. Integrative

Two alternative cleavage sites lie 350 nt apart after a shared stop codon. Tails are equally long. Predict the RNA and protein differences.

Answer and reasoning

The distal isoform is 350 nt longer; the ORF is unchanged.

The retained 3′ UTR differs. Protein quantity or localization may change, but the given coding sequence does not.

C19. Numerical

RNA falls from 1,200 to 150 copies in 90 min after synthesis stops. Assuming exponential decay, find half-life and k.

Answer and reasoning

30 min; approximately 0.0231 min⁻¹.

1200/150 = 8 = 2³, so three half-lives pass. k = ln2/30. This is a net decay constant, not a specific nuclease activity.

C20. Numerical

Control mature formation is 48 molecules/min and k = 0.024 min⁻¹. A processing defect halves formation without changing k. Compare steady states.

Answer and reasoning

2,000 versus 1,000 molecules.

M = s/k. Halving s halves steady-state abundance under the stated assumptions. A different change in k could produce the same abundance.

C21. Numerical

Of 200 primary transcripts, 90% are capped; among capped molecules 80% are correctly cleaved; among these 75% gain tails. Predict fully processed count.

Answer and reasoning

108.

200 × 0.90 × 0.80 × 0.75 = 108. The probabilities are sequential conditional yields, not additive percentages.

C22. Experimental interpretation

Total RNA shows a histone transcript, but oligo(dT)-selected RNA does not. What is the best next interpretation?

Answer and reasoning

It may be a normal nonpolyadenylated replication-dependent transcript.

Confirm the characteristic end structure or use a tail-independent assay. Failure of enrichment is not absence of transcription.

C23. Assertion–reason

Assertion: Every eukaryotic mRNA requires a conventional long poly(A) tail. Reason: Poly(A)-binding proteins can aid canonical translation.

Answer and reasoning

Assertion false; reason true.

Replication-dependent animal histone mRNAs provide a standard exception. A common functional contribution is not a universal necessity.

C24. Experimental design

A reporter with a long tail produces twice as much protein, but its RNA abundance is four times higher. Under matched short-interval conditions, compare protein output per RNA.

Answer and reasoning

One-half of the control's per-RNA output.

Relative efficiency = 2/4 = 0.5, if accumulation history and protein removal are controlled. Total protein does not by itself measure translation efficiency.

C25. Integrative

A bacterial structured decay intermediate is stabilized when tail addition is disrupted. Why does this not contradict the usual eukaryotic tail role?

Answer and reasoning

The tail can recruit or enable different machinery in different systems.

In E. coli, tailing can create a single-stranded entry region for degradative enzymes. Nuclear eukaryotic mRNA commonly uses tail-associated proteins for productive expression.

C26. Experimental interpretation

An RNA becomes 70 nt longer during oocyte activation, but its mapped genomic 3′ boundary is unchanged. What hypothesis is favored and what control is needed?

Answer and reasoning

Cytoplasmic tail extension; verify that the extra residues are terminal adenosines.

Alternative cleavage predicts a changed retained sequence. Tail-specific measurement distinguishes extension from other apparent size effects.

C27. Multiple statements

I Capping adds a nucleotide in an inverted orientation. II PAP extends a 3′ hydroxyl. III The stop codon fixes the cleavage site. IV Termination and cleavage can be experimentally separated. Select true statements.

Answer and reasoning

I, II and IV.

III is false because the 3′ UTR generally lies between the stop codon and cleavage site. The mature end does not locate polymerase release.

C28. Experimental interpretation

A cap–tail interaction mutant reduces reporter protein despite normal RNA abundance and normal cap/tail structures. What further experiment would strengthen a causal interpretation?

Answer and reasoning

Rescue with interaction-competent protein and measure translation rate per RNA.

Structural modifications are present but their recognition/cooperation may be defective. Rescue with wild type versus an interaction-deficient variant helps distinguish specific function from generalized effects.
