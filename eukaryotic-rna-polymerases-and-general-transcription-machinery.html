1. The biological problem: making several kinds of RNA from the same genome

Every nucleus contains a remarkable collection of DNA molecules, but a DNA molecule is not a working copy of every instruction it contains. A cell must select particular stretches of DNA, expose the relevant sequence, copy one strand into RNA, and eventually deliver the RNA to the cellular process that needs it. Consider a cell that is increasing in size. It needs new enzymes to make metabolites, membrane proteins to expand its surface, and thousands of additional ribosomes to support protein synthesis. Each of these needs requires transcription. However, the instructions for a protein and the instructions for a ribosomal RNA have different biological destinations. A protein-coding transcript is an intermediate whose nucleotide sequence will be read by a ribosome, whereas a ribosomal RNA becomes part of the ribosome itself. A transfer RNA does not supply a protein-coding message; it helps the translation machinery interpret one. This distinction between the products explains why it is useful for the nucleus to possess several specialized transcription systems.

The chemical reaction at the center of all these systems is strikingly similar. An RNA polymerase selects an incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate using a DNA template base, joins the nucleotide to the growing RNA, and moves on to another template position. Nevertheless, chemical competence does not explain how the polymerase finds the correct gene. If a polymerase started at arbitrary positions, most RNA molecules would be useless or misleading. Even when it started near an appropriate gene, the product might lack an essential first exon or contain unwanted upstream sequence. A transcription system therefore has two logically distinct responsibilities: specifying an appropriate starting region and carrying out RNA synthesis. Different proteins may be necessary for these responsibilities. Some recognize DNA or stabilize a recognition complex; others help expose the template; the polymerase supplies the catalytic center. These functions cooperate without being chemically identical.

In bacteria, a familiar way to divide these responsibilities is between the RNA polymerase core and its associated sigma factor. The core carries out nucleotide addition and sigma helps select appropriate promoters. The eukaryotic nucleus retains the principle of separating catalytic activity from selective initiation, but implements it in a different and more elaborate way. A typical animal or fungal cell has three distinct nuclear multisubunit RNA polymerases, conventionally numbered I, II and III. Each is associated with its own initiation machinery and transcribes a characteristic group of genes. These are not merely three interchangeable copies of a single enzyme. A mutation in the largest subunit of polymerase II may compromise synthesis of many messenger RNAs even though polymerase I is still producing a ribosomal precursor. Conversely, a defect in polymerase I may reduce ribosome production while the protein-coding genes initially remain capable of transcription.

A common beginner's mistake is to imagine that the polymerase can distinguish a messenger RNA from a ribosomal RNA by reading the meaning of the sequence. The polymerase does not interpret a codon, recognize the future catalytic function of a ribosomal RNA, or count whether the finished product will be translated. Its choice of transcription unit is largely determined by the DNA recognition and recruitment machinery that places it at that site. The consequence can be understood with a conceptual promoter-swap experiment. Imagine that a piece of DNA encoding a short RNA is placed downstream of a functioning polymerase II promoter. If the surrounding sequence and factors allow productive transcription, polymerase II can copy that DNA irrespective of the intended biological use of the RNA. The resulting transcript may differ in its start position, end, or processing from the native RNA. This is precisely why an RNA's biochemical identity is not, by itself, sufficient evidence of which enzyme synthesized it under an engineered condition.

The three nuclear systems also need to be distinguished from transcription in organelles. A mammalian mitochondrial genome has its own transcription apparatus, and photosynthetic eukaryotes have plastid transcription systems. These enzymes cannot be classified as nuclear polymerase I simply because they make ribosomal RNA inside an organelle. The correct classification depends on the enzyme, genomic compartment and biological context. Likewise, a plant can contain additional related nuclear polymerases specialized for particular RNA-mediated regulation. The standard I/II/III scheme is the starting point for nuclear transcription, not an assertion that no other RNA polymerase exists anywhere in a eukaryotic cell.

By the end of this chapter, three complementary questions should become easy to separate. Which polymerase makes the primary transcript of a specified nuclear gene? Which proteins allow that polymerase to be recruited and initiated correctly? Which subsequent biochemical changes connect RNA synthesis to the future fate of the transcript? Distinguishing these questions is particularly important when an examination describes an experiment in which a gene is still present and RNA polymerase is still detectable but mature RNA abundance falls. Such a result could originate in promoter recognition, catalysis, transcript processing or stability. Mechanistic understanding begins by identifying the stage actually tested.

2. Catalytic unity and structural specialization

A multisubunit polymerase is an enzyme assembled from several polypeptides, not a mixture of unrelated single-chain enzymes working independently. Its subunits create a coordinated three-dimensional structure. Some contribute directly to the catalytic region, others stabilize its shape, and still others provide interaction surfaces for DNA, RNA or partner proteins. The largest and second-largest subunits of the eukaryotic polymerases are homologous to one another across polymerase classes. They are also evolutionarily related to the major catalytic subunits of bacterial and archaeal RNA polymerases. Homology refers to shared evolutionary origin, inferred from sequence and structure; it does not imply that every regulatory interaction is identical. The conserved center and the divergent surfaces allow an ancient chemical mechanism to operate inside different cellular architectures.

To appreciate the molecular problem, consider a double-stranded DNA template. The two bases at any position normally interact with one another. A DNA-directed RNA polymerase cannot read a template base while that base remains fully paired and inaccessible. Within an active transcription complex a short region of the duplex is opened. One DNA strand reaches the catalytic center and the emerging RNA remains transiently paired to that strand near its growing end. As the complex advances, DNA can re-form behind it and RNA exits through a separate route. The enzyme is consequently more than a catalyst suspended beside a DNA molecule. Its geometry guides the entry and separation of polymers, stabilizes the appropriate nucleic acid configuration and limits access to inappropriate substrates.

For nucleotide addition, the reactive end of the RNA is its terminal 3′ hydroxyl group. The incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate has three phosphate groups. The phosphorus nearest the nucleotide sugar becomes part of the RNA backbone, and the other two leave together as pyrophosphate. Divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium ions, participate in the catalytic center. They assist the chemical transformations by coordinating negatively charged groups and favoring productive positioning of reacting atoms. Template-directed base pairing contributes to selection of the correct nucleotide, but the polymerase also imposes geometrical constraints. A correctly paired nucleotide is more likely to adopt the productive arrangement than a mismatched one. The combination of sequence information, substrate chemistry and enzyme architecture accounts for ordered synthesis.

The reaction may be represented schematically as RNA(n) + NTP → RNA(n+1) + PPi. The symbol n denotes the number of nucleotide residues already present; NTP refers to ATP, CTP, GTP or UTP as selected by the DNA template; PPi is inorganic pyrophosphate. For an RNA of length 100 residues initiated de novo, there are 99 internucleotide phosphodiester linkages. The first initiating nucleotide retains its original triphosphate at the 5′ end in the unprocessed primary transcript. Each subsequent addition produces a pyrophosphate molecule, so idealized production of a single such transcript involves 99 polymerization-associated PPi molecules. Subsequent modification of the 5′ end or hydrolysis of released PPi is chemically separate from this count. The equation is a model for net nucleotide incorporation; it does not enumerate additional ATP hydrolysis required by accessory proteins during promoter opening.

The common two-metal catalytic principle is not the same as the ATP-dependent action of a separate motor. An RNA polymerase can consume ATP as a building block when the next template base requires adenine in RNA, whereas an ATP-dependent translocase can hydrolyze ATP to generate mechanical changes without adding adenine to the transcript. A kinase can transfer the terminal phosphate of ATP to a protein without moving along DNA. These distinct uses of ATP coexist in transcription, and examination statements that treat them as one process obscure the underlying chemistry. A change in ATP concentration can therefore affect multiple reactions simultaneously. To attribute the outcome to a specific protein, the experimental design must discriminate between substrate incorporation, motor activity and phosphorylation.

The catalytic components are also subject to quality control. An incoming nucleotide must be selected and the enzyme must proceed along the DNA. Misincorporation can interfere with further extension, sometimes allowing a polymerase to reverse its position along the nucleic acid and cleave the erroneous RNA segment before resuming. The general principle is relevant to the common ancestry of RNA polymerases, but detailed elongation factors and pause recovery are not necessary to distinguish polymerases I, II and III at initiation. Importantly, RNA synthesis is not DNA replication. A transcription error normally changes one RNA molecule rather than permanently changing a genomic sequence that will be inherited by daughter cells. This explains why both processes require selectivity but are not governed by identical fidelity mechanisms.

A typical mammalian Pol II contains twelve core subunits. The two largest provide much of the conserved catalytic architecture. Several smaller subunits are shared with Pol I and Pol III, whereas others or their related counterparts are characteristic of one enzyme. In a textbook comparison, the exact total number should be tied to the organism and definition of the enzyme complex. For example, yeast Pol I and Pol III are commonly described as containing fourteen and seventeen subunits respectively, and mammalian descriptions of their complexes may use slightly different conventions. Counting general transcription factors as permanent polymerase subunits would artificially increase the number. The functional distinction is therefore more reliable than a memorized number taken out of context: core subunits are integral parts of the enzyme; general initiation factors interact with that enzyme as part of an initiation apparatus.

Specialized subunits of Pol I and Pol III illustrate the modular nature of evolution. Functions that Pol II carries out with separately recruited factors can have counterparts incorporated more permanently into the other polymerases. This does not mean that the Pol I or Pol III enzyme is independent of initiation factors. Rather, the distribution of functional modules differs. The arrangement changes how partners are recruited, how efficiently an enzyme returns to transcription, and how individual genes respond to perturbations. For NET and SET reasoning, a mutation in a component shared by several enzymes can plausibly affect more than one class of transcript, whereas a mutation in an enzyme-specific partner can yield a narrower defect. Even these predictions require experimental confirmation because cells may alter RNA degradation and compensate for synthesis defects.

3. How biochemical experiments revealed multiple polymerases

Before enzymes can be assigned distinct biological roles, their physical existence must be established. A crude cell extract may synthesize RNA when supplied with DNA and nucleotides. That observation demonstrates RNA-synthesizing activity but does not determine how many molecular species are responsible. Three different enzymes that catalyze the same reaction can all contribute to a single bulk measurement. Conversely, different activity levels measured under different salt conditions do not by themselves demonstrate distinct enzymes, because one enzyme can have a salt-dependent rate. The discovery of multiple nuclear RNA polymerases therefore required a way of separating catalytic activities and asking whether they retained distinct characteristics after separation.

A foundational study by Robert Roeder and William Rutter in 1969 reported multiple DNA-dependent RNA polymerase forms in eukaryotic organisms. Consider the experimental logic underlying chromatographic separation. A mixture of proteins is passed through a material with which different proteins interact to different extents. As the conditions are changed, individual protein populations emerge in different fractions. Fractions can then be assayed for RNA synthesis using an appropriate DNA template and nucleotide substrates. If several reproducible peaks of catalytic activity emerge at distinct positions, the result supports the existence of physically distinguishable active populations. Additional experiments are required to decide whether the populations are different enzymes, differently modified forms of one enzyme, or an enzyme associated with different partners.

The distinction between activity and abundance is crucial. An assay may record incorporated radiolabeled nucleotide, which provides a measure of synthesis under the chosen reaction conditions. The fraction with the greatest incorporation is not necessarily the fraction containing the most enzyme molecules. It could contain an enzyme with a higher catalytic rate on the supplied template, better substrate access, or fewer inhibitors. To compare catalytic activity per molecule, the investigator must know or estimate the active enzyme concentration. Equally, the area under a chromatographic peak integrates measured activity across fractions, whereas the height reflects the maximum activity in one fraction. Peak height and total recovered activity answer different questions.

Subcellular fractionation supplies a second line of evidence. The nucleus contains an organized nucleolus associated with ribosome production and a surrounding nucleoplasm. If a polymerase activity is enriched in a nucleolar preparation while another activity is enriched outside it, that observation suggests functional specialization. However, enrichment is not absolute proof of localization. The compartments can contaminate one another during preparation, and proteins can dissociate or redistribute when cells are disrupted. A reliable conclusion combines biochemical separation with independent indicators of compartment identity and with evidence concerning the RNA products synthesized in each region. The historical distinction between the nucleolar Pol I and the other nuclear polymerases provided a particularly powerful clue when interpreted alongside RNA synthesis experiments.

An inhibitor can provide an independent chemical fingerprint. In 1970, Lindell and colleagues reported that alpha-amanitin strongly inhibits the nucleoplasmic polymerase II from several animal sources, unlike the nucleolar polymerase I under their tested conditions. Later studies identified the characteristic response of Pol III to higher concentrations in animal systems and used that distinction to connect Pol III with tRNA and 5S rRNA synthesis. These results are important not because one can attach a single universal inhibitory concentration to each enzyme, but because separable enzyme activities responded differently to the same molecule. Sensitivity patterns, physical separation and transcript identity are mutually reinforcing forms of evidence.

There are several ways an experiment could mislead. If the inhibitor reduced all RNA synthesis by destroying the DNA template, it would not be a useful discriminator among polymerase activities. If all nucleotide incorporation were measured in an intact cell over a prolonged period, a reduction in one RNA class might result from secondary depletion of essential proteins rather than immediate direct inhibition of its polymerase. An early-time measurement of nascent transcription, a purified enzyme assay or an appropriate nuclear transcription assay can help distinguish direct and indirect effects. Controls without inhibitor establish baseline activity; a solvent control rules out effects of the carrier; comparable substrates and conditions allow meaningful differences to be interpreted.

Historical biochemical discoveries are sometimes summarized as if one chromatographic experiment immediately revealed which RNA each polymerase made. The more instructive view is a sequence of inferences. First establish that multiple catalytic populations can be separated. Then determine their distinctive properties and localizations. Next associate each population with transcripts produced from particular genes. Finally identify the factors and promoter elements that explain selective recruitment. Each stage answers a different question. A modern experimental problem can recreate this logic without requiring knowledge of every historical apparatus. When given three unknown enzyme fractions, a student should seek independent tests that identify the catalytic populations rather than assign them solely from the order in which they emerged.

4. The functional map of nuclear RNA polymerases

RNA polymerase I is principally responsible for synthesis of the large precursor from which most structural ribosomal RNAs are generated. In mammalian cells the primary precursor is commonly described as 47S pre-rRNA, which undergoes processing through intermediates including 45S pre-rRNA. It contains the sequences that become 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNA. These RNAs are separated from one another and from spacer regions through maturation. Budding yeast uses a different conventional precursor designation, 35S, and has 25S rather than mammalian 28S as its large-subunit counterpart. The names reflect sedimentation properties rather than nucleotide counts. A precursor called 47S is not simply a numerical sum of the sedimentation coefficients of its three final components.

The ribosomal RNAs have different positions within the finished ribosome. The 18S RNA is a structural and functional component of the small eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomal subunit, conventionally designated 40S. The 28S and 5.8S RNAs, along with 5S rRNA, occur in the large cytosolic subunit, conventionally designated 60S. Assembly of these subunits ultimately supports the 80S ribosome. The sedimentation coefficient depends on particle mass, shape and interactions with the solvent; the values are therefore not additive in the manner of masses. Students should not infer that a missing 5S RNA changes an 80S ribosome into a 75S particle by subtraction. The important point in this chapter is that the ribosome depends on RNA products made by two different nuclear polymerases.

RNA polymerase II produces the primary transcripts of essentially all ordinary nuclear protein-coding genes. In many cases the transcript is initially a pre-mRNA containing introns and sequences that will be modified or removed. Even the absence of introns does not make a gene a Pol I or Pol III target. Polymerase II also synthesizes numerous non-protein-coding transcripts, including many small nuclear RNAs, many primary microRNA transcripts and long noncoding RNAs. These RNA categories have varied biological functions. In this chapter their relevance is that the statement “Pol II makes mRNA” is a useful first approximation but an incomplete classification rule. A typical noncoding RNA cannot be assigned to Pol III simply because it is not translated.

RNA polymerase III synthesizes nuclear tRNAs, 5S rRNA and several small noncoding RNAs. Examples include U6 small nuclear RNA and, in mammals, the RNA component of the signal recognition particle, 7SL RNA. Although many Pol III products are short, length alone is not a reliable criterion. Some short nuclear RNAs are made by Pol II; a length measurement cannot substitute for promoter and enzyme information. The major practical exception to the shorthand statement that Pol I makes ribosomal RNA is 5S rRNA, which is conventionally transcribed separately by Pol III. Its eventual incorporation into the large ribosomal subunit does not change its transcriptional origin.

The cooperation among these systems can be illustrated with the demand for new ribosomes. Pol I supplies the major precursor rRNA, Pol II supplies messenger RNAs encoding ribosomal proteins and many assembly factors, and Pol III supplies 5S rRNA. tRNAs made by Pol III support the translation needed to produce those proteins. A cell with normal Pol I transcription and defective Pol III transcription could therefore have an abundance of major pre-rRNA without being able to assemble normal numbers of functional ribosomes. A cell with normal major rRNA transcription but reduced synthesis of ribosomal proteins can face a similar imbalance. This network of dependencies explains why a biochemical deficit in one system can eventually alter outputs of the others.

One must also distinguish the enzyme that synthesizes a precursor from the enzymes that produce its mature ends. Pol I is the transcription enzyme for the large rRNA precursor, not the nuclease that separates 18S and 28S sequences. Pol III is responsible for precursor tRNA synthesis, but maturation can involve cleavage, base modification and addition of particular nucleotides by other enzymes. Pol II can recruit proteins that perform RNA processing without itself being a capping enzyme or a spliceosome. Transcriptional origin and maturation mechanism are related through the organization of the nucleus, but they describe different biochemical reactions. These distinctions establish a foundation for later study of RNA maturation.

A useful mental experiment is to imagine a short labeling pulse administered just after selective interruption of a nuclear polymerase. The nascent RNAs directly dependent on that enzyme should respond first if the interruption is rapid and specific. Previously made mature RNA can remain in the cell for a considerable time. A stable mature rRNA pool may consequently appear largely unchanged even while production of its precursor has stopped. Conversely, a short-lived transcript may decline quickly. The duration of labeling and whether the assay measures nascent precursor or mature accumulated RNA can change the apparent result. Thus, a question asking for the immediate effect of enzyme inhibition should not be answered by assuming that every mature product disappears instantaneously.

5. The core promoter as an address and a geometric instruction

A promoter is a region of DNA that contributes to specifying where and in which orientation transcription begins. Its function depends on interactions with the transcription machinery rather than on its presence as an isolated string of letters. In a genome, millions of base pairs might contain short sequences resembling a promoter motif. Only a subset will be recognized in a biologically productive context. A useful starting distinction is between the core promoter, which provides the local setting for assembly and positioning of the initiation machinery, and additional regulatory DNA elements that influence how frequently initiation occurs. The latter may be close to the core promoter or farther away. This chapter focuses on the local recognition task and the general proteins that solve it; the gene-specific mechanisms that modulate initiation belong to the following chapter.

The position at which the first RNA nucleotide is incorporated is conventionally designated +1. Positions upstream of this start are numbered negatively, while positions downstream are positive. There is no position zero in the usual convention. The coordinate system refers to transcription rather than to translation. A gene may be transcribed starting at +1 even though its protein-coding region begins hundreds of nucleotides later. The intervening transcribed sequence is part of the untranslated region of the RNA, subject to any processing that takes place. Some core promoter elements lie upstream of the first transcribed base; others overlap +1 or lie downstream and can be copied into RNA. Therefore, the familiar definition of a promoter as a wholly untranscribed upstream region is an oversimplification. The functional meaning of promoter sequence is its contribution to initiation.

One classical Pol II promoter element is the TATA box, an A/T-rich motif often located around 25 to 30 base pairs upstream of a focused start site in metazoans. Its approximate position is particularly useful because it establishes a geometric relation to where the polymerase must begin. The factor that recognizes this element is the TATA-binding protein, usually abbreviated TBP. The TATA box is not a fixed universal sequence that every protein-coding gene must possess. Many promoters lack a recognizable classical TATA motif and initiate transcription through other arrangements of sequence elements and proteins. This does not invalidate the TATA box model; it establishes its domain of applicability. The correct exam reasoning is to distinguish a representative mechanism from a universal requirement.

The initiator element, abbreviated Inr, illustrates another arrangement. It encompasses the region of the transcription start site and can help define where initiation occurs. An initiator can cooperate with an upstream TATA box or contribute to recognition in a promoter lacking a classical TATA box. The name does not imply that the initiator element is a protein or that it is identical to the initiating ribonucleotide. It is a DNA sequence feature associated with the starting position. Certain downstream promoter elements, including the downstream promoter element or DPE, provide another kind of recognition signal. In the classical focused promoters studied particularly in Drosophila, the DPE functions in a position-dependent relationship with the initiator. Its importance is not that every Pol II gene contains this motif, but that recognition of a start site can use information on both sides of it.

A geometric arrangement is easiest to understand if the two strands of DNA are pictured as a directional track. Suppose that a recognition site lies at positions -31 to -24, and the favored start lies at +1. An inserted segment between the site and the start increases the physical distance between them. A promoter-bound protein may still recognize its site, but the associated machinery might no longer be positioned appropriately for the original start. The resulting transcript could initiate at a different position, initiate less often, or depend more strongly on other promoter features. These are alternative outcomes, not predictions that every insertion will produce exactly the same defect. The effect depends on the flexibility of the initiation apparatus and on the surrounding sequence. An experiment that maps the RNA 5′ end as well as measuring total transcript production can distinguish among these possibilities.

Other Pol II core promoter elements include recognition sequences associated with TFIIB, often called BRE elements. These lie adjacent to the TATA region in promoters where they occur and can influence the recruitment or organization of the initiation machinery. Again, a list of possible elements is not a consensus map of all promoters. A real promoter may contain a particular combination or lack several recognizable motifs. It is possible for a mutation in one element to have a modest effect because other contacts remain intact. A double mutation can have a disproportionately large effect if it removes an alternative route to assembly. Conversely, two mutations can produce no larger effect than one if they impair the same obligatory interaction. Exam questions that compare single and double mutants are often testing this logic of interacting components rather than the memorization of motif names.

The start pattern can also vary among genes. Some promoters favor a narrow region of initiation and produce transcripts whose 5′ ends are closely clustered. Other promoters support a dispersed distribution of start sites. If an RNA mapping experiment identifies several neighboring 5′ ends, the result need not mean that several unrelated polymerases were involved. It may reflect an inherently dispersed Pol II promoter. A population of transcripts can be described by a start-site distribution: the number of transcripts beginning at each coordinate divided by the total number mapped. For example, if 72 of 100 informative reads begin at the most frequent site, the observed fraction is 0.72. The remainder may represent genuine alternative initiation or experimental artifacts. Mapping confidence, RNA degradation and the experimental method must be considered before interpreting every detected end as a transcription start.

Promoter recognition is thus a problem of specificity combined with geometry. Recognizing a short motif is only one part of identifying a productive assembly site. Correct orientation, spacing, interaction with general factors and local template accessibility all matter. When comparing a TATA-containing and a TATA-less promoter, the key question is how the initiation machinery is positioned in each, not whether one contains a sequence that can be memorized from a textbook. This prepares us to understand TFIID, which brings together a DNA-binding protein with additional components capable of recognizing and integrating other promoter features.

6. TFIID and TBP: the foundation of promoter recognition

The abbreviation TFIID refers to transcription factor IID, a multisubunit general factor associated with Pol II initiation. It contains TBP and a set of TBP-associated factors, conventionally called TAFs. TBP and TFIID must not be treated as interchangeable names. TBP is one protein with a characteristic DNA-binding surface; TFIID is a larger assembly containing TBP along with other proteins. A purified TBP molecule can recognize a suitable TATA element under appropriate conditions, but it cannot reproduce every interaction contributed by TFIID. This difference becomes especially important when an experiment compares a simple TATA-directed template with a promoter that depends on contacts made by TAFs.

TBP binds in the minor groove of a suitable TATA sequence and strongly bends the DNA. This is a striking departure from the simple image of a recognition protein merely resting on an undistorted double helix. By engaging the DNA and changing its local shape, TBP helps establish a scaffold for further contacts with the initiation machinery. The deformation has energetic consequences: bending DNA costs energy, whereas formation of favorable protein-DNA interactions can compensate for that cost. The relevant question is whether the combined structure is sufficiently favorable and kinetically accessible to form under the prevailing conditions. An A/T-rich sequence may be compatible with the required geometry, but sequence composition alone is not enough to identify every productive promoter. Context and the other interacting proteins still matter.

The bending of the DNA by TBP must not be confused with creating the complete transcription bubble around the start site. A bent duplex remains largely base-paired along the recognition region. Formation of the open complex requires exposure of a template segment suitable for the catalytic site of the polymerase. In the standard Pol II initiation pathway, the latter transition depends strongly on the action of TFIIH. A biochemical footprint showing that TBP protects a region of DNA, or evidence that the DNA is sharply bent, is therefore not equivalent to demonstration of RNA synthesis. At least three events must be conceptually distinguished: local recognition, template opening and successful nucleotide incorporation.

TAFs contribute surfaces for promoter and protein interactions that extend beyond a single TATA motif. In particular, contacts involving promoter sequences near or downstream of the start site can help stabilize and position TFIID on certain templates. If a TATA box is absent, the remainder of the recognition machinery does not cease to exist. TFIID can support assembly in ways that do not require TBP to recognize a classical TATA box directly. Promoters vary in their reliance on TAF-mediated contacts and other mechanisms of recruitment. This is why substituting purified TBP for intact TFIID in a simplified transcription system can produce different results depending on the template. The experiment is meaningful only if the promoter class is specified.

The term basal transcription is used for synthesis supported by the core initiation machinery under conditions that do not require a specified gene-specific activator. It does not mean that a living cell transcribes every gene at an invariant low rate, nor does it imply that all required proteins are permanently bound to DNA. Basal transcription is often examined in a defined experimental system that contains a template, polymerase, general factors and nucleotide substrates. If TFIID is removed from such a system and transcription decreases, restoring the factor may test whether the loss accounts for the defect. If purified TBP restores activity on one promoter but not another, this suggests that the second promoter relies on functions not supplied by TBP alone. It does not by itself identify the particular TAF or DNA contact responsible.

A further distinction concerns TBP's use by other transcription systems. Its name describes its originally characterized association with a TATA sequence, but its biological role is broader. In the mammalian Pol I machinery, TBP participates in the factor SL1. In the Pol III machinery, TBP is a component of TFIIIB. The surrounding proteins differ from the TAF assembly in Pol II TFIID. Shared use of TBP therefore does not imply that the three polymerases recognize identical core promoters, or that a classical Pol II TATA box is mandatory for Pol I and Pol III transcription. The different assemblies recruit different polymerases and can be positioned through different DNA-recognition mechanisms. The shared component is best understood as a reusable molecular scaffold whose partners determine how it functions.

The difference between a protein and the complexes it joins is a major examination principle. Suppose that a mutation prevents TBP from interacting with a Pol II-specific TAF while leaving its protein stability and its interactions with Pol I and Pol III factors unchanged. A selective Pol II defect is plausible. By contrast, a mutation that causes loss of stable TBP protein could compromise all three systems. Neither outcome is inevitable solely from the label of the mutated protein. The molecular interaction affected by the mutation is what determines the likely scope. This is also why an antibody that detects TBP at a locus cannot, on its own, identify the polymerase at that locus: one must determine the relevant associated proteins or the RNA product.

Consider a simple equilibrium model to formalize the idea of a recognition event. If a protein P associates reversibly with a DNA binding site D to form PD, its dissociation constant is Kd = [P][D]/[PD], using equilibrium free concentrations. If free protein is maintained at concentration p and the DNA sites are independent, the bound fraction is theta = p/(Kd + p). At p = Kd, half the sites are occupied. If a mutation increases Kd from 10 nM to 40 nM at p = 10 nM, the predicted occupancy falls from 10/(10+10) = 0.50 to 10/(40+10) = 0.20. This example describes a hypothetical simple binding equilibrium, not a measured affinity for a particular TBP-promoter pair. In a living nucleus, recruitment can involve cooperative assembly, DNA accessibility and ATP-dependent transitions. Occupancy is consequently not guaranteed to equal transcriptional output. The value of the model is to demonstrate how changing binding affinity can alter the availability of a promoter for a later reaction without changing the catalytic properties of RNA polymerase.

7. TFIIA and TFIIB: stabilizing and orienting the initiation scaffold

TFIIA helps stabilize promoter-associated complexes involving TBP and TFIID in appropriate contexts. Its function can be understood by considering a DNA recognition event that is sufficiently favorable to occur but transient enough that the next protein may not arrive before the original binding interaction is lost. A factor that reinforces the initial complex can increase the chance that productive assembly proceeds. The underlying interactions may include contacts with TBP and DNA, as well as changes in the way other proteins affect the recognition complex. TFIIA is not normally described as the enzyme that makes phosphodiester bonds or phosphorylates the Pol II CTD. Its function belongs to the organization of the initiation apparatus. The magnitude of its requirement can vary with promoter and experimental conditions.

Why would a general factor appear to be essential in one transcription assay but dispensable in another? A minimal in vitro reaction on a favorable naked DNA template can lack some of the competing interactions that exist in a cell. High concentrations of purified proteins can also compensate for weak recruitment steps. Under such conditions a factor that usually stabilizes or facilitates assembly may produce a smaller incremental benefit. Removing it from an intact cell can cause a larger defect because the cell must assemble machinery repeatedly in the presence of competing proteins, chromatin and regulatory constraints. A statement about the minimal set of factors needed in a particular purified reaction must therefore not be converted uncritically into a statement about every gene in vivo. TFIIA illustrates this important difference between mechanistic function and experimental sufficiency.

TFIIB has a distinct role. It interacts with the promoter-associated machinery and helps connect it to RNA polymerase II. In suitable promoter contexts it also recognizes DNA elements near the TBP binding site. Its arrangement contributes to the orientation and placement of the polymerase relative to the start region. Structural studies identify parts of TFIIB that extend toward the catalytic region of Pol II during initiation. Such interactions help explain why a factor located in the recognition machinery can influence where the first RNA nucleotides are made. A change in TFIIB can affect start-site selection even if the polymerase's fundamental chemical reaction remains intact.

An analogy is useful if applied carefully. Suppose that a docking fixture holds a machine at a particular position over a moving track. The machine may be fully capable of its mechanical work, yet incorrect placement relative to the track means it begins at the wrong location. TFIIB is not literally a rigid fixture, and the preinitiation complex is dynamic, but the analogy separates two measurable properties: catalytic competence and positioning. A polymerase that copies an already exposed artificial DNA template can be catalytically active while failing to initiate accurately on double-stranded promoter DNA in the absence of correct TFIIB interactions.

A genetic experiment can distinguish a positioning defect from a complete loss of initiation. Suppose a wild-type template yields a defined RNA start, whereas a mutant TFIIB preparation yields almost as much RNA overall but the start sites cluster several nucleotides downstream. If the assay reliably maps primary transcript ends, this pattern suggests altered start-site selection rather than a general inability to make RNA. An alternative experiment could reveal reduced polymerase binding at the promoter and reduced transcript production. That pattern is more consistent with defective recruitment or complex stabilization, though it cannot specify the exact defective contact. The same named factor can participate in more than one aspect of initiation, and different mutations need not generate identical phenotypes.

The spatial orientation of the transcription machinery is critical because the DNA double helix contains two potential template strands. At a given gene only one strand is normally used for the transcription unit under consideration. Promoter asymmetry, the organization of protein-DNA interactions and the placement of polymerase favor the orientation that exposes the correct strand in the active site. TBP recognition alone may not fully account for directionality; the sequence and interaction context of the larger initiation apparatus help resolve it. This is one reason promoter recognition is described as a cooperative construction rather than as the work of a single DNA-binding protein.

TFIIA and TFIIB thus solve related but distinguishable problems. TFIIA can reinforce the initial DNA-associated scaffold, whereas TFIIB helps connect and position Pol II within that scaffold. Neither should be assigned the ATP-driven promoter-opening function of TFIIH. In an examination, a loss of stable TBP-associated promoter occupancy points toward a defect in recognition or stabilization; a shift in start sites with preserved overall synthesis suggests a positioning problem; and a bound but unopened complex points toward a different transition. These are useful diagnostic tendencies provided the necessary controls establish the status of the other steps.

8. TFIIF and TFIIE: bringing catalytic machinery into the promoter complex

RNA polymerase II must join the promoter-associated scaffold in a configuration that supports productive transcription. Merely increasing the concentration of enzyme near DNA cannot solve the problem because nonspecific DNA binding can compete with the interactions that place the polymerase correctly. TFIIF associates closely with Pol II and helps its recruitment and stabilization within the preinitiation complex. In a simplified assembly model, the polymerase and TFIIF can be considered as an arriving pair. This relationship is different from that between TBP and the TATA region. TFIIF does not provide the classical sequence-specific recognition of the TATA motif. Its partner is primarily the polymerase and the larger assembly in which that enzyme is positioned.

A useful experiment is to compare the binding of purified Pol II to a suitable promoter with its binding to a DNA molecule lacking that promoter. If the enzyme associates with both molecules in the absence of the necessary recognition machinery, the observed binding tells us that it has affinity for DNA, not that it recognizes transcription start sites. Adding the appropriate factors can change the relative probability that the enzyme is retained at the correct promoter. TFIIF contributes to this process by supporting Pol II incorporation into a specific complex and limiting some nonproductive interactions. The relative effect will depend on the ionic conditions, DNA sequences, protein concentrations and other factors present. A general factor should be understood through its contribution to the productive state, not through a simplistic assertion that the polymerase cannot touch DNA without it.

TFIIF also interacts with elements of the polymerase that are relevant to transcription after the earliest recruitment step. General transcription factors are classified by their role in initiation, but classification does not prevent an individual factor from participating in other stages. TFIIF has functions associated with transcription elongation under appropriate circumstances. This fact does not make it identical to the general elongation machinery. For this chapter, its principal importance is how it helps Pol II occupy and function in a promoter complex. A useful prediction is that a defect preventing TFIIF association with polymerase may reduce stable recruitment even if an independent promoter-binding assay shows normal occupancy by TBP and TFIIB.

TFIIE performs another organizational task. It interacts with the assembled polymerase complex and helps recruit and regulate the activities of TFIIH. The name TFIIE gives no clue to its chemical function; these names reflect a historical nomenclature of transcription factors rather than a mechanistic alphabet. A sequence of factor names is therefore less informative than a causal map of their roles. A cell must expose the template strand near the start site after the enzyme has been placed there. TFIIE helps connect the pre-existing assembly with the factor that supplies ATP-dependent promoter-opening activity. It also contributes to stabilization and organization of the initiation complex around the region where DNA becomes accessible.

One way to appreciate this relationship is to imagine three assays performed on otherwise comparable promoter preparations. The first measures occupancy by Pol II, the second measures opening of the DNA near +1, and the third measures primary RNA production. Removal of a particular factor might leave detectable Pol II occupancy but reduce both opening and RNA production. That result locates the defect after recruitment and before or during the transition to a catalytically active configuration. If TFIIE is absent, TFIIH may no longer be effectively recruited or arranged, although precise outcomes depend on the system. Adding more Pol II alone need not repair the defect because the limiting function is not necessarily polymerase availability. Restoring the missing factor is a more targeted test.

TFIIE and TFIIF are sometimes confused because both are required for construction of the larger Pol II initiation apparatus. Their names differ by one letter, but their main contributions in the teaching model are distinguishable. TFIIF is particularly associated with delivery and stabilization of Pol II, whereas TFIIE contributes to organization of the open-complex machinery and association of TFIIH. These simplified assignments are useful because each makes a different experimental prediction. They do not imply that each factor has exactly one binding partner or that every physical contact is indispensable in all organisms. Initiation complexes are networks of interacting surfaces. Removing one component may destabilize several contacts simultaneously.

The ordered assembly diagram used in many textbooks usually depicts recognition by TFIID, contribution of TFIIA and TFIIB, arrival of Pol II with TFIIF, and association of TFIIE and TFIIH. This sequence describes how one can reconstruct a functional initiation complex in a representative system. It should not be interpreted as a compulsory stopwatch sequence for all transcription in living cells. Some components associate before encountering a promoter, and multiple routes may lead to a similar functional arrangement. The general factors can remain associated for different durations after transcription begins. A robust student should be able to describe the functional dependencies even if an experimental system assembles complexes in a different temporal order.

9. TFIIH: separating the motor from the kinase

The final arrangement of DNA and protein in the preinitiation complex must permit the polymerase to read an exposed template. Many double-stranded DNA regions remain paired at normal cellular temperatures, so a correctly positioned enzyme does not automatically acquire a productive single-stranded template. TFIIH contributes an ATP-dependent activity that promotes local promoter opening. The same multiprotein factor contains a kinase module that modifies proteins including the largest subunit of Pol II. These two activities occur in the same factor complex but solve different biochemical problems.

In mammalian TFIIH, the protein XPB supplies the principal ATP-dependent DNA translocase function used for standard promoter opening. ATP binding and hydrolysis support changes in the interaction of XPB with DNA. The motor engages downstream duplex DNA and contributes to movement and distortion of DNA relative to the more constrained upstream promoter-associated machinery. This arrangement favors the creation of a locally opened region near the transcription start. Calling XPB a helicase in a basic textbook description is common, but its role in the transcription initiation complex is more precisely understood as DNA translocation that facilitates opening. It is not necessary to imagine it traveling ahead of RNA polymerase for the entire gene, continually separating the two strands as a replication helicase does.

The distinction between opening and copying can be tested experimentally. A DNA template may contain a deliberately mismatched region near its start site, designed to reduce the energetic barrier to exposure of single-stranded DNA. In a suitable purified system, this may partially bypass a requirement for an ATP-dependent opening reaction. A normal double-stranded promoter would still depend on that opening step. The key comparison is between otherwise equivalent templates, with and without the pre-opened region, tested using the same enzyme and factor preparations. Rescue of RNA synthesis on the artificial open template would suggest that the missing activity normally helps generate accessible template DNA. Failure to rescue could indicate that the affected factor also contributes to assembly or another required step. An artificial bubble is not a faithful substitute for every event at a natural promoter.

The other enzymatic function central to Pol II initiation is supplied by the cyclin-dependent kinase CDK7 in mammalian TFIIH. In association with its regulatory partners, it transfers phosphate groups from ATP to protein substrates, including serine residues in the C-terminal domain of Pol II. A phosphorylation reaction changes the chemical properties of a protein without changing the encoded amino acid sequence. The addition of phosphate introduces negative charge and can change the ability of a protein surface to interact with other molecules. The resulting change in interactions is important for transitions between initiation and productive transcription and for coupling transcription to early processing of the RNA.

ATP has three distinct potential roles in the experiments discussed here. First, it can serve as a substrate for polymerization if the template calls for incorporation of AMP into RNA. Second, hydrolysis of ATP by XPB can contribute energy for rearranging DNA. Third, ATP can serve as the phosphate donor for CDK7. Omission of ATP from a crude reaction can disturb all three roles. A simple decrease in RNA production under ATP-free conditions therefore cannot isolate XPB as the sole affected molecule. Appropriate experiments use specifically altered proteins, template designs or direct readouts of kinase and opening activities. The analytical lesson extends beyond transcription: whenever one metabolite participates in several biochemical reactions, changing its concentration is rarely a selective perturbation of one protein.

Another component of TFIIH, XPD, is especially important for the factor's role in nucleotide excision repair. TFIIH participates in opening and verification processes at DNA lesions during repair, in which XPD's helicase activity is important. In standard Pol II transcription initiation, the promoter-opening motor is XPB rather than XPD. This difference explains how a mutation affecting one enzymatic activity of a shared complex can disproportionately affect one cellular pathway. A mutation that destroys the structural integrity of the whole complex can have broader effects. The named complex remains the same in both contexts, but the cellular partners, DNA configuration and required reactions differ.

Suppose that two mutant preparations of TFIIH are analyzed. Preparation X retains CDK7 activity on an isolated CTD substrate but is deficient in XPB translocation. Preparation K has preserved XPB motor activity but impaired CDK7 kinase activity. When added to a suitable double-stranded promoter reaction, X may assemble with other factors yet show a strong defect in DNA opening. K may support opening but show deficient early CTD phosphorylation and altered promoter escape or processing-factor recruitment. These outcomes follow from separating the two enzymatic functions. They are not guarantees that a mutation in a living cell has no other effect. To interpret the result, the investigator would need to establish that both complexes retain comparable structural integrity and are present at appropriate concentrations.

The coupling of transcription and repair provides an instructive biological application. Genomic information is useful only if it can be read, but lesions can interfere with DNA transactions. A multipurpose protein complex can be recruited into different molecular assemblies depending on the problem the cell must solve. The intersection between transcription and repair helps explain why defects in certain TFIIH components can be associated with human disease involving more than one cellular process. It is not necessary to memorize every clinical syndrome to understand the mechanism. The key is that a shared component may perform distinct functions in different pathways, and changes in catalytic activity, protein stability or partner interactions can produce different biological consequences.

10. From promoter recognition to an initiation-competent complex

The term preinitiation complex describes the molecular assembly that prepares Pol II for synthesis at a promoter. The term does not mean that the transcript has already been synthesized. Recognition of promoter DNA, arrival of the polymerase, local opening of the DNA and catalysis are successive functional requirements. Each can be measured by a different assay. A promoter can contain a stable protein assembly that has not yet made an RNA, and RNA synthesis can begin without immediately producing a long transcript. These distinctions are crucial when interpreting a diagram or data table.

In a representative TATA-directed system, recognition by TBP within TFIID establishes an upstream landmark. Additional interactions involving TFIIA and TFIIB stabilize and organize the structure. Pol II associated with TFIIF joins the assembly, and TFIIE and TFIIH help establish the conditions for local DNA opening. Once the appropriate strand is exposed and aligned in the polymerase catalytic site, the first ribonucleotides can be selected using template information. The chemistry of the first phosphodiester linkage is related to subsequent nucleotide incorporation, but unlike elongation it does not begin from an already long RNA chain. The formation of a sufficiently stable initial RNA-DNA interaction is part of the transition to productive transcription. Detailed gene-specific recruitment and regulation of this assembly will be treated in the next chapter.

It is important to identify what is physically absent in a defective complex. If the promoter is occupied by TFIID but no Pol II can be detected, the block occurs before stable polymerase association or reflects failure to maintain it. If Pol II is present but DNA remains closed, the defect may involve the opening machinery or its configuration. If the DNA is open but no RNA is made, the catalytic site may be mispositioned, the substrates may be unavailable or the polymerase may be inhibited. If short RNAs are present but long RNA is reduced, initiation has occurred at least in some complexes, and a subsequent transition may be compromised. None of these inferences identifies one protein with certainty. They do, however, narrow the range of mechanisms consistent with the observations.

The difference between occupancy and flux can be made quantitative using a simple state model. Let N denote the number of promoter copies, f the fraction occupied by an initiation-competent complex, and k the mean rate at which an occupied promoter generates productive initiation events. If occupancy and k are stable over the measurement interval, the expected production rate is J = Nfk. For ten identical promoter copies, f = 0.50 and k = 0.20 per minute, the predicted mean rate is ten times 0.50 times 0.20, or one productive initiation per minute. If a perturbation doubles occupancy to one but reduces k to 0.05 per minute, the new rate is 0.5 initiations per minute. Increased occupancy can thus coexist with decreased RNA output. This does not violate any conservation principle: the bound complexes are simply less productive.

The state model has limitations. It assumes an average constant occupancy, comparable promoter copies, and a meaningful effective rate for productive initiation. If complex assembly, opening and escape have widely differing timescales, a single k hides important intermediates. If promoter copies differ, the total flux is the sum of their individual contributions rather than the product of a common occupancy and rate. Measurements of binding in an intact cell also may include inactive complexes. Thus, the simple equation should be used to clarify the difference between binding and productive initiation rather than to infer a precise molecular rate from any occupancy measurement. A question containing both occupancy and transcript synthesis measurements invites such careful interpretation.

11. The Pol II C-terminal domain: why a polymerase needs a tail

The largest subunit of RNA polymerase II, RPB1 in common yeast and mammalian nomenclature, possesses a distinctive C-terminal domain, or CTD. The canonical repeated amino acid pattern is Tyr-Ser-Pro-Thr-Ser-Pro-Ser. Numbering the positions within one heptapeptide gives Tyr1, Ser2, Pro3, Thr4, Ser5, Pro6 and Ser7. The number of repeats varies between organisms. Budding yeast typically has 26, whereas the human RPB1 CTD contains 52 repeats, including repeats that depart from the canonical sequence. This distinction between a repeat and an exact copy is important: a domain can have 52 repeat units without every unit having identical residues. The CTD extends from the core enzyme and provides a flexible surface through which the transcription apparatus interacts with other proteins.

A student may wonder why this tail is needed when the core polymerase already contains a functional catalytic center. Synthesis of an RNA molecule is only the first requirement for expression of a nuclear gene. Many Pol II transcripts must be modified as they emerge, and the cell benefits when processing activities are concentrated near the RNA that needs them. A flexible protein domain containing repeated sites for chemical modification can help organize this spatial and temporal relationship. The repeated CTD sequence supplies several potential interaction sites, and phosphorylation changes which interactions are favored. This helps a transcription complex alter its associated factors during the progression from initiation to later stages of RNA synthesis.

The idea is analogous to a changing molecular docking surface rather than a permanent conveyor belt. A processing enzyme may associate preferentially with a particular phosphorylation pattern and thereby encounter the emerging RNA near the time it is needed. However, the CTD does not encode the RNA sequence, specify every splice junction, or directly perform the catalytic steps of capping and splicing. RNA features and processing proteins still determine their own reactions. The tail increases the opportunity for coordination by linking the biochemical state of Pol II with the recruitment of appropriate partners. This functional relationship is often called coupling between transcription and RNA processing.

During early Pol II transcription, CDK7 in TFIIH promotes phosphorylation of CTD residues including Ser5 and Ser7. Ser5 phosphorylation is characteristically enriched near the beginning of many transcription units and contributes to recruitment of factors involved in capping the nascent RNA. Later in transcription, Ser2 phosphorylation becomes prominent and participates in interactions relevant to elongation and downstream RNA maturation. These are broad patterns, not absolute mutually exclusive switches. Different repeats can carry different modifications, several kinases and phosphatases participate, and the distribution varies among organisms and genes. It is therefore inaccurate to describe Ser5 phosphorylation as present only at initiation and Ser2 phosphorylation as present only at termination. The transition is best understood as a change in relative modification patterns and protein interactions.

Phosphorylation requires that a kinase transfer a phosphate group from a donor, generally ATP, to a susceptible residue. Dephosphorylation requires removal of that group by a phosphatase. The dynamic nature of these reactions matters because a polymerase must eventually be available for another round of transcription. A permanently fixed interaction state would limit its ability to change partners. Reversible modification allows the same core enzyme to function in different assemblies without synthesizing a new polymerase for every stage. This is a general theme in cell biology, also encountered in regulation of metabolic enzymes and cell-cycle proteins, although the specific substrates and consequences differ.

A useful numerical exercise concerns the maximum number of potential sites rather than the actual phosphorylation state. If a hypothetical CTD has 20 perfect canonical heptads, it contains 20 Ser2 positions, 20 Ser5 positions and 20 Ser7 positions, or 60 serines at those numbered positions. It contains 140 amino acids within the repeats, because each heptad has seven residues. If all 20 Ser5 positions were phosphorylated in a simplified model, that would mean 20 phosphate additions, not 140. Actual CTD phosphorylation is neither necessarily complete nor uniform, and nonconsensus repeats can change the available sites. Thus, repeat counts describe potential molecular capacity, while measurements of modification describe the state of a particular protein population.

A CTD truncation may leave parts of the catalytic core intact but impair the coordination of transcription with processing. A mutant polymerase might still incorporate nucleotides on a simplified template and yet produce abnormal RNA products in a cell. Conversely, a failure of mature RNA accumulation after CTD mutation need not establish that RNA synthesis was normal. The CTD also participates in transitions that influence transcription itself. To separate these possibilities, researchers can compare nascent RNA production, early processing markers and the distribution of polymerase along the gene. An informative experiment measures more than mature RNA abundance. For example, preserved nascent synthesis with decreased cap-dependent recovery of newly made RNA would be consistent with a coupling defect, provided the recovery assay and transcript stability are controlled.

A phosphorylation-specific antibody can be used to detect polymerase populations carrying a particular modification. Interpretation requires care. If a mutant deletes many CTD repeats, an antibody may bind less strongly simply because fewer recognizable sites remain. A reduction in the antibody signal is not automatically evidence that the fraction of remaining sites phosphorylated is lower. Comparison with an antibody against an invariant region of Pol II can help account for enzyme abundance, but even that does not fully resolve differences in the number of epitopes per polymerase. Distinguishing modification occupancy from total protein and antibody accessibility is an important experimental principle, not merely a technical detail.

The CTD is consequently a defining feature of the Pol II transcription system, particularly in relation to co-transcriptional maturation. Pol I and Pol III have their own interactions with RNA maturation machinery, but they do not use the characteristic long repeated RPB1 tail as the universal coordination platform for their products. This illustrates the larger evolutionary theme introduced earlier: conserved catalytic activity is combined with specialized interaction modules. The polymerase II system is adapted to the many processing requirements of its transcripts, while the other nuclear polymerases coordinate with distinct biological pathways. The chemistry of the cap, cleavage, polyadenylation and splice reactions will be developed in their dedicated chapters.

12. RNA polymerase I and the organization of ribosomal gene transcription

A typical rapidly growing animal cell must make many ribosomes, and each new ribosome contains several ribosomal RNA molecules. The demand for the major rRNAs is unusually high relative to that for the product of most individual protein-coding genes. Nuclear genomes meet this demand partly through repeated ribosomal DNA transcription units. In humans, arrays of these units occur at nucleolar organizer regions on several chromosomes. The active units provide templates for synthesis of the large precursor RNA. The nucleolus is organized in association with ribosomal gene transcription, RNA maturation and ribosomal subunit assembly. It is not surrounded by a lipid membrane like an organelle of the endomembrane system. Its organization instead reflects the concentration and interactions of macromolecules participating in these activities.

A ribosomal DNA repeat is not equivalent to a single mature rRNA sequence. A repeat contains a transcription unit encoding multiple rRNA regions and spacers as well as sequences between transcription units. The primary transcript can include external and internal transcribed spacer segments that must be removed. A single productive transcription event can therefore supply one sequence each for several distinct mature rRNAs. This arrangement differs from a typical protein-coding gene whose primary transcript supplies the information for a particular polypeptide or related alternative products. Understanding the structure of the ribosomal transcription unit is necessary for interpreting both RNA measurements and promoter mutations. A probe within a rapidly removed spacer can report precursor abundance, whereas a probe recognizing the sequence retained in mature 18S RNA can detect a much larger accumulated pool.

Mammalian Pol I promoter recognition involves a core promoter and an upstream control region in a commonly studied rDNA architecture. The precise sequences and functional organization differ among organisms. A characteristic mammalian factor is selectivity factor 1, SL1, which contains TBP and Pol I-specific associated proteins. Its interactions help establish accurate initiation and provide a framework for recruitment of the polymerase machinery. Another factor, UBF, binds rDNA and contributes to organization and activity of transcriptionally competent promoter regions. UBF can influence the shape and accessibility of DNA; it does not replace the catalytic activity of Pol I. A third important functional connection is supplied by RRN3, also called TIF-IA in mammalian nomenclature, which associates with an initiation-competent form of polymerase I and helps connect the enzyme to the promoter-associated apparatus.

This is not simply the Pol II complex with the Roman numeral changed. Although TBP is present in SL1 and TFIID, the associated proteins and the DNA recognition environment differ. The fact that both factors contain TBP cannot establish that the classical Pol II TATA-box geometry is the mechanism at a mammalian rDNA promoter. SL1 contributes promoter recognition in cooperation with other Pol I machinery, whereas TFIID supports assembly at Pol II promoters. Similarly, RRN3 is not a substitute name for TFIIB; it provides a connection important for the Pol I system, while TFIIB participates in Pol II initiation. Functional analogies can be helpful when learning, but a shared general task does not mean the factors are identical or directly interchangeable.

A standard reconstitution question could present a mammalian rDNA promoter and a set of purified proteins. Pol I supplied alone may retain chemical activity on a permissive artificial template yet fail to produce correctly initiated RNA on the natural promoter. Adding promoter-associated factors and an initiation-competent polymerase complex can restore specificity. If selective removal of SL1 lowers accurate promoter-directed transcription, while addition of a competent SL1 preparation restores it, the experiment supports a role for SL1 in the initiation apparatus. It does not show that SL1 itself synthesizes the phosphodiester backbone. To establish that the catalytic function remains intact, the investigator can test the polymerase on a substrate that does not demand the same promoter recognition machinery.

Species-specific differences provide another application of this reasoning. Much of the catalytic chemistry is conserved, but the promoter sequences and factor interactions can evolve. A transcription factor mixture from one organism may fail to initiate on a promoter from another species even when each system works with its own promoter. A carefully designed reciprocal experiment would include both homologous combinations and the two heterologous combinations, using comparable activity and template controls. Failure of both cross-combinations suggests a compatibility constraint. If replacing one component restores accurate transcription, that factor becomes a candidate determinant of specificity. The result would not imply that the polymerase chemistry differs fundamentally between the species. It would indicate that recognition or recruitment interfaces are not fully interchangeable.

The Pol I machinery must also accommodate variable cellular demands for ribosome production. A cell that is growing and synthesizing protein rapidly benefits from abundant rRNA, whereas a nutrient-deprived cell may need to conserve resources. Changes in the fraction of active ribosomal genes, the availability of initiation-competent Pol I, or the frequency of productive transcription can all affect output. A statement that a cell has many rDNA copies does not establish that all copies are simultaneously transcribed. Copy number describes the number of potential templates; active template number depends on the state of the cell and accessibility of the transcription units. This distinction is useful when comparing genomes, cell types or environmental conditions, and it also links the chapter to later study of gene-expression regulation.

For an idealized calculation, suppose a nucleus contains 200 identical rDNA transcription units and 80 are active. If each active unit completes an average of 0.75 precursor transcripts per minute, total precursor production is 80 times 0.75, or 60 molecules per minute. If each precursor is successfully matured into one copy of each of its three major rRNAs, the idealized output is 60 molecules per minute of each type. This would correspond to at most 60 sets of major rRNAs per minute from this source. The number is not 180 complete ribosomes per minute simply because 180 individual rRNA molecules are produced. Each ribosome requires a particular combination of RNA molecules, ribosomal proteins and 5S rRNA supplied separately. Processing losses and limitations in other components can reduce the final number. The calculation connects the stoichiometry of a precursor to its biological function.

A decrease in nucleolar size or change in nucleolar appearance after inhibition of Pol I can be biologically informative, but morphology is an indirect readout. Nucleolar organization depends on multiple ongoing processes, including synthesis and maturation of RNA. An altered nucleolus does not, by itself, identify the defective factor. Combining morphology with direct measurements of nascent pre-rRNA and protein localization gives a stronger interpretation. Similarly, high steady-state rRNA abundance is compatible with either high production or persistence of a previously synthesized pool. The most informative readout for an immediate transcriptional defect is often the newly synthesized precursor rather than total mature rRNA.

13. RNA polymerase III: promoter diversity and a shared assembly principle

Pol III offers a particularly instructive example of promoter diversity because some of its best-known gene classes contain transcription control elements within the DNA that will be transcribed. This arrangement appears surprising only if a promoter has been defined exclusively as upstream DNA that is never copied. A control region is functional because proteins recognize it and organize the initiation machinery, regardless of whether the sequence is subsequently incorporated into RNA. The cell can exploit a DNA segment for recognition while also using its nucleotide information in a functional RNA product. Such overlapping requirements can constrain the evolutionary changes that are tolerated in the sequence.

The common feature of several Pol III initiation pathways is recruitment of TFIIIB, a factor containing TBP and additional Pol III-associated proteins. In the commonly described tRNA and 5S systems, TFIIIB includes Brf1 and Bdp1 alongside TBP. TFIIIB is positioned upstream of the initiation region and supports recruitment and productive initiation by Pol III. Other factors help establish its position. The division of responsibilities is important: an internal DNA-binding factor can recognize a sequence downstream of the start, yet the polymerase is ultimately recruited to begin upstream of that internal recognition site. There is no contradiction between these observations. Protein interactions bridge the spatial separation between the recognition elements and the location where catalysis begins.

Three conventional promoter classes illustrate alternative solutions to this recruitment problem. Type 1 promoters are represented by 5S rRNA genes. Their internal control region is recognized by TFIIIA, a gene-class-specific DNA-binding factor. TFIIIA helps recruit TFIIIC, which in turn contributes to positioning TFIIIB upstream of the initiation region. The resulting assembly permits Pol III recruitment and transcription. TFIIIA is not the enzyme that transcribes 5S DNA, and it is not a universal initiation factor for every Pol III gene. Its characteristic association with the 5S promoter class makes it a particularly useful component in experiments designed to distinguish 5S transcription from tRNA transcription.

The name TFIIIA is potentially confusing for a student who has just studied TFIIA. The extra Roman numeral I belongs to the designation of the polymerase system. TFIIA and TFIIIA are not equivalent proteins with a single amino acid change. The first is a Pol II general transcription factor; the second is classically associated with recognition of the 5S gene internal control region in the Pol III system. Similarly, TFIIIB and TFIIB refer to different factors, although Brf1 and certain other components of Pol III machinery have evolutionary or structural relationships to Pol II counterparts. Examination questions frequently test whether the student can read the nomenclature while retaining a clear functional model.

Type 2 promoters are classically represented by tRNA genes. Two internal elements, conventionally designated the A box and B box, provide recognition sites for TFIIIC. Through interactions with other components, TFIIIC helps place TFIIIB upstream of the transcription start. Unlike the 5S pathway, ordinary type 2 tRNA initiation does not require TFIIIA as the specialized internal recognition factor. The A and B boxes are embedded in sequences copied into precursor tRNA. Some of these sequences also contribute to the structure or function of the eventual tRNA. A mutation can therefore have two distinct consequences: reduced transcription because the DNA recognition site is altered, and defective tRNA function because the RNA sequence itself has changed. Which consequence dominates is an empirical question.

Consider a conceptual mutation in a tRNA gene's B box. If the mutation impairs recognition by TFIIIC, TFIIIB positioning and polymerase recruitment may decline. A transcription assay measuring newly produced precursor tRNA could detect this defect. Suppose instead that precursor synthesis remains normal, but the mature tRNA cannot support an expected translational activity. The result would favor a problem in RNA maturation, folding, recognition or another downstream function. The two effects are not mutually exclusive. Because the same DNA segment contributes both to initiation and to RNA sequence, an informative analysis measures transcription separately from the behavior of the resulting RNA. This is an example of why gene architecture matters when designing and interpreting mutations.

Type 3 promoters, represented by mammalian U6 small nuclear RNA genes, use primarily upstream promoter elements rather than the conventional internal A and B boxes. The proximal sequence element and associated recognition complex SNAPc, together with a TATA-containing region in representative vertebrate U6 promoters, help position the relevant machinery. A form of TFIIIB containing Brf2, rather than Brf1, is characteristic of this pathway in mammals. The resulting complex recruits Pol III. This example establishes two important exceptions: not all Pol III promoters are internal, and not all Pol III transcripts arise using the same versions of the associated factors. It also shows why the presence of a TATA motif cannot be used as a unique diagnostic marker for Pol II.

The U6 example connects the three nuclear systems through RNA processing. U6 participates in the spliceosome, while several other major spliceosomal small nuclear RNAs, such as U1 and U2 in the conventional animal model, are transcribed by Pol II. The mature components cooperate in one molecular apparatus even though their transcriptional origins differ. The fact that an RNA participates in splicing does not determine which nuclear polymerase made it. The correct inference follows from the gene and its transcription machinery. Such examples are particularly valuable in examinations that ask for an exception to a broad statement or require matching a set of RNAs with the appropriate polymerases.

The downstream end of a Pol III transcription unit can often be defined by a short thymidine tract on the non-template DNA strand. Copying the corresponding adenine residues on the template produces a uridine-rich RNA end. The termination details belong to the chapter on elongation and termination, but the sequence relationship is worth distinguishing now from the internal promoter. A T-rich termination tract is a different functional element from an A or B box, and neither should be confused with a classical Pol II TATA box. A mutation in the internal promoter may alter the frequency of initiation, whereas a mutation in a termination region can change the length or end position of transcripts. RNA size and quantity together are more informative than either alone.

A comparative depletion experiment makes the shared and specialized factors clear. A preparation capable of initiating transcription from 5S, tRNA and U6 templates is divided into several reactions. Depletion of TFIIIA is expected to strongly affect the 5S system without necessarily eliminating ordinary tRNA transcription. Depletion of TFIIIC is expected to compromise canonical 5S and tRNA pathways, whereas the upstream U6 pathway can use different recognition machinery. Disruption of a component common to appropriate TFIIIB complexes can affect a broader range of Pol III promoters. In each case the inference assumes that removal is selective, that the depleted factor is not indirectly carrying away another component, and that the tested templates use the representative mechanisms described. Re-addition experiments and direct analysis of the remaining complexes are important controls.

A protein called TFIIIC can be present on certain DNA regions without productive Pol III transcription occurring there. The presence of a recognition factor is evidence of an interaction, not necessarily evidence of initiation. The same general principle was established for Pol II occupancy and applies equally here. To demonstrate a functioning transcription unit, it is necessary to establish the appropriate relationship between occupancy, initiation and transcript formation. A cell may also contain several copies of a tRNA gene family, so loss of one copy does not necessarily eliminate every tRNA with the same anticodon. 14. Selective inhibitors as evidence for enzyme identity

Alpha-amanitin is an especially important classical biochemical tool for distinguishing nuclear RNA polymerases. Under standard textbook conditions for many mammalian or other animal polymerase preparations, Pol II is highly sensitive, Pol III is inhibited at substantially higher concentrations, and Pol I is relatively resistant over the tested range. The relationship is comparative, not an absolute declaration that all molecules of one class are inhibited at one universal threshold. Polymerases of different organisms and variants of the same enzyme can respond differently. A dose-response experiment should therefore specify the organism, enzyme preparation, concentration range, time and measured outcome. General inhibitor mechanisms and comparisons with bacterial transcription will be developed in the later inhibitors chapter; the present purpose is polymerase identification.

One can derive a simple framework for analysis of an enzyme activity assay. Let V0 be the measured rate without inhibitor and Vc the measured rate at a specified concentration c. If the control is nonzero, relative activity is Vc/V0, and percentage inhibition is 100 times the quantity 1 minus Vc/V0. These expressions normalize results to each enzyme's own baseline. Suppose an untreated preparation gives 240 incorporation units per minute and the treated preparation gives 60 units per minute. The relative activity is 60/240 = 0.25, and inhibition is 75 percent. A second fraction with baseline 40 units and treated activity 30 units is inhibited by only 25 percent even though its absolute treated activity is lower. The comparison should use normalized activity, not simply the smallest final signal.

Consider a hypothetical dataset with three separated animal nuclear polymerase fractions A, B and C, all normalized to 100 percent activity without inhibitor. At a low alpha-amanitin concentration, their respective activities are 96, 8 and 91 percent. At a much higher concentration, the activities are 94, 2 and 12 percent. Fraction B has the characteristic highly sensitive response of Pol II. Fraction C resembles Pol III, because it remains largely active at the low concentration but is strongly inhibited at the higher concentration. Fraction A is the likely Pol I activity because it remains high over the tested range. These labels are reasonable in the stated context, not independent proof of the assignments. Identifying the proteins in each fraction or demonstrating their activity on characteristic promoters would strengthen the conclusion.

A mixture illustrates how the same logic can be used quantitatively. Suppose total incorporation in a crude extract is 100 arbitrary units, falling to 65 units after a low concentration of inhibitor and to 25 units after a higher concentration. Under the deliberately idealized assumptions that low concentration completely inhibits Pol II only, high concentration completely inhibits Pol II and Pol III only, Pol I is unaffected, and activities add independently, the low-dose loss is 35 units attributable to Pol II. The additional loss between low and high concentration is 40 units attributable to Pol III. The remaining 25 units are assigned to Pol I. These results are contributions to the measured activity, not necessarily proportions of polymerase molecules. Different enzymes can have different rates per molecule on the assay template.

Real inhibition curves are seldom perfect step functions. If the low-dose condition leaves 10 percent of Pol II activity and modestly reduces Pol III activity, the subtraction procedure above no longer yields exact class contributions. A more accurate analysis would use measured sensitivity coefficients for each purified enzyme under identical assay conditions. Each observed total would then be modeled as a weighted sum of the underlying activities, with the weights corresponding to residual fractions at each concentration. The central principle is that one measurement generally cannot determine several unknown contributions without further constraints. The simplified problem is useful because the assumptions make the calculation identifiable; in a real experiment, the assumptions must be tested rather than silently adopted.

The concentration that produces a 50 percent reduction in a defined activity is often described as an IC50. It is not automatically the same as the equilibrium dissociation constant of an inhibitor for an enzyme. Activity depends on substrate concentrations, incubation duration, inhibitor binding mechanism and other experimental conditions. A concentration-response curve also does not establish that inhibition is competitive with nucleotide substrates. The molecular interaction of alpha-amanitin with Pol II interferes with efficient transcriptional progression, and the characteristic response is used here as a marker of enzyme identity. It should not be classified as a reagent that simply removes the TATA box, prevents TBP recognition, or directly blocks ribosome translation.

A final distinction concerns direct and downstream effects. If Pol II transcription is strongly inhibited in a cell, production of new mRNAs encoding many proteins can decline. Over time, depletion of these proteins can affect cell metabolism, rRNA synthesis and tRNA use. A prolonged decline in a Pol I product does not establish direct inhibition of Pol I by the original reagent. An acute purified enzyme assay addresses the immediate sensitivity question, whereas a time course in cells includes additional biological dependencies. Students should pay close attention to the duration and system described in an examination rather than apply a classification table without considering experimental context.

15. Integrating the systems: transcription, processing and experimental interpretation

The shared features of the three nuclear systems can now be described in a causal sequence. Each contains an enzyme capable of DNA-directed RNA synthesis, a way to recognize appropriate transcription units, and a mechanism that brings the enzyme into a productive relationship with the template. In each case the polymerase's catalytic center supplies nucleotide incorporation, while associated factors determine many aspects of initiation. The systems differ in their typical products, promoter structures, partner proteins, localization and distinctive modes of coupling transcription to subsequent events. The distinction is one of organization around a conserved biochemical task, not three unrelated forms of nucleic acid chemistry.

The collaboration among polymerases is especially evident when considering cellular protein synthesis. The small and large ribosomal subunits contain products derived from the major Pol I precursor, a Pol III-derived 5S component and proteins whose mRNAs are supplied by Pol II. Pol III also supplies tRNAs needed for decoding those mRNAs. Thus, reducing one pathway can limit the performance of a process that requires products from all three. Consider a simplified assembly model in which 100 sets of major rRNAs, 80 copies of 5S rRNA and enough proteins for 90 ribosomes become available in an interval. Assuming one complete set of each category is required per new ribosome and no pre-existing surplus, the theoretical maximum is the minimum of 100, 80 and 90, which is 80 ribosomes. Increasing Pol I output alone would not increase that maximum unless the limiting 5S supply were improved. This stoichiometric model does not describe every regulatory response, but it clarifies the idea of a limiting component in a multi-pathway system.

An equally important integration concerns measurement of RNA abundance. In a simple model with a constant rate s of RNA synthesis, a first-order degradation constant d and RNA abundance R, the rate of change is s minus dR. At steady state the two rates are equal, and R equals s/d. If the initial synthesis rate is 200 molecules per hour and the degradation constant is 0.5 per hour, the predicted steady abundance is 400 molecules. A mutant with synthesis rate 100 molecules per hour and degradation constant 0.25 per hour also has a predicted steady abundance of 400 molecules. Equal mature RNA levels therefore do not imply equal transcription rates. The model assumes a single pool, linear removal, constant rates and no other fluxes. Actual RNA maturation may require separate precursor and mature pools, but the simple calculation is enough to expose a major interpretive trap.

The most useful examination strategy is to associate each experimental measurement with the biochemical state it measures. A footprint measures DNA protection and can suggest binding. An assay detecting exposed DNA bases reports opening under its particular conditions. An RNA incorporation measurement reports synthesis, while mapping primary 5′ ends can report where initiation occurred. A processing-specific assay addresses maturation, and a mature RNA measurement reflects synthesis, processing and removal together. No single readout automatically proves all the preceding events. If two experiments appear contradictory, ask whether they measure different states. The apparent contradiction between high promoter occupancy and low RNA output is a particularly important example.

A reconstitution experiment establishes sufficiency under specified conditions, whereas selective depletion or mutation can test requirement. Reconstitution with a defined set of proteins and a natural promoter is stronger evidence of the capacity of those proteins to support initiation than a binding assay alone. A negative result after removing a factor needs controls for depletion specificity and structural integrity of the remaining machinery. Restoring activity by adding back the factor provides additional support. A mutation that changes a promoter contact while leaving protein stability unchanged is often more mechanistically informative than deletion of an entire protein. Nonetheless, no one experiment settles every question. Different observations must be interpreted together to distinguish direct action, dependence on other factors and indirect consequences.

The general factors do not define the complete explanation of tissue-specific gene expression. A liver cell and a neuron possess much of the same basic Pol II transcription machinery, yet they express different groups of genes. The difference cannot be explained merely by assigning one cell Pol I and the other Pol II. Both require all three major nuclear systems. Gene-specific regulators and the state of chromatin influence where and when the general machinery can be recruited. This is why the next chapter will build on the present molecular vocabulary to explain how activators and repressors affect initiation. The subsequent chapter will follow polymerases after initiation. By keeping these levels distinct, students can reason from a change in one protein or DNA element to a precise prediction rather than memorizing a large, undifferentiated list of transcription factors.

  2. Enzyme-specific occupancy together with nascent RNA identity
  3. Total cell DNA content
  4. The presence of a nucleus alone
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B — Enzyme-specific occupancy together with nascent RNA identity

TBP is shared among nuclear systems, so its occupancy is not diagnostic. Class-specific enzyme evidence and transcript identity strengthen assignment.

47. An unprocessed 160-nucleotide RNA is initiated de novo. How many phosphodiester linkages and polymerization-associated PPi molecules are formed?

numerical

  1. 160 and 160
  2. 159 and 160
  3. 159 and 159
  4. 80 and 80
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C — 159 and 159

A linear chain of n residues has n−1 internucleotide linkages. The first nucleotide retains its 5′ triphosphate; the remaining 159 additions release PPi.

48. A hypothetical promoter-binding protein has Kd = 30 nM. At free protein 60 nM, what is site occupancy in the independent one-site equilibrium model?

numerical

  1. 2/3
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/2
  4. 2
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A — 2/3

θ = p/(Kd+p) = 60/(30+60) = 2/3, dimensionless. A binding fraction cannot exceed one.

49. Twelve equivalent promoters each have occupancy 0.25 and effective productive initiation rate 0.4 min⁻¹ per occupied promoter. What is total mean flux?

numerical

  1. 4.8/min
  2. 0.1/min
  3. 12/min
  4. 1.2/min
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D — 1.2/min

J = Nfk = 12×0.25×0.4 = 1.2 events/min. The model assumes comparable independent copies and stable averages.

50. An idealized crude extract gives 180 units without inhibitor, 100 at a low dose eliminating only Pol II, and 35 at a high dose eliminating Pol II and III. What are Pol I, II and III activity contributions?

numerical

  1. 35, 65, 80
  2. 35, 80, 65
  3. 80, 35, 65
  4. 65, 80, 35
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B — 35, 80, 65

Pol I = 35; Pol II = 180−100 = 80; Pol III = 100−35 = 65. These are assay activities, not enzyme molecule counts.

51. Fraction A falls from 150 to 45 units/min after inhibitor. Fraction B falls from 40 to 20. Which has higher percentage inhibition?

numerical

  1. B, 80%
  2. Both 50%
  3. A, 70%
  4. A, 30%
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C — A, 70%

A loses 105/150 = 70%; B loses 20/40 = 50%. The smaller absolute treated activity of B does not mean greater relative inhibition.

52. A hypothetical CTD has 32 perfect YSPTSPS repeats. How many residues and potential Ser5 positions occur in those repeats?

  2. 96 and 32
  3. 224 and 96
  4. 32 and 224

32×7 = 224 residues. There is exactly one Ser5 position per canonical repeat; all serine positions together would total 96.

53. A nucleus has 150 active rDNA units, each producing 0.6 precursors/min. Processing succeeds for 80% of precursors, yielding one set of three major rRNAs each. How many sets/min?

numerical

  1. 90
  2. 270
  3. 216
  4. 72
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D — 72

150×0.6 = 90 precursors/min; 90×0.8 = 72 complete sets/min. Counting individual rRNAs would give 216, not 216 sets.

54. A mutant synthesizes an RNA at 80 molecules/hour with first-order degradation constant 0.2/hour. Under the simple one-pool steady-state model, what abundance is predicted?

numerical

  1. 16 molecules
  2. 400 molecules
  3. 80 molecules
  4. 0.0025 molecules
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B — 400 molecules

At steady state s = dR, so R = 80/0.2 = 400 molecules. This does not measure transcription rate without knowing degradation.

55. A growing cell obtains 70 complete sets of major rRNAs, 120 copies of 5S, and enough ribosomal proteins for 85 ribosomes in a time interval. With no surplus and ideal assembly, which pathway's output limits assembly?

integrative

  1. Pol III, because 5S is in the large subunit
  2. Pol II, because proteins are required
  3. Pol I-derived major rRNA sets, limiting output to 70
  4. No pathway, because 70+120+85=275
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C — Pol I-derived major rRNA sets, limiting output to 70

Assembly requires one of each specified set, so the minimum is 70. The sum is not a ribosome count, and the other supplies are in excess in this model.

Revision and active recall

Mechanism summary

Pol I: mammalian rDNA recognition by SL1 and UBF, recruitment of an initiation-competent Pol I–RRN3 complex, synthesis of major pre-rRNA. Pol II: promoter recognition and organization, Pol II–TFIIF recruitment, TFIIE and TFIIH-dependent opening, initiation and early CTD phosphorylation. Pol III: promoter-class-dependent recognition, convergence on appropriate TFIIIB, recruitment of Pol III.

Formula sheet

  • RNA length n → n−1 internucleotide linkages and n−1 polymerization-associated PPi molecules for de novo initiation, before processing.
  • Single-site equilibrium: θ = p/(Kd+p), with p and Kd in identical concentration units.
  • Idealized productive initiation: J = Nfk, events per unit time.
  • Percentage inhibition = 100(1−Vc/V0), provided V0 is positive.
  • Single RNA pool at steady state: R = s/d, with s in molecules per time and d in inverse time.
  • Idealized complete assembly is bounded by the least available required component set.

Misconceptions and examination traps

All ribosomal RNA is Pol I-derived
5S rRNA is normally made by Pol III; the major precursor gives 18S, 5.8S and 28S in mammals.
Only protein-coding RNA is made by Pol II
Many noncoding transcripts, including representative U1 and U2 snRNAs, are Pol II products.
Every Pol II promoter has TATA
Many promoters use different recognition elements; TATA is a representative example.
Every Pol III promoter is internal
The U6 type 3 promoter illustrates upstream recognition.
TBP is exclusive to Pol II
TBP participates in TFIID, SL1 and TFIIIB.
TFIIA and TFIIIA are the same factor
They belong to distinct transcription systems and have different functions.
TFIIH is just a helicase
Its XPB translocase supports opening, and its CDK7 kinase modifies CTD; XPD is particularly important in repair.
ATP has one role
Nucleotide incorporation, ATPase-driven movement and phosphate transfer are distinct reactions.
CTD makes the cap
The CTD helps recruit processing proteins; it does not catalyze all processing reactions itself.
High promoter occupancy proves high transcription
A bound complex can fail to open DNA or progress; compare occupancy and productive flux.
Absolute inhibitor thresholds are universal
Species, dose, assay and time influence observed sensitivity. Use the calibrated comparative pattern.
Equal mature RNA amounts imply equal synthesis
Different degradation rates can compensate for different synthesis rates.
Svedberg units are additive
Sedimentation behavior depends on size, shape and solvent interactions, not simple arithmetic sums.

Active recall

Name the three normal nuclear polymerase classes and one exception to the usual shorthand for each.

Pol I: major precursor, not 5S; Pol II: pre-mRNA and many ncRNAs; Pol III: tRNA and 5S plus RNAs such as U6.

Explain why TATA deletion need not completely abolish transcription.

Other core elements and TAF-mediated interactions can help recruit and position the apparatus.

Distinguish TBP, TFIID and TFIIB.

TBP is a shared DNA-associated protein; TFIID contains TBP and TAFs; TFIIB helps connect and position Pol II.

Trace the representative roles of TFIIA, TFIIF and TFIIE.

Stabilization; polymerase recruitment and stabilization; organization of TFIIH.

What two distinct TFIIH functions are most important here?

XPB ATP-dependent translocation and CDK7-mediated phosphorylation.

Write the CTD repeat and explain why its phosphorylation matters.

YSPTSPS; modification changes recruitment of associated factors and coordinates RNA processing.

Distinguish canonical type 1, 2 and 3 Pol III pathways.

5S: TFIIIA and TFIIIC; tRNA: TFIIIC with A/B boxes; U6: upstream SNAPc and appropriate TFIIIB.

What is the characteristic animal alpha-amanitin pattern?

Pol II highly sensitive, Pol III less sensitive, Pol I relatively resistant; context dependent.

How can a promoter be occupied but unproductive?

The bound complex may not open DNA, initiate correctly or progress efficiently.

Why can a cell retain mature rRNA despite a rapid fall in Pol I activity?

Existing rRNA persists; acute precursor synthesis is a different measure from accumulated mature RNA.
