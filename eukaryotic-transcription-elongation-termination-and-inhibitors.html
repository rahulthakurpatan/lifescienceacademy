1. From a successful start to a useful RNA

The discovery that DNA carries genetic information does not, by itself, explain how a cell uses that information. A protein-coding region may be thousands of nucleotides long, while a single RNA polymerase can recognize and incorporate only one incoming nucleotide at its catalytic center at a time. Successful gene expression therefore requires not merely recognition of a promoter but a stable, moving molecular assembly that maintains contact with the template over considerable distances. It also requires a way to stop that assembly when it has made enough RNA. In eukaryotes these requirements are complicated by the fact that the first RNA copy is normally not the same molecule that ultimately reaches the cytoplasm for translation. The polymerase must produce a precursor while cooperating with other enzymes that recognize, modify and sometimes remove parts of that precursor.

Consider two copies of a hypothetical mammalian gene. In the first copy, the promoter recruits many polymerases, but most of them cease making RNA after the first sixty nucleotides. In the second copy, recruitment happens less often, but nearly every polymerase reaches the end. Measuring promoter binding alone would suggest that the first copy is more active. Measuring completed transcripts could give the opposite conclusion. This distinction explains why initiation, elongation and termination are three mechanistically meaningful stages rather than three arbitrary headings in a textbook. A promoter-bound enzyme has not necessarily made any RNA, an initiated enzyme has not necessarily entered productive elongation, and an enzyme that has synthesized the future mature RNA sequence has not necessarily left the DNA.

The principal subject of this chapter is nuclear RNA polymerase II, usually abbreviated Pol II. Its protein-coding products are initially precursor messenger RNAs, and it also makes several classes of noncoding RNA. Nuclear Pol I and Pol III solve related but not identical problems while producing different RNA classes. Their comparison is especially useful when identifying a transcription mechanism from an experimental observation. For instance, a short uridine-rich RNA end may arise because Pol III recognizes a termination signal quite different from the signal used by Pol II at a typical messenger RNA gene. A single universal description of termination would conceal this fundamental difference.

The chapter builds on the distinction between the coding strand and template strand. If the coding strand runs from 5′ to 3′ in the direction of transcription, its sequence matches the RNA apart from replacement of thymine by uracil. The antiparallel template strand runs from 3′ to 5′ in that direction. Polymerase extends the RNA at its 3′ hydroxyl end. This rule remains valid from the first phosphodiester bond to the final nucleotide incorporated before release. A gene on the opposite chromosomal strand is transcribed in the opposite genomic direction, but the chemical direction of RNA extension is unchanged. Promoter escape cannot reverse this polarity, and termination does not require reversal of synthesis.

It is equally important to separate an RNA molecule's position in its transcriptional history from the sequence of the molecule. A nascent RNA is still associated with the transcription machinery; a precursor is an RNA that needs further maturation; a mature RNA has acquired the features required for its normal function. The terms are not mutually exclusive at all times. A nascent Pol II transcript may already have acquired its 5′ cap and lost an intron while its 3′ portion is still being synthesized. A released RNA may still need processing. This overlap becomes intelligible once the polymerase is regarded as a platform for an assembly of processing and elongation factors rather than a solitary enzyme that makes an inert string of nucleotides.

2. How the existence of distinct transcription machines was established

Early biochemical experiments on isolated nuclei established that DNA-dependent RNA synthesis could be studied separately from protein synthesis. The reaction depended upon ribonucleoside triphosphates, DNA and a functional enzyme. Incorporation of a labeled ribonucleotide into an RNA product allowed investigators to quantify new synthesis rather than the large pool of RNA already present in cells. This distinction is essential: ribosomal RNA can dominate the total RNA mass of a cell even when the question concerns the instantaneous rate of messenger RNA production. An incorporation assay preferentially asks what is being made during the measurement interval.

A major advance came when nuclear RNA polymerase activities could be separated chromatographically. Enzymes that have different surface charges or binding properties can elute from a column at different salt concentrations. Each collected fraction can be tested for its ability to make RNA from a DNA template. Separate peaks of activity suggested that eukaryotic nuclei contain multiple transcription enzymes rather than a single bacterial-like enzyme serving every nuclear gene. The classic studies of Roeder and Rutter in 1969 helped establish this distinction. Fractionation alone could not fully identify the biological role of each peak: a protein might have been modified during extraction, or an accessory factor might have separated from a shared enzyme. Additional biochemical and cellular evidence was required.

The 1970 study of Lindell and colleagues demonstrated the unusual sensitivity of nuclear RNA polymerase II to alpha-amanitin. The approach made an important logical connection between an enzymatic activity and a chemical probe that could distinguish it from other activities. If RNA synthesis in one fraction declined markedly after inhibitor exposure while synthesis in another fraction persisted under the same assay conditions, the result supported a molecular difference between the enzymes. Appropriate comparisons required untreated enzyme fractions and identical template and nucleotide conditions. Without these controls an apparently inhibitor-resistant fraction might simply have started with a higher enzyme concentration or an unsuitable substrate for the inhibitor-sensitive reaction.

Subsequent work connected the different enzymes to particular RNA products. Pol I produces the large precursor that gives rise to the major ribosomal RNAs of the cytosolic ribosome, excluding 5S rRNA. Pol II makes nuclear pre-mRNAs as well as other RNAs, including many small nuclear RNAs. Pol III makes tRNAs, 5S rRNA and various other short RNAs. The product classification is an experimentally supported generalization, not a deduction from transcript length alone. Some Pol II transcripts are short, and some Pol III products need substantial processing after synthesis. This is why the identity of a polymerase should be inferred from multiple features, including its promoter system, inhibitor sensitivity and RNA product, rather than from one convenient characteristic.

Later structural studies made it possible to examine an elongation complex containing polymerase, DNA and RNA simultaneously. A structure reveals the spatial organization that permits DNA strands to separate, a short RNA–DNA hybrid to form and the RNA to exit. Such evidence explains why elongation is processive: polymerase does not need to leave the DNA and bind the next nucleotide position afresh after every bond. Structural observations also helped explain why alpha-amanitin can strongly inhibit movement through a template even though the incoming nucleotide binding site is not simply occupied by a molecule resembling a nucleotide. A static structure is a snapshot, however, and evidence for the rates and sequence of molecular transitions must also come from biochemical kinetics.

Another conceptual change concerned the observation that polymerases can accumulate near promoters of genes whose full-length RNA production is low. In metazoan systems, classical studies of Drosophila heat shock genes helped establish that initiated Pol II can remain near the promoter and subsequently be released into productive elongation. A burst of gene expression need not always begin with recruitment of a new enzyme from solution. It can also involve activation of an already engaged enzyme. Chromosome-associated polymerase, newly made short RNA and full-length transcript production therefore describe related but distinct experimental variables. These observations motivated the modern concept of post-initiation transcriptional regulation.

3. Architecture of the elongation complex

The elongation complex is a composite of Pol II, the DNA template, the newly synthesized RNA and associated factors. At a given moment most of the gene remains double-stranded DNA. Only a local region is opened around the catalytic center. One DNA strand enters the active center as the template, while the other follows a separate path. The open region is called the transcription bubble. Behind the moving enzyme the DNA strands can pair again. Consequently the polymerase does not convert the whole gene into a permanent single-stranded region, even when the gene is many thousands of bases long.

Within the bubble a short section of the new RNA remains complementary to the DNA template. This RNA–DNA hybrid is important because it holds the RNA 3′ end in an orientation appropriate for catalysis. The hybrid is typically on the order of eight or nine base pairs in a canonical active elongation complex, whereas the total region of opened DNA is somewhat longer and varies with complex state. The RNA that was made earlier must leave the hybrid and pass toward an exit route. This arrangement solves a geometrical problem: an increasingly long product can emerge from the enzyme without forcing the entire product to remain threaded into the DNA double helix.

The template nucleotide at the active center determines which incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate is favored by complementary base pairing and catalytic selection. Complementarity is necessary for faithful copying, but the enzyme also selects substrates through their shape, positioning and chemical contacts. A deoxyribonucleotide lacks the ribose 2′ hydroxyl group characteristic of RNA nucleotides. Polymerase discrimination against such substrates helps maintain the chemical identity of the product. Unlike DNA replication, transcription normally produces a transient informational copy rather than a permanent duplicate of the genome. The fidelity requirements are therefore not identical, though transcription errors can still produce dysfunctional RNA or protein.

Pol II includes a bridge helix and a mobile trigger loop near its active center. These structural elements participate in nucleotide selection, catalysis and movements that accompany nucleotide addition. At an examination level it is sufficient to understand that the enzyme must alternate between configurations that allow substrate entry, correct positioning, phosphodiester bond formation and advancement to the next template position. Mutations that compromise these movements can affect the rate or fidelity of synthesis even when the enzyme is still capable of binding DNA and RNA. This is a general lesson from enzyme mechanism: the measured affinity of a substrate is not equivalent to the rate at which a complete catalytic cycle occurs.

A clamp-like arrangement helps retain nucleic acids in the complex. The ability to retain a growing RNA and its template through repeated catalytic cycles is described as processivity. It should not be confused with elongation velocity. A highly processive polymerase can move slowly but remain associated over many kilobases. Conversely, an enzyme may move rapidly during individual successful runs yet frequently detach before reaching the gene end. An assay that measures nucleotide incorporation in a short interval primarily reflects the synthesis rate of the active population; an assay that compares full-length and prematurely released products can reveal defects in processivity.

The physical organization of the moving enzyme also establishes why newly formed RNA can be processed during transcription. Its older 5′ region lies outside the catalytic center and can interact with binding proteins and enzymes while the 3′ end is still being extended. The two regions of the same RNA molecule are simultaneously accessible to different processes. An RNA capping enzyme does not have to wait for the polymerase to reach the end of a gene. Nor does it compete for the same 3′ hydroxyl that performs phosphodiester bond formation. Processing chemistry is spatially distinct from polymerization chemistry even when both reactions are coordinated by the same transcriptional assembly.

4. The chemical cycle of RNA chain extension

The chemical reaction during elongation is a nucleotidyl transfer. The 3′ hydroxyl of the terminal RNA residue attacks the alpha phosphate of an incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate. A new phosphodiester linkage joins the previous RNA residue to the incoming nucleotide, while the beta and gamma phosphates depart together as pyrophosphate. In an extended RNA chain, the incorporated unit is consequently a nucleoside monophosphate residue rather than an intact triphosphate. The new nucleotide brings another 3′ hydroxyl, which becomes the substrate for the next extension reaction.

This chemistry explains directionality more rigorously than a memorized arrow. Since each new bond forms at the 3′ end, the 5′ end of the original RNA remains the earlier end. For a template whose sequence in the direction of reading is 3′-TACGGT-5′, the corresponding RNA is 5′-AUGCCA-3′. The first residue is adenosine, and extension proceeds through uridine, guanosine, cytidine and the remaining complementary residues. Writing the RNA as 3′-AUGCCA-5′ would contradict the orientation of synthesis. In a real elongation complex the previously synthesized portion provides the attacking hydroxyl; the special problem of forming the first bond during initiation is already resolved.

Divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium, are central to the catalytic mechanism. In the standard two-metal description, metal coordination helps position reactive groups, promotes the reaction of the hydroxyl nucleophile and stabilizes charge during phosphoryl transfer. Simply providing free nucleotides without an appropriately organized catalytic environment does not reproduce the enzyme's rate or selectivity. Too little available magnesium can impair synthesis; however, high total magnesium does not necessarily correspond to high free magnesium because nucleotides and other molecules bind it. Thus concentrations in biochemical experiments should be interpreted as part of a specified reaction mixture rather than isolated numbers.

The reaction can be represented as RNA(n) + NTP → RNA(n+1) + PPi. Here n is the number of residues already present in the RNA and PPi is inorganic pyrophosphate. Cellular pyrophosphate hydrolysis, PPi + H₂O → 2 Pi, helps favor net polymer formation by removing a reaction product. The energy is provided by the incoming nucleotide's chemical potential. There is no separate ATP hydrolysis requirement for every phosphodiester bond made by Pol II. ATP can be the incoming substrate when adenine is required, and ATP-consuming accessory enzymes can regulate transcription or alter chromatin, but those roles must be distinguished from the direct chemistry of chain extension.

At each position the complex must become ready to accept the next nucleotide. This involves movement of DNA and RNA relative to the active center, often described as translocation. Translocation is not equivalent to DNA helicase action ahead of the polymerase. Once a stable elongation complex is established, the polymerase itself maintains and moves the local transcription bubble. The DNA duplex is disrupted locally ahead and re-established behind as part of the coupled moving complex. Specialized initiation factors that helped open the original promoter are therefore not required to travel along the entire gene and perform the same opening operation at every base.

If an incorrect nucleotide is incorporated, extension can become inefficient because the terminal base is mismatched or poorly positioned. Pol II can pause and backtrack, meaning that it shifts upstream relative to the nucleic acid scaffold. In a backtracked state the RNA 3′ end no longer occupies its normal catalytic position. Cleavage of a short segment of the RNA can regenerate an aligned 3′ end, enabling synthesis to resume. The elongation factor TFIIS promotes transcript cleavage by the polymerase, helping resolve certain backtracked complexes. It is incorrect to describe TFIIS as a separate DNA repair enzyme, or to assume that proofreading always restores the exact correct sequence after any possible error.

The reverse reaction, pyrophosphorolysis, is chemically possible under appropriate conditions, but normal cellular transcription is strongly biased toward net synthesis by substrate availability and product handling. Transcript cleavage during proofreading is distinct from simply reversing nucleotidyl transfer with pyrophosphate. Both can shorten RNA, yet the reactants and molecular circumstances differ. A useful experimental distinction is whether a shortened product appears following addition of pyrophosphate or during a hydrolytic cleavage event in a backtracked complex. The student's broader objective is to associate each observed RNA product with the specific molecular reaction that could have generated it.

5. Promoter escape as a change in molecular organization

The promoter is the DNA region at which initiation machinery assembles and positions the polymerase at a transcription start site. Promoter escape is the transition in which an initially transcribing Pol II becomes capable of moving downstream in a stable elongation complex. Before escape, interactions that helped hold the enzyme in the correct starting position can become obstacles to movement. The RNA grows within the enzyme, the transcription bubble changes shape, and contacts between polymerase and the basal initiation machinery are rearranged. The enzyme must retain the DNA and RNA connections needed for processive synthesis while relinquishing interactions that would otherwise tether it to the promoter.

TFIIH has two especially important functions in the preceding initiation process. Its XPB subunit participates in opening promoter DNA through ATP-dependent translocase activity, and its CDK7 kinase module phosphorylates the C-terminal domain of the largest Pol II subunit. Serine 5 phosphorylation becomes prominent during initiation and early transcription. These events contribute to the change in protein interactions that accompanies promoter escape. It would be misleading to claim that phosphorylation alone mechanically pushes polymerase along DNA. Chain growth, structural rearrangement and changes in factor binding collectively produce the transition; phosphorylation is a regulatory component of that transition.

Not every short RNA proves that a polymerase has escaped successfully. During initial transcription, RNA may be released before a stable elongation complex forms, a phenomenon called abortive initiation. In contrast, a promoter-proximally paused enzyme has usually escaped the earliest initiation state and remains engaged with a nascent transcript while awaiting further transitions. The two phenomena can be distinguished conceptually by their product and complex state. An abortive short RNA has been released; a paused complex retains the possibility of continuing the same transcript. In an experimental assay, the ability of a previously synthesized short labeled RNA to become part of a longer molecule after suitable conditions are restored provides evidence for an engaged, resumable complex.

Some general transcription machinery can remain at or reassemble near a promoter, allowing another round of initiation. This is one reason a gene can be occupied by multiple polymerases at different positions rather than waiting for one complete transcript before starting another. The interval between initiations and the time necessary for one polymerase to traverse the gene are independent parameters within limits imposed by physical crowding and regulatory processes. A long gene can have several elongation complexes simultaneously. Increasing initiation frequency can raise the number of these complexes, but only if downstream elongation and termination can accommodate the resulting flux.

A promoter mutation that prevents efficient recruitment and a CTD-related defect that interferes with early transitions can both reduce mature RNA abundance, yet they predict different intermediate distributions. A recruitment defect should reduce the number of engaged enzymes from the beginning. A defect after recruitment may leave promoter occupancy relatively intact while reducing the number of polymerases in the body of the gene. The distinction is not absolute because perturbing one stage can feed back on other stages. Nevertheless, comparing multiple regions of a transcription unit is a powerful way to narrow down the affected step.

6. The C-terminal domain: changing partners during the transcription cycle

An important feature distinguishing nuclear Pol II from Pol I and Pol III is the extended carboxyl-terminal domain of its largest subunit, RPB1. This region, called the CTD, projects from the main catalytic body of the enzyme. The CTD is not itself the site at which a nucleotide is added to RNA. Rather, it provides a flexible platform on which proteins involved in transcription and RNA maturation can assemble. Because it contains repeated amino acid sequences, multiple sites can be modified and multiple proteins can interact with the same polymerase. The polymerase therefore carries more than a catalytic center as it progresses through a gene: it carries a changing collection of molecular interaction opportunities.

The consensus CTD heptapeptide sequence is Tyr-Ser-Pro-Thr-Ser-Pro-Ser, commonly written YSPTSPS. Numbering refers to positions in one seven-residue repeat rather than positions in the entire RPB1 protein. Ser2 and Ser5 are the serines at the second and fifth positions respectively. The human CTD has 52 repeats, not all identical to the consensus. Budding yeast has 26 repeats in the usual reference sequence. These numbers illustrate evolutionary variation in repeat length; they should not be generalized to every eukaryotic organism. The pattern of repeats allows a combination of modified and unmodified residues to exist on the same molecular tail.

A newly recruited Pol II commonly has a relatively hypophosphorylated CTD. During initiation, the CDK7 kinase module associated with TFIIH phosphorylates CTD sites including Ser5. As the complex moves away from the promoter and enters productive elongation, phosphorylation at Ser2 becomes increasingly prominent across many protein-coding genes. CDK9, as part of P-TEFb, contributes to the transition, and other kinases, including CDK12 and CDK13 in metazoans, also contribute to Ser2 phosphorylation. It is therefore an oversimplification to assign every Ser2 phosphate to CDK9 or to imagine that every Ser5 phosphate must disappear before the first Ser2 phosphate can be added.

Kinases catalyze the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP to protein residues. Phosphatases remove phosphate groups through hydrolysis. Both processes can alter the interaction surface of the CTD by changing charge distribution, hydrogen-bonding opportunities and the recognition of specific short motifs. A protein that binds efficiently to an unmodified repeat may not bind the phosphorylated version, and another protein may prefer a phosphorylated repeat. The CTD cycle thus changes the probability that different partners will associate with the enzyme at different genomic positions. It does not change the identity of the DNA template or impose a different genetic code.

The relation to RNA processing follows from the geometry and timing of transcription. When the first part of an RNA has emerged from Pol II, enzymes responsible for constructing the 5′ cap can encounter its exposed end. Interactions with Ser5-phosphorylated CTD support efficient recruitment and, in some systems, activation of the capping machinery. The association places a processing enzyme close to its substrate at an appropriate stage of synthesis. A freely diffusing enzyme could in principle find RNA by collision, but local recruitment improves coordination and helps distinguish newly synthesized Pol II RNA from other RNA species.

Later in transcription, the emerging RNA contains sequences that permit splice-site recognition and eventually 3′ end processing. CTD-associated interactions help organize these maturation events. This does not mean that every factor binds directly to Ser2P, nor that the CTD alone is sufficient to specify correct splice junctions. Processing factors also recognize RNA sequences, RNA structures and interactions with other proteins. The CTD is one component of a larger recruitment and regulatory system. Its importance lies partly in allowing these components to encounter a transcript in the order in which the relevant sequences become accessible.

At termination and recycling, CTD dephosphorylation helps restore a population of polymerases compatible with new initiation. FCP1 is a well-known CTD phosphatase involved in resetting the cycle; other phosphatases act at particular positions and stages. A defect in dephosphorylation could therefore alter not just one elongation event but the supply of polymerase available for subsequent rounds. In a steady-state experiment, the initial defect may occur near the end of genes while a secondary decrease in recruitment becomes apparent later. This is why the consequences of mutations in a cyclic molecular system should be considered both immediately and after enough time for the system to redistribute its components.

One useful experiment compares the amount of Pol II recognized by an antibody to the total enzyme with the amount recognized by an antibody specific to a phosphorylated CTD residue. Enrichment of Ser5P near the promoter and Ser2P across the gene body and toward the 3′ region supports a spatially changing modification pattern. Such an experiment does not establish that each individual polymerase has followed an identical sequence. Antibodies have different affinities, and measurements average across cells, polymerases and CTD repeats. Nevertheless, comparisons with suitable controls provide a strong basis for the general transcription-cycle model.

A different experiment could compare wild-type Pol II with a CTD mutant that reduces the number of functional repeats. If RNA synthesis on a simple naked template remains possible but processing of the RNA in cells becomes inefficient, the results suggest that the tail has an important coordination function distinct from the essential catalytic core. A mutant that disrupts the entire protein may instead reduce all transcription. The distinction matters in interpretation: an inability to recruit one processing factor is not the same experimental condition as complete loss of Pol II activity.

7. Promoter-proximal pausing and regulated release

Pausing is a temporary reduction or cessation in the forward movement of an engaged RNA polymerase. Pauses can occur at many positions during transcription because nucleotide sequences, local chromatin and the internal state of the enzyme affect the probability of the next catalytic step. Promoter-proximal pausing refers to a particularly regulated early elongation state prominent in many metazoan genes. A typical paused polymerase is found tens of nucleotides downstream of the transcription start site, often around 20–60 nucleotides, although the position and degree of pausing vary widely among genes and organisms.

Two factors involved in establishing this state are DSIF and NELF. DSIF stands for DRB sensitivity-inducing factor and contains SPT4 and SPT5. NELF stands for negative elongation factor and is a multisubunit protein complex in metazoans. DSIF interacts with the elongation machinery, and NELF helps stabilize the paused state. These proteins should not be imagined as universal components of every eukaryotic polymerase. In particular, budding yeast lacks the canonical metazoan NELF complex. It nevertheless regulates elongation and uses conserved Spt4/Spt5-related machinery. Thus the general principle of post-initiation control is broader than the particular DSIF–NELF model.

An important factor that promotes release of the paused enzyme is positive transcription elongation factor b, P-TEFb, containing CDK9 and a cyclin T partner in the standard mammalian description. CDK9 phosphorylates substrates associated with the early elongation complex, including SPT5, NELF and the Pol II CTD. Phosphorylation is associated with loss of NELF from the complex and conversion of DSIF into a factor supporting productive elongation. SPT5 remains associated with elongating Pol II. The pathway illustrates how phosphorylation can change the activity of an existing factor instead of merely destroying it and replacing it with an unrelated protein.

The meaning of a positive elongation factor depends on the state of the system. If no polymerase has been recruited and no RNA has been initiated, activating CDK9 alone cannot manufacture a complete transcript. Conversely, when many engaged polymerases are paused near a promoter, increasing release efficiency can rapidly increase the number entering the gene body without a proportional initial increase in recruitment. The regulatory value of the pause is therefore that it creates a stage at which a gene's output may be altered after promoter recognition. Some rapidly inducible genes exploit this possibility; the detailed transcriptional responses vary with the organism and gene.

A pause need not be harmful. The early transcript can undergo capping while the elongation complex remains near the promoter. Maintaining a transcriptionally engaged polymerase can also make subsequent activation rapid, and pausing can help organize the timing of downstream transitions. However, the claim that pausing always benefits expression would be incorrect. A complex that remains paused for too long may be removed or terminate prematurely. A gene with a high promoter-associated polymerase signal may have a low productive transcription rate. The balance between recruitment, release and premature loss determines whether pausing is a useful regulatory checkpoint or an obstacle.

An instructive comparison is between a pause release defect and a simple catalytic inhibition. Suppose an early elongation factor is impaired but the active center of Pol II remains competent. Polymerases might still make short RNA and accumulate near the beginning of genes. If instead the catalytic center of all engaged Pol II complexes is inhibited, synthesis can be impaired throughout the gene body as well as at the promoter. These outcomes are not perfectly exclusive in a living cell because polymerase movement, initiation and factor availability affect each other. Still, the initial spatial pattern of nascent RNA provides more information than a single measurement of the total amount of mature RNA.

Promoter-proximal occupancy is also influenced by how often new complexes arrive. A high occupancy at one position may arise because each complex waits for a long time or because many complexes enter the region in rapid succession. Imagine a classroom door through which students pass. A large group near the door might indicate a blocked doorway or a large incoming crowd. Only measuring the number in the group does not distinguish these possibilities. For transcription, estimates of residence time, initiation flux and passage into downstream sequences help distinguish them. This caution is particularly important when interpreting an experiment in which both promoter and gene-body polymerase abundance change.

The pause-release model can be tested with a pulse of labeled nucleotide or an assay of engaged RNA polymerases. If transcriptionally competent complexes are abundant just downstream of the start site before stimulation and the signal moves across the gene after stimulation, the result is consistent with release of early complexes. A time series is more informative than two unrelated endpoint samples because the downstream progression of a wave of RNA synthesis supports the predicted causal order. To establish that the wave reflects previously engaged complexes rather than exclusively new initiation, additional experimental controls are required. The existence of multiple complementary tests is more persuasive than assuming a mechanism from the shape of one graph.

8. Elongation through chromatin and topological barriers

The template for nuclear Pol II is chromosomal DNA associated with histones and other proteins. DNA wrapped around a histone octamer forms a nucleosome, and nucleosomes pose a physical and energetic challenge to the advancing polymerase. To read a template base buried in histone-associated DNA, local DNA–histone contacts must be disrupted or rearranged. The challenge is different from opening naked double-stranded DNA because the polymerase must contend with protein–DNA interactions as well as base pairing and stacking.

Nucleosomes are dynamic structures rather than rigid, permanently closed obstacles. DNA can transiently unwrap from the histone surface, providing access to portions of the template. An elongating polymerase can exploit such transient access and can also promote changes in nucleosomal organization. Nevertheless, nucleosomal DNA may increase pausing and backtracking relative to a comparable naked template. A reduction in elongation rate at a nucleosome does not necessarily indicate that the polymerase is defective. It can reflect an increase in the energetic barrier to moving the transcription complex forward.

FACT, whose name refers to facilitation of chromatin transcription, is a histone chaperone that helps polymerase negotiate nucleosomal barriers and supports the restoration of chromatin organization. Histone chaperones interact with histones and assist their handling without needing to act as a DNA-copying enzyme. SPT6 is another transcription-associated histone chaperone that contributes to chromatin maintenance during elongation. ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes can change nucleosome positioning or accessibility in relevant settings, but their ATP use is not the same chemical event as RNA chain extension.

Preserving chromatin behind the polymerase is biologically important. If repeated transcription permanently removed nucleosomes from the gene body without replacement, DNA segments that are normally inaccessible might become available to inappropriate initiation machinery. Internal promoter-like sequences could then drive unwanted RNA production. A histone chaperone defect may consequently have two seemingly different consequences: reduced efficiency of normal elongation through chromatin and increased initiation from abnormal internal positions. These consequences can be related because both arise from disturbed chromatin organization during the transcription cycle.

Transcription also changes the torsional state of DNA. As the polymerase and associated RNA move along a helical template, their relative rotation may be constrained by chromosome organization, associated proteins and the increasing size of the transcriptional assembly. Under such constraints, forward movement tends to generate positive supercoiling ahead of the enzyme and negative supercoiling behind it. This is the twin-supercoiled-domain concept. Positive supercoiling increases the tendency of DNA to resist further local unwinding; negative supercoiling can favor strand separation under some conditions. The magnitude and distribution of torsion depend on how freely DNA and the polymerase can rotate.

Topoisomerases alleviate excessive torsion by transiently changing DNA strand connectivity. Eukaryotic topoisomerase I can relax supercoils through a transient single-strand break and controlled rotation. Topoisomerase II acts through a transient double-strand break and strand passage and uses ATP in its catalytic cycle. These enzymes assist transcription by managing the template's topology rather than directly adding nucleotides to RNA. Inhibition of a topoisomerase may therefore alter transcription without the inhibitor binding the Pol II active center. In addition, some topoisomerase poisons stabilize cleavage intermediates, producing DNA damage; their effects must not be treated as a clean test of elongation chemistry alone.

A comparison between a relaxed DNA template and an otherwise identical torsionally constrained template illustrates the issue. If the constrained template supports normal initiation but elongation becomes progressively less efficient, a buildup of torsion is a plausible explanation. Adding a functional topoisomerase may relieve the defect. Appropriate controls should test that the added protein does not simply increase transcription through an unrelated interaction. A catalytically inactive topoisomerase and a template without the same torsional constraint can help distinguish the effect of DNA relaxation from nonspecific effects of protein addition.

There is also a relationship between RNA production and the possibility of forming an R-loop. An R-loop consists of RNA paired to a DNA strand while the other DNA strand is displaced. It differs from the short, normal RNA–DNA hybrid inside the transcription complex because it can persist outside that active-center arrangement. R-loops have physiological roles in some contexts, but abnormal persistence can interfere with other chromosome-associated processes. RNase H can degrade RNA within RNA–DNA hybrids. For the present chapter, the important point is that the management of the emerging RNA and DNA topology helps prevent an ordinary transcription event from becoming a persistent obstacle. Detailed genome-instability pathways belong to the DNA maintenance chapters.

9. Processivity, speed and backtracking: interpreting the moving enzyme

The rate of RNA synthesis can be reported as nucleotides incorporated per second or kilobases transcribed per minute. These units describe motion or chemical extension, not the number of complete RNA molecules produced each minute. To distinguish those quantities, imagine a gene transcribed by two polymerases located far apart. If both enzymes advance at the same average rate, the gene-level total incorporation rate is approximately twice the rate of one enzyme, even though the time each enzyme needs to traverse the gene is unchanged. The rate of completed RNA production depends on the frequency with which productive transcription events are initiated and survive to completion.

An elongation rate measured across a long gene generally includes time spent in transient pauses. The instantaneous catalytic rate during uninterrupted synthesis may be faster than the average rate inferred from the time a transcription wave takes to cross the gene. A mutation could increase the frequency of pauses without changing the speed of the enzyme when it is moving, or it could slow each catalytic cycle while leaving the number of pauses unchanged. Both mutations could yield the same average transit time. Distinguishing them requires evidence about the distribution of intermediate states rather than just the average.

Backtracking is a special kind of elongation interruption. In a backtracked complex, the RNA 3′ end has moved away from its productive alignment, so adding the next complementary nucleotide is not immediately possible. Pol II may recover spontaneously from a shallow backtrack, or cleavage of the RNA can generate a new 3′ terminus in the appropriate catalytic position. TFIIS facilitates the latter reaction. A mutant defective in TFIIS function can therefore increase the duration of certain arrested complexes without abolishing the normal nucleotide-addition chemistry of Pol II on an uncomplicated template.

This distinction can be illustrated using a defined template containing a sequence that favors backtracking. If wild-type polymerase resumes synthesis after a pause whereas a sample lacking TFIIS remains arrested for longer, the observation is consistent with a role in recovery. Addition of wild-type TFIIS should preferentially stimulate recovery of the arrested complexes. Merely comparing RNA output from two whole-cell samples cannot establish this direct action because TFIIS-dependent changes may affect other aspects of expression. Biochemical reconstitution isolates a specific causal step, whereas cellular assays reveal how that step contributes to the functioning system.

An elongation defect can also change processing outcomes by changing the time during which particular RNA signals remain available before downstream signals emerge. For instance, the exposure of a splice site before a competing site is transcribed can influence recognition. Slower synthesis does not universally increase exon inclusion because different RNA structures and regulatory factors create different outcomes. The principle is kinetic coupling: two processes can affect each other through relative timing even if their chemical reactions occur at distinct molecular sites. This prepares the student for the later chapter on alternative splicing without requiring a detailed account of spliceosome chemistry here.

10. Why the end of an mRNA is not necessarily the end of transcription

An RNA polymerase that has reached the end of a protein-coding sequence has not yet necessarily reached the end of the transcription unit. The coding sequence is the region interpreted by ribosomes as codons, whereas a transcription unit includes RNA regions that are not translated. After the stop codon, many messenger RNAs contain a 3′ untranslated region with sequences involved in RNA processing, localization, stability or translation regulation. The polymerase must continue beyond the stop codon to synthesize these RNA sequences. A stop codon instructs a ribosome during translation; it is not a general termination instruction for RNA polymerase.

Even after the polymerase has copied the future 3′ end of a typical polyadenylated messenger RNA, it often remains engaged with the DNA. The cell defines the end of the upstream RNA product through cleavage of the nascent RNA. Polymerase continues synthesizing a downstream extension for some distance before the elongation complex disassembles. Consequently three positions should be distinguished on a gene map: the translation stop codon, the cleavage site that defines the end of the upstream precursor, and the region in which polymerase actually terminates. The position of the polyadenylation signal itself is generally upstream of cleavage rather than identical to the cleavage site.

This arrangement raises an important biological question. Why would a cell synthesize additional RNA that it intends to degrade? One answer is that recognition of an RNA processing signal must occur after that signal has emerged from the polymerase. The processing machinery needs an accessible RNA sequence rather than merely the corresponding bases buried in double-stranded DNA. Additional downstream sequence can participate in the recognition and cleavage process. The interval beyond the cleavage site also allows the polymerase to change its associated factors and to encounter a termination-promoting mechanism. Synthesis of extra RNA is therefore not simply an accidental failure to locate the correct final base. It is a consequence of how the processing and termination systems are coupled.

The distinction can be demonstrated through the use of different molecular probes. A probe recognizing the 3′ untranslated region of a stable mature mRNA will often detect abundant RNA. A probe recognizing DNA sequences sufficiently downstream of the processing site may detect little or no mature RNA in a cytoplasmic sample. Yet an assay that specifically detects nascent transcription may show polymerase activity in that downstream region. These results are compatible because downstream RNA is produced transiently and can be degraded rapidly. A failure to detect a stable downstream RNA does not establish that the downstream region was never transcribed.

For a student interpreting data, the type of sample is as important as the position of the probe. Total RNA contains a mixture of stable and unstable products, whereas chromatin-associated RNA can be enriched for nascent molecules. Poly(A)-selected RNA preferentially represents RNA molecules with poly(A) tails. Engaged polymerase assays reveal transcriptional activity rather than the final processed product. A termination defect may therefore be readily detected in a nascent RNA assay but much less apparent in ordinary mature mRNA measurements. The choice of assay must follow the mechanistic question.

Termination is beneficial because it releases a polymerase for recycling and prevents persistent transcription into neighboring regions. In a compact genomic neighborhood, a polymerase that fails to terminate may interfere with transcription from a downstream promoter or collide with a transcription complex moving in the opposite direction. The effect depends on gene orientation, distance and the transcriptional activity of the neighboring regions. Termination is consequently a means of preserving the functional independence of transcription units, not merely a way of obtaining an RNA of an appropriate length.

11. Recognition and cleavage of the RNA 3′ end

In a typical metazoan protein-coding gene, an AAUAAA sequence or a related variant functions as a major component of the polyadenylation signal in the nascent RNA. The equivalent sequence on the DNA coding strand would contain thymine in place of uracil. Other RNA elements, including downstream GU-rich or U-rich sequences in many mammalian genes, help specify efficient processing. The cleavage machinery recognizes a combination of signals rather than treating six consecutive bases as an infallible instruction. Similar motifs may occur elsewhere in a transcript without functioning as the principal 3′ end processing site.

Cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor, abbreviated CPSF, contributes to recognition of the polyadenylation signal and assembly of the processing machinery. Cleavage stimulation factor, CstF, helps recognize relevant downstream RNA elements. Other factors contribute to site choice and complex function. CPSF73 is the endonuclease responsible for cleavage in the canonical mammalian complex. An endonuclease cuts an internal phosphodiester bond; an exonuclease removes residues from an available end. This difference becomes essential when following the events that promote transcription termination after cleavage.

At cleavage, the continuous nascent RNA is converted into two molecules. The upstream fragment contains the cap if capping has occurred, the coding region and the RNA sequence that will constitute the 3′ untranslated region. It acquires a new 3′ end that can be used by poly(A) polymerase. The downstream fragment remains associated with the elongating polymerase and has a newly exposed 5′ end, generally a monophosphate suitable for recognition by a relevant exonuclease. The two products have very different fates despite originating from the same transcription event.

Poly(A) polymerase subsequently extends the upstream RNA at its new 3′ end using ATP as substrate. This addition is not a continuation of DNA-templated transcription by Pol II. The adenine residues are generally not copied from a corresponding long thymine tract in the DNA template. The distinction has a direct experimental implication: sequencing the genomic DNA may reveal the recognition and cleavage signals but not a sequence encoding the entire mature poly(A) tail. Conversely, a DNA-encoded tract of adenosines within an RNA should not automatically be interpreted as an added poly(A) tail.

RNA cleavage does not necessarily occur simultaneously with polymerase release. Consider a polymerase located several hundred bases beyond a cleavage site. Its newest RNA residues are still associated with the catalytic center, but the portion containing the cleavage signal has already emerged. When the cleavage machinery cuts that earlier portion, the polymerase can remain physically attached to DNA and the downstream RNA fragment. The upstream RNA may be processed and released from the immediate transcriptional assembly while the polymerase continues. This separation of events is fundamental to cleavage-coupled termination.

A mutation converting a functional AAUAAA motif into a sequence poorly recognized by processing factors may reduce cleavage efficiency at that site. One possible outcome is transcription extending farther downstream, with eventual use of an alternative polyadenylation site if a suitable one exists. The mature RNA population may contain longer 3′ untranslated regions, abnormal RNA ends, or reduced levels of the expected transcript. Which outcome occurs depends on the presence of alternative signals and the RNA surveillance machinery. It is unsafe to conclude that a single mutation must always abolish all transcription from the gene, because the promoter and the active center may remain fully functional.

Changing the downstream processing element can yield a similar phenotype by impairing assembly of the cleavage machinery even if the AAUAAA sequence remains intact. Conversely, restoring a functional combination of signals may rescue cleavage. A useful experimental design compares a wild-type reporter with a reporter bearing a defined signal mutation, while preserving the promoter and coding sequence. Measuring both the abundance of correctly ended RNA and downstream transcription can distinguish defective processing from a simple decrease in promoter activity. A restoration experiment strengthens the inference that the mutated RNA sequence is causally important.

12. Torpedo and allosteric contributions to Pol II termination

The torpedo model connects the cleavage reaction to a physical mechanism for removing the downstream RNA and promoting dissociation of the polymerase. In mammals the relevant 5′ to 3′ exonuclease is XRN2, and in budding yeast the homologous factor is Rat1. Cleavage exposes an RNA end that the exonuclease can use as a starting point. As the exonuclease degrades the downstream RNA, it progresses toward the polymerase, which is still moving along the DNA. When the exonuclease reaches the vicinity of the elongation complex, its action contributes to termination. The name torpedo refers to this pursuit of a moving target rather than to a literal collision between rigid particles.

The exonuclease and polymerase act in different ways on the same RNA fragment. Pol II extends its 3′ end; XRN2 progressively removes residues from its 5′ end. The fragment's length is determined by the difference between these activities, among other factors. If the polymerase continues rapidly while the exonuclease progresses slowly, the gap between them may increase. If the exonuclease approaches faster than polymerase advances, the gap can decrease. This intuitive relationship helps explain why changes in elongation rate or pausing downstream of the cleavage site can influence termination position.

A second mechanistic framework is often called the allosteric model. Recognition of the polyadenylation signal and associated processing events can alter the protein composition and functional state of the elongation complex. Some factors that supported elongation may dissociate or change their modification state; other factors may associate. These changes can reduce the tendency of Pol II to continue transcription and make it more susceptible to dissociation. The term allosteric here describes altered functional behavior associated with changes in the complex rather than a simple assumption that the DNA sequence acts as an immovable roadblock.

The two models should not be treated as mutually exclusive. Changes in the elongation complex can facilitate the action of the exonuclease. If polymerase slows downstream of the processing signal, the exonuclease may have more opportunity to approach it. If the complex loses some elongation-supporting interactions, it may also become easier to terminate. The combined model explains why cleavage, downstream RNA degradation and changes in polymerase behavior can all contribute to effective termination. Their relative contributions depend on the gene, organism and molecular context. For the CSIR-NET level, the central prediction is that failure of a termination-promoting mechanism can allow persistent downstream transcription without requiring an increase in initiation.

A classic experimental approach tests an exonuclease's role by reducing its function and measuring polymerase distribution downstream of normally used cleavage sites. In 2004, studies in yeast and human systems linked Rat1 or XRN2 to termination of Pol II transcription. Increased downstream polymerase or transcriptional signal when exonuclease function is disrupted supports a role for degradation of the cleavage-generated RNA. The strongest interpretation requires controls showing that the observed readthrough is not simply caused by a failure to cleave RNA in the first place. Complementation by a functional exonuclease, when possible, helps establish specificity.

A simple comparison illustrates the importance of this control. In sample A, the normal cleavage product is absent, and RNA containing both the upstream and downstream regions accumulates. In sample B, cleavage products are detected but the downstream RNA persists and polymerase occupancy extends beyond its usual termination region. The first pattern is consistent with a cleavage defect; the second more directly suggests a defect downstream of cleavage, such as impaired exonucleolytic action. A single assay detecting long RNA downstream of the normal processing signal cannot reliably distinguish the two cases.

A mutation that causes an elongating polymerase to move unusually rapidly can also affect termination even if the exonuclease remains active. The result is not guaranteed, because enzyme velocities are not the only determinants of complex release. Yet a longer termination zone is a reasonable prediction under conditions in which the exonuclease must approach the polymerase and downstream slowing normally helps it do so. A slower polymerase may favor earlier termination in a comparable model, but if a mutation instead prevents the structural transition needed for release, slower movement need not produce efficient termination. Good reasoning identifies the assumptions behind a prediction instead of confusing a model's tendency with a universal experimental outcome.

Once the elongation complex dissociates, the template DNA can regain its ordinary duplex configuration and the polymerase becomes available for future transcription cycles after appropriate remodeling and modification changes. The upstream RNA continues through the appropriate maturation and quality-control steps, while the downstream RNA fragment is usually degraded. Termination is therefore not simply the last nucleotide addition reaction. It is an organized transition that resolves the RNA, DNA and protein components of an active transcription assembly into products and reusable machinery.

13. Exceptions to the canonical polyadenylation-coupled pathway

The usual model of mammalian protein-coding gene termination is extremely useful, but not all Pol II products are polyadenylated messenger RNAs. Replication-dependent histone messenger RNAs in animals provide a familiar exception. Their mature 3′ ends are generated by a specialized processing pathway associated with a conserved stem-loop rather than a conventional long poly(A) tail. Therefore, an examination statement that every mature Pol II messenger RNA carries a poly(A) tail is false. This exception does not mean that histone genes have no mechanism for defining the end of their transcript. It means that the RNA signals and participating factors differ from those of canonical polyadenylated messenger RNAs.

Several Pol II-transcribed small nuclear RNA genes also use specialized 3′ processing mechanisms, including the Integrator complex in metazoan systems. These RNAs do not follow the complete maturation itinerary of a typical protein-coding messenger RNA. In yeast, the Nrd1–Nab3–Sen1 pathway provides an example of termination for many short noncoding transcripts. Recognition of the nascent RNA and association with the transcription complex can promote termination without requiring the ordinary mammalian mRNA polyadenylation signal. The detailed protein interactions are not necessary for this chapter; their significance is that the same polymerase can operate with distinct termination systems.

Some transcripts can terminate prematurely before the canonical end of a gene. Premature termination may occur because an early processing signal becomes accessible, a paused complex fails to enter stable elongation, or other features of the transcriptional environment favor release. Such events can contribute to gene regulation or reflect a defect. It is useful to distinguish premature termination from a simple reversible pause. The former ends the productive lifetime of the particular transcription event; the latter leaves open the possibility that synthesis of the same RNA will resume.

Alternative polyadenylation provides another reason that transcription and RNA processing should not be described as having one invariant end per gene. A gene may contain more than one usable 3′ processing signal. Selection of different signals can alter the length or, in some contexts, the coding capacity of the mature RNA. Changes in signal recognition, processing factor availability or elongation timing may influence site usage. The details of how polyadenylation affects gene regulation belong to the subsequent processing chapter. Here, the important mechanistic point is that termination can follow recognition of different processing sites within a single transcription unit.

14. Termination by Pol I and Pol III

Pol I transcribes the major ribosomal RNA precursor in the nucleolus. In mammals the initially synthesized large pre-rRNA contains sequences that are processed into 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs. The 5S rRNA is transcribed separately by Pol III. Ribosomal DNA commonly exists as multiple repeated transcription units, and efficient release and reuse of Pol I contribute to the high output needed for ribosome production. The mature rRNA boundaries are defined by extensive processing of a precursor rather than by Pol II-like polyadenylation of each mature rRNA molecule.

In the mammalian Pol I system, DNA-bound termination factor TTF-I recognizes specific terminator sequences downstream of the transcribed rRNA region. Interactions at these sites can promote polymerase pausing and facilitate termination in conjunction with other components. Yeast Pol I systems use related principles involving sequence-specific DNA-binding factors and additional termination mechanisms, but the exact factors and sequence requirements should not be treated as identical across species. At examination level, the essential contrast is that a typical Pol I terminator can involve recognition of a downstream DNA element by a termination factor rather than the ordinary messenger RNA cleavage/polyadenylation system.

It is important to distinguish a terminator's function from the processing of the ribosomal RNA precursor. A polymerase release defect can permit transcription into the intergenic spacer even if the sequences encoding the mature rRNAs remain intact. A processing defect can instead cause accumulation of abnormal pre-rRNA intermediates despite relatively normal transcription termination. Northern analysis using probes for spacer sequences and mature rRNA regions can help distinguish these cases, particularly when combined with measurements of nascent transcription. A decrease in mature rRNA alone does not tell which process has failed.

Pol III has a comparatively compact termination signal. In the conventional example, a short run of thymidines in the non-template DNA strand corresponds to adenosines in the template DNA and generates uridines in the RNA. The exact number of thymidines sufficient for efficient termination depends on species and sequence context. It is reasonable to associate a tract of four or more thymidines with many mammalian Pol III terminators, but this should not be memorized as an absolute threshold for all organisms or all genes. A canonical Pol III terminator does not require the upstream RNA hairpin that is characteristic of the usual bacterial intrinsic terminator model.

Several features help explain why Pol III can terminate efficiently at such a simple signal. The rU–dA hybrid formed at the terminator is relatively weak, and features of the polymerase and non-template DNA contribute to the termination response. Pol III includes subunits involved in recognition and efficient release, including C37/C53 in the commonly studied systems. The polymerase is not simply responding to the fact that the RNA contains uridines anywhere in its sequence. The position, length and sequence context of the tract and its recognition within an elongation complex are important.

Suppose a Pol III-transcribed reporter contains the coding-strand terminator sequence 5′-TTTTTT-3′. The template strand runs 3′-AAAAAA-5′ in the direction of transcription, and the RNA produced across the tract is uridine-rich. Mutating several thymidines to other bases can impair termination and generate longer readthrough transcripts. A comparison with a bacterial terminator is useful: disrupting the stem-forming sequence of a typical bacterial intrinsic terminator may strongly impair release even while its uridine tract remains. A Pol III terminator need not share that dependence. This distinction allows students to interpret diagrams and mutations without assuming that all weak RNA–DNA hybrids function identically.

Pol I, Pol II and Pol III thus share the same general need to maintain stable elongation until a suitable point and then destabilize that state. Their distinct termination systems reflect differences in transcript class, processing pathway, polymerase architecture and the organization of their genes. The comparison also illustrates a recurring theme in molecular evolution: a common fundamental chemical reaction can be embedded in different regulatory mechanisms suited to different biological outputs.

15. Transcription inhibitors as mechanistic probes

A transcription inhibitor is a compound that reduces RNA synthesis by interfering with a process required for transcription. This definition does not imply that every inhibitor binds RNA polymerase directly. Some act on a polymerase, others change the DNA template, and still others inhibit an accessory enzyme required for a particular transcriptional transition. A compound can also reduce RNA abundance indirectly through cellular stress, metabolic disruption or changes in degradation. An experimental claim about transcription inhibition should therefore identify the measured step rather than assume that a decrease in total RNA proves direct polymerase inhibition.

Three classical agents illustrate these distinctions: alpha-amanitin, actinomycin D and rifampicin. Alpha-amanitin is particularly useful in comparisons of nuclear eukaryotic polymerases. Actinomycin D binds DNA and can interfere with progression of multiple RNA polymerases. Rifampicin principally targets bacterial RNA polymerase and prevents productive extension of very short newly initiated transcripts. Their differences allow students to distinguish a polymerase-selective effect, a template-mediated effect and a stage-selective effect on bacterial transcription. These distinctions are more robust than memorizing a single unqualified list of drugs and targets.

The time at which an inhibitor is introduced can be as important as its identity. Adding a compound before an initiation reaction tests all stages from recruitment to product formation. Adding it after a stable elongation complex has formed focuses attention on the steps that remain necessary to extend the existing RNA. A compound that prevents productive initiation but does not strongly affect established complexes may completely suppress full-length product in the first experiment while permitting completion in the second. An elongation inhibitor, in contrast, may reduce RNA extension even when the complex was assembled before exposure.

The simplest test uses a defined DNA template, a source of polymerase, appropriate factors and substrates. The amount and length of newly synthesized RNA are compared between inhibitor-treated and untreated samples. A vehicle control is needed if a solvent is used to deliver the compound. The concentration of enzyme, template and nucleotides should be comparable. An experiment testing inhibition of one purified enzyme cannot directly establish selectivity in a living cell because the compound may be transported, modified or excluded differently by different cells. Conversely, a cellular result can be biologically important even when the precise direct target cannot be inferred from it alone.

An important interpretive variable is the concentration of inhibitor. If one polymerase is inhibited at a low concentration while another is affected only at a much higher concentration, the compound is relatively selective within a certain range. Calling the second polymerase completely resistant under all circumstances would be unjustified. The concentration required to produce a specified effect may also depend on the experimental system and species. An examination problem should therefore be solved using any concentrations and response data explicitly given, rather than applying an absolute threshold learned from an unrelated assay.

16. Alpha-amanitin and the motion of Pol II

Alpha-amanitin is a cyclic peptide toxin associated with certain Amanita mushrooms. It is historically important as a probe of eukaryotic nuclear transcription because Pol II is especially sensitive to it. The toxin interacts with the enzyme near structural elements involved in nucleotide addition and translocation. Structural and biochemical studies indicate that alpha-amanitin interferes with conformational movements required for efficient forward progression. In particular, interactions near the bridge helix and trigger loop help explain its strong suppression of productive RNA synthesis.

This mode of action should be distinguished from competition between two alternative nucleotide substrates. Alpha-amanitin does not need to resemble an incoming ATP or GTP in order to inhibit synthesis. If it restricts an essential conformational transition, increasing the concentration of the ordinary NTPs need not restore normal movement. A student accustomed to competitive inhibition models in enzyme kinetics might expect every inhibitor to occupy the substrate binding site and be overcome by excess substrate. The transcription example demonstrates why enzyme inhibition must be classified from the underlying molecular interaction and experimental data rather than from a general assumption.

A Pol II elongation complex can retain nucleic acids in the presence of alpha-amanitin even when its ability to move along the template is strongly compromised. Consequently an assay of polymerase binding to DNA need not immediately show the same reduction as an assay of new RNA synthesis. With prolonged exposure in living cells, additional effects can occur, including changes in the stability of the largest Pol II subunit. The early biochemical effect and the later cellular response should be distinguished. Otherwise one might incorrectly interpret a late decrease in polymerase protein abundance as the sole mechanism through which the toxin inhibits RNA synthesis.

The standard mammalian nuclear-polymerase comparison is as follows. Pol II is inhibited by relatively low concentrations of alpha-amanitin. Pol III is generally less sensitive and can be inhibited at higher concentrations in appropriate systems. Pol I is relatively resistant in standard assays. This pattern supports a familiar examination strategy: if production of nuclear pre-mRNA is strongly inhibited at a dose that leaves the large pre-rRNA synthesis relatively intact, the result is consistent with inhibition of Pol II. If production of tRNA or 5S rRNA decreases only at a substantially higher dose, the pattern is consistent with the lower sensitivity of Pol III.

However, the phrase relatively resistant matters. Polymerase III sensitivity differs considerably among organisms, and cellular accessibility of the toxin also affects apparent sensitivity. A dose response in an isolated mammalian nuclear enzyme preparation should not automatically be applied to a fungal, plant or intact cell experiment. A purified enzyme assay and a living-cell assay may differ in the exposure time needed to detect inhibition. An examination question that explicitly describes a particular species or supplies measured response curves provides stronger evidence than a generalization about the mammalian pattern.

The earliest classic alpha-amanitin experiments helped establish the identity of Pol II as a distinct molecular activity. Later structural work explained how inhibition was possible. These two types of evidence answer different questions. An inhibitor-sensitivity assay can distinguish enzymes without revealing the exact binding geometry. A structure can show where a compound binds without independently determining the size of its effect on gene expression in a living cell. When the molecular interaction and functional response are consistent, together they support a more complete mechanism.

Alpha-amanitin poisoning also illustrates the physiological consequences of inhibiting a central cellular reaction. Cells depend on continued synthesis of messenger RNAs to replace transcripts and proteins as they turn over. A sustained failure of nuclear Pol II transcription can therefore compromise essential cell functions even if many ribosomes and previously synthesized proteins are initially still present. Tissues differ in their exposure and susceptibility, and the clinical features of mushroom poisoning involve additional physiological considerations beyond this chapter. The educational conclusion is that a molecular inhibitor can have delayed consequences at the cellular and organismal levels because the immediate target participates in a network of synthesis and turnover.

17. Actinomycin D: inhibition through the DNA template

Actinomycin D, also known as dactinomycin, is a compound whose planar chromophore can intercalate between stacked bases in DNA, with preference for particular sequence contexts such as guanine–cytosine steps. Its peptide portions interact with the DNA groove and contribute to stable binding. Intercalation changes local DNA structure and can obstruct the progression of RNA polymerase along the template. The primary target in this classical description is DNA, not a unique active-center pocket of eukaryotic Pol II.

Because all nuclear polymerases require DNA templates, an obstruction in DNA can affect Pol I, Pol II and Pol III. The effects need not be equal at every concentration. Ribosomal RNA synthesis by Pol I is often especially sensitive to relatively low actinomycin D exposure in cellular contexts. The arrangement and activity of ribosomal DNA, sequence properties and accessibility can contribute to this preferential inhibition. At higher exposures the compound can interfere broadly with transcription. Thus it is both incorrect to call actinomycin D absolutely Pol I-specific and incorrect to infer that identical inhibition of all three enzymes occurs under every experimental condition.

A student can distinguish template-mediated inhibition from enzyme-specific inhibition through the design of an experiment. Suppose two different RNA polymerases are tested on comparable templates. If the inhibitor acts on a DNA sequence or structure common to both reactions, inhibition may depend strongly on that template's susceptibility. Changing the DNA while holding the polymerase constant could alter the response. By contrast, a mutation in a specific polymerase binding site may produce resistance to a compound whose direct target is that enzyme. These observations are not individually conclusive, because changes in DNA or polymerase structure can have multiple effects, but they motivate useful experimental controls.

Actinomycin D has often been used to estimate RNA stability by reducing new transcription and following the loss of an existing RNA population. The method relies on the distinction between production and disappearance. If synthesis ceases rapidly and degradation continues according to its ordinary kinetics, the decline in RNA abundance provides an estimate of its lifetime. However, broad transcription inhibition can itself alter cellular physiology, RNA degradation pathways and stress responses. The apparent half-life measured during inhibition may differ from the RNA's lifetime in untreated cells. Partial inhibition also causes systematic errors if continuing synthesis is ignored.

A useful control in such an experiment is an independent measurement of nascent transcription, rather than assuming complete inhibition merely because the drug was added. The abundance of a highly stable RNA can serve as a normalization reference only if it is appropriate for the time course and experimental conditions. A reference RNA may itself change after treatment. Normalization to a known amount of added material or to another validated reference can help distinguish a genuine biological change from a difference in sample recovery. These are general principles of data interpretation rather than specialized experimental protocols.

Dactinomycin is used medically as an antineoplastic agent. The connection to transcription is instructive because rapidly proliferating cells require continuous production of RNA and protein. Nevertheless, broad interference with DNA-associated processes is not limited to malignant cells, and the drug's cellular effects cannot be reduced to a simple statement that it selectively kills dividing cells by blocking Pol II. In the context of this syllabus, the key application is understanding why an inhibitor of transcription can affect diverse cell types and why selectivity is crucial in interpreting both biological and biochemical experiments.

18. Rifampicin and the prokaryotic contrast

Rifampicin is a classical inhibitor of bacterial RNA polymerase. It binds to a pocket in the beta subunit, encoded by rpoB, in a position that interferes with the path of the growing RNA during the earliest stages of synthesis. The enzyme may still carry out the first nucleotide-addition reactions, but the developing RNA cannot extend efficiently beyond a very short length, often described as two or three nucleotides in the canonical mechanistic model. Hence rifampicin is usually classified as an inhibitor of bacterial transcription initiation or early RNA extension rather than a general inhibitor of every elongating complex.

This classification can be confusing because synthesis of the second or third nucleotide is chemically an extension event. The distinction is between extension within the initiation-stage complex and extension by an established productive elongation complex. If the short RNA cannot pass through the available path, the enzyme cannot enter normal processive elongation. By contrast, an elongation complex that already contains a longer RNA may be much less susceptible to the drug. The structural state of the polymerase therefore determines how the inhibitor affects transcription.

A classic time-of-addition experiment takes advantage of this distinction. If rifampicin is introduced before a bacterial transcription reaction is initiated, new full-length transcripts are strongly reduced. If it is introduced after synchronized, productive elongation complexes have formed, those complexes can often continue toward their normal ends. RNA synthesis may not fall to zero instantly in an asynchronous bacterial population because many polymerases were already elongating at the time the drug was added. As those complexes complete transcription and are not replaced by new productive complexes, the amount of ongoing synthesis decreases.

This time course can be used conceptually to distinguish the contribution of initiation from that of elongation. Imagine that all genes in an idealized system are of equal length and all polymerases travel at an identical rate. When new initiation stops, the number of engaged polymerases would decline as existing complexes reach their endpoints. The time at which the last complex finishes depends on the remaining distance and elongation rate. Real cells contain genes of different lengths, pauses and different transcriptional responses, so a single time constant cannot be assumed. Nevertheless, the pattern is distinct from inhibition of all active centers at the same moment.

Resistance to rifampicin can arise from alterations of the bacterial RNA polymerase beta subunit. Such mutations provide genetic evidence for the target of the compound: if the altered enzyme remains functional while its sensitivity to the inhibitor decreases, the relationship between the protein and inhibition is strengthened. Not every mutation in rpoB produces resistance, and the effect of a particular mutation must be measured. The antibiotic's selective effect on bacterial transcription is a key reason it has clinical utility against susceptible bacteria, including in treatment regimens for tuberculosis. Detailed treatment schedules and drug-resistance management are outside the present transcription chapter.

The bacterial polymerase and nuclear eukaryotic polymerases share core catalytic principles, but they differ in accessory machinery and drug-binding properties. Nuclear Pol II is not normally identified using rifampicin in the way bacterial RNA polymerase is. Organellar transcription systems and specialized polymerases provide additional evolutionary exceptions, so a claim about nuclear Pol II should not automatically be generalized to all RNA synthesis in a eukaryotic cell. In plants, plastids can contain a bacterial-type plastid-encoded transcription machinery as well as a distinct nucleus-encoded polymerase system. The broader lesson is that cellular compartment and evolutionary origin matter when interpreting an inhibitor response.

19. Quantitative reasoning: time, density and transcript output

Consider an idealized transcription unit of length L nucleotides, traversed at a constant average elongation velocity v nucleotides per minute. If a polymerase begins at the start site and moves continuously at that effective velocity, the time T required to reach the specified endpoint is L/v minutes. This equation follows from the definition of average velocity as distance divided by time. The endpoint must be explicitly defined: if the question concerns the arrival at the RNA cleavage site, use the distance to that site; if it concerns polymerase release, include the downstream termination distance. Confusing the two will give the wrong transit time.

Suppose a gene's cleavage site lies 9,000 nucleotides downstream of its transcription start site, and Pol II travels at an average of 1,800 nucleotides per minute after escaping an early pause. The travel time to the cleavage site is 9,000 divided by 1,800, or five minutes, if the early pause is excluded from the stated average. If the polymerase spends an additional 30 seconds in a separate promoter-proximal paused state, the predicted time from the start to arrival is 5.5 minutes. If the average velocity was instead measured across the entire path including the pause, adding the pause time separately would count it twice. Examination problems often distinguish an effective average speed from a speed measured only during motion.

In a steady-state idealized gene, let J be the number of polymerases entering productive elongation per minute. If no polymerases drop off and every productive event reaches the endpoint, the completed-transcript flux is also J after the initial transit delay. The average number of elongating complexes N is then J multiplied by the mean time spent in the gene, N = JT. This relationship follows from counting the average number of arrivals over the period for which each arrival remains present. It is an application of a general flow principle and does not require that individual complexes be equally spaced.

For the preceding 9,000-nucleotide gene, if the productive entry rate is six complexes per minute and the gene-body transit time is five minutes, the average number of gene-body complexes is thirty. If the velocity decreases by half while the productive entry rate is maintained, the transit time doubles to ten minutes, and the predicted occupancy increases to sixty complexes. Yet in this ideal steady-state example the completed-transcript flux remains six per minute. Increased polymerase occupancy can therefore accompany slower transcription without an increase in the production of complete RNA molecules.

In a real gene, the assumption that entry rate remains constant may break down when polymerases are crowded or pause sites become congested. If slow elongation causes upstream queues, it can reduce initiation or productive entry. If the probability of premature release increases, the output can decrease even if the entry rate is unchanged. The equation remains useful as a baseline, but it must not be applied to a congested or non-steady-state system without modification. The experimentally observed difference between the ideal prediction and the real result can itself provide information about regulation.

A simple survival model introduces a probability p that an initiated productive complex reaches the specified completion point. If J complexes enter per minute and p is constant under the stated conditions, the expected completion rate is Jp. With an entry rate of eight complexes per minute and completion probability of 0.75, the output is six complete transcripts per minute. The same output can be obtained with an entry rate of six and a completion probability of one. Hence measuring only output cannot establish which underlying mechanism has changed. To separate the alternatives, one needs measurements of entry and prematurely terminated products or of polymerase distributions.

Another useful relationship concerns the pause index. Define it explicitly as the normalized polymerase signal per unit length in a promoter-proximal window divided by the normalized signal per unit length in a gene-body window. Suppose 100 signal units are counted across a 200-base promoter region and 400 units across a 4,000-base body region. The densities are 0.5 and 0.1 units per base, so the ratio is five. Merely dividing the total promoter signal by the total body signal gives 0.25, which is not the density-based pause index. The windows must be specified because changing their length can alter the index even when the underlying distribution is unchanged.

The pause index is a descriptive ratio, not a direct measurement of a release rate. If the promoter signal remains constant while gene-body signal falls, the index rises. If promoter signal rises proportionally with body signal, the index may remain unchanged even though overall transcription changes. Different assay efficiencies, background signals and choice of gene-body region can affect the value. A meaningful interpretation combines the ratio with independent information about transcription initiation, elongation and the time of measurement.

The torpedo model can also be represented with a simple pursuit calculation. Let the exonuclease begin degrading the downstream RNA when the polymerase is d nucleotides ahead. Let the exonuclease progress at constant velocity u and the polymerase at constant velocity v, both measured in nucleotides per second. If u is greater than v, the gap decreases at rate u − v, and the time to close the initial gap is d/(u − v). In a hypothetical example with d = 240 nucleotides, u = 60 nucleotides per second and v = 30 nucleotides per second, the time is eight seconds. During that interval the polymerase advances another 240 nucleotides.

This calculation describes a simplified spatial relationship, not an experimentally established universal speed of XRN2 or Pol II. It assumes a continuous RNA substrate, identical reference directions, constant velocities, no additional loading delay and release upon catch-up. If the polymerase moves as fast as or faster than the exonuclease under those assumptions, the gap will not close. In an actual cell, pauses and changes in the elongation complex may make termination possible even when the average velocities elsewhere in the gene do not satisfy this condition. The model is valuable precisely because it identifies a testable relationship rather than claiming to reproduce every molecular event.

Finally, transcript abundance and synthesis rate can be related with a minimal turnover model. Let M be the number of RNA molecules, S the synthesis rate in molecules per minute, and k the first-order degradation constant in reciprocal minutes. Then dM/dt = S − kM. At a steady state S = kM, so M = S/k. Two conditions with the same RNA abundance can have different synthesis and degradation rates. For instance, 100 molecules can be maintained by a synthesis rate of ten per minute with k = 0.1 per minute, or by a synthesis rate of five per minute with k = 0.05 per minute. Measuring abundance alone cannot distinguish these states.

If synthesis is completely blocked and the degradation constant remains unchanged, the model becomes dM/dt = −kM. Separating variables and integrating gives M(t) = M(0)e^(−kt). At the half-life, M(t)/M(0) = 1/2, so k t₁/₂ = ln 2 and t₁/₂ = ln 2/k. If the RNA amount falls from 800 to 200 molecules in 40 minutes, two half-lives have elapsed, giving a half-life of 20 minutes and k approximately 0.0347 per minute. This inference is valid only if the stated assumptions hold. Continuing synthesis, changing degradation pathways or unequal recovery of RNA samples would alter the observed decay curve.

20. Experimental evidence: identifying the defective transcriptional step

When a cell produces less mature messenger RNA after a treatment, several explanations remain possible. The promoter may recruit fewer polymerases; the recruited polymerases may initiate but fail to escape; they may pause excessively or terminate prematurely; normal transcription may continue while the RNA is processed inefficiently; or the mature RNA may be degraded faster. An experiment that measures only mature RNA abundance cannot distinguish these alternatives. An effective investigation begins by asking which observable variable should change first if the proposed mechanism is correct.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation, often abbreviated ChIP, provides one way to examine the distribution of a DNA-associated protein. DNA–protein associations are preserved under suitable conditions, fragments of chromatin are recovered with an antibody recognizing the protein of interest, and the corresponding DNA regions are measured. A polymerase-specific antibody can reveal enrichment near a promoter or within a gene body. The interpretation must remain precise: enrichment is evidence of association of the recognized protein with a genomic region, not proof that every recovered complex was catalyzing phosphodiester bond formation at the moment of measurement. Input chromatin and a suitable negative antibody control help evaluate the specificity and background of recovery.

Using antibodies that recognize different phosphorylated forms of the CTD adds another dimension to this experiment. In an ordinary active mammalian gene, one may expect a relatively prominent Ser5P signal near the early region and increasing Ser2P enrichment farther downstream. A treatment that reduces CDK9 activity might affect both the distribution of engaged polymerase and the modification state of its CTD. A change in one phospho-specific signal should be interpreted relative to the total Pol II signal, because fewer polymerases in a region will also produce less signal for many polymerase-associated modifications. A lower Ser2P signal alone cannot establish that the phosphorylation fraction per polymerase has changed.

A nuclear run-on assay addresses a related but different question. Transcription complexes already engaged in nuclei are allowed to extend their nascent RNA using labeled or otherwise identifiable nucleotides under defined conditions. The newly labeled products provide evidence for polymerases capable of extension under those conditions. Modern run-on approaches can map the positions of engaged complexes at high resolution, but the central examination principle does not require mastering the sequencing methods. In a comparison between occupancy and run-on assays, a region with abundant polymerase association but weak extension signal may contain a population of complexes whose ability to synthesize RNA is compromised. Other explanations, including assay-specific differences in recovery, must still be considered.

A Northern blot can distinguish RNA molecules by their approximate length and hybridization to a complementary probe. It is especially useful when a mutation produces an unexpected readthrough transcript or an abnormal precursor. A probe near the 5′ end of a gene can detect both full-length and prematurely terminated products if they contain the probe sequence and are sufficiently abundant. A downstream probe can identify RNA molecules that extend beyond the normal processing region. If a long band hybridizes to both probes, the result supports the presence of a continuous RNA containing both regions, although further analysis may be required to map its precise ends.

Reverse-transcription quantitative PCR can compare the abundance of selected RNA regions after conversion to complementary DNA. A primer pair spanning an exon junction can preferentially detect a processed RNA, whereas a pair targeting an intron can detect an unspliced precursor. A pair spanning the normal cleavage site detects continuous RNA across that region rather than separate upstream and downstream fragments. Appropriate controls include a reaction without reverse transcriptase, which can reveal contamination by genomic DNA, and normalization to a reference validated for the conditions. A difference between two primer pairs must not be interpreted as an absolute difference in molecule counts unless their assay efficiencies and other relevant factors are accounted for.

Consider a hypothetical cell line in which a factor needed for productive pause release is depleted. Before treatment, normalized engaged polymerase signals in three equal-length windows are 100 near the start, 60 in the middle and 55 near the end. After depletion, the corresponding signals are 180, 15 and 12. The pattern is compatible with retention of many complexes near the beginning and a reduction in the number reaching downstream regions. It is not compatible with a simple model in which every polymerase throughout the gene is reduced by the same proportion. Restoring a functional version of the depleted factor and observing recovery of the downstream signal would strengthen the causal interpretation.

Now consider a second hypothetical perturbation. The promoter and gene-body signals are approximately unchanged, but nascent transcription beyond the normal termination region increases fourfold. A correctly cleaved upstream RNA end is still detected. These observations point more specifically toward a problem in termination after cleavage than toward a primary initiation defect. However, a change in the relative rates of elongation and termination could also produce the pattern. One should therefore test the candidate termination factor and measure the distribution of downstream RNA ends rather than claim that any increase in readthrough uniquely identifies XRN2 loss.

A pulse-chase experiment helps distinguish newly synthesized RNA from pre-existing RNA. During a brief pulse, a detectable precursor is incorporated into RNA made within the labeling interval. During the chase, unlabeled precursor replaces the labeled source, and the fate of the labeled RNA is followed. A short labeled RNA that later appears within longer products can provide evidence for continued extension, whereas loss of label from a particular population can indicate processing, degradation or release depending on the assay. The total amount of labeled RNA is not a universal measure of one specific process, because both redistribution among RNA classes and chemical turnover can alter the observed signal.

Time-resolved measurements are particularly valuable in studying inhibitors. If a selective initiation inhibitor is applied, polymerases already moving along a long gene may reach distal regions before the supply of newly initiated complexes is exhausted. A reduction in nascent signal can appear progressively from the promoter toward the gene end. If a compound directly impairs the catalytic motion of elongating polymerases, an immediate reduction in extension activity may be observed across much of the gene. These simplified expectations are most useful when the inhibitor acts rapidly relative to the transcription cycle and when the measurement specifically detects newly synthesized RNA.

21. Comparative synthesis and functional significance

Bacterial and eukaryotic transcription share the need to read DNA in one direction and extend RNA in the opposite chemical orientation. Both types of polymerase establish an open region of DNA, retain a short hybrid and undergo repeated nucleotide-addition cycles. Both can pause, backtrack and terminate. These shared properties are expected because their multisubunit RNA polymerases possess related catalytic architecture. The evolution of additional transcription factors and multiple nuclear enzymes in eukaryotes has modified the regulation and processing context, not replaced the fundamental chemistry of nucleic acid synthesis.

In bacteria, transcription and translation can occur in the same cellular compartment, allowing a ribosome to begin reading an RNA before its synthesis has finished. In a conventional eukaryotic nuclear gene, transcription occurs in the nucleus and most translation occurs in the cytoplasm after export of suitable RNA. This separation creates a particular need for RNA maturation and quality control during or after transcription. The Pol II CTD is especially significant in this context because it helps coordinate the appearance of RNA sequences with the machinery that prepares the transcript for later use. Bacteria also process RNA, and eukaryotic transcripts can have specialized maturation pathways; the contrast concerns the typical organization of the systems rather than an absolute absence of processing in bacteria.

Bacterial intrinsic termination commonly depends on formation of a structure in the nascent RNA followed by a uridine-rich region. Rho-dependent termination instead involves an ATP-dependent RNA translocase that can approach an elongation complex. The torpedo pathway of Pol II resembles Rho-dependent termination in the broad sense that a factor acts on RNA associated with a moving polymerase. The molecular mechanisms differ: XRN2 degrades RNA from a cleavage-generated 5′ end, whereas Rho uses ATP-dependent translocation along RNA and does not perform the same exonucleolytic reaction. An analogy based on pursuit is helpful only when these biochemical distinctions are preserved.

Pol III intrinsic termination and bacterial intrinsic termination share the formation of a weak uridine-rich RNA–DNA hybrid in many examples. Yet Pol III recognizes a thymidine tract in a manner that does not require the canonical bacterial upstream hairpin. Pol I often relies on DNA-bound termination factors, and can also use RNA cleavage and exonuclease-dependent mechanisms in particular organisms. Thus neither the RNA product nor the apparent simplicity of a terminator is sufficient to identify the precise molecular pathway. Mechanistic classification should be based on the signal, the relevant polymerase and the required factors together.

The biological consequences of transcriptional regulation extend beyond the synthesis of any one messenger RNA. During a cellular response to stress, growth signals or developmental cues, a change in the release of already engaged Pol II can alter the rate at which new transcripts become available. During cell proliferation, continuous production of ribosomal RNA and tRNA supports the increased demand for protein synthesis. Defects in transcription elongation or termination can therefore change the relative supply of several macromolecular systems even when the DNA sequence encoding the proteins remains intact. The molecular principles explained here connect directly to cellular physiology and gene-expression regulation, without requiring a separate account of every regulatory network.

In biotechnology, an engineered expression cassette contains more than a promoter and a coding sequence. Appropriate RNA end formation and termination signals help produce a correctly processed transcript and reduce unwanted transcription into adjacent sequences. When expression from a reporter is unexpectedly low, replacing the promoter is not always the most informative intervention. Measuring the length and abundance of the precursor, the mature product and the downstream transcription signal may reveal a defective RNA processing or termination element. Such reasoning is a practical application of the distinction between initiation, elongation, processing and release.

The next stage in the learning sequence is to examine the chemical construction of the two ends of a eukaryotic messenger RNA. The present chapter establishes why those reactions are often associated with a moving polymerase and why the final RNA boundary cannot simply be inferred from the point at which polymerase leaves DNA. Understanding this separation provides the basis for learning the cap linkage, cleavage signal, poly(A) addition and their consequences for RNA stability, export and translation. The mechanistic story of gene expression is continuous even though the reactions are studied in separate chapters.

After complete synthesis inhibition, RNA declines from 640 to 80 molecules in 45 min. What is its first-order half-life?

  1. 5 min
  2. 15 min
  3. 22.5 min
  4. 45 min
Answer and explanation

B — 15 min. 640→320→160→80 is three half-lives; 45/3 = 15 min. The inference assumes the degradation constant is unaffected.

43. Numerical

At steady state M = 400 molecules and k = 0.05/min. If synthesis falls to 10/min and k stays constant, what is the new steady-state abundance?

  1. 800
  2. 100
  3. 200
  4. 400
Answer and explanation

C — 200. Initial synthesis = kM = 20/min. New M = S/k = 10/0.05 = 200 molecules. The transition is gradual, not an immediate halving.

44. Numerical

A 2,500-nt RNA is elongated from an existing 100-nt nascent RNA. How many NTP incorporation events and PPi products are required for the remaining length, assuming no errors?

  1. 100 each
  2. 2,399 each
  3. 2,400 each
  4. 2,500 each
Answer and explanation

C — 2,400 each. 2,500−100 = 2,400 additions. Each adds one residue and produces one PPi. Initiation chemistry is not counted.

45. Experimental

Hypothetical equal-length windows show promoter/body run-on signals 100/80 before treatment and 170/15 afterward. Which mechanism is most consistent?

  1. Enhanced RNA decay alone
  2. Loss of Pol I only
  3. Uniform loss of all engaged polymerases
  4. Impaired promoter-proximal release
Answer and explanation

D — Impaired promoter-proximal release. Early signal rises while downstream signal collapses. This supports post-initiation failure; mature RNA decay alone does not directly predict the spatial run-on change.

46. Experimental

A mutant retains correctly cleaved upstream RNA but shows increased downstream Pol II occupancy and persistent downstream RNA. Which step should be investigated first?

  1. XRN2-associated post-cleavage termination
  2. Ribosomal decoding
  3. 5S rRNA assembly
  4. Promoter recognition by TBP
Answer and explanation

A — XRN2-associated post-cleavage termination. Cleavage still occurs, narrowing the candidate defect to post-cleavage events. XRN2 is a plausible target, not uniquely proven without rescue or direct evidence.

47. Experimental

Reporter mutation eliminates a normal 3′ cleavage product and produces a longer RNA spanning the former site. Which conclusion is best?

  1. The promoter must be deleted
  2. A 3′ processing defect is plausible
  3. RNA polymerase is absent from the cell
  4. A translation stop codon has been created
Answer and explanation

B — A 3′ processing defect is plausible. Continuous RNA and loss of cleavage product support defective processing; further measurements of downstream transcription can test coupling to termination.

48. Experimental

An inhibitor reduces new bacterial initiation products but permits preassembled elongation complexes to finish. Which compound best matches?

  1. α-amanitin
  2. Rifampicin
  3. Actinomycin D as a general DNA obstruction
  4. Cycloheximide
Answer and explanation

B — Rifampicin. Rifampicin characteristically blocks the very early RNA extension pathway. The other agents either act differently or inhibit translation.

49. Experimental

In a mammalian nuclear preparation, low α-amanitin reduces synthesis of X to 5% while Y and Z remain near 100%. Higher concentration reduces Z to 20%, Y remains near 100%. Assign X/Y/Z.

  1. Pol II/III/I
  2. Pol I/II/III
  3. Pol II/I/III
  4. Pol III/II/I
Answer and explanation

C — Pol II/I/III. High sensitivity identifies Pol II; lesser sensitivity is consistent with Pol III; relative resistance is consistent with Pol I. Use the stated mammalian assay.

50. Experimental

Ser2P ChIP falls by half in a gene body, and total Pol II ChIP falls by half too. What can be inferred?

  1. The signals alone do not establish a changed phosphorylation fraction
  2. Pol III has replaced Pol II
  3. Ser2 phosphorylation per enzyme certainly doubled
  4. Ser2 phosphorylation per enzyme certainly halved
Answer and explanation

A — The signals alone do not establish a changed phosphorylation fraction. The two normalized signals decline proportionally. Antibody efficiency and population averaging limit interpretation; a change in fraction is not established.

51. Experimental

A Pol III reporter's non-template TTTTTT tract is changed to TCTGTT. The promoter is unchanged. What result is most plausible?

  1. Guaranteed complete initiation failure
  2. Creation of a poly(A) tail
  3. Conversion to Pol II
  4. Longer readthrough RNA
Answer and explanation

D — Longer readthrough RNA. Disruption of the terminator tract may impair release; it does not by itself change polymerase identity or initiation.

52. Experimental

A purified Pol II mutant extends a naked template normally but accumulates at a nucleosome-rich template; adding functional FACT improves progression. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. Chromatin traversal is a likely limiting step
  2. The RNA template must be replicated
  3. Ribosomal initiation is blocked
  4. The catalytic center cannot make phosphodiester bonds
Answer and explanation

A — Chromatin traversal is a likely limiting step. Naked-template activity and FACT rescue support a chromatin-related barrier. The experiment does not rule out every other cellular function of FACT.

53. Experimental

An engaged backtracked Pol II makes no longer product until TFIIS is added; a shorter RNA then appears before extension resumes. What mechanism fits?

  1. Transcript cleavage restores a catalytic 3′ end
  2. TFIIS creates a new promoter
  3. The RNA 5′ cap catalyzes extension
  4. A DNA primer is inserted
Answer and explanation

A — Transcript cleavage restores a catalytic 3′ end. The shorter intermediate and renewed extension are consistent with cleavage-assisted recovery. The existing complex need not initiate again.

54. Integrative

Two samples have equal mature mRNA abundance, but sample B has twice the nascent synthesis flux at steady state. Assuming first-order degradation, what is expected in B?

  1. No degradation
  2. Half the degradation constant
  3. Twice the degradation constant
  4. Equal degradation constant
Answer and explanation

C — Twice the degradation constant. M = S/k. If M is equal and S doubles, k must double in the simple model; half-life becomes half. Other assumptions must be tested in real cells.

55. Integrative

A faster Pol II variant shows normal cleavage but transcription extends farther downstream. Which hypothesis is most reasonable?

  1. Pol III has been converted into Pol I
  2. The 5′ cap is necessarily absent
  3. Faster movement may make exonuclease catch-up more difficult
  4. Stop codons cease to function
Answer and explanation

C — Faster movement may make exonuclease catch-up more difficult. In a pursuit model, faster polymerase reduces the rate at which the exonuclease closes the gap. This is consistent, not definitive; allosteric changes can also affect release.

Common misconceptions and examination traps

Promoter occupancy equals gene output. Occupancy is a stock, completed transcript flux is a rate. Paused complexes can increase occupancy while output falls.

Promoter escape and pause release are identical. Escape leaves the initiation state; regulated early pausing can follow escape.

The CTD is the catalytic active center. The flexible CTD recruits and coordinates factors; the catalytic core performs nucleotidyl transfer.

CDK9 accounts for every Ser2 phosphate. Several kinases contribute. Modification patterns overlap and vary with organism and gene.

NELF is essential in every eukaryote. Canonical metazoan NELF is absent from budding yeast.

Backtracking is necessarily termination. A backtracked complex may resume synthesis, including after cleavage assisted by TFIIS.

Cleavage, polyadenylation and termination occur at one position. They are different reactions and Pol II often continues beyond the cleavage site.

XRN2 removes the upstream mRNA cap. Its canonical termination substrate is the downstream cleavage fragment with an exposed 5′ end.

Torpedo versus allosteric is an either/or choice. Altered polymerase state and exonuclease pursuit can cooperate.

Pol III requires the bacterial hairpin. The canonical Pol III signal is a non-template T tract; a hairpin is not universally required.

All Pol II mRNA has a poly(A) tail. Replication-dependent animal histone mRNAs are a classic exception.

Actinomycin D is Pol I-specific. It interacts with DNA; preferential inhibition at low doses is not absolute specificity.

Rifampicin stops every active elongation complex immediately. It predominantly blocks early extension and established complexes are relatively less susceptible.

Pol III is universally resistant to α-amanitin. Relative sensitivity varies across species and doses; typical mammalian Pol III is less sensitive than Pol II.

Higher ChIP signal proves faster RNA production. Association does not prove extension or flux; combine positional occupancy with nascent RNA measurements.

Active recall

What changes when the RNA polymerase translocates?

DNA and RNA shift relative to the active center; the next template nucleotide can be read.

What distinguishes a paused complex from an abortive RNA?

A paused complex remains engaged; abortive RNA has been released.

Why can high promoter occupancy coexist with low output?

Long residence or inefficient release can increase occupancy while downstream flux declines.

What exposes the XRN2 substrate?

Cleavage creates an exposed 5′ end on the downstream RNA.

Which DNA strand contains the Pol III T tract?

The non-template strand; the template contains A and RNA contains U.

Why is measuring nascent RNA useful during inhibitor experiments?

It distinguishes synthesis from pre-existing RNA and degradation.

How does the half-life change if k doubles?

It halves under the first-order model.

What evidence distinguishes cleavage failure from post-cleavage termination failure?

Detect correctly cleaved upstream RNA and compare downstream nascent transcription.
