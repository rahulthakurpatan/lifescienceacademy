1. The problem that transcription initiation solves

A multicellular organism contains many kinds of cells even though most of those cells inherit substantially the same genome. A liver cell, a neuron and a muscle cell do not usually acquire their different properties by replacing their DNA with different collections of genes. They use different portions of the genome at different rates and times. This observation leads to a fundamental molecular question. How does a cell select a particular DNA region, establish the correct direction of transcription and begin synthesizing RNA at a useful rate? The same question applies to a single-celled eukaryote that encounters a change in nutrients. Its genome remains largely the same, but its physiological needs change. Regulation must convert information about the cell into changes in RNA production.

Transcription initiation is the collection of events that prepares an RNA polymerase to start copying a selected DNA template and allows synthesis of the first RNA nucleotides. The word initiation is important because binding to DNA and making RNA are different accomplishments. A protein may recognize a promoter without recruiting the polymerase. A polymerase may be present near the transcription start site but unable to access the template strand. It may even synthesize a short RNA and fail to progress efficiently into a productive transcript. A useful explanation therefore identifies the particular transition being controlled, rather than treating transcription as an instantaneous event that is either on or off.

The focus here is nuclear RNA polymerase II, which transcribes most eukaryotic protein-coding genes and several classes of noncoding genes. The biochemical reaction catalyzed by polymerase II is the same type of phosphodiester-bond formation introduced earlier: ribonucleoside triphosphates supply the growing RNA, and the DNA strand read in the opposite direction determines their sequence. Yet an isolated polymerase does not normally identify and efficiently initiate at a particular native double-stranded promoter by itself. It requires a set of collaborating proteins that position the enzyme, shape promoter DNA and promote its opening. Other proteins determine whether those collaborators can act efficiently at a particular gene under a particular condition.

It is helpful to distinguish three sources of information. The sequence of the template determines the sequence of RNA. The architecture of the promoter supplies positional information specifying where initiation is likely to occur. Regulatory DNA elements and the proteins that recognize them supply conditional information about when and how strongly the promoter operates. These three functions are not necessarily carried out by three separate physical segments; a regulatory site may lie within a promoter, and downstream promoter elements can overlap transcribed DNA. Nevertheless the distinction prevents confusion between a mutation that changes a protein's amino acid sequence and one that changes the amount of otherwise unchanged protein produced.

An everyday analogy is a library containing instructions for many tasks. The information in a book is not altered when a librarian retrieves it more frequently. Similarly, a transcriptional activator commonly changes the frequency with which an existing DNA sequence is copied into RNA rather than rewriting that sequence. The analogy has a limit: in a cell, molecular interactions are probabilistic, so a promoter is not a rigid shelf and an activator is not a conscious selector. Different proteins collide, associate and dissociate, and the time spent in productive configurations determines average output. A weak promoter can sometimes initiate, and a strongly activated promoter need not be occupied continuously.

Initiation can be economical for the cell because deciding not to make an unnecessary RNA avoids expenditure on its synthesis, processing and potential translation. However, it is not the only economical regulatory step. Regulation after initiation can allow rapid changes in output, especially when a polymerase has already started a transcript. A measured decrease in mature messenger RNA does not by itself demonstrate inhibition of initiation: RNA processing, degradation and polymerase progression can also change the result. Throughout this chapter the distinction between the mechanism and its experimental readout will be maintained.

The central reasoning strategy is to ask four successive questions: can the DNA be recognized, can the polymerase be recruited and positioned, can the template be exposed and copied, and can the new transcript leave the initial configuration? A gene-specific regulator can influence more than one of these questions. A general transcription factor usually contributes to the common machinery that answers them. Once those roles are understood, apparently difficult examination scenarios involving promoter mutations, missing proteins, recruitment assays and transcription measurements become variations of a single mechanistic framework.

2. Promoter coordinates, direction and functional DNA elements

Before discussing the proteins, establish a map of the DNA on which they operate. The transcription start site is the nucleotide position at which the first ribonucleotide is incorporated into an RNA. By convention this position is called +1. Positions before it in the direction opposite transcription are assigned negative numbers, such as −25 and −100; positions further along the transcribed direction receive positive numbers. In this convention there is no position zero. A promoter is a DNA region whose sequence and molecular context support the initiation of transcription. The core promoter is the region close to the start site that participates directly in positioning and initiating polymerase II. Its precise boundaries depend on promoter architecture rather than a universal length.

A frequent mistake is to equate the transcription start site with the start codon. The former is a DNA coordinate for RNA synthesis; the latter is a triplet in the RNA used to initiate translation. A protein-coding transcript can contain a substantial 5′ untranslated region between these landmarks. An upstream mutation may prevent transcription altogether without modifying any coding triplet. Conversely, a start-codon mutation can interfere with translation while allowing transcription from the promoter at an unchanged rate. When a question describes a change near +1, determine whether the stated coordinate refers to transcription or translation before drawing a conclusion.

Promoter orientation is relative to the direction of the gene, not to the page on which the DNA is drawn. A promoter that initiates leftward on a genome map has its upstream region to the right. If the coding strand is displayed in the 5′ to 3′ direction from left to right, a rightward RNA has the same sequence as that strand apart from the replacement of thymine by uracil. The antiparallel DNA strand is read by polymerase from 3′ to 5′. At a leftward promoter the identities of the coding and template strands are reversed for that transcription unit. Neither the physical upper strand nor the physical lower strand is a permanent template for every gene on a chromosome.

Some polymerase II promoters contain a TATA box, often near −25 to −30 in familiar metazoan examples. An initiator element, often abbreviated Inr, can overlap the start site. Other examples include TFIIB recognition elements and downstream elements that contribute to TFIID-dependent recognition in particular promoter classes. These elements are sequence patterns that participate in molecular recognition, not instructions requiring the polymerase to start at the center of every motif. The geometry and spacing between elements can be important because the proteins that contact them occupy physical positions relative to each other. A sequence with excellent individual binding affinity but incorrect placement can support a less productive assembly.

Many eukaryotic polymerase II promoters lack a recognizable consensus TATA box. Some initiate at a relatively focused site; others initiate across a region containing several nearby start positions. Promoters with broad initiation are common among CpG-rich mammalian promoter regions, but this is a tendency rather than a universal rule. For such genes, a single +1 in a diagram may represent one selected start or the most abundant mapped position. The existence of dispersed initiation does not contradict the need for initiation machinery. It indicates that different promoter architectures and chromatin environments can allow productive complexes at more than one nearby coordinate.

The core promoter should also be distinguished from promoter-proximal regulatory sequences and more distant regulatory elements. A promoter-proximal element may recruit a transcription factor whose activity helps the core machinery. A distal enhancer can increase transcription from a compatible promoter even when the element lies far away in linear sequence. A silencer is an element through which repression can occur. These words describe functional behavior established experimentally; merely detecting a candidate sequence motif does not establish enhancer or silencer activity. A regulatory element located within an intron can function as a DNA element even though the region is later transcribed into pre-mRNA.

Consider a hypothetical rightward promoter with a TATA element at −28, an activator site at −85, a transcription start at +1 and a translation start codon at +121. Deleting the activator site may reduce the frequency of initiation while leaving the main start site and amino acid sequence unchanged. Altering the TATA sequence may reduce initiation or redistribute start positions. Changing the translation start codon may affect protein output even if RNA abundance is normal. These predictions distinguish coordinates, functions and experimental measurements. They are hypotheses to be tested, not guarantees that all genes with such coordinates behave identically.

3. From purified polymerase to a multicomponent initiation system

The discovery that eukaryotic RNA synthesis involves several polymerases did not, by itself, explain how any polymerase selects an appropriate promoter. The decisive question was whether the enzyme that catalyzes RNA synthesis also contains all the information required to initiate accurately on a DNA template. If it did, purified polymerase II, an intact promoter and the necessary substrates should produce RNA with the expected start position. If the enzyme needed collaborating proteins, promoter-specific initiation should depend on additional fractions of the cell extract.

Biochemists addressed this distinction by developing cell-free transcription systems. Nuclear extracts supplied the soluble proteins needed to copy defined DNA templates. Researchers could separate the proteins by differences in their biochemical properties, measure the activities of individual fractions and then combine them. A fraction might fail to produce the correct transcript by itself but restore initiation when supplied to a mixture lacking that activity. This approach is called biochemical complementation or reconstitution. It makes the otherwise invisible requirements of the reaction experimentally accessible because the investigator controls which components are present.

Flanagan and colleagues reported in 1990 that fractionation of a yeast nuclear extract identified multiple activities required in addition to polymerase II for accurate initiation. Work in metazoan systems likewise established an array of general factors. The historical number of resolved biochemical activities should not be treated as a permanent count of individual proteins. A fraction can contain a multiprotein complex, and further purification can separate components that were previously grouped together. The modern names TFIIA, TFIIB, TFIID, TFIIE, TFIIF and TFIIH identify canonical factor classes used in describing polymerase II initiation. Roman numeral II indicates association with the polymerase II system; the letters are historical names rather than chronological instructions about binding order.

Reconstitution separates requirement from sufficiency. If omission of a factor prevents initiation and readdition restores it, that factor is required for the measured reaction under those conditions. But the factor alone is not sufficient to synthesize RNA. It collaborates with the promoter, polymerase and other components. A negative result must also be interpreted carefully. The absent activity might reflect instability of a complex during purification, loss of a cofactor, or the particular promoter used. An activity that is dispensable in a simplified assay might be valuable in a cellular environment where DNA is packaged with histones. Therefore a purified system reveals a mechanistic minimum under stated conditions, not a complete inventory of physiological regulation.

The distinction between accurate initiation and general RNA polymerase activity is central. Suppose an enzyme incorporates labeled ribonucleotides into RNA when provided with an accessible synthetic template. This establishes that its active site can catalyze chain growth. If the same preparation fails to make an RNA beginning at the expected promoter position, a defect in promoter recognition, template loading or early complex formation remains possible. Counting total incorporated radioactivity cannot necessarily tell whether the correct gene was initiated. A transcript of known size or a method mapping its 5′ end can provide stronger evidence of specific initiation.

Imagine two hypothetical templates that are identical except that one carries a mutation in the core promoter. Complete extract produces a predominant RNA of the expected size from the wild type and almost none from the mutant. When a purified polymerase is given an already exposed RNA–DNA scaffold, it extends the RNA normally with either preparation. The defect can be assigned upstream of ordinary chain elongation with greater confidence than if only total RNA had been measured. It still does not specify which initiation factor contacts the altered DNA. A protein-binding experiment or selective reconstitution can refine the inference.

An important lesson of these experiments is that molecular biology frequently establishes causal mechanisms through subtraction and restoration. Removing a component and observing loss of a product is informative; restoring the product after readdition is much more informative. Introducing an inactive version of the component provides a way to separate structural contributions from catalytic ones. Such comparisons are the basis for understanding why transcription factors are not simply accessories to polymerase II. They create the physical and regulatory conditions in which its catalytic potential can be used at a selected promoter.

4. The TATA-binding protein and the first architectural decision

The TATA-binding protein, generally abbreviated TBP, is a central component of the initiation machinery. Its name reflects its ability to recognize certain A/T-rich promoter elements, particularly the TATA box. In the canonical polymerase II pathway it is associated with TFIID, a complex containing TBP and TBP-associated factors known as TAFs. It is necessary to distinguish the protein from the complex: TBP is one polypeptide, whereas TFIID has multiple interacting subunits. These associated factors extend the range of promoter recognition and supply interactions with other regulatory proteins. TBP is also used in other nuclear polymerase systems through different factor complexes, so its presence is not an exclusive marker of polymerase II activity.

The molecular structure of TBP explains why its binding matters beyond simple sequence recognition. Crystallographic studies in 1993 by Kim and coworkers revealed a saddle-shaped protein that interacts with the minor groove of a TATA-containing DNA duplex. DNA is sharply bent and kinked in the bound state. The protein does not cleave the DNA backbone, and the duplex has not yet become the transcription bubble at the start site. Instead, local deformation creates a distinctive geometry upon which subsequent proteins can assemble. The bending energy is compensated by favorable protein–DNA interactions; recognition is therefore a balance of intermolecular contacts and the energetic cost of changing DNA shape.

Most sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins recognize features exposed in the grooves of double-stranded DNA. The major groove often provides a particularly rich set of patterns of hydrogen bond donors, acceptors and hydrophobic groups that distinguish base pairs. TBP's use of the minor groove is a useful examination exception to the common association between sequence recognition and the major groove. Its selectivity includes the ease with which certain sequences adopt the distorted conformation. The DNA sequence is therefore more than a string of chemical identities. It also has mechanical properties that influence protein binding.

A promoter mutation can affect TBP recognition in several ways. Replacing an A/T-rich segment with a sequence that favors a different shape may reduce the stability of the bound complex. A change outside the central motif can affect the trajectory of flanking DNA or the location of another contact. A spacing change may preserve TBP binding yet misalign the rest of the machinery with the start site. These possibilities explain why binding strength and transcriptional output need not be perfectly correlated. A mutation that leaves an early binding step unchanged can impair a later structural transition.

TFIID is particularly important for reasoning about TATA-less promoters. The lack of a conventional TATA sequence does not imply the absence of TBP-related function. TAFs and additional promoter-associated interactions can recognize other features of the DNA and help establish an initiation-competent assembly. An initiator or downstream promoter element can contribute to recognition in a suitable promoter context. It would therefore be incorrect to predict that replacing every TATA-like sequence in a genome would eliminate all polymerase II transcription. The promoter architectures differ, as do their dependencies on particular factor contacts.

TFIIA commonly stabilizes the promoter-bound TBP or TFIID arrangement and can help counter inhibitory interactions. TFIIB contacts TBP and DNA and makes connections toward polymerase II. These interactions help transform a bent promoter into a spatially organized platform for the active enzyme. It is useful to imagine a structural scaffold with both a positional mark and an orientation. The mark locates the complex relative to the DNA; the orientation establishes which downstream segment can enter the polymerase cleft. Although individual proteins fluctuate and assemble through alternative routes in cells, the requirement that the template reach the catalytic center with the correct orientation remains.

An experimental prediction follows from this structural model. If a mutant TBP binds DNA weakly, footprinting or a mobility shift assay may reveal reduced stable occupancy before transcription is measured. If another mutant binds and bends DNA normally but has lost the interaction surface for TFIIB, early occupancy could remain normal while subsequent polymerase recruitment and RNA synthesis fall. The two mutants affect different steps even though both might produce less RNA. Structural knowledge makes these outcomes intelligible and suggests why an assay of one protein cannot be substituted for measurement of the complete pre-initiation complex.

The 1993 structures provide direct evidence of a particular bound DNA conformation. They do not prove that every promoter bends identically in every cellular condition, nor do they by themselves identify the rate-limiting event in initiation. A static structural picture should be combined with biochemical evidence and measurements of transcript formation. Such integration is characteristic of molecular explanations in which shape, interactions, motion and reaction products all contribute distinct forms of evidence.

5. Assembling the closed pre-initiation complex

A pre-initiation complex, or PIC, is a molecular assembly containing promoter DNA, RNA polymerase II and the general factors needed to place the enzyme in a configuration suitable for starting RNA synthesis. The word pre-initiation emphasizes that the complex has formed before the first RNA bonds are made. A closed complex contains promoter DNA that is still substantially double stranded in the region that must enter the active site. The name does not mean that no deformation or transient fluctuations occur. It means that the template strand is not yet stably loaded into the catalytic center in the configuration needed for synthesis.

The standard textbook model begins with TFIID associating with a suitable core promoter. TFIIA may stabilize this platform and TFIIB associates with the TBP–DNA arrangement. TFIIB provides connections to polymerase II and contributes to the selection of the start site. In structural models, parts of TFIIB project toward the polymerase cleft. Thus the molecule helps link the upstream recognition complex to the geometry of the catalytic enzyme. If a problem states that a TFIIB mutation changes the positions of the transcript 5′ ends while leaving overall polymerase abundance unchanged, an altered positioning or start-site selection function is a plausible explanation.

Polymerase II is often recruited in association with TFIIF. TFIIF has several related contributions, including stabilizing productive interactions of the enzyme with the initiation apparatus and reducing inappropriate association with DNA in model systems. A chromosome offers an enormous excess of potential nonspecific binding sites. A factor that favors productive promoter association over random DNA interactions can make the initiation process more selective without changing the sequence recognized by TBP. This is a general principle of biological specificity: selection depends both on stabilizing desired states and on avoiding unproductive ones.

TFIIE joins and helps recruit and regulate TFIIH. The resulting assembly positions TFIIH close to downstream promoter DNA. This positioning has functional significance because DNA opening cannot be accomplished merely by binding somewhere in the vicinity of a promoter. Mechanical work must be applied to the DNA in a geometry that permits the template strand to enter the polymerase. The contacts among TFIIE, TFIIH, polymerase and downstream DNA create such a geometry. TFIIE itself does not polymerize RNA or carry out the CDK7 protein phosphorylation reaction.

The sequence TFIID, TFIIA/TFIIB, polymerase II with TFIIF, TFIIE, and TFIIH is a useful model for learning the contributions of these components. It should not be interpreted as a universal movie showing precisely the same order and dwell time at every promoter. Some factors associate with each other before binding DNA, and coactivators can affect multiple interactions during assembly. Different promoters may favor different routes to a similar final arrangement. What is conserved at the explanatory level is the need to recruit and orient polymerase and support template opening and initiation. A question explicitly referring to the classical sequential model may expect the conventional order, while a question about real cellular assembly may emphasize its flexibility.

One way to analyze the assembly is to separate its physical interfaces. The promoter provides DNA recognition sites. TFIID and associated factors interact with the promoter and each other. TFIIB helps position polymerase relative to that platform. TFIIF accompanies and stabilizes polymerase in an initiation-compatible arrangement. TFIIE helps position and control TFIIH. TFIIH supplies important ATP-dependent functions, discussed in the next section. The components need not all make independent sequence-specific contacts. A protein recruited largely through protein–protein interactions can be essential for promoter function even though it has no separate consensus motif at every regulated gene.

Suppose an experiment compares intact promoter DNA with a mutant whose upstream recognition region is disrupted. For intact DNA, immunodetection reveals promoter-associated TBP, TFIIB and polymerase II. For the mutant, all three signals decrease. Loss of the initial recognition platform could explain the later losses because the downstream binding events depend on it. A different mutant preserves TBP but reduces both TFIIB and polymerase. This pattern more narrowly suggests a defect after initial recognition. A third mutant preserves occupancy of all three yet fails to produce RNA; in that case the defect may lie in promoter opening, nucleotide incorporation or the stability of the productive state. Comparing multiple states is far more informative than reporting only whether a transcript is detectable.

The concept of a closed PIC prepares us to understand why recruitment is a necessary but insufficient explanation of transcriptional activation. Raising the number of promoter-associated polymerases can increase output only if those molecules can reach the open state and begin copying. An activator that increases recruitment might have little effect at a promoter whose opening step is blocked. This is also why a promoter can exhibit a strong protein occupancy signal without a proportional amount of RNA.

6. TFIIH, ATP-dependent promoter opening and template loading

The central physical obstacle remaining after closed-complex assembly is the double-stranded structure of promoter DNA. The polymerase active site must read one strand and use its exposed bases as a template for complementary ribonucleotides. A stable duplex cannot simply be inserted into this site as though both strands were to be copied together. A small segment must separate, and the resulting template and non-template strands must follow different paths within the enzyme. This event is called promoter opening, and the productive arrangement is often called an open complex.

Separating DNA strands requires disruption of base stacking and hydrogen-bonding interactions. The cost depends on sequence, neighboring bases, temperature, ionic conditions and protein contacts. A textbook statement that A/T-rich DNA is generally easier to melt than G/C-rich DNA is a helpful approximation, not a complete description of promoter opening. TBP binding upstream deforms DNA, but the deformation at a TATA element is not identical to the strand separation near the transcription start site. Likewise, a promoter with no TATA box still needs an accessible template to initiate.

TFIIH has a structural core and a kinase module. A key component of its core, XPB in humans and Ssl2 in budding yeast, is an ATP-dependent DNA translocase. Older teaching accounts often refer to TFIIH simply as a helicase that unwinds the DNA. The more precise initiation model emphasizes that XPB acts on downstream double-stranded DNA and promotes its movement or rotation relative to the rest of the constrained initiation complex. This mechanical activity helps feed DNA into the polymerase cleft and allows local strand separation. It differs from a simple picture of a conventional helicase that travels along an already separated strand and unzips a long DNA duplex ahead of a replication fork.

Fishburn, Tomko, Galburt and Hahn investigated yeast TFIIH in a 2015 study that linked the Ssl2 ATPase to double-stranded DNA translocation. The evidence supports a model in which downstream DNA movement contributes to the generation of the open complex. The mechanistic lesson is not that the precise same number of base pairs must move in every species or every reaction. It is that ATP hydrolysis can be converted into a change of DNA geometry within a physically organized assembly. A mutation that preserves the association of TFIIH with the PIC but eliminates the motor activity may consequently permit closed-complex formation while impairing promoter opening.

XPD is another ATP-dependent helicase associated with TFIIH. Its contribution should not be confused with the principal XPB-dependent promoter-opening mechanism. XPD has an especially important catalytic role in nucleotide excision repair. TFIIH participates in both transcription and repair, but the arrangement and relative functions of its subunits differ between the two processes. Thus a mutation in a shared complex can create more than one cellular phenotype. It would be unjustified to infer that every TFIIH-associated disease or DNA repair defect is caused solely by inability to open promoters.

ATP has more than one biochemical role in transcription. ATP can be one of the four substrates incorporated into RNA when the corresponding template base is thymine. ATP can also be hydrolyzed by XPB to support mechanical movement. The kinase module of TFIIH can transfer the terminal phosphate from ATP to a protein substrate. These processes use the same chemical species for distinct molecular purposes. If ATP is omitted from a conventional reaction, failure of transcript formation may reflect loss of more than one activity. An informative experiment must use appropriately designed templates, catalytic mutants or pre-opened substrates to distinguish these possibilities.

A pre-melted promoter template provides a useful conceptual bypass experiment. Imagine that a small region near the start site is deliberately rendered noncomplementary so that the strands are already locally separated. If a preparation containing inactive XPB is defective on duplex DNA but recovers some initiation on the pre-melted substrate, the observation supports a role for XPB in overcoming the opening barrier. It does not prove that XPB has no other functions. The artificial mismatch can alter template geometry or stability, and recovery of short RNA synthesis is not necessarily recovery of normal promoter escape. Appropriate wild-type controls on both substrates are needed because the mismatch may change transcription even when the motor is fully functional.

Promoter opening is a local event, not global chromosome denaturation. Most upstream and downstream DNA remains double stranded, while an exposed region allows the template bases to pair transiently with incoming ribonucleotides and the growing RNA. The non-template strand is displaced from this local region and is not used as the template for the selected transcript. The enzyme must maintain the proper strand identity and position as synthesis begins. Because opening and reannealing are physical transitions, a closed and an open state can interconvert under some conditions before synthesis commits the complex to later stages.

A useful analysis of an opening-defective preparation would compare promoter occupancy, a probe for exposed bases and the formation of short correctly initiated RNA. Normal occupancy accompanied by weak evidence of strand opening and little RNA would be consistent with an opening defect. Normal opening accompanied by weak RNA would instead direct attention to catalytic activity, nucleotide availability or the alignment of the start site. In neither case should a decrease in final mRNA be attributed to an arbitrary general factor without supporting evidence.

7. First phosphodiester bonds, start-site choice and early transcription

Once the template is accessible and correctly positioned, RNA polymerase II can use ribonucleoside triphosphates to start a chain without a pre-existing RNA primer. The first nucleotide is selected by base pairing to the template in the catalytic center, and subsequent addition occurs at the 3′ hydroxyl of the growing RNA. For each new bond, the 3′ hydroxyl attacks the alpha phosphate of an incoming ribonucleoside triphosphate, with pyrophosphate released. Magnesium ions participate in the catalytic mechanism. The overall direction of RNA growth is 5′ to 3′, while the template is read 3′ to 5′. Initiation produces a new RNA end, unlike DNA polymerases that normally extend a pre-existing primer.

It is necessary to distinguish template availability from template placement. A region of DNA can be single stranded yet not be aligned appropriately with the polymerase active site. The start site selected by a productive PIC depends on promoter architecture, the positions of participating factors and the ability of DNA to reach the catalytic center. In some metazoan model promoters the relation between a TATA element and the start site is relatively stereotyped, but budding yeast can select start sites over a broader downstream range through mechanisms involving scanning. The student should not transfer one particular spacing rule to all eukaryotic promoters. A common biochemical requirement is satisfied through somewhat different promoter organizations.

For example, imagine the template around a chosen start position written from 3′ to 5′ as TACGAT. If transcription begins at its first T, the RNA segment is 5′-AUGCUA-3′. That sequence happens to begin with AUG, but its appearance at the RNA 5′ end is incidental to this example. Polymerase II does not require the translation initiation codon as a transcription start sequence. If the selected start moves two bases downstream, the RNA begins 5′-GCUA-3′ in this simplified segment. The change in start can alter the 5′ untranslated region and sometimes downstream expression without changing the physical DNA sequence.

Early transcription presents a mechanical challenge. A polymerase initially held in place by promoter-associated interactions begins producing RNA while those interactions still constrain the complex. A growing RNA and the changing RNA–DNA hybrid can interfere with contacts that were useful during initiation. Some initiation events can produce short abortive products that are released instead of developing into long transcripts. Promoter escape involves reorganization or loss of some initial contacts and the conversion of the initiation complex toward a form capable of sustained elongation. The precise transition is not defined by a universal transcript length. It depends on the promoter, organism and experimental system.

A distinction should be made between initiation rate, initial RNA synthesis and the rate of production of complete RNA molecules. Consider a promoter at which ten initial events occur per minute but half fail before becoming productive. The rate of productive events is then five per minute under the stated assumption. If a mutation reduces early failures to one in ten without changing the number of initial events, nine per minute become productive. This is an increase in output despite unchanged frequency of first-bond formation. Conversely, a mutation that increases short initiated products but reduces promoter escape can create the misleading appearance of activation when only very short products are measured.

Start-site mapping provides a way to investigate positioning defects. In a simplified comparison, wild type may yield an RNA that begins at +1 in most molecules, while a mutant of TFIIB or a promoter element may produce a larger fraction starting at neighboring coordinates. If the total number of initiated molecules remains similar, the principal defect is not necessarily reduced recruitment. A change in the distribution of 5′ ends implies altered start choice. If both the total and the distribution change, the mutation may affect multiple steps, or the altered start choice may be coupled to lower productivity. Examination problems often supply data that allow these alternatives to be separated.

One can visualize initiation as a series of reversible associations followed by chemical steps and changing structural constraints. Once RNA synthesis begins, the state of the molecule is no longer adequately described only by which proteins are bound. It is also necessary to know how many nucleotides have been incorporated and whether the RNA remains associated with the polymerase. This is why methods that observe DNA binding, DNA opening, short nascent RNA and full-length RNA are complementary. Each reports a different property of a dynamic reaction.

8. CTD phosphorylation and the boundary between initiation and elongation

The largest subunit of RNA polymerase II contains a distinctive carboxyl-terminal domain, commonly called the CTD. In typical model eukaryotes it includes repeated sequences based on the heptapeptide Tyr-Ser-Pro-Thr-Ser-Pro-Ser. The positions within each repeat are denoted Tyr1, Ser2, Pro3, Thr4, Ser5, Pro6 and Ser7. Repeat number and deviations from the consensus vary among species. The CTD is separate from the central catalytic function of the enzyme and provides a flexible platform for interactions with factors at different stages of transcription.

During the assembly of an initiation complex, polymerase with a relatively low level of CTD phosphorylation is often favored for interactions with the initiation apparatus and Mediator. The kinase component of TFIIH includes CDK7, cyclin H and MAT1 in mammals. CDK7 phosphorylates CTD residues, particularly Ser5 and Ser7 in conventional descriptions of early transcription. The term phosphorylation here means addition of a phosphate group to the protein, not incorporation of a ribonucleotide into RNA. The phosphate transfer changes chemical properties such as charge and the availability of recognition sites for interacting proteins. A modification can therefore influence which partners the polymerase associates with even if its RNA catalytic center is unchanged.

An important outcome of the early CTD modification pattern is coupling of transcription to RNA maturation. Enzymes involved in early RNA processing, including capping, recognize features of polymerase and its phosphorylated CTD. This coordination is useful because a nascent transcript becomes accessible while the polymerase is still near the promoter. Bringing processing machinery to the enzyme increases the opportunity for newly emerging RNA to be modified at the appropriate time. The detailed chemistry of the cap is reserved for the dedicated node on end processing, but the connection explains why mutations in an initiation-associated kinase can affect events observed on the RNA product.

The changing CTD state is also associated with transitions in transcription machinery. Mediator interactions with the low-phosphorylation CTD can be weakened by phosphorylation, helping the polymerase change its set of contacts. Some early factor associations are reorganized as synthesis progresses, while other factors can remain at or return to the promoter and support subsequent initiation events. A simple textbook picture of all general factors simultaneously falling off at one moment is too rigid. Different components have different dynamics, and a promoter can retain a platform that assists another round of transcription.

One must avoid assigning every elongation function to CDK7 or every initiation function to CTD phosphorylation. In later transcription, other kinases contribute to additional modification patterns, particularly increased Ser2 phosphorylation in common metazoan models. These are treated with elongation in the next chapter. The relevant principle now is that polymerase II changes interaction states as it moves through the transcription cycle. A CTD modification is often a marker and a regulator of a transition, but it is not an infallible clock indicating the exact position of every polymerase molecule in a living nucleus.

A comparison of two TFIIH mutants makes the difference between motor and kinase activity clearer. In a hypothetical preparation containing an XPB motor-defective protein, the PIC may be present but opening of intact promoter DNA is compromised. In a second preparation with reduced CDK7 activity, promoter opening may be less directly affected, while CTD phosphorylation, efficient early transition or recruitment of RNA processing machinery may be altered. The outcomes depend on the exact mutation, the system and the assays used. If a mutation destabilizes the whole TFIIH complex, both activities can be affected. Selective catalytic perturbations are more useful for assigning a specific function.

Experimental measurements of phosphorylated CTD require attention to the difference between phosphorylation per polymerase and polymerase occupancy. Imagine that a treatment halves the number of polymerases at a promoter but leaves the average Ser5 phosphorylation per remaining polymerase unchanged. The total promoter-associated Ser5-phosphorylated signal may also fall by half. This observation alone does not establish a defect in the kinase. Measuring total polymerase and normalizing the modified signal to the total under appropriate assay conditions can distinguish changes in abundance from changes in modification state. Antibody specificity and accessibility remain relevant limitations.

The CTD is consequently a bridge between initiation, later transcription and RNA processing. Its study illustrates the general idea that biological machines are regulated not only by their active sites but also by surfaces that recruit collaborators. A mutation in such a surface may have a dramatic physiological consequence even if an isolated enzyme remains capable of forming phosphodiester bonds. This principle will recur in the study of transcriptional activators, which often work through the interaction surfaces of other proteins rather than acting as catalysts themselves.

9. Sequence-specific transcription factors and the information in DNA grooves

A general factor helps the polymerase use a promoter. A gene-specific transcription factor helps explain why one promoter is used differently from another. Such factors are often called sequence-specific DNA-binding proteins because their distribution among genomic regions is influenced by recognition of particular DNA sequences. The term gene-specific should not be understood literally as one factor for one gene. A single transcription factor can control many genes that share compatible binding sites and regulatory contexts, and a single gene can receive input from several factors. The distinction between general and regulatory factors is primarily a distinction of function within the initiation system.

A DNA recognition sequence is commonly described by a motif. A motif summarizes preferences at a group of positions, rather than requiring one exact sequence in every functional binding site. Some positions are strongly constrained because a protein side chain recognizes a distinctive chemical feature. Other positions tolerate different bases because they contribute mainly to shape or make relatively weak contacts. This is why a consensus sequence is a statistical description and not a guarantee of strong binding. Even a perfect match may be inaccessible inside a nucleosome or lack an appropriate neighboring partner. Conversely, a nonconsensus site may function efficiently when supported by cooperative interactions.

A transcription factor typically obtains its specificity from a folded DNA-binding region. Common examples include zinc fingers, homeodomains and basic regions associated with leucine zippers or helix-loop-helix structures. In a classical C2H2 zinc finger, coordination of a zinc ion helps stabilize a fold that can present an alpha helix toward DNA. Zinc acts primarily as a structural cofactor in this example; the transcription factor is not thereby a zinc-dependent enzyme catalyzing transcription. A homeodomain uses a helix-based recognition arrangement to contact DNA. These domain classes are useful illustrations, not a complete catalog of regulatory proteins.

In a basic leucine zipper protein, a helical dimerization region helps associate two protein chains, while an adjacent basic region contacts DNA. The hydrophobic interface created by periodically spaced leucine or related residues contributes to pairing of the helices. Positively charged and other residues in the DNA-binding region make contacts with DNA. The zipper does not itself constitute the DNA template-reading catalytic center of polymerase. A mutation that disrupts dimerization can weaken DNA binding because the functional binding surface requires the appropriate pair of subunits, even if the amino acids directly contacting bases are unaltered.

Dimerization expands the possible information encoded by a limited number of proteins. If proteins A and B each contain compatible dimerization interfaces, the A–A, B–B and A–B combinations can have different preferences for arrangements of DNA half-sites. Their relative concentrations also affect how frequently the complexes form. Thus the presence of protein A alone does not tell us which sites it will occupy in every cell. Protein B may compete for A, create a new recognition preference or modify its activation capacity. These possibilities explain why specificity can depend on the composition of a cell as well as on the sequence of its DNA.

DNA binding is reversible. A transcription factor with a stable fold and an appropriate recognition sequence may bind more often or remain bound longer than it does at a mismatched sequence. Noncovalent interactions, including hydrogen bonds, electrostatic attractions, dispersion interactions and changes in hydration, contribute to binding. However, the difference between strong and weak binding is quantitative rather than absolute. At sufficiently high protein concentration, some lower-affinity sites can become occupied. The cell must regulate not only transcription-factor production but also its free concentration, localization, conformation and interaction partners.

It is useful to distinguish direct sequence recognition from recruitment to DNA through another protein. A DNA-binding regulator may bring a second regulator to its site even if the second protein lacks an independent DNA-binding motif. In that case the second protein's presence at a chromosomal region is evidence of association, not necessarily direct contact with the bases. Crosslink-based occupancy experiments cannot automatically make that distinction. To establish direct recognition, a purified protein may be tested against defined DNA sequences or mutated recognition sites. To establish regulatory function, an additional measurement of transcription is needed.

The functional result of binding is equally context-dependent. A factor can occupy a site with high affinity yet fail to stimulate transcription if its activation surface is damaged, if the promoter cannot respond to it or if required cofactors are missing. Occupancy is a molecular intermediate between a DNA motif and RNA production. Once this is understood, mutations affecting DNA-binding specificity can be separated from mutations affecting the ability to alter initiation after binding has occurred.

10. Activator domains, modularity and the transition from binding to activation

A transcriptional activator increases transcription from a responsive promoter. Its most characteristic function is not simply recognizing DNA, but increasing the probability that the machinery at a target gene will produce RNA. In many activators, DNA recognition and transcriptional stimulation are carried out by distinguishable regions of the same protein. A DNA-binding domain positions the protein, while an activation domain interacts with other proteins that influence transcription. Additional regions can mediate dimerization, ligand binding, regulatory modification or nuclear localization.

This modular organization is particularly helpful when reasoning about mutations. Imagine an activator whose DNA-binding domain remains normal but whose activation domain has been deleted. The shortened protein can still associate with the recognition sequence if its fold and localization are preserved. However, it may no longer recruit or stimulate the interacting proteins needed to enhance initiation. A gel-shift experiment may detect strong binding while a reporter experiment shows little activation. This pattern differs from a mutation that disrupts the DNA-binding domain, which can eliminate both occupancy and activation because the regulator cannot be positioned at the target.

In some circumstances the DNA-binding but activation-defective protein may behave as a dominant inhibitor. If it competes with normal activator molecules for a limited set of recognition sites, it can occupy the sites without delivering the activation function. The presence of the mutant can then reduce output below the value obtained with normal activator alone. Dominance in this example is a consequence of molecular competition, not a requirement that the mutant directly repress the promoter through a newly acquired repression domain. Whether such an effect is observed depends on relative concentrations, affinity and the extent to which the mutant competes for functional sites.

An influential test of activator modularity was reported by Brent and Ptashne in 1985. They constructed a hybrid containing the DNA-recognition capability of the bacterial LexA repressor and the transcriptional activation function of the yeast GAL4 system. In yeast, the hybrid could stimulate transcription of a gene associated with the appropriate LexA recognition site. This experiment demonstrated that a eukaryotic activation function could be directed to a new DNA target through a different recognition region. The fact that LexA acts as a repressor in its normal bacterial setting did not make its DNA-binding region intrinsically repressive in the hybrid. The functional consequence depended on the additional molecular interactions made possible by the fusion.

The most important evidence in such an experiment is the dependence of the output on both the targeting site and the activation function. A site-free control asks whether activation requires the specific DNA recognition element. A fusion lacking a functional activation region asks whether occupancy alone is sufficient. A comparison with the native activation system helps establish that the reporter can respond to transcriptional stimulation. Protein abundance and nuclear localization must also be considered: failure of a fusion to activate could reflect a poorly expressed or misfolded protein rather than an inherently nonfunctional interaction surface. These are principles of experimental interpretation, not claims that every one of these controls was measured in precisely this form in the historical publication.

Activation domains have historically been described using compositional categories such as acidic, glutamine-rich or proline-rich regions. These categories can aid recognition of familiar examples, but no one amino-acid composition guarantees that a protein domain will activate every promoter. A functional activation domain must interact productively with relevant partner proteins. Some activation regions are flexible or only acquire a more ordered configuration when they bind an interaction partner. The student needs the principle that a protein surface can communicate regulatory information, not a memorized assumption that a particular percentage of acidic residues defines a universal activator.

An activator may act by helping a promoter form a productive initiation complex, stabilizing an existing arrangement, increasing the availability of the core promoter, or facilitating a transition after recruitment. Different activators can influence distinct events, and a single regulator can have more than one molecular effect. It is not always correct to state that an activator binds directly to polymerase II. The contacts can be mediated through a coactivator or a general factor, and the effect may involve changes in local DNA accessibility. A promoter-specific activator is consequently best considered an input into an interacting system, not a miniature enzyme that independently copies DNA.

The modular model has practical value in biotechnology. A DNA-targeting region can in principle be joined to a functional activation region to direct a regulatory effect toward a chosen promoter. The same conceptual separation underlies many engineered transcription systems. However, success depends on more than choosing a sequence: the target must be accessible and appropriately positioned, and the regulatory domain must function in the cell type being studied. Such an engineered protein changes the probability of transcription at a locus rather than permanently altering its nucleotide sequence unless it is combined with a separate DNA-modifying activity.

11. Enhancers, proximal elements and the importance of promoter context

When a sequence-specific activator binds near a core promoter, the distance between its activation domain and the initiation machinery may be small enough for relatively local contacts. Eukaryotic regulation also frequently involves regulatory DNA sequences located much farther away along the chromosome. An enhancer is a DNA element that increases transcription of a responsive promoter in an appropriate context. Enhancers can occur upstream, downstream or within transcribed regions. Their activity often shows more flexibility in position and orientation than the activity of a conventional directional core promoter.

The possibility of distance-independent effects was surprising when gene regulatory mechanisms were initially interpreted mainly in terms of adjacent promoter sequences. Banerji, Rusconi and Schaffner reported in 1981 that certain SV40 DNA segments increased expression of a linked rabbit beta-globin gene even when placed at different locations and orientations relative to the transcription start. Their work helped establish the enhancer concept. The observation did not imply that all regulatory sequences act at arbitrary distances on arbitrary genes. It showed that a functional regulatory element could act without being immediately adjacent to the start site and that its orientation requirements could differ from those of the core promoter.

DNA is a three-dimensional polymer, and linear separation does not equal physical separation in the nucleus. A region hundreds or thousands of base pairs away can come close to a promoter as DNA bends and associated proteins interact. DNA looping provides a useful model for how a bound activator can communicate with promoter-associated machinery. A drawing that shows a loop should not imply that every enhancer is continuously attached to a promoter or that a single stable physical loop is obligatory for all activation. Contacts may be transient, and local protein assemblies and chromosomal context can influence their effectiveness. For the examination-level mechanism, the key point is that proteins can transmit regulatory effects across linear DNA distances without requiring RNA polymerase to start transcription at the enhancer and travel to the target start site.

A promoter must be able to respond to the regulatory element. Certain combinations of promoter architecture and enhancer-associated factors are more effective than others. If an enhancer stimulates promoter P1 strongly but has little effect on promoter P2 in an otherwise comparable reporter construct, this can reflect compatibility between the factors assembled at the enhancer and those used by each promoter. It does not necessarily mean the enhancer failed to bind its activators. The experiment therefore should compare regulator occupancy at the element and transcription from the two promoters. The basal activities of P1 and P2 must also be considered when interpreting fold activation.

Spacing and orientation provide additional clues about mechanism. A proximal element that functions through a short-range protein contact can be sensitive to moving the recognition site by a few base pairs. DNA is helical, so inserting a sequence changes both distance and the relative face of the duplex on which the proteins are positioned. A distant enhancer can be less sensitive to small changes in spacing because the DNA can bend over a larger length, but this is not a universal rule. Conversely, some enhancers require precise combinations and arrangements of sites within the element itself even when the whole enhancer can be inverted relative to the promoter. The distinction is between internal organization and the orientation of the element as a whole.

A cis-regulatory element acts on a linked DNA molecule in the usual genetic definition. A trans-acting factor is a diffusible molecule, commonly a protein, that can act on recognition sites present on different DNA molecules within the same cellular environment. If a regulatory site mutation affects only the copy of a gene that carries it, supplying a wild-type copy of the transcription factor may not repair the missing binding site. If the defect lies in a diffusible activator, introducing a functional copy of the factor gene may restore transcription of responsive genes. This difference helps distinguish two classes of regulatory mutations that can produce similar phenotypes.

Consider two reporter plasmids within one cell, each carrying a differently detectable reporter under an otherwise equivalent promoter. Reporter A has an intact activator recognition site; reporter B has a mutated site. Addition of an active factor strongly increases transcription of A but not B. Because both reporters share the same intracellular factor concentration, the difference supports a requirement for the linked sequence. If neither reporter responds and a second assay reveals that the activator is absent from the nucleus, the defect may instead lie in the trans-acting input. The design controls environmental differences by comparing constructs in the same cellular context.

A regulatory DNA element is therefore not sufficient merely because it is present in a sequence database. Its effects depend on the availability of binding proteins, chromatin accessibility, local arrangement, the responsive promoter and the experimental conditions. The regulatory logic of extensive enhancer networks belongs to later chapters. Here the central objective is to understand how a DNA-bound protein located at a regulatory element can change the initiation machinery at a selected promoter.

12. Mediator and other coactivators as communication surfaces

The biochemical problem of activation is that the regulatory protein and the polymerase often have different tasks and may not contact each other directly. A sequence-specific factor recognizes regulatory DNA, while polymerase II performs catalysis at the core promoter. A coactivator is a protein or complex that facilitates transcriptional activation, commonly through interactions with activators and other components of the transcription apparatus. A coactivator need not recognize a particular DNA sequence independently. It can acquire specificity through the regulatory protein that recruits it.

Mediator is a major multiprotein coactivator complex associated with RNA polymerase II transcription. Its name captures the idea of communicating between gene-specific regulators and the initiation machinery. The complex presents multiple interacting surfaces and helps organize contacts with polymerase, the general factors and regulatory inputs. In common structural descriptions, its core includes regions referred to as head, middle and tail modules. The head and middle participate in interactions near the polymerase and initiation complex, while the tail is a prominent interaction region for certain activators. These are functional tendencies rather than an exclusive assignment of every activator to one fixed module.

The importance of Mediator emerged from the observation that basal transcription in vitro and activated transcription were not always reconstituted by the same set of purified components. An initiation system could make a detectable transcript from a suitable promoter, yet respond poorly to a sequence-specific activator. This discrepancy suggested that another activity was needed to connect the regulatory input to the basal apparatus. Work by Kelleher, Flanagan, Kornberg and colleagues around 1990–1991 helped identify the missing mediator activity. In 1994 Kim and colleagues isolated a yeast multiprotein mediator that supported responses to activators in a reconstituted transcription system and interacted with the polymerase II CTD.

The logic of a reconstitution experiment is particularly instructive. A reaction containing polymerase and basal factors can be compared with the same reaction containing an activator and a target recognition site. If the activator has little effect, adding a candidate coactivator may restore the response. Controls lacking the activator, its site or the core promoter reveal whether the extra activity merely increases nonspecific RNA production or helps the appropriate regulatory input. The interpretation is stronger if the RNA has the expected start site and if addition of the coactivator does not simply increase the total amount of polymerase supplied to the reaction.

A hypothetical quantitative example makes this distinction clear. Suppose a basal system generates 20 units of correctly initiated RNA per fixed assay interval. Adding an activator produces 22 units, whereas adding a candidate coactivator alone produces 25 units. Combining both produces 120 units. The increase from 25 to 120 in the presence of the coactivator is much larger than the increase from 20 to 22 without it. The interaction is consistent with the candidate facilitating the activator response. This simple result does not establish a direct physical contact between the two proteins; direct interaction experiments and further functional perturbations would be needed for that conclusion.

Mediator can support initiation through more than a single recruitment event. By stabilizing productive contacts, organizing the PIC and influencing the structural environment of factors such as TFIIH, it can affect the probability that an occupied promoter proceeds to RNA synthesis. Its interactions with the CTD also make it relevant to the transition out of an initiation state. A change in Mediator function could therefore reduce transcription even if a sequence-specific activator still binds its DNA site. Conversely, overexpressing the activator might not rescue a defect in the coactivator surface required to transmit the signal.

Some Mediator-associated activities can contribute to negative as well as positive regulation, depending on context. It is not useful to describe Mediator as an invariant on switch. The term coactivator identifies an important role in activated transcription, but the complex participates in the broader regulatory machinery. Different organisms and cell states can exhibit differences in associated modules and interactions. For the present chapter, the important distinction is between DNA recognition by regulatory factors and communication with polymerase by interacting protein complexes.

Other coactivators may influence promoter access through changes in chromatin structure or histone modifications. A DNA-bound activator can recruit an activity that makes a core promoter more accessible to TFIID and the other general factors. This is a route to activation even if the regulator does not contact polymerase directly. The details of chromatin modification and remodeling are reserved for Subunit 3.D, but their immediate effect on the initiation problem is essential: changing the probability of promoter exposure can increase the population of DNA molecules on which a PIC can assemble. Coactivation is thus a mechanistic category encompassing several ways in which an activator's binding event is converted into transcriptional output.

13. Repressors: blocking activation and preventing productive initiation

A transcriptional repressor decreases transcription from a responsive promoter. Like an activator, it may recognize a particular DNA sequence directly or be recruited through interactions with another regulatory protein. Repression is not the same as degradation of an RNA molecule that has already been synthesized. A repressor acting at initiation changes the probability that new transcripts will be initiated successfully. Although both transcriptional repression and RNA degradation can reduce measured RNA abundance, they act on different molecular processes and require different experiments to distinguish them.

One elementary mechanism is competition for overlapping DNA-binding sites. Suppose an activator and a repressor recognize sequences that overlap so extensively that they cannot occupy them simultaneously. When the activator is bound, it promotes productive assembly of the initiation machinery. When the repressor is bound, the activator cannot deliver that positive input. In this case, the repressor can decrease activation simply by competing for occupancy. It need not possess an independent catalytic activity or contact polymerase directly. The effect depends on the concentrations and binding affinities of both regulators and on the relative contribution of the activation pathway to overall transcription.

Competition may also occur at an interface involving the general machinery. A regulator positioned near a core promoter might sterically interfere with a factor that needs to contact an adjacent sequence or with the placement of a protein complex. A site mutation that prevents repressor binding could permit the PIC to form more efficiently. This prediction is especially plausible if the repressor and initiation apparatus require physically incompatible positions. It would nevertheless be incorrect to assume that every eukaryotic repressor acts by literally covering the TATA box. Many repressors act from regulatory elements outside the core promoter, and some reduce transcription while substantial polymerase occupancy remains detectable.

Another mechanism is inhibition of the activation function without removing the activator from DNA. A repressor can interact with an activator or a relevant cofactor and prevent a productive contact with the general apparatus. This type of local antagonism is often described as quenching in classical discussions of eukaryotic regulation. The defining logic is that an occupied activator becomes less effective. If occupancy of the activator remains high while recruitment of a specific coactivator falls, interference with activation is a plausible mechanism. If both activator and coactivator occupancy fall, competition for DNA binding or an effect on accessibility might instead be involved.

A repressor may also recruit a corepressor, meaning a protein or complex that assists repression through protein–protein interactions and other activities. A corepressor need not independently recognize a particular DNA sequence. The DNA-binding regulator supplies the address, and the recruited complex performs or facilitates the inhibitory function. In a common type of example, a corepressor recruits histone deacetylase activity or other machinery that makes productive promoter association less favorable. Such interactions influence the number or lifetime of accessible promoters. Detailed histone marks, remodeling pathways and epigenetic inheritance are separate syllabus topics, but the immediate link to initiation is clear: a promoter that is less accessible will generally be less available for productive factor assembly.

A useful distinction is between passive loss of activation and active repression of basal transcription. Imagine a promoter produces ten units of RNA without an activator and forty with it. Addition of a competing protein brings the output back to ten. This pattern is consistent with removal of the positive input. If the competing protein reduces output to two units even in the absence of the activator, a mechanism affecting basal activity or another positive input is likely. This conclusion assumes comparable template amount, no general toxicity and equivalent RNA detection. It does not identify the exact repression mechanism without additional measurements.

Some proteins change their regulatory effect with cellular conditions. A ligand-regulated DNA-binding protein may recruit one type of coregulator in one condition and a different type in another. Similarly, alternative dimerization partners can alter the interactions available after DNA binding. The same protein name is consequently not always sufficient to predict whether a particular promoter will be stimulated or repressed. For a useful mechanistic prediction, identify the regulatory state of the protein, the DNA element, the relevant interaction partners and the stage of the transcription cycle being measured.

A repressor's action can be specific without changing the DNA sequence. If a repressor is removed rapidly from a promoter, transcription may recover because the positive machinery can again assemble or function. In contrast, a permanent mutation that removes a core promoter element can make the promoter unresponsive even after the repressor is eliminated. These possibilities produce an important genetic distinction. Removing a negative input can restore transcription only if the remaining positive machinery has the capacity to use the promoter. Failure of derepression may therefore reflect a defect in activation or basal machinery rather than persistence of the original repressor.

14. Chromatin accessibility and the immediate physical environment of the promoter

A double-stranded DNA molecule in a purified reaction differs from a promoter inside a eukaryotic nucleus. Most nuclear DNA is associated with histones and other proteins. A nucleosome contains DNA wrapped around a histone octamer, so a sequence that is chemically intact can be poorly accessible to a protein that needs to contact it. The promoter-recognition machinery must therefore solve not only a sequence-recognition problem but also a problem of physical access. The presence of a consensus motif in a genome cannot guarantee that the corresponding transcription factor or general factor can bind under the conditions of a living cell.

Nucleosomal DNA is not completely inaccessible at every moment. Local fluctuations can expose parts of the wrapped DNA, and the binding of other proteins can stabilize accessible configurations. The position of a regulatory site within a nucleosomal region can affect how readily a transcription factor approaches it. An exposed segment near a linker may be more easily available than an otherwise equivalent sequence buried in a stable histone–DNA arrangement. Such differences can produce large functional consequences even when the sequences recognized by the transcription factor are identical.

Certain regulators can help initiate changes in accessibility. Some sequence-specific factors can engage relatively inaccessible chromatin more efficiently than other factors and thereby facilitate the action of additional proteins. The term pioneer factor is often used for regulators that help establish an accessible regulatory environment. It does not mean that every activator can freely penetrate every nucleosome or that a pioneer factor necessarily makes a promoter fully active without other collaborators. For initiation reasoning, the important point is that the order of regulatory events can depend on which proteins can first access the DNA.

A regulator can recruit ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling machinery that changes nucleosome positions or DNA exposure. ATP is then consumed by a remodeling enzyme whose immediate substrate is the chromatin arrangement. This is a different ATP-dependent process from the action of XPB in opening promoter DNA inside the PIC. Moving a nucleosome to expose a promoter does not create the local transcription bubble by itself. Likewise, a functioning XPB motor does not guarantee that TFIID can first recognize a promoter buried in an unfavorable nucleosomal arrangement. One form of accessibility concerns protein contact with regulatory and core promoter DNA; the other concerns local separation of the DNA strands within the initiation machinery.

Histone modification can also influence access. Acetylation of certain lysine residues neutralizes their positive charge and can alter interactions involving histones and other proteins. In addition, modified histones can provide recognition surfaces for proteins associated with transcriptionally favorable environments. It is too simplistic to argue that acetylation activates every gene solely because a positively charged residue becomes neutral. The effect depends on the location of the modification, the associated readers and the rest of the regulatory system. A histone-modifying coactivator can change the probability of productive initiation indirectly by altering the molecular environment of the promoter.

Consider a hypothetical activator whose presence increases transcription from a chromatinized template tenfold but increases transcription from naked DNA only modestly. If its binding site is intact on both templates and the core transcription apparatus is unchanged, its major contribution may involve relief of a chromatin-associated restriction. A different activator stimulates both naked and chromatinized DNA substantially, suggesting that at least part of its function is independent of a nucleosomal access barrier. However, a difference in fold activation could also arise because the naked template already has higher basal transcription. Comparing absolute output and appropriate controls is necessary before concluding that an effect is chromatin specific.

A chromatin-accessibility measurement can provide additional evidence. Suppose DNA around a promoter becomes more susceptible to cleavage by an accessibility-sensitive enzyme after a regulatory protein is added. This observation suggests that the region is less protected or more exposed. If general factor occupancy also increases and nascent RNA synthesis follows, the combined data are consistent with an accessibility-mediated recruitment mechanism. If accessibility changes without increased transcription, exposure alone may be insufficient because another required factor is absent. A promoter can be accessible without being active, just as a polymerase can be present without producing a transcript.

This relationship provides a bridge to the Academy's earlier chapters on nucleosomes and chromosome organization and to the later treatment of chromatin-based gene control. The present node deliberately stops at the immediate consequences for promoter access and initiation efficiency. The broad inheritance of chromatin states and extensive regulatory circuits require separate treatment. At the molecular level, the key lesson is that DNA sequence and chromatin state jointly determine which promoter interactions are feasible. Any experiment that removes the chromatin environment may simplify the mechanism, but the conclusions drawn from such a reaction must be transferred cautiously to a nucleus.

15. Quantifying binding: affinity, occupancy and competitive regulation

The binding of a transcription factor to a single DNA recognition site can be treated as a reversible molecular association. Denote the free factor by A and the unoccupied site by D. The complex AD forms when A and D associate and disappears when they dissociate. At equilibrium, the dissociation constant Kd is defined by the relation Kd = [A][D]/[AD], where the square brackets represent concentrations. In a simple reaction involving one factor per DNA site, Kd has concentration units such as nanomolar. A smaller Kd indicates that less free factor is required to achieve a given equilibrium occupancy under comparable conditions.

To derive the fractional occupancy, recognize that the total concentration of sites is the sum of unoccupied and occupied sites: [D]total = [D] + [AD]. The occupied fraction is θ = [AD]/([D] + [AD]). The equilibrium relation gives [AD] = [A][D]/Kd. Substituting this expression into both numerator and denominator and dividing by [D] gives θ = [A]/(Kd + [A]). The result assumes a single class of independent sites, equilibrium binding and a known free concentration of factor. If DNA is present at high concentration relative to the factor, the free concentration may differ considerably from the amount added, and the latter must not be substituted blindly.

When the free activator concentration equals Kd, half of the available sites are occupied at equilibrium. If Kd is 10 nM and free activator is 30 nM, θ = 30/(10 + 30) = 0.75. A recognition-site mutation that raises Kd to 90 nM, with the same free activator concentration, gives θ = 30/(90 + 30) = 0.25. The mutation has reduced occupancy from 75 percent to 25 percent. This does not imply that transcription must fall to one third of its original value. The output also depends on basal initiation and on how much activation occurs when the site is occupied.

To connect binding with RNA production, assume that a promoter initiates productively at rate r0 when its regulatory site is unoccupied and at rate rA when occupied by the activator. If switching between states is sufficiently rapid relative to the interval of interest, the average rate in this simplified model is r = r0(1 − θ) + rAθ. This is equivalently r = r0 + (rA − r0)θ. If r0 = 2 transcripts per minute and rA = 10 transcripts per minute, the wild-type occupancy of 0.75 gives r = 2 + 8 × 0.75 = 8 transcripts per minute. The mutant occupancy of 0.25 gives r = 4 transcripts per minute. Although occupancy decreased threefold, the total rate decreased only twofold because basal transcription remains possible.

This model makes it possible to interpret a second class of mutation. If an activation-domain mutant has unchanged Kd but reduces rA from 10 to 2 transcripts per minute, the predicted rate becomes 2 irrespective of the occupancy. Raising the concentration of that mutant protein can saturate the DNA site without restoring activation. In contrast, a moderate binding-affinity defect may be partially overcome by raising the free concentration of a factor whose activation domain is intact. This prediction is a useful distinction between mutations in molecular recognition and mutations in communication with the initiation machinery.

Now consider a repressor R that competes with activator A for the same site. In an equilibrium model the site has three mutually exclusive states: empty, A-bound and R-bound. Let a = [A]/Kd,A and b = [R]/Kd,R. Relative to the empty-state concentration, the activator-bound and repressor-bound state concentrations are a and b times as large, respectively. The sum of the relative weights is 1 + a + b. Consequently the probabilities of the three states are 1/(1 + a + b), a/(1 + a + b) and b/(1 + a + b). These probabilities add to one, as they must.

For a concrete educational example, let [A] = 30 nM, Kd,A = 10 nM, [R] = 20 nM and Kd,R = 5 nM. Then a = 3 and b = 4. The state probabilities are one eighth empty, three eighths activator-bound and four eighths repressor-bound. If the empty and repressor-bound states each produce two transcripts per minute and the activator-bound state produces ten, the predicted average is 2/8 + 30/8 + 8/8 = 5 transcripts per minute. In the absence of repressor, the activator occupancy would be 3/4 and the output eight transcripts per minute. Repression in this example arises through competition without needing to reduce the basal rate of an unactivated promoter.

The equations are models rather than universal laws of transcriptional output. Real promoters can have multiple binding sites, cooperative binding, non-equilibrium transitions driven by ATP, chromatin restrictions and additional states after factor binding. The occupancy equation itself can fail if the system has not reached equilibrium. The linear rate interpolation can fail when factor binding changes promoter accessibility for a period after the factor leaves, or when initiation requires a sequence of events longer than the lifetime of the bound state. Nevertheless, simple models are valuable because they force a student to identify variables, state assumptions and make quantitative predictions that can be compared with observations.

16. Initiation kinetics, cooperation and why occupancy is not output

Equilibrium occupancy addresses the fraction of time or molecules in a particular binding state. Transcription is a process that produces RNA molecules over time. Consequently a model that predicts occupancy is not necessarily sufficient to predict the rate of synthesis. To connect structure with kinetics, consider a deliberately simplified series of steps: a promoter becomes competent for recruitment, forms a closed complex, opens the DNA, initiates RNA and returns to a state from which another productive event can occur. The slow or frequently failing step can limit the output even if other molecular interactions occur readily.

For an elementary model with two irreversible first-order stages in sequence, suppose stage one has rate constant k1 and stage two has rate constant k2. The mean waiting time for a single first-order event is the reciprocal of its rate constant. If each promoter completes the two stages before starting the next cycle and the stages have independent waiting times, the mean cycle duration is T = 1/k1 + 1/k2. The average long-run throughput per promoter is approximately 1/T. This derivation assumes no parallel processing, reverse reactions or aborted cycles. It is a useful demonstration of serial bottlenecks, not a complete kinetic theory of transcription.

Let recruitment occur with k1 = 0.2 s⁻¹ and opening with k2 = 0.05 s⁻¹ in an illustrative model. Their mean waiting times are five seconds and twenty seconds, respectively. The mean duration of the cycle is twenty-five seconds, corresponding to 0.04 productive events per second or 2.4 events per minute. If an activator doubles recruitment to 0.4 s⁻¹ while opening remains unchanged, the cycle duration becomes 22.5 seconds and the rate becomes approximately 2.67 events per minute. If another activator instead doubles opening to 0.10 s⁻¹ while recruitment stays at its original value, the cycle takes fifteen seconds and produces four events per minute. The same fold change in the individual rate constant has different effects on overall output because the original waiting times were different.

The bottleneck can change when the conditions change. A regulator that greatly improves slow recruitment may leave opening as the new dominant delay. Increasing the regulator concentration further may then produce only a small benefit even if its binding is not completely saturated. A graph showing saturation of transcriptional output therefore has more than one possible explanation. The regulatory binding site may be nearly fully occupied, the basal machinery may be limiting, another transition may be slow, or an assay may have reached its detection limit. Measuring occupancy and rates separately helps distinguish these cases.

Several activators can act at a promoter. In a simple independent-binding model with two sites, if the probability that factor A occupies its site is p and the probability that factor B occupies its own site is q, the probability that both are bound at the same time is pq. If p = 0.6 and q = 0.5, the double-bound fraction is 0.30. The probability that at least one is bound is p + q − pq = 0.80. A promoter that requires both regulatory contacts for efficient transcription behaves differently from one that responds when either factor is present. The first rule resembles an AND gate and the second resembles an OR gate, but biological behavior can be graded rather than perfectly digital.

Cooperativity changes these relationships. If binding of factor A creates a favorable interaction surface for factor B, the presence of A can increase B's affinity for the nearby site. The occupancy of the double-bound state may then exceed what would be expected from an independent-binding calculation under comparable conditions. Yet a transcriptional response greater than the sum of two individual responses does not necessarily demonstrate cooperative binding to DNA. The two factors might bind independently but act on different rate-limiting steps of initiation, or one may improve accessibility while the other recruits machinery. Such cooperation in transcriptional output is mechanistically distinct from direct thermodynamic cooperativity in DNA binding.

A Hill-type response is sometimes used to describe how the occupied fraction or measured response changes with ligand or regulator concentration. A common empirical form is f = Cⁿ/(Kⁿ + Cⁿ), where C is concentration, K is the concentration producing half of the modeled maximal response and n is the Hill coefficient. When n exceeds one, the curve can be steeper around its midpoint than a simple single-site binding curve. The value of n should not automatically be interpreted as the exact number of regulator molecules bound to the promoter. It is a measure of the response shape in the model, and the molecular origin of the steepness requires further evidence.

These examples illustrate a central examination skill: translate a molecular diagram into states, probabilities and transitions, but do not confuse the mathematical simplification with the complete biological system. A closed complex with long residence time may generate fewer transcripts than a rapidly cycling complex, even though it produces a strong occupancy measurement. A short burst of transcription may produce substantial RNA while the promoter is occupied only intermittently. Knowing which experimental variable represents time, abundance, fraction or rate is necessary for deciding which mathematical relationship can legitimately be used.
