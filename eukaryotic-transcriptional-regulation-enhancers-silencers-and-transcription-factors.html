1. The problem of selective transcription

A differentiated cell does not generally discard the genes that it does not express. A skeletal muscle nucleus retains genes needed in other tissues, even though its own transcriptional program favors proteins for contraction and associated metabolism. The problem is therefore to explain how a nearly shared genome supports distinct patterns of RNA synthesis. Eukaryotic transcriptional regulation supplies a selective interface between the information encoded in DNA and the molecular conditions within a nucleus. It determines which transcription units receive productive polymerase activity, when that activity changes, and how strongly it responds to environmental or internal signals.

The expression of a gene is not synonymous with the presence of its protein product. Transcription creates RNA; processing, RNA turnover, translation and protein turnover can subsequently alter the quantity of functional product. In this chapter, transcriptional regulation means a change in the production of RNA from a DNA template, including changes in the frequency of productive initiation or the fraction of initiated polymerases that proceed into useful transcription. A measured increase in a mature RNA concentration is suggestive but insufficient by itself, because a reduction in degradation could produce the same increase. This distinction is particularly important when evaluating experiments designed to identify enhancers.

The common machinery that transcribes protein-coding genes is already present in many cell types. If this machinery were enough to make every gene equally active, a hepatocyte would express the same program as a neuron. The necessary additional information resides partly in the regulatory DNA adjacent to or distant from individual transcription units, and partly in the available proteins that interpret that DNA. Both conditions are required. A nucleus that contains an appropriate activator can nevertheless fail to transcribe a particular gene if its recognition sequence has been damaged. Conversely, a perfectly intact regulatory sequence can remain ineffective if the corresponding activator is missing, inactive or unable to reach that sequence.

This distributed arrangement also provides an economical means of coordination. A single regulatory protein can influence many genes carrying its recognition motif, while any one gene can receive input from several proteins. The first property permits coherent cellular responses; the second permits selective responses even when a signal is common to many tissues. For example, a hormone-activated factor can be present in several cell types, yet different genes respond in each because other necessary regulators or accessible target sequences differ. Transcriptional control is therefore a problem of combining addresses with molecular inputs, not simply a universal switch that turns all genes on or off.

A gene may be weakly transcribed in a condition conventionally described as off. Regulatory terms are normally comparisons rather than absolute statements. Activation means an increase relative to a defined baseline; repression means a decrease relative to a defined comparison state. A gene can be activated by one factor and simultaneously restrained by another. Its measured output represents the combined consequences of both activities. A useful examination strategy is to specify what is measured, what the comparison is, and which regulatory step is being changed before assigning the label activation or repression.

2. How experiments revealed modular regulation

Early models of gene regulation benefited from bacterial genetics, where a regulatory protein could be linked to a specific DNA sequence through the behavior of mutants. Eukaryotic systems presented a further difficulty: a gene could depend on a sequence located well away from the point where RNA synthesis began. An experiment had to distinguish DNA that specified the starting position from DNA that increased the amount of transcription. An independently movable regulatory sequence was a particularly informative test, since a requirement for fixed local geometry would predict that moving the sequence abolished its effect.

In 1981, Julian Banerji, Sandro Rusconi and Walter Schaffner investigated the effect of regulatory sequences from simian virus 40 on a linked beta-globin gene. The study demonstrated that DNA segments derived from SV40 could increase expression from the globin construct even when positioned remotely. This was not evidence that all regulatory sequences function equally at all distances. It established that the location of an activating sequence need not coincide with the location of the transcription start site. The distinction became central to the concept of an enhancer. In a teaching reconstruction of the logic, a reference construct contains an intact promoter, whereas the test construct additionally contains the candidate activating sequence. Comparing RNA originating at the same promoter distinguishes increased output from an unintended shift in the site of initiation.

A different question concerned the regulatory proteins. Did the surface that recognized a DNA sequence also necessarily perform the work that stimulated transcription? In 1985 Roger Brent and Mark Ptashne reported a hybrid protein that combined the DNA recognition property of bacterial LexA with the activation function of the yeast protein GAL4. In yeast the hybrid stimulated a gene when an appropriate LexA recognition sequence was present near its start site. The central inference was that target recognition and transcriptional activation could be combined as distinct functions within a protein. The bacterial DNA-binding portion was not intrinsically destined to cause repression; when coupled to a eukaryotic activation function, its sequence specificity could direct a positive regulatory effect.

This hybrid-protein experiment is stronger than a mere correlation between protein binding and transcription. It changes the recognition specificity while retaining an activation function and examines whether the target sequence changes accordingly. If activation occurred equally without the cognate DNA sequence, the result would not support recruitment of activation through a specific address. If the fusion failed to bind DNA, an absence of transcription would not distinguish defective recognition from a failure of the activation domain. This logic illustrates why domain-specific mutations require separate measurements of DNA binding and transcription output.

A third line of work asked whether activators needed an intervening component to affect RNA polymerase II. In 1990 Kelleher, Flanagan and Kornberg reported biochemical evidence for a mediating activity in a yeast transcription system. In 1991 Flanagan and colleagues demonstrated a requirement for an additional mediator activity for stimulation by particular activators in vitro. The distinction between basal transcription and enhanced transcription was essential: a system able to produce RNA in the absence of an activator did not necessarily possess everything needed to respond to one. Biochemical reconstitution made it possible to separate these two capacities. These findings helped establish the concept of a multiprotein Mediator complex rather than the idea that every sequence-specific activator must directly contact the catalytic center of polymerase.

The historical findings answer complementary questions. Remote DNA experiments separated regulatory position from the transcription start site; hybrid factors separated sequence recognition from the function imposed at that address; biochemical experiments separated basal activity from regulated activity. Together they explain why a eukaryotic regulatory region is best analyzed as a collection of functional parts whose contributions can be tested separately. They do not establish that any chosen domain, enhancer or cofactor will operate in every possible gene context. That limitation becomes an important principle when moving from artificial constructs to endogenous chromosomal genes.

3. Regulatory DNA as a set of addresses

Coordinates of a transcription unit are normally described relative to a defined transcription start site, designated +1. Positions upstream in the direction opposite transcription have negative coordinates and positions downstream have positive coordinates. Upstream and downstream are therefore properties of the orientation of a gene, not of the left and right sides of a drawing. A gene transcribed toward lower chromosome coordinates has its upstream region on the higher-coordinate side. This matters when interpreting diagrams that place a regulatory sequence between two genes with opposite transcription directions.

The core promoter is a DNA region involved in directing initiation at a particular site or range of sites. Different promoters contain different combinations of recognizable motifs, and some lack a recognizable TATA box. The basal initiation machinery discussed earlier in the curriculum carries out the actual formation of a transcription complex. The present regulatory question is how the activity of that machinery is varied at different promoters. A proximal regulatory element is a recognition site situated relatively near the promoter, for example a binding motif for a factor that stimulates transcription under particular conditions. The exact boundary between proximal and distal should not be treated as a universal fixed number of base pairs.

An enhancer is a functional DNA element that can increase transcription from a compatible promoter under suitable conditions. It may reside upstream, downstream or within an intron of a gene. Its regulatory effect may survive reversal of its orientation or relocation in a reporter construct, a classical property that differentiates many enhancers from directional promoters. This flexibility does not make all internal sequences of an enhancer interchangeable. Its individual motifs may require particular spacing, orientation and cooperative interactions, and the overall effect may depend on chromosomal surroundings.

A silencer is a regulatory DNA element that decreases the expression of a linked gene in a given context. The term describes the function of the DNA rather than a particular protein structure. In one cell type it may bind a repressor that is absent in another. A silencer need not overlap the core promoter, and it need not cause a permanent change in gene activity. Conversely, a sequence that fails to activate a reporter is not necessarily a silencer: an inhibitory effect must be demonstrated relative to a suitable construct lacking the candidate sequence.

The same DNA sequence can have different regulatory consequences in distinct contexts. A motif that recruits a protein capable of interacting with an activation partner in one cell might recruit a different complex in another. Functional classification is therefore based on a specified promoter, cell, condition and comparison. It is scientifically useful to distinguish a short recognition motif from an entire enhancer: the motif is a molecular binding address, whereas the enhancer is the region whose aggregate regulatory activity has been demonstrated. Destroying one motif can leave the enhancer partly active if other motifs continue to recruit functional regulators.

Cis and trans describe the relationship of a regulatory determinant to its target, not the distance between them. A conventional enhancer acts in cis because its effect is exerted on a linked transcription unit. A diffusible transcription factor acts in trans because the protein made from one gene copy can act at eligible sites on another DNA molecule or homologous chromosome. A gene encoding a trans-acting factor itself has cis-regulatory sequences. A mutation in the factor's own promoter may therefore first change expression of that factor in cis and subsequently alter expression of numerous target genes in trans. The distinction requires tracing the causal chain rather than labeling an entire chromosome cis or trans.

Suppose a diploid cell contains two distinguishable copies of gene X. A regulatory mutation removes an essential enhancer motif from one allele but leaves the other allele unaltered. In the simplest model, the mutant allele loses the response while the normal allele retains it, even though both encounter the same nuclear proteins. Supplying additional normal activator does not recreate a deleted recognition sequence. In contrast, if a missing diffusible factor is responsible for the reduced output of both alleles, restoring a functional copy of the factor may restore both responses. A dominant negative protein may complicate rescue by interfering with the normal factor; such an outcome does not invalidate the underlying cis–trans distinction.

4. How transcription factors read DNA

A transcription factor must distinguish its relevant targets from an enormous background of DNA. Binding specificity arises through noncovalent molecular interactions, not through synthesis of a complementary nucleic acid strand. The major groove of double-stranded DNA exposes distinctive arrangements of chemical groups associated with base-pair identities. An appropriately positioned amino acid side chain can form a hydrogen bond or make a favorable van der Waals contact with a particular arrangement. Contacts with the negatively charged phosphate backbone contribute binding energy and orientation, whereas differences in the exposed bases and local DNA shape contribute discrimination among sequences.

The distinction between sequence-specific and nonspecific DNA binding is one of relative preference. Many sequence-specific proteins can associate weakly with other DNA sequences, and their recognition motifs often tolerate substitutions at some positions. A consensus motif is a summary of favored sequence features rather than a guarantee that every sequence matching it will be occupied in a living nucleus. Neighboring bases can modify DNA shape, another protein can compete for an overlapping sequence, and the location can be inaccessible because of packaging. Thus a motif prediction is a hypothesis about possible recognition, not proof of physiological regulation.

Different protein folds solve the recognition problem in different ways. A homeodomain contains a helix-turn-helix arrangement in which a recognition helix typically contributes contacts within the major groove, with other regions providing additional interactions. A classical C2H2 zinc finger is stabilized by coordination of a zinc ion by conserved cysteine and histidine residues; the resulting folded region can present an alpha helix for DNA recognition. Multiple fingers can contribute recognition of a longer sequence. Zinc is a structural contributor in this example rather than a chemical reagent consumed when a factor binds DNA.

The basic leucine zipper family illustrates the distinction between recognition and dimer formation. A leucine zipper promotes association of protein helices, whereas adjacent basic regions contribute DNA contacts. AP-1 family proteins are examples of this general architecture. Changing the dimerization surface may prevent formation of a functional DNA-binding assembly even when the basic residues remain unchanged. The basic helix-loop-helix family similarly combines a basic recognition region with an association interface, but its folding arrangement is different from a leucine zipper. In both cases, changing the available partner can change the set of complexes and consequently their sequence preferences or regulatory effects.

Many nuclear receptors contain a DNA-binding domain with cysteine-coordinated zinc modules, together with regions that respond to ligands and interact with regulatory proteins. These modules are structurally distinct from the classical C2H2 fingers. It is therefore incorrect to assume that all proteins described as zinc-finger factors recognize DNA by identical geometry. The glucocorticoid receptor provides a representative connection between ligand-dependent regulation and sequence recognition. Its abundance, ligand-dependent activity, genomic binding and recruitment of regulatory proteins are distinct quantities; any one of them can limit a transcriptional response.

An activation domain is a region of a factor that interacts with components capable of stimulating transcription. A repression domain similarly helps recruit or influence machinery that reduces output. Such regions are not always independently folded like classical DNA-binding domains, and interactions can involve distributed short motifs and flexible protein segments. A single protein may also contain nuclear localization sequences or regulated modification sites. This modularity makes it possible for two mutations in the same factor to produce very different phenotypes. A recognition mutation may abolish target binding; a change in the activation region may leave binding intact but remove the transcriptional response.

The quantitative basis of binding is favorable free energy. Specific contacts, displacement of water, electrostatic interactions and changes in protein or DNA conformation all contribute to the overall free-energy difference between bound and unbound states. Formation of a complex does not automatically require ATP hydrolysis. ATP-dependent processes can change accessibility or sustain a non-equilibrium transcription cycle, but equilibrium DNA recognition can occur without an energy-consuming chemical reaction. A useful experiment distinguishes the ability to bind an exposed DNA target in a purified system from the ability to access and regulate that target inside a nucleus.

5. Activation, coactivators and Mediator

A general transcription factor contributes to the core transcription system at many RNA polymerase II promoters. A gene-specific factor instead recognizes particular regulatory sequences or otherwise supplies gene-selective input. The distinction is functional, not a statement about whether the factor is present in one tissue or many. A broadly expressed factor such as Sp1 can still recognize particular motifs and contribute differently to different promoters. Conversely, altering a general factor may affect many genes without reducing every gene's transcription to the same degree, since promoters differ in their dependencies.

Consider a target promoter at which productive initiation is infrequent. An activator binds its cognate regulatory DNA and presents a surface that can recruit additional regulatory proteins. This interaction can stabilize an initiation-competent assembly, increase the probability of productive initiation or facilitate other rate-limiting steps in transcription. The activator has not itself copied DNA into RNA. It has altered the likelihood that the RNA polymerase II system performs that work. Removal of the binding site breaks the targeting step; mutation of the interaction surface can break the communication step; loss of a necessary downstream cofactor can break the response even when targeting and binding remain intact.

A coactivator is a regulatory protein or complex that assists activation, often through interaction with sequence-specific factors rather than through recognition of the same DNA motif. Some coactivators provide interaction surfaces for multiple proteins; others possess catalytic functions that alter the local chromatin environment. The same broad functional class therefore includes proteins with different molecular activities. CBP and p300, for instance, are coactivators with acetyltransferase activity as well as protein interaction functions. Their enzymatic contribution can support accessibility or change protein interactions, but detailed histone modification mechanisms belong to the subsequent chromatin regulation chapter.

Mediator is a multisubunit complex that helps transmit regulatory information to the RNA polymerase II machinery. A sequence-specific factor can interact with one or more Mediator components, while other surfaces communicate with the initiation apparatus. Thinking of it as a communication platform is more accurate than treating it as a single soluble enzyme that recognizes every enhancer motif. Mediator does not replace the general factors that carry out promoter-dependent initiation. It helps regulate transcription in response to factors that supply information about the particular gene or condition.

An important distinction is between recruitment and activation. Recruitment increases the association of a component with a regulatory site or promoter. Activation is an increase in productive transcription. If a mutant activator recruits polymerase but fails to promote a subsequent rate-limiting transition, polymerase occupancy may be high despite low RNA synthesis. Similarly, a factor that improves the effectiveness of already recruited polymerase could raise transcription without producing a large change in measured promoter occupancy. An assay that measures binding of polymerase and an assay that measures newly synthesized RNA answer different questions.

In a simplified reconstitution experiment, basal transcription machinery and promoter DNA generate a small amount of RNA. Addition of an activator alone fails to raise output, but addition of a missing coactivator restores the response only when the activator and its cognate binding site are present. This hypothetical result supports the requirement of a communication component under the tested conditions. An essential control is the coactivator in the absence of activator; otherwise a general increase in enzyme activity might be mistaken for selective communication. Another control measures basal transcription after depletion of the coactivator to distinguish regulatory failure from total collapse of the transcription system.

6. Repression, silencers and competing regulatory inputs

Transcriptional repression can occur through several distinct mechanisms. A DNA-bound repressor may compete with an activator for an overlapping sequence, decreasing the fraction of time during which the activator can occupy its target. If neither protein can bind simultaneously, increasing the concentration of one can alter occupancy of the other. This is a mechanism of competition at the targeting step. It does not require the repressor to possess an enzymatic activity or to interact directly with polymerase.

A second form of repression occurs when a protein interferes with the function of an activator even though both are bound to DNA. Such interference is often described as quenching in classical regulatory models. The binding measurements can therefore appear normal while the transcriptional response declines. A third possibility is that a repressor interacts with the basal apparatus or its regulatory partners and reduces productive transcription. These mechanisms can be distinguished experimentally because their predicted changes in occupancy, interaction and transcription are not identical.

A fourth mechanism involves recruitment of corepressors that modify the local molecular environment or inhibit activator function. In appropriate contexts, corepressor complexes can recruit histone deacetylase activity. The consequent changes can reduce the ability of transcription factors or initiation machinery to work efficiently. The sequence-specific repressor supplies targeting; the corepressor supplies a regulatory function. The histone deacetylase need not recognize the silencer motif independently. Detailed chromatin chemistry is reserved for Node 3.D.09; the essential point here is the distinction between targeting and execution of a repressive effect.

The thyroid hormone receptor illustrates a change in regulatory function under different ligand conditions. At certain positively regulated response elements, unliganded receptor can associate with corepressors, whereas hormone binding favors release of those partners and association with coactivators. A DNA-associated receptor can consequently have different effects on the same target as the ligand environment changes. The example is deliberately restricted to well-characterized positively regulated targets, because negative regulation and other nuclear receptor contexts can involve different mechanisms. It is not correct to say that every unliganded nuclear receptor invariably represses every target.

Deleting a silencer can increase basal expression without changing the maximal output when a strong activator is present, or it can broaden expression to an inappropriate cell type. A silencer deletion need not produce a change under all test conditions. If the relevant repressor is absent from a given cell line, both wild-type and silencer-deleted constructs may have the same output. This negative result would not prove that the candidate region has no regulatory function. A test in a repressor-expressing context or a controlled addition of the repressor would be informative.

Repression by an enhancer-directed regulatory mechanism is also distinct from degradation of the mature RNA. If a repressor is recruited and nascent transcription declines before the mature RNA pool changes, the temporal order supports an effect on production. If the amount of newly synthesized RNA is unchanged while mature RNA abundance declines, an explanation involving RNA turnover becomes more plausible. A phenotype described broadly as gene silencing must therefore be localized to the relevant step before a molecular mechanism is assigned.

7. Enhancer–promoter communication in three-dimensional DNA

The distance between two DNA sequences along a chromosome is not identical to their separation in three-dimensional space. A flexible DNA-containing chromatin fiber can bend so that a distal regulatory element approaches a promoter. A DNA loop is a useful conceptual representation of this spatial arrangement. Proteins bound at the two regions may interact directly or through other components, allowing the regulatory information at a distant element to influence a promoter without moving the transcription start site.

Looping explains why certain activating sequences retain function when placed far from the promoter and why a simple rule based on adjacent base pairs is inadequate. It does not imply that distal activity is independent of distance under all conditions. The probability that two chromosomal segments approach one another depends on their separation, structural environment and the proteins associated with them. A promoter may be compatible with one enhancer but not another, and an enhancer can skip the nearest promoter. The regulatory target of an enhancer must therefore be established functionally rather than assigned solely from a genome map.

A classical diagram places an activator on an enhancer and a coactivator near the promoter, with the intervening DNA drawn as a loop. This diagram expresses a possible spatial mechanism, not a requirement that every transcription event must occur in an identical stable architecture. Regulatory contacts can be transient; the frequency, duration and functional consequences of contacts may differ among genes. Some contacts can exist before activation and persist during a change in transcriptional output. Consequently, the detection of a physical association is evidence of proximity but not, by itself, proof that an enhancer causes transcription.

Imagine a chromosomal interval containing a distal enhancer between two promoters. In a particular tissue the enhancer increases transcription of gene A while gene B shows little response. Both genes may be equally close in linear distance. A mutation that eliminates the promoter element required for A's response can remove activation of A without necessarily increasing B. This result is compatible with promoter specificity: the enhancer is not simply a source of a freely spreading activation signal. To evaluate the alternative explanation that gene B lacks an essential transcription factor, the experiment must characterize B's basal activity and its response to an independently functional regulatory input.

Boundary elements can restrict communication between some regulatory regions. A classical enhancer-blocking assay asks whether inserting a candidate element between an enhancer and promoter decreases enhancer-dependent transcription while leaving a suitable basal promoter control functional. A candidate that inhibits when placed anywhere in the construct may instead be a general repressive sequence. Neither a boundary nor an enhancer has to behave identically in every chromosomal neighborhood. The basic functional distinction is sufficient here; detailed chromosome architecture is addressed elsewhere in the curriculum.

A chromosome conformation assay can support a model of regulatory proximity by measuring how often particular DNA regions are recovered together following a method designed to capture nearby chromosomal segments. The result is a population-level measure with technical biases. It does not provide a direct movie of every cell, and a change in measured contact frequency is not automatically a change in causal regulation. Pairing a proximity result with a selective enhancer perturbation and a nascent transcription measurement provides stronger reasoning than interpreting either observation alone.

8. Cooperative and combinatorial control

A transcriptional control region can contain multiple regulatory motifs. If each factor recognizes only one class of motif, the combination of bound factors can carry more information than any single factor. Consider a factor A present in liver and muscle and a factor B present in liver and brain. A gene that requires both could be activated preferentially in liver even though neither factor is exclusive to that tissue. The specificity emerges from the intersection of two expression patterns. This is a useful idealization of combinatorial control, rather than a claim that every tissue-specific gene uses a strict Boolean gate.

Cooperative binding is a molecular phenomenon in which association of one factor makes association of another more favorable. Two factors may interact directly or alter the local DNA conformation so that their joint occupancy is favored. Functional synergy describes a combined transcriptional output greater than an explicitly defined additive expectation. The two concepts are related but not equivalent. Independently bound proteins can act synergistically if they promote different rate-limiting steps, and cooperatively bound proteins need not produce a super-additive transcription response if downstream machinery is saturated.

Suppose an enhancer contains two activator motifs and the basal promoter produces ten arbitrary units of nascent RNA per fixed interval. Factor A alone increases output to thirty units and B alone increases output to forty units. The increments above baseline are twenty and thirty units. An additive prediction for both is therefore sixty units, not seventy. If both factors produce one hundred units, the observed excess above that additive prediction is forty. The result demonstrates functional synergy relative to this additive model, but it does not identify the molecular origin. Measuring binding when each factor is present alone and when both are present can test a cooperative occupancy hypothesis.

Conversely, two inputs may produce less than the additive prediction without one necessarily repressing the other. If both recruit the same limiting coactivator, increasing both can lead to saturation. A promoter that cannot initiate above a fixed maximum also exhibits subadditive output when inputs approach that limit. A student should distinguish a direct inhibitory interaction from a shared ceiling on a positive process. In a response matrix, comparison of both inputs with basal and single-input conditions provides the first evidence; perturbing the relevant cofactor or comparing output across input concentrations can then refine the mechanism.

Combinatorial control can also incorporate repressors. If an activator is present in multiple tissues but a repressor is present in one subset, expression may occur wherever activation is present without the opposing repressive condition. Such a regulatory region can be represented by an activation requirement combined with a NOT condition. When both inputs are graded rather than binary, the actual output depends on relative occupancy, activation and repression strength, and other context. Boolean representations are most useful for idealized extreme conditions and should not be extended to quantitative predictions without an appropriate model.
