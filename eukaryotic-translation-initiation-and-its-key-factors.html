1. Why translation needs a controlled beginning

Imagine a cell that has transcribed a gene, removed its introns and exported the mature messenger RNA to the cytoplasm. The RNA carries an ordered nucleotide sequence, but a protein does not appear simply because that sequence is present. The ribosome must locate the appropriate beginning of the protein-coding region, align the RNA correctly and establish the first amino acid before it can use the genetic code repeatedly. These preparatory events constitute translation initiation. The beginning is not merely the first nucleotide of the RNA, nor is it necessarily the first occurrence of the letters AUG. It is a molecular decision that determines which triplets will subsequently be interpreted as codons.

Consider a messenger beginning with a segment of twenty nucleotides that is not meant to encode the main protein. If a ribosome were to divide the message into triplets beginning at nucleotide one, its groupings would generally differ from those intended by the gene. A shift of one nucleotide changes almost every downstream triplet; a shift by three nucleotides preserves the frame but changes where the protein begins. Initiation consequently defines both the identity of the first residue and the reading frame. Later proofreading cannot usually repair an incorrectly chosen start, because the subsequent codons may be perfectly legitimate in the wrong frame. Establishing the frame is therefore a fundamentally different accuracy problem from selecting an aminoacyl-tRNA during elongation.

In a typical eukaryotic cell, the main messenger is synthesized and processed in the nucleus, whereas the majority of its translation takes place on ribosomes in the cytoplasm, including ribosomes associated with the cytosolic face of the endoplasmic reticulum. The initiation machinery recognizes properties of the mature RNA rather than binding a promoter in chromosomal DNA. RNA polymerase and transcription factors select a transcriptional start site. Ribosomes and translation initiation factors select a translational start site. The two sites may be separated by many nucleotides. A mutation in a promoter may change the amount of messenger made; a mutation around an initiation codon may instead change the fraction of available messengers that produce a protein. A measurement of total protein alone cannot distinguish these explanations.

Eukaryotic cytosolic ribosomes consist of a small 40S subunit and a large 60S subunit. Their assembled particle is called 80S. The small subunit has the principal responsibility for positioning messenger RNA and checking its interaction with transfer RNA. The large subunit contributes the catalytic centre at which peptide bonds are formed. During initiation these tasks are arranged in a useful sequence: a small subunit, assisted by soluble factors, selects the start before the large subunit joins it. If a complete ribosome were assembled prematurely without an appropriate messenger and initiator tRNA, the resulting particle would not automatically possess a useful reading frame. Delaying subunit joining makes the decoding apparatus available for a search step and helps couple complete ribosome formation to successful recognition.

The starting amino acid in the standard pathway is methionine attached to a specialized initiator transfer RNA. This tRNA is not merely an elongator tRNA arriving a little earlier than usual. It has structural features that permit interaction with initiation factors and placement in the ribosomal P site. The distinction is needed because the first tRNA establishes the donor side of the first peptide bond, whereas later incoming aminoacyl-tRNAs normally enter the A site. The eukaryotic cell therefore coordinates three sorts of information: the chemical identity of the initiating amino acid, the location of a suitable start codon, and the spatial arrangement of messenger and tRNA within the ribosome.

The molecules assisting this coordination are collectively called eukaryotic initiation factors, abbreviated eIFs. Some recognize RNA ends, others bind nucleotide triphosphates, and still others influence ribosome conformation or link components into complexes. Their names are historical identifiers and should not be interpreted as a simple numerical order of action. The factor eIF4E acts on the messenger end, whereas eIF2 supplies the initiator tRNA; their preparatory reactions can occur in parallel. Understanding the pathway as cooperating branches that converge on a small ribosomal subunit is more accurate than imagining a single file of factors arriving in numerical sequence.

The pathway also explains why initiation is an effective point at which to control protein output. If fewer ribosomes begin, fewer new chains enter the subsequent elongation cycle. The cell need not stop every ribosome already synthesizing a chain to reduce the rate of production. This observation will become useful when translational regulation is considered separately. For now the aim is to establish what a normal initiation complex contains, how it locates a start codon, and how it becomes competent for the first elongation reaction.

2. The architecture of the messenger and the significance of its ends

A conventional mature nuclear protein-coding messenger can be described from its five-prime end to its three-prime end. At the beginning is a modified cap, followed by a leader region called the five-prime untranslated region, commonly abbreviated 5′ UTR. The main coding sequence begins at a translation initiation codon and ends at a termination codon. Downstream is a three-prime untranslated region, often followed by a polyadenylate tail. This arrangement does not imply that every nucleotide outside the principal coding region is dispensable. Both untranslated regions contain information that influences the way the RNA interacts with cellular proteins, and some leaders contain short coding regions of their own. The term untranslated region is used relative to the main coding sequence.

The five-prime cap of a typical RNA polymerase II messenger contains 7-methylguanosine, written m7G, attached to the first transcribed nucleotide through an unusual five-prime to five-prime triphosphate linkage. The linkage distinguishes the cap from an ordinary guanosine residue within an RNA strand, whose neighbours are connected by conventional three-prime to five-prime phosphodiester bonds. The methylated base and linkage provide a distinctive chemical surface for cap-binding proteins. The mature cap is not a codon and does not contribute a guanosine to the protein sequence. Its importance for initiation lies in providing a molecular recognition feature at a defined end of the message.

The cap participates in more than one biological process. Nuclear cap-binding proteins can contribute to processing and export, whereas eIF4E is a central cap-binding component in the standard cytosolic initiation mechanism. These activities are related because they act on the same chemical feature, but the complexes and biological tasks are different. It is therefore imprecise to describe every cap-binding protein as a translation initiation factor. The identity of the protein currently occupying the cap, its interaction partners, and the cellular compartment all matter. A mature capped RNA may remain untranslated temporarily if the relevant cytoplasmic machinery cannot assemble on it.

The leader connects the cap to the intended initiation site. Its sequence, length and tendency to fold into secondary structure influence the accessibility of the route followed by a scanning small subunit. RNA is a flexible polymer, but complementary stretches can form stems through intramolecular base pairing. A hairpin consists of a paired stem and an unpaired loop. Stable paired regions can obstruct access to a segment or increase the work needed for the scanning complex to progress. A long leader is not necessarily inhibitory if it is accessible, and a short leader is not necessarily efficient if its start codon is too close to the end or if the region is highly structured. Sequence arrangement matters more than length considered in isolation.

A poly(A) tail is a run of adenylate residues appended at the three-prime end of many mature eukaryotic messages. Cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein associates with this tail and can interact with the initiation scaffold eIF4G. Through such interactions the two ends of an RNA can be brought into functional proximity, a relationship often called the closed-loop model. This is not a covalently circular RNA, and the model need not imply a rigid circle present at every instant. The five-prime and three-prime ends remain chemically distinct. Their association can favour initiation and reinforce productive interactions between the messenger and initiation machinery.

To isolate the role of the cap experimentally, one can compare two otherwise identical synthetic messenger RNAs that differ only in cap status. If their amounts are equal throughout an assay and the capped RNA produces more protein, the cap has enhanced a step between RNA availability and protein production. Even this controlled comparison does not by itself establish whether cap recognition or an indirect alteration in RNA structure is the immediate cause. A complementary experiment that reduces eIF4E activity and selectively removes the capped RNA's advantage would provide stronger mechanistic evidence. If the uncapped RNA also disappears much faster, the difference in total output combines initiation efficiency with RNA stability. Measuring intact RNA at relevant time points is essential.

There are genuine biological exceptions to the conventional end structure. Replication-dependent animal histone messenger RNAs, for example, usually terminate in a specialized stem-loop rather than a conventional poly(A) tail. Other messages may be translated by routes that lessen their dependence on the five-prime cap. The common cap and tail arrangement should therefore be understood as an effective and widely used architecture, not a universal chemical definition of a translatable RNA. A feature can improve translation without being logically indispensable for every messenger in every organism.

3. Methionine, the initiator tRNA and the first P-site occupant

The genetic code assigns AUG to methionine in the standard coding system. An AUG located inside an already translated coding region is ordinarily read as methionine during elongation, while an AUG selected as the start has a second role in establishing the beginning of a chain. Both events rely on complementary recognition by a methionine tRNA, but the molecules that deliver methionine in the two stages are functionally distinct. The initiator is often written tRNAiMet when uncharged and Met-tRNAiMet after aminoacylation. An elongator methionine tRNA has a related anticodon but different structural properties and interacts with the elongation delivery apparatus.

The aminoacylation reaction is performed by methionyl-tRNA synthetase. Methionine first reacts with ATP at the enzyme to form an enzyme-associated methionyl-adenylate intermediate and pyrophosphate. Transfer of the activated amino acid to the terminal adenosine of tRNA produces the aminoacyl-tRNA and releases AMP. This uses one ATP molecule converted to AMP, conventionally counted as two high-energy phosphate-bond equivalents when pyrophosphate hydrolysis is included. The resulting ester bond stores chemical energy used during the later transfer of the nascent peptide. The initiation factors do not substitute for this synthetase reaction. A shortage of charged initiator tRNA can reduce initiation even when the total number of initiator tRNA molecules is normal.

The anticodon for AUG is conveniently written 3′-UAC-5′ if it is aligned directly beneath the codon 5′-AUG-3′. Written in its own five-prime to three-prime direction it is CAU. This reversal of order is a consequence of antiparallel base pairing, not a second type of methionine anticodon. Pairing recognizes the nucleotide sequence of the messenger, whereas aminoacylation establishes the chemical identity of the residue carried by the tRNA. If a suitably recognized initiator tRNA were artificially mischarged, base pairing alone could not prove that the attached amino acid is methionine. In living cells the usual accuracy of aminoacylation and the selectivity of initiation machinery reduce such errors.

Both initiating and elongating tRNAs must reach the ribosome, yet they enter through different molecular pathways. In the canonical eukaryotic mechanism the initiator tRNA is associated with eIF2 bound to GTP. Most aminoacyl-tRNAs used for elongation are instead delivered by eEF1A bound to GTP. The initiator's structural identity favours its selection by eIF2 and disfavors participation in the ordinary elongation route. A mutation in a tRNA may disrupt factor recognition while leaving its anticodon unchanged. Such a mutation could allow normal charging in an isolated synthetase assay but interfere with initiation inside a cell. Separating assays for charging, factor binding and ribosome recruitment can identify where the defect lies.

The ribosomal P site is named for its association with peptidyl-tRNA during elongation. At the start of translation it contains a single amino acid attached to initiator tRNA rather than an extended peptide. This is not a contradiction. The site provides the location from which the first aminoacyl residue will be transferred when an incoming elongator tRNA arrives at the A site. The mature initiation complex does not yet contain a first peptide bond. The amino group of the A-site amino acid will attack the ester-linked carbonyl of the initiating methionine, creating the first peptide bond and placing the growing dipeptide on the A-site tRNA. These chemical events belong to elongation; their prerequisites are established by initiation.

In ordinary nuclear-encoded cytosolic translation, eukaryotes use methionine without the N-formyl modification characteristic of many bacterial initiators. A bacterial initiator tRNA is initially charged with methionine and may subsequently be formylated to produce fMet-tRNAfMet. This is a modification of the amino group of the already attached amino acid, not an alternative genetic code for the AUG triplet. Certain mitochondria and plastids use organelle-specific translation systems, some of which initiate with formylmethionine. The compartment must therefore be specified when contrasting bacterial and eukaryotic mechanisms. It is also possible for a newly synthesized protein to lose its initiating methionine during maturation. The absence of methionine at the amino terminus of the mature protein does not establish that translation began with a different amino acid.

4. The eIF2 nucleotide cycle and the ternary complex

A charged initiator tRNA has the necessary amino acid, but it still needs to be placed on the ribosome. Eukaryotic initiation factor 2, or eIF2, is a heterotrimer composed of alpha, beta and gamma subunits. The gamma subunit contains the principal guanine nucleotide-binding region and contributes strongly to initiator tRNA recognition. The alpha and beta subunits participate in interactions important for the behaviour of the complete factor. The useful starting state for canonical initiation is eIF2 bound to GTP. In this state the factor binds Met-tRNAi with a sufficiently high affinity to produce a stable transport complex. Since the resulting assembly contains eIF2, GTP and aminoacylated initiator tRNA, it is termed the ternary complex. The designation counts these three functional components; it does not mean that eIF2 is a single polypeptide.

Binding a guanine nucleotide can alter the shape and interaction properties of a protein. This is the basis of the molecular switch behaviour exhibited by many GTPases. GTP has three phosphate groups, whereas GDP has two. Hydrolysis removes the terminal phosphate, producing GDP and inorganic phosphate. The change in chemical state allows a GTPase to occupy a different set of conformations and to bind partners with different affinities. In the case of eIF2, the transition away from its GTP-bound state is connected to the conversion of a scanning complex into a start-selected initiation complex. Before the start is selected, the tRNA must remain available to test messenger triplets. After selection, the tRNA must be retained by the ribosome while the delivery factor is removed. A nucleotide-dependent change in affinity helps make this transfer directional.

A crucial distinction is that GTP hydrolysis and phosphate release need not be simultaneous. Experiments with reconstituted initiation systems support a model in which hydrolysis of GTP associated with eIF2 can take place before complete commitment to a start codon, while the inorganic phosphate remains associated with the complex. Correct start recognition promotes phosphate release and the accompanying conformational changes. A standard simplified description states that recognition of AUG stimulates eIF2 GTP hydrolysis and release. This captures the direction of the pathway but should not be interpreted as establishing the exact timing of every chemical intermediate. For reasoning about mutations, the distinction matters: a factor that permits cleavage but prevents phosphate release could still interfere with commitment to the start even if GTP hydrolysis is detected.

eIF5 promotes the GTPase activity of eIF2. It is a GTPase-activating factor in this reaction, rather than the guanine nucleotide-bearing substrate whose GTP is cleaved. The initiation complex also contains several factors that influence the response to codon recognition, including eIF1 and eIF1A. Their interactions help prevent the loss of scanning competence before an appropriate start is encountered. A simplistic equation showing eIF2 plus GTP forming the ternary complex cannot describe this coordination in full. It is useful for chemical bookkeeping, but the correct reaction occurs in an assembly where RNA recognition and factor rearrangements affect the timing of the nucleotide transition.

After eIF2 is released in its GDP-associated state, it must be recycled before it can deliver another initiator tRNA efficiently. The guanine nucleotide exchange factor eIF2B facilitates removal of bound GDP and permits GTP to occupy the nucleotide-binding site. This process does not add a phosphate to the existing GDP molecule while it remains bound. GDP leaves, and a GTP molecule from the cellular nucleotide pool replaces it. Cellular nucleotide metabolism maintains the pool from which GTP is supplied. Distinguishing nucleotide exchange from nucleotide phosphorylation is important in many areas of cell biology, because an exchange factor acts by altering occupancy rather than directly synthesizing a higher-energy nucleotide.

The availability of total eIF2 is not necessarily equivalent to the availability of active ternary complex. Suppose a cell has a normal concentration of all three eIF2 subunits, but their GDP-to-GTP exchange is inefficient. A large proportion may accumulate in a state unable to deliver initiator tRNA productively. A biochemical measurement of eIF2 abundance alone would miss this defect. A measurement of nucleotide-bound states or ternary-complex formation, together with an assay for start-site assembly, would reveal a more informative relationship. The regulation of this exchange system by phosphorylation is assigned to the later chapter on translation regulation. Its essential mechanistic prerequisite is the existence of a reusable cycle connecting the GTP-bound delivery state with the GDP-bound released state.

A useful analogy is the movement of a loaded carrier between a supply station and a destination. Loading requires a particular carrier state, while release leaves a carrier in a state that must be reset before reuse. Unlike a mechanical carrier, however, eIF2 does not simply transport a passenger into a fixed compartment. The tRNA becomes part of a dynamic ribosomal recognition system, and the identity of the codon influences the transition from searching to commitment. The analogy is valuable only if it preserves this central difference. The chemical energy associated with GTP provides direction and timing for molecular interactions, not the energy stored in the methionine-tRNA ester bond.

5. Preparing the small subunit: construction of the 43S complex

The ternary complex can bind a small ribosomal subunit, but efficient canonical initiation requires several other factors. The assembly containing the 40S subunit, initiator tRNA delivered by eIF2-GTP, eIF1, eIF1A, eIF3 and commonly eIF5 is described as a 43S preinitiation complex. The exact composition and timing of assembly can vary with organism, messenger and experimental conditions. The essential conceptual boundary is that the term refers to a small-subunit preparation for messenger recruitment and searching, not to an elongation-competent ribosome. The 60S subunit is not part of this small-subunit complex. In many descriptions the 43S complex is assembled before stable engagement with the messenger RNA.

The numerical labels originate in sedimentation behaviour and historical biochemical characterization. They should not be used as a mass-addition formula. Adding several factors and one tRNA to a 40S particle does not mean that their individual Svedberg values total three units, just as the assembled 80S ribosome is not a 100S particle. Sedimentation depends on mass, buoyancy and friction, and the shape and interactions of a complex affect how it moves in a centrifuge. The terminology is useful because it separates recognizable functional assemblies. A student should identify them by components and biological state before attempting to interpret a sedimentation profile.

eIF3 is a large multisubunit initiation factor whose precise subunit composition differs between eukaryotic lineages. It associates with the 40S particle and supports the formation and stability of the preinitiation complex. It contributes to productive recruitment of the messenger and helps prevent inappropriate joining of the large subunit at early stages. The prevention of premature association is especially important because the cell contains both free ribosomal subunits and factors interacting with them. eIF3 is not simply a passive spacer between two ribosomal halves; it coordinates several interactions that favour the initiation-competent form of the small subunit. In the standard mammalian model it participates in bridging the small subunit to the cap-associated eIF4G scaffold.

eIF1 is particularly important for fidelity during selection of the start site. It favours a conformation that allows the small subunit to move along the message without prematurely committing to an unsuitable triplet. It also makes inappropriate initiation less likely when the nucleotide context or codon pairing is poor. When a suitable start is selected, the interactions holding eIF1 in the scanning complex change, and its displacement is part of the transition to a more closed, committed assembly. Removing eIF1 need not merely reduce the number of complexes formed. It can alter which start sites are accepted. An experiment that measures total output but does not determine the selected initiation site could therefore fail to detect a major fidelity defect.

eIF1A, despite its similar name, is a different protein with its own ribosomal binding position and functions. It occupies a region associated with the small-subunit A site and helps stabilize the organization of the preinitiation complex. Its interactions support scanning and later steps of start recognition and subunit joining. The factor's function changes in relation to the state of the complex; it does not merely plug the A site permanently. Before elongation can commence, the A site must become available for the next aminoacyl-tRNA. eIF1A remains associated during late assembly events and is released as the initiation machinery completes the transition to an elongation-competent 80S ribosome.

The two factors illustrate an important point about a molecular assembly: different steps can require different affinities and geometries. A form that is ideal for scanning should be able to change after codon selection. An assembly locked in the initial configuration could reach an appropriate AUG yet fail to commit to it; an assembly locked in the later configuration could commit to unsuitable sites or fail to search effectively. Mutations can therefore have opposite effects even when they affect the same factor. A loss-of-function mutation and an alteration that stabilizes one conformation are not necessarily equivalent. Exam questions may describe one mutant as favouring a closed state and another as favouring an open state, in which case the appropriate prediction follows from the specific state rather than the general name of the protein.

Not every small subunit recovered after cell disruption will retain all its normal initiation factors. Dilution, changes in salt concentration, loss of nucleotides and prolonged isolation can alter the equilibrium between bound and unbound states. A biochemical fraction containing a small ribosomal subunit should not automatically be labelled 43S just because initiation factors are detectable elsewhere in the sample. Demonstrating that the relevant molecules associate in the same complex requires additional evidence, such as co-sedimentation under suitable conditions, specific binding measurements or an assay for initiation competence. In vitro reconstruction using purified components is useful because the contribution of each factor can be tested systematically, but the resulting minimal system may omit interactions that enhance the pathway inside a living cell.

6. eIF4F and preparation of the messenger for recruitment

While the small ribosomal subunit acquires initiation factors and initiator tRNA, the messenger RNA is prepared for productive engagement. The classical cap-associated complex eIF4F contains three principal components: eIF4E, eIF4G and eIF4A. The three names distinguish proteins whose roles are complementary. eIF4E recognizes the methylated guanosine cap; eIF4G acts as a scaffold linking several interacting proteins; and eIF4A is an ATP-dependent RNA helicase that helps overcome RNA secondary structure. Together they allow a chemical feature at the beginning of the messenger to influence the position and recruitment of a much larger ribonucleoprotein complex. The designation eIF4F refers to the combined complex, not to another single cap-binding polypeptide that replaces eIF4E.

The cap-binding pocket of eIF4E recognizes the distinctive modified guanosine of the cap. Interactions involving the aromatic surfaces and chemical groups of the cap contribute to specificity. It would be disadvantageous if eIF4E bound every guanosine inside an ordinary transcript with the same affinity as its methylated cap, because the internal nucleotide sequence would no longer provide a reliable distinction between the five-prime end and other locations. By recognizing an end-associated chemical structure, eIF4E provides an anchoring point. The protein does not by itself identify the AUG start site. It has no requirement to base-pair with an initiator anticodon, and its correct binding to a cap does not demonstrate that the correct translation start will later be selected.

The scaffold eIF4G has interaction regions for other initiation proteins. In a widely used mammalian model these include eIF4E, eIF4A, eIF3 and poly(A)-binding protein. By bringing cap recognition, local RNA remodelling and small-subunit recruitment into the same functional assembly, eIF4G increases the probability that the mRNA-bound machinery will interact with a prepared ribosomal subunit. A scaffold need not catalyse a reaction to have a large effect on pathway efficiency. If two factors diffuse freely, their productive encounter depends on their concentrations and binding orientations. Connecting them through a scaffold can raise their effective local concentration and orient them favourably. Disrupting the connection can therefore impair initiation even when the cap-binding and ATPase activities of the individual proteins remain normal.

eIF4A belongs to the DEAD-box family of RNA-dependent ATPases. It acts on RNA structure through repeated nucleotide-dependent binding and remodelling reactions, often assisted by eIF4G and auxiliary proteins such as eIF4B. The name helicase expresses its ability to disrupt RNA pairing, but it is unwise to picture eIF4A as a solitary processive motor that travels continuously from the cap to AUG, consuming exactly one ATP for each nucleotide crossed. DEAD-box proteins can promote local strand separation; the scanning apparatus is a cooperative assembly whose behaviour depends on the structure of the RNA and the factors present. The exam-level consequence is that ATP-dependent RNA remodelling makes recruitment and scanning more effective on many structured messengers.

A paired RNA stem reduces the accessibility of the nucleotides involved in its base pairs. Although the ribosome and associated proteins can disrupt some structures, more stable or awkwardly positioned stems can obstruct productive assembly. The effect depends on position. A stable structure immediately downstream of the cap may reduce the opportunity for the small subunit to engage a suitable region; a stable structure farther down the leader may permit initial recruitment but impede movement towards the start. Both cases can lower total protein output, but an assay that distinguishes recruitment from start-site occupancy may separate them. A cap-bound eIF4F complex detected on an RNA does not establish that scanning succeeded, because the cap-associated factors can perform their first interactions without guaranteeing completion of the search.

eIF4B is an accessory initiation factor that stimulates the RNA-remodelling activity associated with eIF4A. It is not one of the three defining core components of eIF4F. This distinction is often tested because the letters in the names make it tempting to assume that any eIF4-family protein belongs to the same core complex. A practical way to remember the division is to identify the three central molecular tasks: the cap-binding activity of E, the organizing role of G and the ATP-dependent RNA remodelling role of A. The auxiliary factor B helps the RNA-remodelling process. Other eIF4-related proteins exist, but a catalogue of their isoforms is not needed to understand the ordinary canonical pathway.

Cap-dependent recruitment is often presented as a sequence in which eIF4E first binds the cap and then acquires the remaining factors. That is a useful diagram of functional relationships, but it does not establish that the proteins must always assemble in that exact chronological order. Components can associate in solution, exchange partners, and interact with the RNA in several orders. What defines productive activation is the availability of the connected activities at the appropriate region of the messenger. A biochemical experiment that finds preformed eIF4F in solution is therefore compatible with the cap-recruitment model. It is the topology of the interactions and the resulting competence for recruitment that matter most.

7. Recruitment and the physical meaning of scanning

Two preparations now converge: a small ribosomal subunit carrying initiation factors and initiator tRNA, and a messenger whose cap-associated proteins can support recruitment. The cap-proximal region must be accessible enough for the messenger to engage the small subunit. In the commonly illustrated mammalian mechanism, interactions involving eIF4G and eIF3 facilitate that encounter. The RNA does not enter the ribosome as a complete folded messenger ribonucleoprotein particle. A portion of the leader engages the channel that accommodates single-stranded messenger RNA, while other regions of the transcript and its bound proteins remain outside. This is why recruitment involves both protein-protein contacts and the physical condition of the RNA itself.

The interaction between eIF4G and eIF3 is an example of how recruitment can be achieved indirectly. The cap is recognized by one protein, the small subunit by other proteins, and the scaffold links them through a series of contacts. No direct Watson–Crick pairing between the cap and ribosomal RNA is required. The mechanism is different from the usual bacterial arrangement in which a messenger ribosome-binding sequence can pair with a complementary portion of 16S rRNA. Recruitment to the eukaryotic leader generally establishes an entry region rather than specifying the final initiation triplet. Consequently, productive cap binding can occur even when the message has a mutated AUG and cannot make the intended protein.

After engagement, the preinitiation complex inspects the leader in the five-prime to three-prime direction. This movement is called scanning. It is not equivalent to the movement of an elongating 80S ribosome, because the small subunit is still locating the place at which a stable reading frame will be established. An elongating ribosome normally advances by one codon at each translocation event. During scanning the candidate positions are not restricted to a preselected triplet register. The initiator anticodon can in principle encounter a potential start at successive nucleotide positions. Thus, if AUG begins at nucleotide 37 of a leader, the scanning apparatus need not have already decided that nucleotides 1 to 3 constitute a codon. No peptide is being synthesized as the standard scanning assembly moves across the untranslated sequence.

The open configuration of the small subunit provides an arrangement conducive to RNA movement and inspection. Initiation factors help maintain this configuration and permit the initiator tRNA to sample possible start sites without becoming fully locked into the P site at every candidate. The aminoacylated initiator tRNA is already present in the ternary-complex-associated assembly; it does not need to be recruited anew each time a new triplet reaches the decoding region. This arrangement brings the search criterion along with the searching apparatus. A mutation that prevents initial association of the ternary complex can therefore impair start-site recognition even if the messenger itself has an ideal cap and an excellent nucleotide context around its AUG.

RNA structures encountered during scanning are dynamic. Thermal motion can open and close short regions, and proteins can stabilize or disrupt them. ATP-dependent remodelling assists the scanning machinery in dealing with structures that would otherwise impede progress. It is tempting to imagine that every nucleotide traversed corresponds to a fixed ATP hydrolysis step. This is not a generally valid interpretation. Different leaders present different structural obstacles, and simplified reconstituted systems can scan relatively unstructured RNA under conditions in which the usual ATP-dependent accessory factors are dispensable. The energy requirement of the natural pathway must therefore be discussed in relation to the messenger and its physical environment rather than as a universal integer per base.

A common terminology question concerns the names of messenger-bound complexes. The expression 43S preinitiation complex usually identifies the prepared small subunit before messenger engagement. Once it binds RNA and scans, some accounts describe the messenger-bound assembly as a scanning 48S complex, while others reserve the name 48S initiation complex for the stable start-selected state. In an examination the most reliable distinguishing features are messenger association, start-codon pairing and the presence or absence of the large subunit. If a question shows a small subunit already positioned at a start codon with the initiator tRNA in the P site, 48S is the conventional answer. If the small subunit lacks the messenger but carries the initiating machinery, 43S is the appropriate answer. Neither state is an 80S ribosome.

The spatial placement of a start codon relative to the cap matters because the small subunit needs to engage an adequate stretch of RNA. A candidate triplet immediately next to the cap may be less effectively recognized in a standard scanning system, even if its local nucleotide sequence would otherwise be favourable. This is a geometric limitation rather than a different assignment of the genetic code. When designing synthetic expression constructs, placing the initiation codon after a suitable accessible leader is a way to provide the machinery with room to operate. The precise minimum spacing is context dependent and should not be treated as an invariant number across eukaryotic organisms and all experimental systems.

Scanning gives eukaryotic cells a way to use information distributed along an RNA leader. A candidate start located before the main coding region may capture some complexes, whereas an inhibitory structure may prevent others from reaching either start. Translation output is thus influenced by the order in which the scanning complex encounters these features. Two messages with identical principal coding sequences can produce different amounts of protein because their leaders differ. The coding sequence tells the ribosome which amino acids to join after initiation, but the leader helps determine how often the opportunity to translate that coding sequence is realized.

8. The Kozak context: why identical AUG triplets differ

AUG is a strong signal for initiation in the canonical system because its three nucleotides can pair with the anticodon of methionyl initiator tRNA. However, there may be multiple AUG triplets in a single RNA. An accurate cell cannot treat every occurrence as equally suitable, and the initiation machinery responds to features outside the triplet itself. Studies of vertebrate messenger sequences and targeted changes around start codons led to identification of a favourable sequence environment often called the Kozak context. An often quoted vertebrate consensus is 5′-GCCRCCAUGG-3′, where R represents a purine, either adenine or guanine. Expanded versions of the consensus include additional flanking residues. These are statistical and functional descriptions of favourable context, not an invariant compulsory sequence.

The notation for the positions around AUG is especially important. The A of the AUG initiation codon is assigned position +1, U is +2 and G is +3. The nucleotide directly preceding A is −1; there is no position zero in this convention. A purine at −3 and a guanine at +4 are particularly influential features in many vertebrate messengers. To identify them in a sequence, first underline the actual candidate AUG, then count three positions upstream from A and one position downstream from the final G. The +4 base is the first nucleotide of the next coding triplet if this AUG is selected as the start. The −3 base is in the preceding leader unless an upstream coding region overlaps the position.

For example, consider 5′-GCCAUGGCU-3′. Immediately before AUG are the bases G, C and C, so the −3 position is G; immediately after AUG is another G, occupying +4. These two positions provide favourable features. In 5′-UCCAUGUCU-3′, the −3 position is U and the +4 position is U. Both features are less favourable in the usual vertebrate context. Neither example changes the identity of the AUG itself. The difference is in the probability that a scanning complex will commit to it. If the second sequence is used as an internal site of a longer RNA and a better downstream start is available, some complexes may proceed to the downstream site. Context affects site selection without changing the standard codon assignment.

There is no requirement that an entire consensus sequence be perfectly matched for an AUG to function. A naturally occurring message may have a strong signal at one important position and a weaker signal at another. The behaviour can depend on the surrounding leader and on the initiation machinery of the cell. The Kozak context should not be treated as a promoter, because it is read as part of a messenger during translation rather than as a DNA regulatory site for RNA polymerase. Nor should it be equated with the bacterial Shine–Dalgarno sequence. The latter helps position a start through complementary interaction with ribosomal RNA; the Kozak context influences whether an encountered start is accepted within a scanning pathway.

One useful way to understand the effect of flanking bases is to distinguish the three-base recognition event from the total stability of the start-selected assembly. An initiator anticodon may form complementary base pairs with an AUG, but the mRNA occupies a channel within a ribonucleoprotein complex that contacts neighbouring nucleotides as well. Favourable context can make the transition to a committed conformation more likely. A mismatch at the central triplet weakens the central recognition event, whereas a change outside the triplet can alter the surrounding interactions. Both may influence initiation efficiency, but the genetic consequences of the mutations differ. An alteration at −3 does not itself replace the initiating amino acid or shift the open reading frame. An alteration at +4 may additionally change the second amino acid if the coding sequence is translated from that AUG.

The possible coding effect of a +4 mutation creates an experimental design problem. Suppose a researcher changes G at +4 to U and sees less activity of a reporter enzyme. The result might be caused by weaker initiation, but the altered nucleotide can also change the second amino acid, potentially affecting enzyme activity or protein stability. To test initiation specifically, it is useful to quantify the amount of newly synthesized protein as well as its enzymatic activity, or to use an independent start-site occupancy assay. Depending on the codon, one may not be able to change its first nucleotide synonymously. Careful comparison with a noncoding-context mutation at −3 provides another route to isolating initiation effects. A reduction in reporter activity should not automatically be equated with a reduction in initiation frequency.

The context recognized most efficiently is not identical in all eukaryotic lineages. Yeasts, plants and animals show characteristic biases in sequences around start sites. The vertebrate consensus remains the standard illustrative example for examinations, but a question specifying an organism with different preferences should be interpreted using its stated evidence. The conserved principle is that initiation depends on the triplet and its environment, not that every eukaryotic gene must carry an identical run of bases. This distinction also prevents a common bioinformatics mistake: a sequence that fails to match a strong vertebrate consensus cannot be excluded automatically as a genuine initiation site in another species.

9. Choosing the start: scanning arrest and commitment

When a scanning complex encounters a suitable initiation site, the initiator anticodon can pair with the candidate triplet in the decoding region. The resulting interactions favour a rearrangement of the small subunit and associated factors. The open, mobile state is replaced by a closed state in which the initiator tRNA is more fully accommodated in the P site. In simplified structural terminology the less fully accommodated state is sometimes called P-out and the committed state P-in. These names describe alternative positions or modes of binding of the initiator tRNA, not two separate P sites on the same ribosome. The key educational point is the switch from an arrangement that supports inspection to one that retains the selected codon and tRNA in a stable relationship.

eIF1 helps oppose inappropriate commitment while the small subunit is searching. A suitable start promotes rearrangement of its interactions and eventual displacement from the committed complex. This contributes to the connection between correct start recognition and the nucleotide cycle of eIF2. eIF5 assists the hydrolysis reaction, and the selected state favours release of inorganic phosphate. As a result, the stable interaction between eIF2 and the initiating tRNA is lost and eIF2 can leave in its GDP-associated form. The ribosome must retain Met-tRNAi even as the transport factor leaves. The base-paired tRNA and its interactions with the small subunit provide the arrangement that makes this transfer possible. Simply dissociating eIF2 from an uncommitted complex would not necessarily yield a usable start-selected particle.

This mechanism gives direction to what would otherwise be a reversible series of encounters. During scanning, short-lived interactions with unsuitable triplets can be abandoned. At a suitable start, the rearrangements and nucleotide transition favour persistence at that position. It would be excessive to describe the system as perfectly irreversible: biological complexes can dissociate and initiation sometimes fails even after substantial assembly. Nonetheless, the use of nucleotide-driven transitions permits distinct functional states to be linked in an orderly direction. A strong initiation site is one at which productive commitment occurs with relatively high probability under the relevant conditions, rather than one at which every individual scanning complex must stop with absolute certainty.

The selected AUG is positioned at the small-subunit P site. The following coding triplet is positioned for eventual decoding in the A site of the assembled ribosome. This arrangement defines the phase of subsequent translation. Consider an RNA segment containing 5′-AUGGCUAAAUAA-3′. If initiation occurs at the displayed AUG, the next two sense triplets are GCU and AAA, which encode alanine and lysine, followed by UAA as a termination signal. The initial chain produced from these three sense codons is methionine-alanine-lysine before any maturation. If a different AUG upstream established an overlapping reading frame, the same letters might be divided differently and the apparent UAA might not be a stop in that frame. Termination is frame dependent just as ordinary decoding is.

Start recognition does not itself catalyse the first peptide bond. At this point the complex still needs to recruit the large subunit, rearrange or release initiation factors and expose the A site for an elongator tRNA. An experiment could detect a stable start-selected small-subunit complex even when no protein is produced. This is a central concept for experimental reasoning. A loss of output may reflect failure before start selection, failure to join the large subunit, or failure to proceed after an apparently complete initiation complex. Exam questions often provide an observation about the accumulated intermediate; the correct interpretation is the step immediately downstream of that intermediate, provided the preceding reactions can still supply it.

A defect in fidelity can have consequences beyond changing total protein abundance. If an inappropriate start in the same reading frame is used upstream of the normal start, the resulting protein may contain an amino-terminal extension. If initiation occurs at a downstream in-frame AUG, the chain may lack an amino-terminal segment. If the selected start establishes another frame, the product may have an entirely different sequence and terminate at a different stop codon. These consequences depend on actual sequence relationships and must not be inferred merely from the relative order of AUG triplets. A sequence analysis that labels the candidate AUG positions and computes their differences modulo three can establish whether the predicted coding frames coincide. Protein size and sequence measurements can then test the prediction.

The transition from open to closed states is a useful example of a broader biological principle: the same molecular apparatus can perform sequential tasks by changing shape. A receptor can respond to a ligand through a conformational change, an enzyme can close around a substrate, and a ribosomal preinitiation complex can close around a selected codon. The analogy does not imply that these proteins have identical structures or use the same energy source. It illustrates how molecular recognition can be coupled to a later event without requiring a separate machine to inspect every molecule after recognition. Here the inspection event establishes the starting point from which the entire polypeptide will be generated.

10. From 48S to an elongation-competent 80S ribosome

At a selected start the small subunit possesses the messenger and the correctly positioned initiator tRNA, but the catalytic machinery of the large subunit has not yet been assembled around them. An additional factor, eIF5B, promotes the joining of the 60S subunit. This protein is itself a GTPase and is distinct from eIF5, the factor that stimulates the GTPase reaction of eIF2. The similarity of their names is a source of frequent mistakes. eIF5 acts primarily in the transition associated with the ternary complex and recognition of the start; eIF5B acts prominently during subunit association and the final maturation of the initiation complex. There are thus two functionally distinct guanine nucleotide-dependent transitions in the canonical pathway, rather than two interchangeable proteins acting at one step.

The small and large subunits make a specific set of contacts when they join. The initiator tRNA must fit into a position compatible with both the decoding region of the small subunit and the peptide-bond-forming region of the large subunit. eIF5B supports this arrangement and promotes productive subunit joining. This does not mean that eIF5B supplies the large subunit with its ribosomal proteins or manufactures a ribosome. Both subunits are pre-existing particles derived from ribosome biogenesis or recycling. The initiation factor facilitates their association at the proper site and time. Its activity couples an already established relationship between messenger and initiator tRNA to construction of the catalytic assembly.

eIF5B bound to GTP is competent to act in the joining pathway. Once the subunits associate productively, GTP hydrolysis and rearrangements promote release of eIF5B and eIF1A. This allows the ribosomal A site and surrounding factor-interaction surfaces to become available for elongation. The complete 80S particle at this stage has an appropriately placed initiator tRNA, and the next coding triplet is available to be read by an incoming aminoacyl-tRNA. Its messenger is aligned and its essential initiation factors have been displaced or rearranged to permit the next phase. The initiator tRNA remains associated with the P site rather than leaving along with the protein that brought it to the ribosome.

Consider a mutant factor that binds GTP and can promote association of the two subunits but cannot readily hydrolyse its nucleotide or depart. Sedimentation could reveal an abundance of 80S-sized particles even though protein synthesis is low. This is consistent with the formation of structurally associated but functionally incomplete particles. In contrast, a mutant that cannot interact productively with the 60S subunit might permit the accumulation of start-selected 48S complexes and reduce 80S formation. The two mutations affect the same named factor but are predicted to produce different molecular intermediates. The important question in either case is whether the complex reaches the stage in which the affected activity becomes necessary.

Subunit joining has a useful historical comparison with bacterial translation. Bacterial IF2 is related evolutionarily to eukaryotic eIF5B. Both are guanine nucleotide-dependent factors involved in initiation and subunit association. Eukaryotic eIF2, despite sharing a numeral with bacterial IF2, is a separate type of factor that performs the principal ternary-complex delivery function in the standard eukaryotic pathway. Correspondence between names is therefore not a reliable method for identifying evolutionary relationships. Functions can be divided among several factors in one lineage even when another lineage organizes related tasks differently. A comparison of the molecular work performed is more informative than simply aligning columns by factor number.

A purified 80S particle isolated from a cell is not necessarily an actively translating ribosome. Some 80S particles may lack a productive messenger or contain stalled or otherwise inactive assemblies. The designation 80S describes the particle and its sedimentation behaviour, not a guarantee of peptide synthesis. For functional identification one needs information about messenger association, tRNA placement, factor occupancy or the capacity to synthesize peptide. A preparation with a large 80S peak and little newly synthesized protein cannot be interpreted as evidence of enhanced initiation merely from the peak size. It is the relationship among multiple measurements that supports an assignment of molecular state.

At the boundary between initiation and elongation, no elongator aminoacyl-tRNA has yet been accommodated in the A site in the standard starting arrangement. The initiation process has created a P-site donor and an available A-site codon. When the first elongator tRNA enters, the machinery begins the repeating cycles considered in the next chapter. This boundary gives a precise answer to an otherwise ambiguous question about the end of initiation: the end is not the detection of the first methionine residue, nor merely the presence of a large subunit; it is the establishment of a complete ribosomal assembly that is able to enter the elongation cycle.

11. Productive mRNA organization and repeated initiation

The main protein-coding sequence of a messenger can be translated more than once. After one ribosome moves away from the start, another small subunit may engage the RNA and initiate independently. The cell therefore does not require a new transcript for every newly synthesized polypeptide. This is a major amplification step in gene expression. The cap-associated machinery and the proteins interacting with the three-prime end of a messenger can influence repeated opportunities for recruitment. In a simplified closed-loop model, cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding protein on the tail contacts eIF4G, which in turn interacts with cap-associated eIF4E and RNA-remodelling factors. The model identifies a potential route through which a feature far from the initiation codon can affect its utilization.

The RNA need not remain in the same shape throughout translation. The messenger is flexible, and proteins can associate with and dissociate from it. An interaction between proteins at its two ends can create transient proximity without producing a permanently circular topology. It is therefore better to think of the closed loop as a functional organization than as a ring of fixed circumference. A diagram that draws the messenger as a circle represents contact or proximity between its ends. It does not imply that the first nucleotide is joined by an ordinary phosphodiester bond to the last nucleotide. True covalently circular RNAs are different molecular entities.

Poly(A)-binding protein, often abbreviated PABP, can enhance the efficiency of initiation through its interactions with eIF4G and other components. It can also contribute to the stability and organization of the messenger. The influence of the tail on protein accumulation is consequently not restricted to one measurable variable. A longer-lived messenger provides more time for ribosomes to begin translation, while a messenger with more efficient recruitment may produce protein at a higher rate even when its lifetime is unchanged. In many biological systems both effects are relevant. A comparison between tailed and untailed messages should therefore distinguish RNA abundance from the rate of protein production per available transcript.

A useful experiment to investigate the relationship between the cap and the tail uses four otherwise comparable messages: capped with a tail, capped without a tail, uncapped with a tail, and uncapped without a tail. Measuring only the first and last conditions combines two changes and does not reveal whether one feature depends on the other. The full four-condition design permits evaluation of the separate effects and of their interaction. If the benefit of adding a tail is much larger in a capped RNA than in an uncapped one under otherwise matched conditions, the two features may cooperate. This is consistent with a protein bridge that links the ends, but it does not prove the physical arrangement by itself. A mutation specifically disrupting the relevant protein interaction would test the proposed link more directly.

There are distinct ways of expressing cooperation quantitatively. For an additive model, the expected increase caused by applying both changes is the sum of their individual increases above a common baseline. For a multiplicative model, the expected relative fold change is the product of the two individual fold changes. The same dataset can appear synergistic on one scale and additive or independent on another. If a question asks about cooperation, inspect the model it supplies. For example, a baseline of ten protein units, a cap-only value of twenty and a tail-only value of thirty give an additive expectation of forty and a multiplicative expectation of sixty. An observed value of fifty exceeds the additive expectation but not the multiplicative one. The arithmetic cannot identify a biological interaction without stating which interpretation is intended.

A highly translated messenger may carry several ribosomes simultaneously. Their presence is consistent with repeated initiation, but the number of ribosomes on a message also depends on how long each remains there. A slow elongating ribosome occupies the message for longer, potentially increasing ribosome density even if initiation has not become more frequent. On the other hand, a severe initiation defect can cause a decline in ribosome occupancy as previously initiated ribosomes complete translation and depart. This simple relationship between entry and residence time provides a bridge to the later chapter on polysomes. At the initiation stage it warns us that the presence of many ribosomes is not a direct measurement of the rate at which new chains are being made.

The repeated recruitment of ribosomes imposes a requirement on soluble initiation factors. A molecule of eIF2 that remains trapped in the GDP-associated state or an eIF5B molecule that remains bound to an assembled ribosome cannot support the same number of subsequent initiation events as efficiently as a factor that is recycled normally. Factor availability therefore depends on both total abundance and turnover through its functional cycle. The messenger itself can be reused and the ribosomal subunits can be recycled, but each new chain requires its own initiator tRNA molecule and a fresh set of nucleotide-dependent transitions. Reuse of the apparatus reduces the cost of constructing new molecular machinery, not the requirement for chemical energy in each productive initiation event.

12. Leaky scanning, alternative start sites and noncanonical entry

The canonical scanning mechanism often selects the first suitable AUG encountered from the five-prime end, but it does not invariably select the first AUG. When an upstream triplet lies in a poor context, some scanning complexes continue downstream instead of committing to it. This behaviour is called leaky scanning. The word leaky refers to the passage of a fraction of complexes through a potential start site, not to loss of messenger RNA from the nucleus or an opening in the ribosomal structure. A single messenger can thus support initiation at two different sites if both are accessible and neither captures every eligible complex. The relative production of the resulting proteins depends on the recognition efficiencies of the sites and the probability of reaching them.

If two alternative AUG triplets are in the same frame and there is no intervening stop in that frame, initiation at the upstream site may produce a longer polypeptide with an amino-terminal extension. Initiation at the downstream site can produce a shorter version sharing the same carboxyl-terminal sequence. The extra sequence may contain a targeting signal or affect stability, so the difference can be biologically important even when most of the residues are identical. If the two sites are in different frames, however, their products are not simply long and short versions of the same protein. The difference must be established by reading the coding triplets from each site. A difference of three, six or nine nucleotides between the positions of the initiating A residues preserves the frame; a difference of four or five nucleotides does not.

A short upstream open reading frame, abbreviated uORF, may start and terminate before the principal coding region begins. Some scanning complexes bypass its start, while others initiate and translate the upstream sequence. When translation of the uORF ends, the possibility of a small subunit remaining associated with the RNA and acquiring the components needed to initiate again is known as reinitiation. Reinitiation differs from leaky scanning because the ribosome has actually translated an upstream coding sequence before the next initiation event. A scanning complex that bypasses an upstream AUG has not yet completed an initiation and termination cycle. Detailed regulation by uORFs belongs to the later regulation chapter; the distinction between the two physical routes is essential for understanding why mutating an upstream AUG can increase translation of the principal coding region.

In some contexts a triplet differing from AUG by one nucleotide, such as CUG, GUG or UUG, can function as an initiation site. Initiation at a near-cognate codon is generally less efficient in the ordinary pathway and is influenced by context and factor activity. Importantly, recognition of such a site by the conventional methionyl initiator tRNA need not insert the amino acid normally encoded by that triplet during elongation. The identity of the delivered amino acid depends on the tRNA and initiation route used. Some specialized alternative routes differ in this respect, but they should not be treated as the standard rule. For an examination question in which the conventional initiator tRNA is explicitly specified, use its attached methionine when reasoning about the first residue.

Not every messenger needs to recruit the ribosome by the ordinary five-prime cap mechanism. Internal ribosome entry sites, or IRES elements, provide a different route in certain messages, particularly well-established examples in viral RNAs. An internal RNA region can recruit ribosomal components and initiation factors through its sequence, structure and associated proteins, reducing or removing the dependence on the standard cap-binding step. This is a mechanistic exception to the usual entry route, not an exception to the need to establish a productive reading frame. Different IRES elements use different subsets of the translation apparatus, and some still require scanning over a portion of the RNA. The term IRES alone does not specify exactly which factors are required.

Cap-independent initiation and internal initiation are related but not identical descriptions. A message may recruit machinery near its five-prime end by a route that does not depend strongly on eIF4E and then scan; that would be cap-independent but not necessarily internal entry. Conversely, a naturally capped message can contain an element that supports internal initiation under appropriate conditions. The existence of a cap does not prove that every protein synthesized from the RNA depended on that cap. The physical entry site, the chemical features needed for recruitment and the factor requirements should be treated as separate questions. This separation is useful in interpreting experiments where cap recognition is inhibited but a subset of messages remains translated.

An RNA structure that supports internal recruitment may be beneficial under conditions in which conventional entry is less efficient. However, it is not valid to assume that any protein whose synthesis persists during stress must be translated by an IRES. Alternative explanations include changes in messenger lifetime, residual activity of cap-dependent initiation, changes in elongation or differential sensitivity of particular messages to limited factors. Demonstrating internal entry requires evidence about the initiation mechanism itself. The present chapter establishes the principle and the essential controls; the detailed viral strategies and wider regulatory programmes belong to later curriculum topics.

13. Why bacterial and eukaryotic initiation differ

The bacterial initiation mechanism studied in the preceding chapter and the cytosolic eukaryotic mechanism solve the same central problem. Both must select a starting triplet, position an initiator tRNA and assemble small and large ribosomal subunits. The differences lie in how the messenger is recruited, how the starting triplet is located and how soluble proteins coordinate the events. In a standard bacterial example, the small 30S subunit can recognize a ribosome-binding site through pairing of a Shine–Dalgarno sequence with a complementary region near the three-prime end of 16S rRNA. The spacing between the pairing region and the start contributes to placement of the AUG at the P site. This arrangement can recruit ribosomes near an internal coding sequence without requiring them to scan from the physical beginning of the transcript.

In the canonical cytosolic eukaryotic case, the modified five-prime cap provides an entry feature and a 40S preinitiation complex examines the leader. The usual recognition signal is therefore distributed across two elements: the cap-associated entry region and the local context of the potential initiation codon. A Shine–Dalgarno sequence is neither required nor expected in most such messengers. Adding a short purine-rich bacterial binding sequence upstream of an AUG in a vertebrate expression construct does not automatically enhance initiation, because the corresponding pairing mechanism is not the principal route used by the vertebrate cytosolic ribosome. It may instead alter the leader's folding or accidentally introduce other sequence effects. Functional expression constructs must be designed for the recipient translation system.

This difference helps explain the prevalence of bacterial polycistronic messengers. A single bacterial transcript may carry several coding regions, each with an appropriate internal recruitment arrangement. A ribosome that translates the first coding region need not be the same ribosome that later translates the second one. In many eukaryotic nuclear messengers the principal translation product is encoded by a single main open reading frame, consistent with a cap-associated search mechanism that often selects an early suitable AUG. There are eukaryotic exceptions involving alternative initiation and reinitiation, and bacterial exceptions to ordinary Shine–Dalgarno-mediated recruitment. The contrast is a description of typical organization rather than an absolute division between all prokaryotic and all eukaryotic genes.

The chemical identity of the first tRNA also distinguishes representative examples. Typical bacteria use an initiator carrying N-formylmethionine, whereas the ordinary cytosolic eukaryotic initiator carries methionine without that formyl group. In both cases the first tRNA is positioned in the P site and the next sense codon is made available in the A site. The evolutionary continuity of this spatial arrangement is particularly striking: the messenger must be held in a common relationship to the donor and acceptor sites so that the first peptide bond can form correctly. The differences in cap recognition and scanning do not change the fundamental polarity of mRNA decoding or peptide synthesis. Both systems generally read messenger RNA from five-prime to three-prime and synthesize the chain from its amino terminus towards its carboxyl terminus.

The bacterial initiation factors are commonly called IF1, IF2 and IF3. IF1 has a functional and evolutionary relationship to eukaryotic eIF1A; both act near the small-subunit A site during initiation. Bacterial IF2 is related to eukaryotic eIF5B and contributes to initiator delivery or positioning and subunit joining in the bacterial pathway. Bacterial IF3 helps maintain the small subunit in an appropriate initiation state and contributes to selection fidelity. Several eukaryotic proteins, including eIF1 and eIF3, contribute to functions broadly related to fidelity and prevention of premature joining, but it is misleading to assert a one-to-one structural equivalence between bacterial IF3 and the entire eukaryotic multisubunit eIF3 complex. Similar functional problems can be addressed by different molecular organizations.

Archaea should not simply be treated as bacteria because both lack the nuclear architecture characteristic of eukaryotic cells. Archaeal translation initiation includes factors that are related to eukaryotic initiation components, and archaeal messages differ in their recruitment and leader arrangements. A student who learns the standard bacterial and eukaryotic examples should therefore attach the appropriate biological scope to each description. Mitochondrial and plastid translation also differs from ordinary cytosolic eukaryotic translation and retains features related to bacterial ancestry, although these organelles possess specialized systems of their own. These comparative observations demonstrate that conserved decoding tasks can be preserved while entry and accessory mechanisms diverge over evolution. They also explain why the word cytosolic is important in the title and scope of the standard eukaryotic pathway.

In a typical eukaryotic cell, nuclear transcription is spatially separated from cytoplasmic translation. A bacterial ribosome can sometimes begin translating a nascent messenger before transcription is completed because both processes occur in a shared cellular compartment. That contrast provides context for why the cap, export status and messenger maturation are important in the eukaryotic system. It does not imply that every eukaryotic transcript is translated immediately after export or that every bacterial transcript is translated cotranscriptionally. The timing of translation is subject to the availability of the messenger and the initiation machinery. The common exam error is to transfer the rules of one representative system without checking which cellular compartment and organism the question actually describes.

14. Historical experiments that established scanning and context dependence

The scanning model arose from an experimental question: how can a eukaryotic ribosome distinguish the beginning of a coding region from the many other triplets on a messenger? If the ribosome independently binds wherever an AUG is present, upstream and downstream AUGs of comparable context should be selected without strong dependence on their order. If the small subunit usually enters near the five-prime end and searches in sequence, an efficiently recognized upstream start should reduce the number of eligible complexes reaching a later one. These hypotheses have different consequences for engineered messengers containing more than one candidate start. Comparing the proteins made from such messages gives information about the route of initiation rather than merely its final product.

Marilyn Kozak's studies of eukaryotic initiation, including work published in 1978 and subsequent analyses of start-site context, helped establish and refine the scanning model. The essential insight was that the relationship between the position of a candidate AUG, its sequence environment and translation output could be tested by altering messenger sequences. An initiation model had to explain not only why many main coding regions begin at the first AUG but also why exceptions occur. A model allowing context-dependent acceptance of candidate triplets can account for both patterns. This is more powerful than a rule stating that the first AUG is always selected, because it provides a mechanistic basis for predicting what happens when the context of that AUG is changed.

Kozak's 1984 study of rat preproinsulin initiation and the 1986 work on point mutations surrounding AUG used targeted sequence alterations to examine the influence of flanking bases. The general experimental strength was that the candidate initiation codon could remain AUG while neighbouring positions were changed. If the codon itself were replaced, a reduction in initiation could simply reflect loss of the usual anticodon complementarity. By keeping AUG intact, the experiments could examine the contribution of nearby nucleotides to its recognition. The results supported the importance of particular context positions, especially a purine at position −3 and the nucleotide immediately following AUG. In the 1986 preproinsulin construct study, different substitutions within the preferred start environment produced proinsulin yields spanning more than twenty-fold; this was a result of that particular experimental system, not a universal fold-change for context mutations. The consensus developed from such investigations describes favourable contexts rather than a new four- or ten-letter genetic codon.

A modern student can reproduce the logic of the experiment without assuming that the original measurements had any particular numerical values. Suppose an intact messenger with a strong AUG context produces a particular protein, and a second messenger differs only at position −3. Measuring less newly synthesized product from the second messenger, while showing equal intact RNA abundance, supports an effect at the translational level. An additional measurement showing less occupancy of the original start site by small-subunit complexes strengthens an initiation interpretation. If a downstream start is present, the appearance of an additional shorter protein provides an independent indicator of changed site selection. The strength of the inference increases when the observations agree with the predictions of the scanning model across several distinct readouts.

Position-dependent effects of leader structure also support a search mechanism. An RNA stem that lies in the path of a scanning complex can influence its ability to reach a downstream start. A comparison between an original leader, a variant with a deliberately strengthened paired region and a compensatory or destabilizing variant can test whether structure rather than one particular sequence accounts for altered output. Such a comparison must distinguish an RNA that cannot recruit the small subunit from an RNA that recruits it but cannot support movement to the start. A structure near the end and a structure near the AUG may have different effects. A stable stem may even favour recognition of a weak start in some placements by slowing passage. Thus the direction and magnitude of a structural effect cannot be predicted solely from the existence of complementary bases.

Historical discoveries are best understood as the gradual refinement of a model rather than a single observation establishing every detail. Protein product measurements can reveal the functional consequences of start-site changes but do not directly display the positions of all initiation factors. Reconstitution experiments, in which ribosomal subunits and purified factors are combined in defined conditions, made it possible to investigate which components are needed to form particular intermediates. Subsequent biochemical and structural studies have helped distinguish scanning-compatible and start-selected arrangements. The exam-level model integrates these different forms of evidence. It does not require assuming that every microscopic event was observed in the earliest studies of the scanning pathway.

15. Reconstitution, toeprinting and factor-omission logic

A biochemical pathway can be studied by assembling its components outside the cell. In a reconstituted translation initiation assay, the researcher supplies appropriate ribosomal subunits, a defined messenger, initiator tRNA, nucleotides and selected soluble factors. An assay can then examine whether a complex forms at a particular start or whether a later product appears. The advantage is that one can change a single component while keeping others constant. The disadvantage is that purified systems may differ from the cellular environment in factor concentrations, RNA structure, competing RNAs and accessory proteins. A conclusion about what is sufficient under a particular in vitro condition must not be confused with a conclusion about what is dispensable in every living cell.

A factor-omission experiment compares a complete reaction with one from which a specific factor has been excluded. If a stable start-selected complex forms in the complete system but not in the omission reaction, the missing factor contributes to the formation of that complex under those conditions. Adding it back should restore the activity if the reaction has otherwise remained intact. The restoration is an important control because the omission procedure may have changed other properties of the mixture. If omission has no detectable effect, the interpretation is that the activity was not essential for the measured event in that system, not that the factor is biologically useless. An unstructured leader, for example, can reduce the dependence of a simplified reaction on RNA-remodelling factors.

Toeprinting is a useful method for locating ribosomal complexes on a messenger. A short DNA primer anneals to the RNA downstream of the region being examined. Reverse transcriptase extends the primer, reading the RNA template toward its five-prime end. A ribosomal complex bound to the message can obstruct further extension. The resulting complementary DNA product ends at a characteristic position relative to the ribosome's placement. When the lengths of such products are compared with a sequence ladder or appropriately mapped markers, the location of the obstruction can be inferred. In many established eukaryotic initiation assays a 48S complex at an AUG produces a stop approximately 15 to 17 nucleotides downstream of the A of the start codon. This distance is a useful assay convention and is not the length of the whole ribosome.

The reverse-transcription product is not itself a peptide, and a strong toeprint does not measure the rate of protein synthesis directly. It reports occupancy of a position by a complex that impedes the enzyme under the experimental conditions. RNA secondary structure can also interfere with reverse transcription, so a lane containing RNA and reverse transcriptase but no ribosomal complex is an essential control. A characteristic stop appearing only when the required factors and small subunit are supplied is more informative than a band already present in the RNA-only reaction. If an AUG mutation shifts the ribosome-dependent stop to a downstream site, the shift provides evidence about alternative start selection. The precise assignment of complex composition may require independent fractionation or factor-binding analysis.

Reconstitution studies by Pestova and colleagues helped establish distinct contributions of initiation factors to messenger recruitment, scanning and subunit joining. Their work on eIF5B published in 2000 provided direct support for a separate factor-dependent joining step following formation of the start-selected complex. The important logical distinction is between detecting 48S assembly and detecting the productive association of the 60S subunit. If a reaction containing the components needed for 48S formation fails to generate an active 80S complex until a joining factor is provided, that result places the additional factor at a later functional stage. A test of nucleotide hydrolysis and factor release can then determine whether the joining reaction has produced an assembly capable of entering elongation, rather than simply two physically associated subunits.

Different assays answer different questions. A toeprint may locate the start-selected small subunit; sedimentation can separate assemblies by hydrodynamic behaviour; an immunoblot can report the presence of particular proteins; and synthesis of a defined peptide measures a functional consequence. The strongest interpretation comes from their combination. If a mutant gives a start-site toeprint, fails to generate an 80S peak under appropriate joining conditions and produces very little peptide, a defect downstream of start recognition and before productive joining is plausible. If the 80S peak is normal but peptide formation is low, the defect may lie in final release of initiation factors or in early elongation. One must avoid forcing all observations into a single mechanism without checking whether the assay actually distinguishes the alternatives.

16. Quantitative reasoning: initiation probability and energetic cost

The selection of an initiation site can be described using a simple probability model that is helpful in examination problems. Let N denote the number of scanning complexes that arrive at the first candidate AUG during a defined interval. Let p1 denote the probability of initiating there, conditional on arrival. The expected number initiating at the first site is N multiplied by p1. If every complex that does not initiate continues scanning and reaches the second site, the number arriving there is N multiplied by the quantity one minus p1. If the second site has a conditional recognition probability p2, the expected number initiating at the second site is N times one minus p1 times p2. The remaining fraction is the product of one minus p1 and one minus p2. The three outcome probabilities add to one because each complex is assigned to exactly one outcome in the model.

For a hypothetical message receiving 200 scanning complexes, let the first site's recognition probability be 0.30 and the second site's probability be 0.75. The first start receives 60 initiations. Of the 140 complexes that continue, 105 initiate at the second start, and 35 initiate at neither. The fraction of all arriving complexes that initiate at the second site is therefore 105 divided by 200, or 0.525. Among the 165 complexes that initiate at either site, the second site's share is instead 105 divided by 165, approximately 0.636. The two percentages answer different questions. If an experimental report measures only synthesized products and not unsuccessful scanning complexes, it may be appropriate to compare the relative product shares rather than the fractions of all arriving complexes.

Suppose the first AUG context is strengthened so that p1 becomes 0.80, while recruitment and the recognition probability of the second site remain unchanged. The expected first-site output becomes 160, and only 40 complexes continue. Thirty now initiate at the second site and ten at neither. The second site's efficiency conditional on arrival has not changed; fewer complexes reach it. Its absolute output falls from 105 to 30. Thus a mutation upstream can decrease initiation at an otherwise unchanged downstream site. This conclusion relies on the directionality of the scanning model and the assumption that complexes committing to the first start do not subsequently initiate at the second. If the first site belongs to a short upstream open reading frame and reinitiation can occur, an additional term is needed to account for that route.

Recruitment and survival during scanning can be represented explicitly. Let R be the number of productive recruitment events per messenger per minute, let s be the probability that a recruited complex reaches the intended start without dissociating, and let p be the conditional probability of commitment there. The rate of successful initiations per messenger is R times s times p, with units of events per minute per messenger. For 50 identical messengers, each receiving two productive recruitments per minute, with s equal to 0.8 and p equal to 0.5, the cell population in this model initiates 40 chains per minute. Raising p to 0.9 while reducing s to 0.4 gives 36 chains per minute. Improving a start-site feature does not guarantee an increase in output if another change impairs access to the site. The multiplication of conditional probabilities makes this dependence explicit.

The energetic bookkeeping of the canonical pathway begins before the initiator tRNA reaches the ribosome. Aminoacylation consumes an ATP molecule converted to AMP and pyrophosphate; subsequent pyrophosphate hydrolysis gives the conventional cost of two high-energy phosphate equivalents. One GTP associated with eIF2 is hydrolysed during the delivery and commitment cycle. A further GTP associated with eIF5B is hydrolysed in the subunit-joining and final release pathway. RNA-remodelling factors, including eIF4A, may hydrolyse additional ATP molecules. Because the amount of RNA structure encountered varies and nonproductive events can also consume nucleotide, there is no generally valid fixed ATP total for scanning an arbitrary leader. An exam question that requests a single cost must state which steps are to be included and which are assumed to be absent or ignored.

For a hypothetical calculation, assume a successful initiation event includes charging one initiator tRNA, hydrolysis of one GTP by eIF2 and one by eIF5B, and exactly three ATP-to-ADP hydrolysis events for RNA remodelling. Under these stipulated conditions the cost is one ATP-to-AMP reaction, three ATP-to-ADP reactions and two GTP-to-GDP reactions. Six nucleotide triphosphate molecules are consumed as substrates, but the conventional high-energy phosphate-equivalent cost is two plus three plus two, or seven. If charging was performed before the interval being measured, only the two GTP and three ATP hydrolysis reactions belong to that interval, giving five equivalents. Neither result should be called the universal energetic cost of eukaryotic initiation. The distinction between nucleotide molecules and phosphate equivalents is essential when comparing calculations.

A related quantitative trap is to confuse initiation frequency with the number of completed products measured immediately after an intervention. A newly initiated ribosome still needs time to translate the coding sequence. If initiation is abruptly stopped at time zero, ribosomes already translating can continue to complete their chains for a period determined by their remaining translation times. The protein production rate measured immediately afterward may not fall to zero. In a simple steady-state model with a fixed elongation time of one minute and a constant prior initiation rate of ten chains per minute, completion may continue for up to one minute after complete initiation arrest, assuming no other effects. This temporal separation is useful when comparing pulse-label experiments with endpoint protein measurements. The next chapter develops the elongation component that makes the delay possible.

17. Testing alternative entry and diagnosing failures of initiation

Internal entry can be investigated using a message containing two reporter coding regions separated by a candidate RNA element. In a simple bicistronic design the first reporter is translated by ordinary cap-associated recruitment. The candidate element is placed between the stop of the first reporter and the start of the second. If the element genuinely recruits initiation machinery internally, it can enhance production of the second reporter even when ordinary cap-dependent initiation favours the first one. The first reporter provides a useful internal comparison, but its signal alone does not establish that the two proteins were synthesized from the same intact message. This distinction is fundamental when evaluating evidence for noncanonical initiation.

Several alternative mechanisms can produce a second reporter signal. A DNA-based reporter construct may contain an unintended promoter within the inserted sequence, leading to a shorter messenger containing only the second coding region. Splicing can also generate a shorter RNA. An RNA cleavage event could produce a new accessible end from which recruitment occurs. Reinitiation after translation of the first coding region, or readthrough of its stop, can produce additional signals under some arrangements. A convincing experimental design checks the size and integrity of the RNA, excludes unintended transcription or splicing, and considers whether protein products have the expected sizes. Delivering an intact synthetic RNA rather than transcribing a plasmid in the nucleus reduces some transcriptional and splicing-related alternatives, although it does not exclude RNA cleavage after delivery. The goal is to establish that the second start was reached by an internal recruitment event on the intended RNA.

Initiation defects can be localized by asking whether a messenger is available, whether an appropriate small-subunit complex is formed, whether the start is selected, and whether the large subunit joins productively. Imagine a capped reporter whose RNA concentration and integrity are unchanged, but a mutation in the cap-binding pocket of eIF4E reduces protein output. If the amount of cap-associated eIF4E and the amount of messenger-bound small subunit both decline, an early recruitment defect is a plausible interpretation. Replacing the cap-binding protein with a functional version should restore the relevant interactions if no other components are limiting. The result would not show that the mutated eIF4E changed the genetic code. Its effect concerns the probability of initiating on an available message.

A mutation in an eIF4G interaction region can have a different pattern. eIF4E may still bind the cap normally, and eIF4A may retain its intrinsic ATPase activity, yet recruitment of the small subunit may decline if the necessary connection is lost. The separation of biochemical activity from complex assembly is often decisive. In a comparison between a defective scaffold and a defective ATPase, increasing the amount of eIF4A may not restore an absent eIF4G interaction, whereas supplying functional scaffold can restore a missing bridge. Conversely, an intact scaffold cannot necessarily compensate for an RNA-remodelling defect when a structured leader demands helicase activity. These examples illustrate why complementation should be interpreted in the context of the specific molecular lesion rather than simply by asking whether a factor is present or absent.

A defect in eIF2-dependent delivery is expected to reduce the supply of properly prepared small-subunit complexes. In contrast, an eIF1 fidelity defect may shift initiation from favourable AUGs towards normally rejected sites. A joining defect involving eIF5B may preserve a start-site toeprint but impair formation or maturation of the 80S ribosome. These patterns are useful starting hypotheses, not universal fingerprints for every mutation. A protein may perform more than one function, and a cellular response to prolonged impairment can change other factor concentrations. Experiments on purified components can help separate a direct molecular effect from such secondary responses. Combining positional, compositional and functional readouts is generally more informative than attributing a decline in protein output to the first initiation factor mentioned in a question.

The messenger sequence itself can also cause initiation failure. An AUG mutation may remove the normal start; an upstream context mutation may divert some scanning complexes; a new stem in the leader may reduce access; and a changed downstream nucleotide may alter both context and the resulting protein. To distinguish these possibilities, compare RNA abundance, initiation positions, newly synthesized protein sizes and total protein abundance. A mutation that leads to a shorter product from a downstream in-frame AUG can leave the messenger concentration unchanged. A mutation that produces a protein with reduced stability can lower steady-state protein abundance even if initiation is unaffected. The distinction between production and turnover will recur in the chapter on protein degradation. For this chapter, the main lesson is that initiation must be measured through its own molecular intermediates or through readouts that are carefully controlled for other stages.

18. Biological significance, applications and the transition to elongation

The efficiency and fidelity of eukaryotic initiation have consequences for the cellular distribution of proteins. Different initiation sites on one messenger can produce proteins with different amino-terminal regions. If the upstream portion contains a sequence required for localization, a shorter product may reach a different destination. This connects translation initiation with the later curriculum topics on protein targeting. The connection does not imply that initiation factors themselves transport newly synthesized proteins into organelles. Instead, initiation determines which amino-terminal sequence is produced, and the sequence can influence subsequent recognition by targeting machinery. The biological significance of alternative initiation therefore depends on the function of the sequence that is included or omitted.

In biotechnology, the same principles guide the design of a eukaryotic expression construct. A promoter controls transcription, RNA processing signals help determine the mature messenger, and an appropriate leader and start context support translation. Improving the promoter without considering the RNA leader can generate abundant messenger but little protein. Conversely, improving the Kozak context may increase protein yield from a similar amount of RNA. The organism in which the construct is expressed must be considered, because the typical bacterial ribosome-binding sequence and the typical mammalian cap-scanning architecture are not interchangeable design rules. In a simple comparative experiment, expressing matched coding sequences with different leaders and measuring newly synthesized protein per intact RNA molecule provides a useful starting estimate of translation efficiency.

The pathway also helps interpret naturally occurring variants in untranslated regions. A change outside the principal coding sequence can have a substantial phenotype if it changes the frequency or site of initiation. A new upstream AUG may introduce a competing start, while a nucleotide substitution that stabilizes a stem may reduce the ability of the scanning machinery to reach the main start. Not every such variant has a large functional effect; the outcome depends on context, position, secondary structure and the available factors. A predicted sequence effect is a hypothesis until supported by biological evidence. The important generalization is that protein abundance and protein identity can be altered without changing the amino-acid coding sequence of the principal reading frame itself.

A compact way to reconstruct the full ordinary pathway is to start with separate subunits and a mature messenger. Methionyl-tRNA synthetase supplies charged initiator tRNA, eIF2-GTP forms the ternary complex, and eIF1, eIF1A, eIF3 and associated proteins organize the 40S preinitiation complex. The cap-associated eIF4F components and supporting factors make an appropriate leader accessible and promote recruitment. The small subunit scans until a suitable candidate triplet is recognized, adopts a committed conformation and retains the initiator in its P site. Changes involving eIF1, eIF5 and the nucleotide state of eIF2 allow the delivery machinery to leave. eIF5B promotes large-subunit association and its GTP-dependent transition helps complete factor release. Each event establishes a condition necessary for the next: a charged initiator, a scanning-competent apparatus, access to the messenger, a chosen reading frame and finally a complete catalytic ribosome.

At this final point the starting methionine remains ester-linked to its tRNA in the P site, and the next sense codon is exposed in the A site. The translation system now changes from a search-and-selection process into a repeating synthesis cycle. Elongator tRNAs will be selected according to the next codon, peptide bonds will be formed by the large subunit, and translocation will move the messenger and tRNAs in an ordered manner. Those tasks use their own factors, including eEF1A and eEF2, which should not be confused with eIF2 or eIF5B. The integrity of the newly established frame makes it possible for the remaining coding information to be interpreted correctly. Understanding the initiation state therefore supplies the necessary starting conditions for the next chapter on translation elongation.

Answer and explanation

C. 31 and 46 only

46 − 31 = 15, divisible by three. 62 − 31 = 31 and 62 − 46 = 16, neither divisible by three. Frame sharing does not itself prove that no stop intervenes.

36. Numerical

At two in-frame AUGs at positions 16 and 43, both ORFs share a stop beginning at position 106, with no intervening stops. What are the predicted primary chain lengths?

  1. 29 and 20 residues
  2. 30 and 21 residues
  3. 31 and 22 residues
  4. 90 and 63 residues
Answer and explanation

B. 30 and 21 residues

The number of sense codons is (106 − 16)/3 = 30 or (106 − 43)/3 = 21. The stop is not translated. The upstream product has nine additional residues.

37. Numerical

In a hypothetical cap/tail experiment the protein synthesis rates per equal amount of RNA are neither=5, cap only=15, tail only=10, both=25 units. What are the additive and multiplicative expectations for both?

  1. 20 and 30
  2. 30 and 20
  3. 15 and 10
  4. 25 and 25
Answer and explanation

A. 20 and 30

Additive: 5 + (15−5) + (10−5) = 20. Multiplicative: 5 × (15/5) × (10/5) = 30. The observed 25 exceeds additive but not multiplicative expectation.

38. Numerical

A hypothetical message has a start A at nucleotide 80 and a characteristic toeprint 16 nt downstream of it. If start selection shifts 30 nt downstream and the toeprint offset remains the same, what are the old and new mapped positions?

  1. 64 and 94
  2. 96 and 110
  3. 80 and 110
  4. 96 and 126
Answer and explanation

D. 96 and 126

The first obstruction maps to 80 + 16 = 96. The second start is 110, so its obstruction maps to 126. The toeprint is displaced from the P-site start.

39. Experimental interpretation

Capped and uncapped versions of a reporter initially have equal RNA amounts. After 30 minutes the capped RNA retains 90% and the uncapped RNA retains 10%. Protein accumulation is six-fold higher from the capped RNA. Which claim is supported?

  1. The cap changed the reporter protein sequence
  2. The cap increases initiation exactly six-fold
  3. The difference combines possible RNA survival and initiation effects; measure intact RNA over time and synthesis rate
  4. The uncapped RNA cannot ever recruit a ribosome
Answer and explanation

C. The difference combines possible RNA survival and initiation effects; measure intact RNA over time and synthesis rate

RNA abundance differs during the measurement, so the accumulated product cannot isolate initiation efficiency. Equal starting concentrations are not enough. An instantaneous or time-resolved synthesis measure normalized to intact RNA is more diagnostic.

40. Experimental interpretation

A reconstituted system lacking eIF5B produces a start-dependent toeprint and abundant 48S complexes, but almost no 80S particles or peptide. Adding wild-type eIF5B restores both. Where is the defect most directly localized?

  1. Initiator tRNA aminoacylation
  2. Productive large-subunit joining
  3. Stop-codon recognition
  4. Initial cap methylation
Answer and explanation

B. Productive large-subunit joining

The start is already occupied and the defect is rescued by the joining factor. Upstream cap and charging steps must have supplied the observed 48S complex in this system; termination is downstream of peptide formation.

41. Experimental interpretation

A GTP-hydrolysis-defective eIF5B variant allows abundant 80S assembly but remains ribosome-associated, with low first-dipeptide synthesis. Which interpretation fits best?

  1. Final factor release or maturation is impaired despite physical joining
  2. The cap became an amino acid
  3. The mutation necessarily changes the stop codon
  4. No 40S particles exist
Answer and explanation

A. Final factor release or maturation is impaired despite physical joining

The assembled particles are not automatically elongation competent. Persistent factor binding supports a late initiation defect. An eIF5B joining-only defect would instead tend to prevent formation of the 80S particle.

42. Experimental interpretation

A mutant eIF4G binds eIF4E and eIF4A normally, but cannot associate with eIF3 in a defined mammalian recruitment assay. Cap occupancy is preserved; 43S recruitment falls. What is the best interpretation?

  1. eIF4A must be an elongation factor
  2. The 60S peptide-transfer centre is defective
  3. Cap binding itself is necessarily abolished
  4. The defect is consistent with loss of a recruitment bridge
Answer and explanation

D. The defect is consistent with loss of a recruitment bridge

The preserved cap binding and ATPase partners distinguish scaffold connectivity from loss of individual activities. The observed reduction precedes start selection and does not identify a catalytic defect of the large subunit.

43. Experimental interpretation

A structured and unstructured leader share an identical strong start. Reducing eIF4A activity lowers output from the structured leader much more strongly at equal RNA abundance. What additional control most directly tests whether secondary structure contributes?

  1. Measure genomic DNA concentration only
  2. Replace the reporter protein with a longer one only
  3. Disrupt base pairing in the structured leader without changing the start, and compare a compensatory stem-restoring mutant
  4. Remove the stop from both constructs
Answer and explanation

C. Disrupt base pairing in the structured leader without changing the start, and compare a compensatory stem-restoring mutant

Disruption and compensatory restoration separate a structure-dependent effect from the exact sequence of one mutant. Protein length and DNA abundance do not isolate leader structure.

44. Experimental interpretation

An AUG→AUC change eliminates the first ribosome-dependent toeprint and produces a new toeprint near a downstream AUG. The new protein is shorter. Which condition is necessary to conclude that it is an N-terminally truncated version of the same protein?

  1. The downstream triplet must be a termination codon
  2. The downstream AUG is in frame with the original and shares the downstream coding region
  3. The two AUGs must have identical cap structures
  4. The new AUG must use fMet
Answer and explanation

B. The downstream AUG is in frame with the original and shares the downstream coding region

A shorter product can be encoded in a different frame, so length alone is insufficient. Check the frame and sequence or a common C-terminal epitope. The cap is shared by the message.

45. Experimental interpretation

Two reporter messages have the same AUG. A −3 purine-to-pyrimidine change reduces start-site occupancy; RNA concentration and protein stability are matched. What is the most parsimonious interpretation?

  1. The altered local context reduces commitment to AUG
  2. It deletes the cap chemically
  3. It creates a ribosomal RNA
  4. The nucleotide changes the initiating amino acid
Answer and explanation

A. The altered local context reduces commitment to AUG

The −3 base is outside the candidate AUG, so the anticodon match is retained. The positional readout and controls support altered start selection rather than protein instability.

46. Experimental interpretation

A +4 G→U mutation lowers reporter enzyme activity, but immunoblotting shows the same amount of newly synthesized reporter protein. What inference is strongest?

  1. eIF4E has no function
  2. The −3 nucleotide is necessarily mutated
  3. Initiation is definitely ten-fold lower
  4. The coding change may alter enzyme activity without lowering initiation; verify sequence and initiation occupancy
Answer and explanation

D. The coding change may alter enzyme activity without lowering initiation; verify sequence and initiation occupancy

The +4 base starts the second sense codon. The same protein amount but reduced activity suggests a protein property may explain the signal. Reporter activity alone is insufficient evidence of initiation.

47. Experimental interpretation

A cell extract has normal total eIF2 but little eIF2-GTP and poor ternary-complex formation. Functional eIF2B restores both. Which step is supported as limiting?

  1. Covalent closure of the mRNA
  2. Peptide termination
  3. GDP-to-GTP exchange on eIF2
  4. AUG anticodon synthesis by the ribosome
Answer and explanation

C. GDP-to-GTP exchange on eIF2

Restoration by the exchange factor and measured nucleotide state identify the active-factor supply. Normal total eIF2 does not imply normal GTP-bound eIF2.

48. Experimental interpretation

A mutation weakening eIF1-dependent start discrimination increases initiation at an upstream near-cognate triplet while reducing main-AUG product, with unchanged total mRNA. Which explanation is most consistent?

  1. The RNA polymerase must transcribe backwards
  2. The early site captures more scanning complexes
  3. The mutation eliminates every initiating complex
  4. The 60S is no longer required
Answer and explanation

B. The early site captures more scanning complexes

A fidelity change can redirect initiation rather than simply reducing total recruitment. More upstream commitment can reduce the flux reaching the main AUG.

49. Experimental interpretation

A bicistronic DNA reporter gives high second-reporter activity after insertion of a candidate IRES. RNA analysis detects a shorter transcript carrying only the second reporter. What should be concluded?

  1. An unintended monocistronic RNA offers an alternative explanation; test intact bicistronic RNA and transcription/splicing controls
  2. The first reporter cannot have been translated
  3. The IRES must be an enzyme
  4. Internal initiation is proven
Answer and explanation

A. An unintended monocistronic RNA offers an alternative explanation; test intact bicistronic RNA and transcription/splicing controls

A shortened RNA can recruit at its own end. The original result cannot distinguish that mechanism from true internal entry. RNA integrity and promoter/splicing controls are necessary.

50. Experimental interpretation

A cap-binding perturbation lowers the first reporter of a bicistronic RNA while the second remains active. Both coding regions are detected on full-length RNA. Which is the most careful conclusion?

  1. All eukaryotic translation is cap independent
  2. The second reporter must be transcribed independently
  3. No ribosomal factors are needed
  4. The second product is relatively resistant to this perturbation; test reinitiation and internal entry before assigning a mechanism
Answer and explanation

D. The second product is relatively resistant to this perturbation; test reinitiation and internal entry before assigning a mechanism

Resistance to a particular cap perturbation is evidence about dependence, not definitive proof of the route. Full-length RNA detection helps but does not alone exclude all alternative origins or reinitiation.

51. Analytical reasoning

A short uORF precedes a main AUG. A mutation abolishes the uORF start; main product rises. Which model is compatible without invoking changed transcription?

  1. The upstream ORF is now translated twice
  2. The main AUG no longer has a reading frame
  3. More scanning complexes bypass the former competing start and reach the main AUG
  4. The mutant necessarily prevents every initiation event
Answer and explanation

C. More scanning complexes bypass the former competing start and reach the main AUG

The mutation removes a competing recognition site. If RNA abundance is unchanged, altered initiation flux is a plausible explanation. Reinitiation could modify the quantitative response in the original RNA.

52. Analytical reasoning

A cell is exposed to an intervention that selectively stops new initiation while leaving elongation unchanged. After enough time for existing ribosomes to finish, which result is expected?

  1. Existing ribosomes accumulate forever on every RNA
  2. Ribosome occupancy and new peptide synthesis generally decline as existing ribosomes run off
  3. Every message is immediately destroyed
  4. The ribosomal small subunit becomes bacterial
Answer and explanation

B. Ribosome occupancy and new peptide synthesis generally decline as existing ribosomes run off

No new entrants replace the completing ribosomes. A transient output can remain immediately after intervention, but occupancy and synthesis decline after runoff; the model assumes RNA stability is unchanged.

53. Experimental interpretation

A toeprint near AUG appears in both the complete reaction and the no-ribosome RNA control, at comparable intensity. Which next step is most justified?

  1. Test whether RNA structure or reverse transcriptase pausing explains the band and seek a ribosome-dependent signal
  2. Count the band as a peptide
  3. Assume the 60S has joined
  4. Declare a 48S complex at that position
Answer and explanation

A. Test whether RNA structure or reverse transcriptase pausing explains the band and seek a ribosome-dependent signal

A structure-dependent stop could explain both bands. A ribosome-dependent additional signal and suitable controls are needed to infer a bound complex; a band alone does not establish its composition.

54. Analytical reasoning

An upstream in-frame AUG produces a longer protein containing a targeting sequence. A context mutation favours a downstream AUG and protein shifts from an organelle to the cytosol. Which mechanistic connection best explains the change?

  1. The downstream AUG must encode a stop
  2. PABP attaches a targeting peptide to the cap
  3. The mutation forces the mature ribosome into the organelle
  4. The shorter primary product may lack the amino-terminal targeting information
Answer and explanation

D. The shorter primary product may lack the amino-terminal targeting information

Alternative initiation changes the amino-terminal coding segment. Loss of a targeting signal can alter localization without changing transport machinery itself. Test the new product's amino terminus and restore the signal to strengthen causality.

55. Experimental interpretation

A purified unstructured RNA supports scanning in a defined assay lacking eIF4A and added ATP-dependent remodelling factors, whereas a matched structured RNA fails. Which conclusion is valid?

  1. eIF4E necessarily performs peptide-bond catalysis
  2. eIF4A is unnecessary in every cell
  3. The defined unstructured substrate can reduce the requirement for ATP-dependent RNA remodelling in this assay
  4. All cellular leaders are unstructured
Answer and explanation

C. The defined unstructured substrate can reduce the requirement for ATP-dependent RNA remodelling in this assay

The comparison establishes conditional sufficiency for the tested system. Cellular messages and accessory interactions vary. It is not a general claim of in vivo dispensability.

Key definitions

Establishment of a productive ribosome at a selected start codon.
Scanning
Inspection of candidate start positions by a small-subunit complex traversing the leader.

eIF2–GTP bound to charged Met-tRNAi.

Nucleotide environment that influences selection of a candidate start in the standard scanning mechanism.

Passage beyond a candidate start without initiating there.

Initiation after translation of an earlier ORF on the same RNA.

Recruitment through an internal RNA element rather than the usual end-associated entry route.

Formula sheet

Two-site scanning: N₁ = Np₁; N₂ = N(1 − p₁)p₂; Nnone = N(1 − p₁)(1 − p₂), assuming no dissociation or reinitiation and conditional probabilities p₁ and p₂.

Single-site flux: J = Rsp per messenger per unit time, where R is recruitment rate, s is survival to start and p is conditional start selection.

Frame comparison: two start positions a and b are in the same frame if (b − a) mod 3 = 0. If a shared in-frame stop begins at c, chain length from a is (c − a)/3 residues.

Energy accounting: in the specified canonical single successful event, one ATP→AMP for charging (two phosphate equivalents) plus two GTP→GDP reactions and any stipulated ATP-dependent remodelling. Count precharged tRNA separately when specified.

Mechanism map

Met-tRNAi + eIF2–GTP → ternary complex → 43S preparation; cap + eIF4F + accessory factors → mRNA recruitment; prepared PIC + accessible RNA → scanning → suitable start / 48S → eIF2 release and eIF5B-dependent joining → elongation-competent 80S.

Common misconceptions and traps

  1. The cap is not the start codon. It supplies an entry feature. The scanning complex can engage a capped RNA yet fail to select an appropriate AUG.
  2. The 43S and 48S are not two different ribosome subunits. Both centre on 40S. A start-selected 48S includes messenger and correctly placed initiator tRNA.
  3. AUG is not always the first translated triplet encountered. An upstream AUG with an unfavourable context may be bypassed.
  4. Kozak context is not a Shine–Dalgarno sequence. The former influences start acceptance in a scanning pathway; the latter commonly positions bacterial initiation by rRNA pairing.
  5. The A of AUG is +1. There is no position zero; −3 is upstream and +4 is the next coding nucleotide.
  6. eIF4B is not a core eIF4F member. eIF4F is defined by eIF4E, eIF4G and eIF4A; eIF4B assists RNA remodelling.
  7. eIF5 and eIF5B are distinct. eIF5 stimulates eIF2 GTPase activity. eIF5B is a joining GTPase.
  8. eIF2B does not phosphorylate GDP. It promotes nucleotide exchange on eIF2.
  9. The initiator enters the P site. The next aminoacyl-tRNA is accepted at A in elongation.
  10. Do not count a fixed ATP per scanned nucleotide. RNA-remodelling costs vary with the leader and the system.
  11. Physical 80S assembly is not sufficient evidence of elongation competence. Late factor release can still be defective.
  12. Cap independence is not automatically IRES activity. An internally recruited ribosome must be distinguished from recruitment at an alternative RNA end.
  13. A bicistronic reporter signal is not conclusive by itself. Cryptic promoters, RNA processing or cleavage and reinitiation are possible alternatives.
  14. Protein amount is not a pure measure of initiation. RNA abundance, elongation, protein stability and reporter activity can affect the result.
  15. Eukaryotic cytosolic rules do not describe every organelle. Mitochondria and plastids can have specialized initiation systems.

Active recall

Which two preparations converge before scanning?

A 43S PIC carrying initiator tRNA and an mRNA-associated recruitment assembly.

What is the three-component ternary complex?

eIF2, GTP and Met-tRNAi.

What resets eIF2 for reuse?

eIF2B-mediated GDP-to-GTP exchange.

Which three proteins define eIF4F?

eIF4E, eIF4G and eIF4A.

Why is eIF1 important?

It supports the scanning state and discrimination against unsuitable starts.

Why distinguish eIF5 from eIF5B?

eIF5 promotes the eIF2 GTPase step; eIF5B promotes joining and final maturation.

What happens to the initiator tRNA during factor release?

It remains positioned in the P site.

What positions dominate the standard vertebrate Kozak example?

A purine at −3 and G at +4.

How does a weak upstream AUG change downstream output?

It may be bypassed, allowing more complexes to arrive downstream.

What does a toeprint measure?

A positional reverse-transcription stop caused by a bound complex, with controls for structural pauses.

What evidence is needed for an internal initiation claim?

Demonstration of translation from the intended intact RNA with controls excluding alternative transcripts and entry routes.

What does a GTPase-defective eIF5B that remains bound suggest?

A late maturation defect despite possible physical joining.

Which state starts elongation?

An 80S complex with P-site Met-tRNAi, an available A site and productive release/rearrangement of initiation factors.

  • Draw the ordinary mRNA and label cap, leader, start, main coding region, stop, 3′ UTR and tail.
  • Explain initiator tRNA selection and distinguish aminoacylation from delivery.
  • Trace the eIF2 cycle and identify eIF2B and eIF5 functions.
  • Build 43S, explain recruitment by eIF4F, and describe scanning without assuming a pre-established frame.
  • Number Kozak positions and predict alternate-site outcomes.
  • Explain 48S commitment, eIF5B-mediated joining and the final 80S state.
  • Compare standard bacterial and cytosolic eukaryotic initiation while acknowledging exceptions.
  • Interpret factor-omission, toeprinting and reporter experiments with controls.
  • Solve conditional-probability and energy-bookkeeping questions with explicit assumptions.
  • Distinguish initiation, elongation, translation regulation and protein turnover.
