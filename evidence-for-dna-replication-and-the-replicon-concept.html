1. The biological problem: copying information without losing the original

A dividing cell inherits more than a collection of enzymes and membranes. It inherits the instructions needed to replace those components, regulate their production and respond to changes in its surroundings. In ordinary cellular organisms these instructions are carried in DNA. A chromosome is not consumed when a gene is expressed, but its information must be duplicated before a descendant cell can receive a complete genome. The challenge of reproduction is therefore distinct from the challenge of metabolism: producing two chemically similar cells is not enough unless each contains the information required to maintain its identity.

Imagine a bacterial cell containing a single completed chromosome immediately before the beginning of a round of genome duplication. If that chromosome were simply separated from the rest of the cell and passed to one daughter, the other daughter would lack the genetic basis for sustained growth. An increase in cell volume, synthesis of proteins and formation of a new membrane would not solve this deficiency. The cell must instead make another complete copy of the chromosome. After copying, the copies must be physically separated. Replication and segregation are related activities but they are not interchangeable. A cell can successfully synthesize DNA and still fail to distribute the resulting chromosomes correctly.

In a eukaryotic cell the same requirement is distributed over many chromosomes. Each nuclear chromosome needs to be copied before normal mitosis. During the S phase, a diploid nucleus normally increases its DNA content from 2C to 4C, where C denotes the DNA content of one haploid genome. This change does not mean that the cell has become tetraploid in the ordinary chromosome-set sense. A duplicated chromosome consists of two sister chromatids, which remain associated until they separate. The increase in DNA mass is therefore a quantitative consequence of replication, whereas the division of those chromatids into separate nuclei is a subsequent event. This distinction links the study of replication to the cell-cycle chapters and chromosome chapters of Unit 2.

At the molecular level, the task is to increase the number of copies of a base sequence. It is not to conserve every atom in every descendant molecule. Living cells continually replace atoms through metabolism. A new DNA molecule can have almost the same sequence as its parent while containing a completely different collection of atoms. Conversely, two molecules can contain the same total numbers of adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine yet encode very different sequences. When biologists ask how the parent DNA is conserved, they must specify whether they mean conservation of sequence, conservation of material or conservation of a particular structural unit such as a polynucleotide strand. This chapter is principally about the third question.

The need for sequence conservation also explains why ordinary DNA synthesis is guided by a template. A cell cannot produce a genome simply by joining the four nucleotide building blocks in the proportions present in the parent chromosome. Those proportions do not uniquely determine the order of bases. Consider two short strands, AAGCTT and AGACTT. They have the same nucleotide composition but their orders differ. A chemical analysis measuring total amounts of each base would not distinguish them. A copying process must preserve the position of individual bases, not merely reproduce their bulk composition. The paired structure of DNA offers a direct mechanism for specifying that order.

DNA replication is often introduced as a simple statement: one old strand and one new strand occur in each daughter duplex. Understanding that statement requires knowing what a strand is, what a template does, how a molecular label can reveal the history of a strand and why competing models predicted different patterns of parental material. The experimental history is especially important because the structure of DNA did not, by itself, constitute proof that cells used the most obvious copying mechanism.

2. Structural prerequisites: why a double helix suggests a copying mechanism

Each ordinary DNA strand is a covalently connected chain of deoxyribonucleotides. The repeating sugar-phosphate framework provides continuity along the chain, while the bases project from that framework and participate in pairing. The direction of the backbone is conventionally described by its 5′ and 3′ ends, which refer to carbon positions in the deoxyribose sugar. Neighboring nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester linkages. A double-stranded DNA molecule consists of two such chains associated through noncovalent interactions, principally complementary base pairing and base stacking.

The two chains in a normal DNA duplex are antiparallel. If one is written from 5′ to 3′ from left to right, its complementary partner runs from 3′ to 5′ in that same spatial direction. For example, if the upper strand is 5′-AGTC-3′, the aligned lower strand is 3′-TCAG-5′. Written in the usual 5′ to 3′ convention, the lower strand is 5′-GACT-3′. Confusing an aligned complement with a reverse complement is a common error in examination problems. The orientation of a sequence matters even if the names and number of its bases are unchanged.

The ordinary pairing rules are A with T and G with C. These relationships do not require a cell to remember the sequence independently of the DNA. Once an existing strand becomes accessible, its sequence can direct the choice of complementary nucleotides. The mechanism is not magical recognition of an entire chromosome. Each successive position provides a local chemical and geometrical constraint on the incoming nucleotide. In actual cells, polymerases and accessory proteins implement this templating process and greatly improve its reliability. Their detailed coordination will be considered in subsequent chapters [7].

Base pairing allows the two strands to act as complementary records of the same double-stranded sequence. One should not say that they are identical strings when both are read 5′ to 3′. In most cases they are reverse complements. Nevertheless, either intact strand contains sufficient information to reconstruct the corresponding partner. This built-in redundancy contributes to repair as well as replication. If a base on one strand is altered, the opposite strand can sometimes preserve the information needed to restore it. The validity of that repair logic depends on the nature of the lesion and on correct identification of the appropriate template, topics that will be studied later.

The stability of a duplex poses an apparent contradiction. DNA must be sufficiently stable to preserve information, yet sufficiently accessible for a copying apparatus to read it. The distinction between covalent and noncovalent bonds resolves much of the difficulty. Separation of the strands disrupts interactions between them without requiring wholesale cleavage of the sugar-phosphate backbones. The existing chains can therefore remain intact while complementary chains are synthesized. In a cell, strand separation is localized and controlled. The chromosome does not ordinarily need to become two completely naked single-stranded molecules before any copying can begin.

The B-form double helix also introduces a geometrical problem. Its chains wind around one another. If a region opens while the neighboring duplex is constrained, rotational strain is redistributed into surrounding DNA. This is particularly significant in circular chromosomes or DNA domains whose ends cannot rotate freely. The possibility of local separation does not eliminate this topological problem. Controlled changes in DNA topology and coordinated fork movement make progressive copying possible. The distinction between unwinding base pairs and changing the linking of DNA strands belongs to the physical foundations of replication; the detailed enzymes are reserved for the fork architecture node.

The 1953 double-helix model proposed by Watson and Crick, supported by structural and chemical evidence from several investigators, made complementary templating an especially attractive hypothesis [6]. If the two parental strands separated and each specified a new partner, each daughter duplex would retain an intact parental chain. This was a powerful inference from structure, but it was still an inference. The cell might have achieved copying through a more complicated process that rearranged parental material. An experiment that distinguished physical models was necessary.

3. Three competing models of parental strand distribution

For the classical comparison, consider one double-stranded DNA molecule at the beginning of a replication round. Label both original strands H for old or heavy material, and assume that all new DNA synthesized afterward receives label L for light material. The labels are conceptual markers at this stage; their physical implementation will be explained later. Three models can preserve the parent base sequence while distributing the original material in different ways.

In the conservative model, the original duplex is retained as a unit. One daughter duplex contains both of its original strands and the other contains two newly synthesized strands. The old molecule has somehow served as a source of information without permanently separating its two old chains into different products. Its defining prediction is an HH molecule alongside an LL molecule after the first complete round. Conservative does not mean that the cell refrains from making new DNA, nor does it imply that the sequence is copied more accurately. It refers to the proposed retention of both old strands together.

In the semiconservative model, the two parental strands serve separately as templates. Each becomes paired with a newly formed complementary strand. After the first round both daughter molecules are HL. The entire old duplex is not preserved as a single molecular object, but each of its two constituent chains is preserved in a different daughter. In the next round, each of those HL molecules yields one HL daughter and one LL daughter if the new nucleotide pool remains light. The old strand is physically inherited by only one daughter molecule, while both daughters acquire the necessary sequence information.

In the classical dispersive model, parental DNA is distributed in segments among both daughter molecules. Each daughter contains a mixture of old and newly synthesized material within its strands. After one complete round the average proportion of old material is one-half in each molecule. After a second round the average becomes one-quarter. In the simplest idealization, the dispersed segments are numerous and uniformly distributed, so the population exhibits one intermediate class whose average label progressively declines. Realistically, variation in the number and length of segments could broaden a distribution. For examination predictions, the standard homogeneous dispersive model is used unless a problem explicitly specifies another arrangement.

These three models differ in the identity of the physically conserved unit. The conservative model conserves an original double helix. The semiconservative model conserves parental single strands. The dispersive model conserves smaller segments, not intact old strands. The term dispersive refers to the distribution of parental material within the products, not to the random dispersal of entire chromosomes during cell division.

An important feature of all three models is that, under the assumption of no degradation or loss, the total quantity of original material remains the same as the population expands. Beginning with two heavy strands, the cell synthesizes two light strands in the first round, four additional light strands in the second round and eight additional light strands in the third round. The total material becomes four, eight and sixteen strand equivalents. The old fraction becomes one-half, one-quarter and one-eighth regardless of which physical copying model is used. Measuring only the overall fraction of heavy nitrogen therefore cannot distinguish these models. The decisive information is how the material is distributed among separate DNA molecules.

This is a useful general principle of experimental biology. Different underlying mechanisms can produce the same population average. To discriminate among them, one must measure a variable that is sensitive to the organization of the components. Measuring mean DNA density for an entire sample without separating molecules would conceal precisely the distribution needed to answer the replication question. A method that resolves distinct density classes can reveal whether the population is composed of two extreme classes, a fixed hybrid class or molecules whose density changes progressively.

A second distinction is between a description and an explanation. Calling replication semiconservative describes the distribution of strands. It does not specify how DNA opens, how a new chain is initiated or how a cell ensures timely genome duplication. Even after establishing the distribution, many mechanistic problems remain. The evidence in this chapter therefore provides the conceptual foundation for studying the enzymes in subsequent nodes; it does not replace their mechanisms.

4. The isotope-labeling strategy: marking molecular ancestry

Nitrogen is a constituent of the bases in DNA. Most naturally occurring nitrogen is nitrogen-14, which has seven protons and seven neutrons in its nucleus. Nitrogen-15 has the same number of protons but one extra neutron. Because the electronic structure of the neutral atom is essentially the same, these isotopes can participate in the same types of chemical bonds. Their differing masses provide a way to distinguish chemically similar molecules. Both nitrogen-14 and nitrogen-15 are stable isotopes. Nitrogen-15 is not a radioactive tracer.

Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl exploited this mass difference by studying DNA in Escherichia coli. The conceptual design was to establish an old population of DNA with an enriched heavy isotope label and then make newly synthesized DNA in an environment that supplied predominantly light nitrogen. As DNA was copied, the density of individual DNA molecules would reflect their mixture of old and new material. Their classic study was published in 1958 in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences [1].

The original bacteria were grown with a nitrogen-15 source long enough for DNA to become strongly enriched in the heavy isotope. Nitrogen provided in the growth medium entered cellular metabolic pathways and was incorporated into the nitrogenous bases synthesized for DNA. The label was not a special heavy chemical attached to the end of a chromosome. It was distributed through the DNA as part of its normal chemical composition. Growing the population in heavy nitrogen for multiple generations ensured that most pre-existing DNA molecules provided a useful heavy reference.

The cells were then transferred to a medium providing nitrogen-14. During subsequent genome duplication, newly produced DNA became predominantly light. Samples were collected as growth proceeded, and DNA isolated from them was separated by density. The experimental logic was to observe a changing molecular population while retaining information about the material from which each molecule had been built.

Switching the medium does not instantly transform old DNA from heavy to light. An isotope incorporated into a nitrogenous base remains part of that molecule unless a chemical process removes or replaces it. The new growth medium instead changes the source of material used for subsequent biosynthesis. In the idealized calculations used later, the switch is treated as instantaneous at the level of newly synthesized DNA, and existing heavy DNA is assumed not to be degraded. Actual cells possess intracellular precursor pools, and their isotope composition may not change instantaneously. Thus real band positions and shapes should be interpreted with calibration and knowledge of experimental conditions.

For a useful molecular ancestry experiment, a label should satisfy several requirements. It should be incorporated reproducibly, remain detectable long enough to follow the process of interest and not strongly disrupt the biological event being studied. It should also allow the old and new material to be distinguished by a measurement with adequate resolution. Stable isotope labeling is particularly elegant for this question because heavy and light nitrogen participate in the ordinary chemistry of DNA. However, a small isotope effect or imperfect labeling should not be dismissed solely on theoretical grounds. Reference samples and growth observations are valuable checks.

It is easy to confuse two different tracing strategies. One may first label old material and follow its dilution after a switch, as in this experiment. Alternatively, one may briefly label DNA synthesized during a selected time interval and follow the location of that material later. The former is particularly useful for distinguishing the distribution of parental strands. The latter is particularly useful for finding the positions where chromosome copying occurred during a pulse. Both strategies are used in the historical development of replication biology, but their experimental observations and calculations are different. The distinction will become important when interpreting chromosome autoradiography and DNA fibers.

5. Why buoyant density can separate chemically similar DNA molecules

A solution subjected to very rapid centrifugation experiences an effective outward field. In an appropriate cesium chloride solution, the dissolved salt becomes distributed nonuniformly along the radial direction until the outward tendency of the salt and its tendency to diffuse establish a stable gradient. The region farther from the axis of rotation has higher solution density. DNA placed in such a gradient does not merely accumulate indiscriminately at the outer end. A DNA molecule approaches the region where the surrounding solution has a density matching its effective buoyant density.

The idea can be understood by comparing the direction of movement in two regions. If the local solution is less dense than the DNA in its solvated state, there is an effective outward tendency. If the local solution is more dense, there is an effective inward tendency. Around the matching point, either displacement is opposed by a restoring tendency. Diffusion prevents all molecules from occupying exactly one mathematical position, so a molecular population forms a band with finite width.

The technique is called equilibrium density-gradient or isopycnic centrifugation. It differs conceptually from a separation in which a larger particle simply travels farther in a given time because of its sedimentation behavior. In equilibrium banding, molecular composition and effective density are central to band position. Molecular size can influence the approach to equilibrium and the width of the resulting distribution, but it is not correct to interpret a lower band simply as a longer DNA fragment. A short heavy DNA fragment and a long light fragment can have very different buoyant densities even though the light fragment has a greater total molecular mass.

Consider a DNA fragment composed of many nucleotides. Replacing nitrogen-14 with nitrogen-15 increases its mass while leaving its underlying chemical structure closely similar. Under the same solvent conditions this substitution produces a measurable shift in its buoyant density. DNA with mostly heavy nitrogen bands farther outward in the gradient than DNA with mostly light nitrogen. A molecule containing one heavy chain and one light chain has an intermediate density. The bands do not separately show the two strands while those strands remain paired in a duplex; the intact duplex bands according to its combined physical properties.

The original experiment used this ability to resolve differently labeled DNA populations. It is useful to imagine an ideal density axis with three reference positions: light at the lower-density end, hybrid near the midpoint, and heavy at the higher-density end. Textbook diagrams frequently show the heavy band toward the bottom of a vertically drawn tube. The meaningful physical interpretation is the increasing density along the gradient, not the arbitrary orientation of the printed diagram. Always identify the density axis before interpreting a graph.

Density is also sensitive to properties other than isotope content. The G+C content of double-stranded DNA affects its buoyant density in cesium chloride, and denaturation changes the effective density of DNA. A density comparison between unrelated DNAs cannot automatically be interpreted as a comparison of isotope enrichment. The clean experimental strategy compares the same organism's DNA under equivalent physical conditions with different labeling histories. A fully heavy reference and a fully light reference calibrate the relevant endpoints. A known mixture of the references tests whether the separation can actually resolve two molecular populations.

A band is evidence that many molecules have a similar density, not evidence that every molecule has an identical base sequence or length. If two different molecular structures have the same average isotope composition, they can occupy the same density class. This is why a single intermediate band after the first copying round cannot by itself discriminate between semiconservative and uniformly dispersive replication. The physical measurement is powerful, but its information must be combined with a prediction about molecular ancestry.

6. The first generation: eliminating the conservative prediction

Before any newly synthesized light DNA is present, all three ideal models predict one heavy band. This initial observation establishes the position of the heavy reference but does not favor any of the models. The experiment becomes informative only after copying has occurred using light precursors. It is important to understand what one generation means here. In the idealized problem, each original duplex has been copied completely once. In an actual growing bacterial population, the sampling time corresponds to growth measurements and DNA synthesis in cells that need not all be at exactly the same stage. The simplified generation diagrams describe completed molecular replication rounds rather than every intermediate structure present in the real culture [9].

Under the conservative model, the parental HH duplex persists and the newly produced LL duplex occupies the light position. After one round, half the completed molecules are HH and half are LL, assuming equal molecule lengths and equal recovery. The model therefore predicts two distinct density bands of approximately equal DNA quantity. There is no intermediate class. Mixing all the DNA together before making a bulk density measurement would produce an intermediate average, but equilibrium banding does not require those populations to merge into an average.

Semiconservative replication makes a different first-round prediction. Each heavy chain becomes paired with a light chain, producing two HL duplexes. Although the total amount of old label equals that in the conservative scenario, it is distributed between the two products. Every completed daughter duplex has approximately the same intermediate buoyant density. The expected pattern is therefore one hybrid band. Notice that the term hybrid in this setting refers to a duplex composed of one heavy and one light chain, not to a genetic cross between organisms or an RNA-DNA hybrid.

Dispersive replication also predicts an intermediate band after the first round. Under its idealization, each duplex contains equal amounts of old and new material, arranged as mixed segments. The buoyant density of an intact duplex cannot reveal whether its old material lies in one complete strand or is divided across both strands. Consequently, the observation of an intermediate band is not by itself a unique demonstration of semiconservative replication. An experiment must be evaluated against all the predictions that remain compatible with its result.

Meselson and Stahl found a population of intermediate-density DNA after approximately one generation of growth in light medium [1]. This observation was inconsistent with the simple conservative model because the anticipated separate heavy and light populations were absent at that stage. It was consistent with both the semiconservative and classical dispersive alternatives. The authors therefore continued to follow DNA through subsequent growth rather than treating the first intermediate band as a complete answer.

A useful exam technique is to write the possible molecules before drawing the bands. For a starting HH duplex, the first conservative products are HH and LL. The semiconservative products are HL and HL. The dispersive products are two mixed molecules, each with an average of one-half old material. Once the products have been specified, the density classes follow directly. Memorizing the appearance of test tubes without constructing the molecules makes it difficult to solve a modified problem in which the isotope switch is reversed or the starting DNA is already hybrid.

The absence of a distinct heavy band supports rejection of the conservative model only when the experiment has sufficient resolution and the starting labels are appropriate. Suppose the heavy and light reference densities could not be separated by the measurement. Two populations might then appear as one unresolved peak. A reference mixture tests this possibility. Alternatively, if the sample were taken before any meaningful new synthesis or after many generations in light medium, the comparison would have different power. A strong inference depends on the labeling design, timing and resolving ability together, not on the name of the instrument alone.

7. The second generation: distinguishing intact parental strands from dispersed segments

The second copying round supplies the critical difference between the remaining models. Start with the two hybrid molecules expected under semiconservative replication. In each hybrid, the heavy strand provides a template for synthesis of a new light partner. The original light strand also provides a template for a new light partner. Each hybrid thus produces one hybrid and one entirely light daughter. Starting with one HH duplex two rounds earlier, there are now four completed molecules, two HL and two LL. None are HH.

The semiconservative prediction is not that the hybrid band shifts progressively toward the light position. The composition of an HL duplex remains one heavy chain and one light chain in the ideal model. Its density is therefore approximately constant. What changes is the relative quantity of DNA in the hybrid band. A new light band appears, and as successive generations proceed it contains an increasing fraction of the population. This difference between the position of a band and its integrated amount is central to interpreting density data.

In the ideal dispersive model, each intermediate-density molecule produced in the first round contains mixed parental and new segments. During the next round these segments become diluted again throughout its daughter products. Each resulting duplex has an average of one-quarter original heavy material and three-quarters light material. The model predicts a new intermediate density closer to the light reference than the first-generation intermediate. It does not predict the combination of a fixed midpoint hybrid class and a separate fully light class that is characteristic of the simple semiconservative model.

The second-generation result of the classic experiment showed light DNA alongside the intermediate class [1]. The density of the intermediate class remained consistent with the earlier hybrid DNA. Continued growth increased the relative amount of light DNA. This result, together with the earlier observation and additional physical tests, supported semiconservative inheritance of parental DNA subunits rather than either classical alternative. It is appropriate to identify this as decisive evidence among the three conventional replication models. It is less appropriate to claim that a two-band photograph by itself logically excludes every hypothetical molecular mechanism one could invent.

One can understand the result by following the original heavy chains individually. Let them be H₁ and H₂. In the first round H₁ acquires a light complement L₁, and H₂ acquires L₂. During the next round H₁ remains in one new hybrid molecule, while L₁ becomes the old template of an entirely light molecule. Likewise H₂ remains in another hybrid, while L₂ templates a second entirely light molecule. The two ancestral heavy strands are still present, but they occur in only two members of a population that now contains four duplexes. The physical ancestry has become localized to a decreasing proportion of products.

This lineage reasoning answers a common puzzle. If every daughter molecule contains an old strand, why are some second-generation molecules entirely light? The word old is relative to the current replication round. The L₁ strand, synthesized in the first round, is an old template during the second round even though it does not carry the original heavy label. Thus a light-light molecule is perfectly consistent with semiconservative replication. It contains one strand inherited from its immediate parent and one strand synthesized during the current round. Being unlabeled with heavy nitrogen does not imply that both strands were made simultaneously.

Under ideal assumptions, each of the two original heavy chains persists in a different molecule regardless of how many additional rounds occur. In a population of eight completed molecules after three rounds, two remain hybrid and six are light. After four rounds, two remain hybrid and fourteen are light. The relative contribution of hybrid DNA decreases, while the absolute number of hybrid molecules descended from the original duplex remains constant. A real experiment samples portions of a culture and may use varying total DNA quantities, so absolute optical signal need not remain constant between plotted profiles. The theoretical molecular count describes the complete descendant population before sampling.

This is a recurring distinction in quantitative biology. A population fraction can fall even though the absolute amount of the relevant component does not decrease. A student who says that the number of old strands halves each generation confuses dilution with destruction. In the ideal experiment the original heavy strands are not destroyed; the total number of DNA strands doubles around them. Dilution is a change in the denominator.

8. Heat denaturation: asking whether the label lies in complete chains

A more direct way to investigate the structural meaning of a hybrid duplex is to separate its constituent strands. DNA denaturation disrupts the noncovalent association of complementary chains without ordinarily cleaving their entire covalent backbones under suitable conditions. When a duplex is heated sufficiently under appropriate solution conditions, its paired strands become separate single-stranded molecules. The original chemical label remains associated with the nucleotides that carry it.

The prediction from semiconservative replication is straightforward. A population of first-generation HL duplexes, after denaturation, should yield two kinds of chains: heavy and light. If the old heavy label instead were uniformly dispersed within each covalent strand, denaturation alone would not separate the heavy segments from the light segments. Each chain would retain its mixed composition and therefore exhibit an intermediate single-stranded density. This experimental perturbation turns the unit being measured from an intact duplex into its individual constituent chains.

The comparison illustrates how a structural hypothesis can be tested by selectively disrupting an interaction. The bond disrupted during denaturation is not the same as the bond whose continuity defines the inherited unit. Separating the two chains preserves the question of whether parental material is confined to a single chain. Cutting every strand into individual nucleotides would destroy that information. The effectiveness of the experiment depends on changing one level of molecular organization while retaining the next.

In the original Meselson–Stahl work, heat-denaturation observations supported the interpretation of hybrid DNA as containing distinguishable parental and newly synthesized subunits [1], [10]. Because denaturation itself changes the physical behavior of DNA, one must not equate the numerical density of a heavy single strand with that of a heavy duplex. Comparisons should be made between denatured samples and appropriately denatured heavy and light references. A diagram that shows heavy and light single-stranded bands at the same plotted locations as their corresponding duplex bands is acceptable only when its axis is schematic or separately calibrated.

There is a subtle distinction between proving that heavy material occupies distinct physical subunits and proving every detail of a chromosome's covalent architecture. The experiment examines DNA preparations containing molecules or fragments, and the resolution is finite. The observations were remarkably persuasive in conjunction with the double-helix structure and the predicted behavior of strands. They did not directly visualize every phosphodiester linkage across the entire chromosome or establish the identities of the proteins that perform replication. Later work on chromosome structure and replication intermediates addressed complementary questions.

Consider an examination scenario in which two hypothetical models both produce an intermediate duplex band. Model A predicts one fully heavy strand and one fully light strand; model B predicts that each strand contains a fine mixture of heavy and light nucleotides. A suitable next test would be controlled denaturation followed by density separation, not merely a repeat measurement of intact duplex density. The outcome should be compared with model-specific predictions. If two distinct single-stranded density classes are resolved, that supports model A. If the chains retain a single intermediate class, model B remains compatible, provided that denaturation is complete and the method can resolve the controls. The conclusion arises from the distribution of isotope after separation rather than from heating itself.

This style of reasoning applies well beyond DNA replication. Suppose a protein complex contains two different subunits with the same overall average label as a uniformly labeled protein. A bulk measurement alone might not distinguish them, whereas dissociation of the complex followed by analysis of its constituent polypeptides could. Experimental design often relies on choosing the appropriate level of organization at which alternative hypotheses give different predictions.

9. A general mathematical model of heavy-label dilution

Quantitative replication problems become easier when their assumptions are stated explicitly. Begin with N₀ completed, fully heavy, equal-length duplexes. Each duplex contains two polynucleotide chains. Assume that all descendants are retained, each existing duplex completes exactly one round of duplication per generation, newly synthesized chains contain only light nitrogen, parental chains are not degraded or exchanged, and no additional heavy nitrogen is incorporated. Let n be the nonnegative integer number of completed rounds after the isotope switch.

The number of completed duplexes after n rounds is N(n) = N₀ × 2ⁿ. This result follows because each duplex is replaced by two daughter duplexes in each complete round. The initial number of heavy chains is 2N₀. Under the model assumptions their number remains 2N₀. The total number of chains after n rounds is 2N₀ × 2ⁿ. Consequently the fraction of all strands that carry the original heavy label equals 2N₀ divided by 2N₀ × 2ⁿ, which simplifies to 2⁻ⁿ. For equal-length chains with equivalent labeling, this also describes the fraction of original heavy material relative to the total strand material.

To calculate the hybrid fraction, use the fact that each original heavy chain resides in a separate hybrid duplex once the first round is complete. Thus for n ≥ 1 the number of HL duplexes is 2N₀. The fraction of hybrid duplexes is 2N₀ divided by N₀ × 2ⁿ, or 2^(1−n). The number of fully light duplexes is N₀ × 2ⁿ − 2N₀, giving a light fraction of 1 − 2^(1−n). These expressions must not be applied to n = 0: before the first round the DNA is HH, not HL, and the formula would yield an impossible hybrid fraction of two. State the applicable range whenever presenting a formula.

The hybrid fraction is twice the fraction of heavy strands in this specific scenario. Each hybrid duplex contains one heavy chain among its two chains, so its contribution to the total heavy-strand fraction is one-half its contribution to the total duplex count. This relationship is not true for an arbitrary population containing heavy-heavy molecules. In the general case, let fHH, fHL and fLL be the fractions of duplexes in the three categories. Their sum is one, and the fraction of heavy chains is fHH + fHL/2. Derive this by counting the heavy chains contributed by each class: two per HH duplex, one per HL duplex and none per LL duplex. Dividing by two chains per duplex gives the weighted expression.

For example, consider a population beginning with 25 HH molecules. After three complete rounds, the population contains 25 × 8 = 200 duplexes. There are 50 HL molecules and 150 LL molecules. There are 50 heavy chains among 400 total chains, yielding a heavy-strand fraction of 0.125 or 12.5%. The hybrid fraction is 50/200 = 0.25 or 25%. A question asking for the percentage of DNA molecules containing heavy nitrogen therefore has a different answer from one asking what percentage of all chains are heavy. The denominator must be identified before substitution.

A second example can be solved in reverse. Suppose that a population initially contains only heavy duplexes, and a later sample contains 6.25% hybrid molecules and 93.75% fully light molecules. Under the ideal semiconservative model, 0.0625 = 1/16 = 2^(1−n). Equating exponents gives 1−n = −4, so n = 5. If instead 6.25% were the fraction of heavy strands, the equation would be 2⁻ⁿ = 1/16, giving n = 4. The same numerical percentage has different biological meanings according to what was counted.

The conservative model can be represented mathematically using the same assumptions about total DNA accumulation. At every completed round the original N₀ heavy-heavy molecules remain, and the other N₀ × (2ⁿ − 1) molecules are light-light. The heavy duplex fraction is therefore 2⁻ⁿ and the light fraction is 1 − 2⁻ⁿ. There are no hybrid duplexes in the simple model. The heavy-chain fraction is also 2⁻ⁿ because every heavy-containing duplex contributes two heavy chains. This equality of the bulk heavy fraction across both models is an explicit demonstration of why bulk isotope measurement is insufficient.

For the ideal dispersive model, all molecules possess the same average heavy fraction h(n) = 2⁻ⁿ. The quantity h is a composition variable within each molecule, not the proportion of a distinct class of fully heavy strands. If n is finite, and the old material is uniformly distributed across all molecules, every duplex contains some ancestral material. In a real finite sequence, the idea of indefinite uniform mixing eventually reaches a physical limit because nucleotide segments are discrete. The standard model is a simplified prediction over the early generations relevant to the original experiment, not a literal rule for infinitely many copying cycles.

A convenient way to map composition onto density in exam problems is the linear approximation ρ(h) = ρL + h(ρH − ρL), where ρL and ρH are the measured buoyant densities of the light and heavy references in the same physical state, and h is the fractional heavy-label composition. If the reference densities are 1.710 and 1.724 g cm⁻³ in a hypothetical calibrated experiment, a hybrid duplex has approximate density 1.717 g cm⁻³. A uniformly dispersive product after two rounds has h = 0.25 and density 1.7135 g cm⁻³. These values are illustrative; they are not reported measurements from the historical paper. The approximation treats the effects of labeling as additive under fixed solution conditions.

In this illustrative calibration, a two-generation semiconservative population contains equal amounts of DNA at 1.717 and 1.710 g cm⁻³. Its average density is 1.7135 g cm⁻³, precisely the value predicted for the single dispersive population at that stage. A conservative population containing one-quarter HH and three-quarters LL also has the same mean density: 0.25 × 1.724 + 0.75 × 1.710 = 1.7135 g cm⁻³. The equality is not accidental. All three ideal models have the same overall heavy-material fraction. Only the distributions, not their means, distinguish them.

Band intensity must also be interpreted carefully. In an ideal measurement proportional to DNA quantity, integrated band area is more useful than peak height for estimating the amount in each density class. A broad peak and a narrow peak of identical total DNA quantity may have different maximum heights. If all recovered fragments have the same length and comparable optical response, the DNA-quantity fractions correspond to molecule-number fractions. If lengths differ systematically, a larger number of short fragments could produce the same mass signal as a smaller number of long fragments. Thus the conversion from a density profile to a molecular count is conditional rather than automatic.

10. Reversing the isotope switch and tracking general starting populations

An understanding of strand ancestry permits more complicated isotope sequences to be solved without new memorized rules. Suppose that a culture begins with LL DNA and then receives heavy nitrogen. After one complete round, each daughter duplex is LH. After two rounds in heavy medium, each hybrid gives one LH daughter and one HH daughter. The appearance of a fully heavy class is now the mirror image of the classic experiment. Semiconservative replication does not intrinsically favor a particular isotope; the direction of the change is set by the label supplied to new synthesis.

A more instructive case begins with HH DNA, includes two completed rounds in light medium and then switches back to heavy nitrogen for one further round. Immediately before the second switch there are two HL and two LL duplexes descended from the single original. During the heavy round, each HL yields one HH and one HL because its heavy template receives a heavy partner and its light template receives a heavy partner. Each LL produces two HL duplexes. The eight products therefore consist of two HH and six HL, with no LL. The absence of LL reflects the fact that every product received a newly synthesized heavy chain during that last completed round.

The fraction of heavy-labeled chains in these final eight duplexes is (2 × 2 + 6)/16 = 10/16 = 62.5%. The fraction of duplexes containing at least one heavy chain is 100%. However, only two of the ten heavy chains are the original heavy chains present before the first switch. A density experiment cannot distinguish the newly introduced heavy isotope from the chemically identical original heavy isotope. A molecule that bands as HH in this final sample does not necessarily preserve the two ancestral heavy strands together. In fact, ordinary semiconservative replication predicts that the two original heavy strands remain separated in different molecules. Density class and genealogical ancestry become different kinds of information when a label is reused.

The general rule can be written for arbitrary initial counts. Let a, b and c be the numbers of HH, HL and LL duplexes respectively. In one complete round with exclusively light precursors, each HH gives two HL; each HL gives one HL and one LL; and each LL gives two LL. The resulting counts are a′ = 0, b′ = 2a + b and c′ = b + 2c. Their sum equals 2(a+b+c), as required by complete duplication. In one round with exclusively heavy precursors, each HH gives two HH; each HL gives one HH and one HL; and each LL gives two HL. The resulting counts are a′ = 2a + b, b′ = b + 2c and c′ = 0. The algebra follows directly from the templating relationship.

For example, a mixture starts with 40 HH, 20 HL and 40 LL molecules and undergoes one light round. There are 100 hybrid duplexes and 100 light duplexes in the 200-molecule product. A second light round generates 100 hybrid duplexes and 300 light duplexes. The hybrid fraction is now 25%. Applying the special formula 2^(1−n) without accounting for the mixed starting population would give the wrong result because that formula assumes that every starting molecule is HH. The general transformation is more robust than a shortcut.

One can check these calculations using conservation of heavy strands. The initial mixture contains 2 × 40 + 20 = 100 heavy chains. After the first light round the 100 hybrid duplexes contain exactly 100 heavy chains. After the second light round the number remains 100. If a proposed answer contained more than 100 hybrid duplexes after the first light round, it would imply creation of heavy chains despite the absence of a heavy nitrogen source. Conservation provides a quick independent check on the arithmetic.

The transformations also reveal a limitation of the first-generation test. A population initially composed of a mixture of heavy and light DNA can give a different pattern from a completely heavy starting population. Observing two density classes in such an experiment would not automatically contradict semiconservative copying. The correct interpretation requires knowledge of the initial state. In general, before deciding what a graph means, identify the labeling history, the starting population, the number of completed copying rounds, whether a band reports duplex or single-strand density and whether the ordinate represents molecule count or DNA quantity.

11. When the population does not complete a round synchronously

Textbook experiments often describe a generation as if every molecule duplicated at the same instant. This idealization isolates the difference between the competing replication models. Living populations are more complex. At an arbitrary sampling time, some chromosomes may have initiated but not completed replication, some may have completed a round, and some may contain more than one active replication bubble. The density measured from an isolated DNA preparation depends on both the state of the cells and the molecular form of the recovered DNA.

A simple hypothetical mixture demonstrates the effect without requiring advanced population models. Begin with 100 HH duplexes and suppose 60 complete one round in light medium while the other 40 do not initiate. Ignore partly copied molecules for this illustrative example. The 60 duplicated parental molecules produce 120 HL products; the remaining 40 stay HH. The resulting population contains 160 duplexes, 40 heavy and 120 hybrid. Thus the heavy duplex fraction is 25%, not 40%, even though 40% of the original molecules did not replicate. The reason is that the copied subgroup contributes twice as many completed products as the uncopied subgroup.

More generally, if a fraction p of N₀ HH molecules completes one light round and the remainder stays unchanged, the product count is N₀(1+p). The HH count is N₀(1−p), and the HL count is 2pN₀. Their respective fractions are (1−p)/(1+p) and 2p/(1+p). If a measured sample under these assumptions contains an HH fraction of one-third, solve (1−p)/(1+p) = 1/3. Multiplying gives 3−3p = 1+p, so p = 1/2. Half the original molecules completed the round even though only one-third of the final molecules are HH.

The simplified model excludes partially replicated chromosomes, which cannot always be classified as completed HH, HL or LL duplexes. A replicating chromosome contains both copied and uncopied regions. If the DNA is fragmented during preparation, different fragments from the same chromosome can have different labeling histories. This means the density profile can reveal molecular composition without specifying how many intact chromosomes are present or where each region lies. The physical state of the sample is part of the measurement model.

In rapidly growing bacteria, another complication arises when new rounds begin before earlier rounds have finished. The doubling time of the population may be shorter than the time required for two forks to complete copying one chromosome. Cells can solve this timing problem by initiating replication in advance on already duplicated origin regions. Such overlapping rounds alter the relation between cell divisions, DNA content and the number of active forks. A problem explicitly describing this arrangement should not be solved using a one-chromosome, one-round-per-division picture. The detailed initiation control is treated in the next node; the immediate lesson is that molecular copying rounds and population doubling times are not universally interchangeable.

A related difficulty is isotope carryover. If heavy nucleotide precursors persist inside cells after transfer to light medium, newly synthesized strands might contain a small amount of heavy nitrogen. This would shift their measured density away from the ideal light reference. Incomplete initial labeling can likewise shift the initial heavy band. Such effects are not evidence for a different strand inheritance mechanism unless the observed pattern remains incompatible after the precursor composition and other controls have been considered. Real experimental reasoning requires distinguishing failures of a molecular hypothesis from failures of the assumptions used to translate it into a measurement prediction.

The goal of the mathematical models in this chapter is not to reproduce every feature of the original density profiles. It is to make the logical differences explicit, derive exact answers under stated assumptions and teach the student to recognize when the assumptions have changed. In Part C problems, the most important step is often selecting the correct model of the population rather than evaluating a difficult expression.

12. Chromosome autoradiography: evidence from a eukaryotic cell

The bacterial experiment established a molecular pattern of inheritance by measuring the buoyant density of isolated DNA. Eukaryotic chromosomes presented a complementary opportunity: one could investigate whether labeled DNA becomes distributed between visible sister chromatids in successive cell cycles. An important study by J. Herbert Taylor, Philip S. Woods and Walter L. Hughes appeared in January 1957, before the publication of the Meselson–Stahl paper [2]. These experiments used root-tip cells of the broad bean, Vicia faba, and a radioactive form of thymidine to follow chromosome duplication. The chronology is worth noting because teaching accounts often explain the bacterial density experiment first for conceptual reasons, even though the chromosome study was published earlier.

Thymidine consists of the base thymine attached to deoxyribose. Cells can use thymidine through salvage metabolism to produce nucleotide precursors for DNA synthesis. In the Taylor experiment the thymidine carried tritium, a radioactive isotope of hydrogen. Newly synthesized DNA could therefore acquire a radioactive label during an interval in which the precursor was supplied. Unlike the stable nitrogen label used in the bacterial experiment, tritium can be detected through radioactive decay. This difference explains why the two experiments used different physical methods to obtain information about replication.

Autoradiography detects the spatial distribution of a radioactive substance by recording decay events in a sensitive photographic medium. In the chromosome experiment, grains formed in association with regions containing tritium-labeled DNA. A collection of grains along a chromatid indicated that DNA synthesized during the labeling interval contributed to that chromatid. The grains were not individually resolved nucleotide positions. The method reported the distribution of labeled material at a larger structural scale. In interpreting the results, one compares the labeling of sister chromatids rather than attempting to identify each separate nucleotide.

To follow the simplest predicted pattern, start with a chromosome containing an unlabeled duplex U/U. During the first S phase in labeled medium, each parental strand templates a newly synthesized radioactive partner R. The duplicated chromosome contains two sister chromatids, each with a U/R duplex. Both chromatids should therefore show radioactive labeling. They need not produce exactly the same number of grains in a particular image because radioactive decay and detection are probabilistic. The predicted symmetry is a property of the underlying incorporation of label, not a requirement for identical grain counts.

Now follow one of the first-generation chromatids into another cell cycle in unlabeled medium. Its duplex contains one U strand and one R strand. The U strand templates a new U strand, producing an unlabeled sister chromatid. The R strand templates a new U strand, producing a labeled sister chromatid. Thus the second duplication produces a chromosome with one labeled and one unlabeled chromatid, under the ideal assumptions of complete first-round incorporation and effective removal of radioactive precursor during the chase. The physical distribution of label is exactly what one would expect if intact parental strands are inherited into separate products.

Taylor and colleagues observed the characteristic transition from labeling of both chromatids to preferential labeling of one chromatid in the subsequent duplication [2]. Occasional exchanged segments complicated the simple pattern, providing evidence of sister chromatid exchange rather than invalidating the overall model. For a student, the most important lesson is that the label follows the history of DNA material within chromatids. It is not a permanent property of a chromosome number or homologous pair. At the second division a single previously labeled chromatid can give rise to one labeled and one unlabeled sister.

This is also why sister chromatids must be distinguished from homologous chromosomes. A homologous pair consists of corresponding chromosomes inherited from two parental lineages; the homologs can carry different alleles. Sister chromatids are the two products of replication of a particular chromosome. The prediction of one labeled and one unlabeled chromatid in the chase concerns sisters created by copying one previously labeled duplex. It does not require one maternal homolog to be radioactive and one paternal homolog to be nonradioactive. Confusing those relationships would produce incorrect diagrams of the experiment.

The conservative model would make a different ideal prediction during the initial pulse. If an old unlabeled duplex remained intact and a completely new radioactive duplex were made, one sister chromatid would contain unlabeled DNA and the other would contain labeled DNA. The dispersive model would initially predict label in both sisters, but would generally predict further distribution of labeled segments into both sisters during a subsequent unlabeled duplication. The chromosome-level observation therefore provides discrimination complementary to the density-band patterns. Its power arises from following the organization of label within physically identifiable products.

An important limitation concerns the actual size and organization of eukaryotic chromosomes. A large chromosome initiates synthesis at many regions, and different regions may be copied at different times during S phase. A short pulse will not label the entire length of both chromatids uniformly. To interpret the ideal two-cycle diagram, the question must assume that the radioactive precursor is available during the relevant DNA synthesis across the chromosome. If only a short interval is labeled, the first-generation pattern can be localized, and the chase should be interpreted for the labeled regions. Absence of grains from a late-replicating region during an early pulse does not show that the region is never replicated.

The experiment also illustrates the distinction between DNA replication and cytological chromosome duplication. DNA synthesis occurs during S phase, whereas sister chromatids are especially easy to distinguish in condensed mitotic chromosomes. An autoradiograph prepared during mitosis reports incorporation that occurred earlier. A student should not infer that mitotic condensation is the period when the observed DNA was synthesized. The timing of label administration and the timing of observation are separate variables.

13. What the two landmark experiments establish together

The results obtained in bacteria and plant cells are especially persuasive when considered as independent lines of evidence. A density gradient measures physical differences between extracted nucleic acid molecules. Chromosome autoradiography measures the location of radioactive material associated with chromosomal structures. The first technique distinguishes heavy, hybrid and light molecular populations; the second distinguishes the labeling pattern of sister chromatids. The two methods have different limitations. Agreement between their predictions therefore makes a common interpretation stronger than repeated use of a single measurement would.

One can organize the comparison around three questions. First, what is labeled? In the density experiment nitrogen in DNA bases is enriched with a stable heavy isotope before the switch. In the chromosome experiment a radioactive thymidine precursor marks DNA synthesis during a chosen interval. Second, what is measured? One method measures buoyant-density distributions and the other records the spatial distribution of radioactive signal. Third, what is the conclusion? Both support a pattern in which strands are inherited into separate products, but neither identifies the complete protein machinery, the detailed regulation of replication origins or the speed of individual forks.

The distinction between these questions is valuable when interpreting scientific experiments that appear in entrance examinations. An examiner may describe an experiment in an unfamiliar organism using a different label or detection technology. Instead of searching for a memorized name, identify the material marked, the time during which marking occurs, the physical signal recorded and the alternative predictions. A method can test semiconservative copying only if its observable is connected to the distribution of parental DNA. A general measurement of increased DNA quantity confirms synthesis but cannot by itself distinguish the three classical models.

A related issue is the difference between a marker's chemical identity and its spatial meaning. Stable nitrogen enrichment is a compositional property of DNA. Tritium incorporation is a radioactive marker of material synthesized during a pulse. A fluorescence signal from a nucleotide analogue used in modern chromosome-labeling experiments marks DNA that incorporated that analogue during exposure. None of these signals intrinsically distinguishes leading strands from lagging strands, nor does any intrinsically identify a replication origin. Additional spatial or temporal evidence is needed to establish those relationships.

The conclusions also need to be stated at the correct biological level. Semiconservative replication describes ordinary duplication of duplex DNA. Certain specialized synthesis processes, such as some recombination-associated DNA synthesis, can generate local inheritance arrangements that depart from the simple chromosome-duplication picture. Some viral genomes are single-stranded or use unusual intermediates. Those examples do not overturn the established mechanism of ordinary cellular chromosome replication. They instead remind us that a statement about the distribution of material has a specified substrate and process. Extrachromosomal replication strategies and recombination are treated later in this subunit.

By the late 1950s, the principal issue was no longer merely whether a duplex could be copied with one parental strand in each product. A new set of questions followed naturally: where does copying begin, how does it spread along a long molecule, and how does a cell arrange for every part of its genome to be duplicated in time? These questions require a spatial description of replication, and they lead directly to the vocabulary of origins, replication forks and replicons.

14. The origin: a location at which copying begins

The presence of complementary strands explains how sequence information can be copied locally, but it does not determine where the first base pair opens. If DNA synthesis were initiated at arbitrary positions with no means of coordinating the result, some segments might be copied several times while other segments remained unreplicated. Cells instead organize ordinary chromosome replication around controlled initiation events. A replication origin is a location or region of a DNA molecule at which a replication event begins.

For a small circular bacterial chromosome, a familiar model is initiation from one chromosomal origin followed by movement of copying machinery around the chromosome. The origin is not a physical end of the molecule because a circle has no ends. It is a biologically distinguished position within a continuous duplex. A linear eukaryotic chromosome also contains origins that are typically internal rather than simply at the telomeres. Origins define a starting location in a copying program, not the direction in which a chromosome is drawn on paper.

The term origin has both sequence-related and event-related meanings in molecular biology. In some well-studied systems, specific DNA sequences are recognized by proteins that organize initiation. In other systems, especially many multicellular eukaryotes, initiation can occur within broader regions whose usage depends on chromatin, cellular context and regulation. An experimentally observed origin marks where initiation occurred in a particular molecule or population. A potential origin is a region capable of participating in initiation under appropriate conditions. The two should not be treated as synonymous because a potential origin need not fire during every S phase.

A controlled beginning matters because the genome is long relative to the molecular machinery that copies it. If all regions became single-stranded simultaneously, the exposed bases would be vulnerable to inappropriate interactions and damage. Progressive opening and copying limit the length of DNA that must be handled in an unpaired state at any one position. Local initiation also creates an organized boundary between copied and uncopied material. The subsequent movement of that boundary makes it possible for the cell to duplicate a long template without first separating its two complete strands throughout the chromosome.

The earliest stages of an initiation event involve formation of an accessible region in the duplex and assembly of a functional copying apparatus. DNA helicase and other factors cooperate to expose template strands, and synthesis of new chains must be initiated. These are general principles rather than a complete description of an origin-initiation mechanism. The differences among bacterial initiators, eukaryotic origin recognition and licensing, and the roles of individual loading proteins belong to Node 3.A.02. Here, the origin is the starting point needed to understand the geometry of genome duplication.

It is useful to contrast an origin of replication with a promoter. A promoter participates in initiation of transcription, which makes RNA from a selected DNA template. A replication origin participates in initiation of DNA duplication, ultimately contributing to copying both parental strands. A gene can be transcribed many times while its containing region is copied only once during an ordinary eukaryotic S phase. An origin is not required to coincide with the beginning of a gene. The spatial and regulatory logic of these two processes differs because their biological goals differ.

A terminus is also distinct from an origin. A replication termination region is where the remaining copying fronts complete or meet, depending on the chromosome and mechanism. A termination position may be influenced by fork progression from neighboring origins and need not be an invariant sequence-defined site. When two equal-speed forks begin at one point on a circular molecule, the simplest geometrical prediction is that they meet approximately opposite the starting position. If the forks progress at different rates or encounter obstacles, the meeting position changes. Termination is an event determined by completion, not necessarily the recognition of an obligatory stop signal at an exact coordinate.

15. Replication bubbles and forks: the geometry of an ongoing reaction

A replication bubble is a locally expanded region of a DNA molecule in which the parental duplex has been opened and duplication is taking place or has taken place. The familiar diagram shows two boundaries enclosing an eye-shaped structure. These boundaries are replication forks. At each active fork, the uncopied parental duplex transitions into the two parental templates and the growing daughter duplexes. The term fork refers to the branch-like geometry of the replication intermediate, not to a single protein.

Consider a linear stretch of DNA drawn horizontally. If an origin in its center initiates bidirectionally, copying machinery progresses toward both ends of the stretch. The left fork moves toward lower coordinates and the right fork toward higher coordinates. Between them, each base-pair position has been represented in two daughter duplex regions. Outside them, the original unreplicated duplex still exists. The replication bubble therefore increases in length as the two moving boundaries move apart. When a fork meets a neighboring fork or reaches an appropriate terminal boundary, the geometry of the intermediate changes as synthesis is completed.

The shape of a bubble diagram can mislead a beginner into thinking that the two old strands remain bare throughout the expanded region. The lines drawn inside a bubble commonly represent daughter duplexes or the two parental backbones with new complementary strands added. The large open space is a schematic separation of the branches for readability; it does not mean that all DNA inside is persistently single-stranded. Immediately behind a functioning copying front, much of the newly formed DNA is already paired with its template. Unpaired regions occur transiently and are managed by replication factors.

The two new strands at a fork are not synthesized in opposite chemical directions. Ordinary DNA polymerases extend new DNA in the 5′ to 3′ direction, adding to an existing 3′ end. Because the parental strands run antiparallel, the geometry of a moving fork creates a coordination problem. One new strand can grow in the same overall direction as that fork's progression; the other must be produced discontinuously in segments whose individual extension is also 5′ to 3′. These are leading and lagging strand synthesis, respectively. The central point at this stage is that the direction in which a fork moves is not identical to the chemical direction of every new strand synthesized there.

At the left and right forks of a bidirectional bubble, the leading-template roles are exchanged between the two parental strands. Suppose the upper parental strand is drawn 5′ to 3′ from left to right. A fork moving to the right can use the lower strand, which runs 3′ to 5′ in that spatial direction, as the leading template. The left-moving fork can use the upper strand as its leading template. Thus a parental strand that serves as the leading template at one fork can serve as the lagging template at the opposite fork. The designation belongs to a local fork and direction, not to an entire chromosome strand independently of context.

The presence of two visible boundaries does not automatically prove that both are actively moving. A unidirectional replication intermediate can have an origin-proximal boundary that remains stationary while the active copying front moves away. An origin diagram may also show a paused fork. Determining whether a boundary is active requires temporal or molecular evidence, not simply counting Y-shaped lines. In a simplified examination diagram explicitly described as an expanding bidirectional bubble, two active forks are a justified assumption. If the question instead describes one moving fork, the same shape cannot be used to override the stated mechanism.

A fork can stall when the copying machinery encounters a DNA lesion, an unusually difficult physical structure or a limitation of the resources needed for synthesis. In the simplest geometrical model, a stalled fork stops moving while its partner may continue. The bubble then expands asymmetrically. This observation would not by itself reveal the cause of the stall. To connect a change in fork speed to a particular molecular defect, an experiment must use appropriate comparisons and ideally more than one indicator of replication activity.

The distinction between an origin and a fork can now be stated precisely. The origin identifies the event's beginning in the coordinate system of the DNA. A fork identifies a moving or potentially stalled copying boundary. The bubble is the region between boundaries created by the copying process. The replicon, introduced below, refers to a larger functional unit of DNA whose duplication is controlled by an initiation event. These concepts describe different properties of the same overall process and should never be substituted for one another.

16. The replicon model: a functional unit rather than a fixed length

The concept of a replicon emerged from the need to explain how genome copying could be controlled. The classical model associated with François Jacob, Sydney Brenner and François Cuzin distinguished a DNA element involved in controlling initiation from a regulatory factor acting on it. The model was presented in 1963, and its historical importance is discussed in a modern review of replication origins [3]. The conceptual innovation was to treat initiation as an event governed by interactions between information encoded in DNA and cellular molecules capable of recognizing or acting upon that information.

A replicon is a unit of DNA whose replication is controlled from an origin or initiation event. In the simplest single-origin system, the entire DNA molecule copied from that origin constitutes one replicon. In a chromosome with multiple origins, each origin that initiates contributes to copying a region extending to the positions where its forks finish or meet forks from neighboring origins. The term is functional: it describes the organization of genome duplication rather than a specific number of base pairs. Different replicons can have different lengths, and the effective region replicated from an origin can vary when neighboring origins are activated at different times.

The related word replicator refers to a cis-acting DNA element that participates in directing initiation in the classical genetic model. Cis-acting means that the element exerts its primary effect on the DNA molecule on which it resides. An initiator is a trans-acting factor, commonly a protein, capable of recognizing or acting on suitable DNA substrates. Trans-acting means that the factor need not be encoded immediately adjacent to the origin on the very molecule whose initiation it regulates. It can be synthesized elsewhere and reach its target through cellular processes.

The distinction is easiest to see with two conceptual DNA molecules in the same cell. Suppose molecule A carries a functional replication-control element and molecule B carries a defective version. If a suitable initiator is present in the cell, it may support initiation on molecule A but cannot necessarily repair the local DNA defect on molecule B. Supplying an additional normal initiator gene on another DNA molecule can rescue a deficiency of the diffusible protein in an appropriate system. Supplying an unrelated DNA sequence elsewhere does not automatically replace the defective cis-acting site on molecule B. The contrast helps explain why genetic complementation can distinguish local sequence requirements from diffusible factor requirements.

This reasoning is not a claim that every experimentally observed origin consists of one short unique sequence or that every organism uses the same initiator. Origin architecture and regulation vary among organisms. The useful core of the replicon concept is the connection between a regulated initiation event and the DNA whose copying depends on that event. The molecular details of origin recognition in bacteria and eukaryotes are treated in the next node, where the limitations of applying a simple bacterial model to every chromosome become especially important.

A replicon is not synonymous with a gene. A single replicon may include hundreds or thousands of genes as well as noncoding DNA. Genes are units associated with functional products and regulation of expression, whereas replicons are units of DNA duplication. A replicon is also not necessarily synonymous with a chromosome. A chromosome may contain a single conventional replicon or many. Conversely, an extrachromosomal DNA molecule can constitute an independently controlled replicon if it possesses or can use an appropriate system for its replication. Detailed examples involving plasmids and organelles belong to Node 3.A.06.

Nor is a replicon synonymous with a replication fork. A typical bidirectional replicon contains two active forks during part of its duplication. A unidirectional replicon can have one active fork. The number of forks describes the number of advancing copying fronts at a specified time, whereas the number of replicons refers to the functional organization of DNA copying. A replicon can exist as a potential or defined unit before its origin is activated, and its forks cease to exist as active copying boundaries once duplication is complete.

In simple textbook diagrams, the borders of adjacent replicons are often shown midway between neighboring origins. That midpoint is appropriate only if the origins fire at the same time and both approaching forks have equal speeds. If one origin fires earlier, its fork gains a head start, and the meeting position shifts toward the later origin. If one fork is slower, the other may copy a larger share of the interval. A useful mental picture is that neighboring initiations compete to cover the intervening DNA; the functional completion boundary emerges from their timing and progression.

This flexible interpretation is essential in eukaryotic cells. Many possible initiation sites are not used in every cycle. A region containing a potential origin may be copied passively by a fork from a neighboring origin before the potential origin initiates. Counting all possible origin sites and equating that count with the number of actual initiation events would overestimate the number of active replicons in the sampled cycle. Conversely, a microscopic replication focus may contain activity associated with several origins and cannot automatically be counted as a single replicon.

17. Evidence for replication intermediates and multiple origins

A density experiment can establish how parental material is distributed but cannot determine the coordinates at which copying starts. The next historical advance required methods capable of examining long DNA molecules while they retained recognizable spatial relationships. In 1963 John Cairns published autoradiographic observations of DNA from Escherichia coli [4]. The work revealed long circular bacterial DNA molecules, including branched replication intermediates resembling the Greek letter theta. A theta-shaped intermediate consists of duplicated paths and a remaining unreplicated path connected at branch points. It is direct structural evidence that genome duplication can progress along a circular molecule rather than requiring instantaneous copying of the entire circle.

The geometry matters because a circular chromosome with an internal region of duplication cannot be represented simply by two fully separated rings until replication is complete. At an intermediate stage, the two copies share a remaining unreplicated region. In a simplified drawing, one path connects the two branch points through DNA that has not yet been duplicated, while two other paths represent the copied region. Counting all three paths as three complete chromosomes would confuse an intermediate with its products. The final products require completion of copying and resolution of the physical connections between them.

Cairns' images did not by themselves establish that every circular bacterial chromosome uses exactly the same origin and fork program. Nor does a theta-shaped structure alone identify which branch points are advancing, because a static image reports geometry rather than velocity. Changes in labeling over time and subsequent studies of replication patterns established the usual bidirectional behavior of E. coli chromosome replication. The distinction between an observed physical structure and the inferred movement of that structure is important. A still picture of a moving object does not uniquely determine its direction unless the picture also contains information that can be related to a temporal sequence.

A related approach was applied to mammalian chromosomes by Joel Huberman and Arthur Riggs in the 1960s. DNA fiber autoradiography made it possible to examine the distribution of label along extended chromosome-derived DNA and to infer patterns of initiation and fork progression [5]. The observation of multiple replication regions within long chromosomal fibers supported a model of multiple initiation events along a eukaryotic chromosome. This arrangement helps solve the practical problem posed by chromosome length. A cell need not wait for one copying apparatus to travel from one end of a very large chromosome to the other.

Modern DNA fiber assays illustrate the same reasoning with two distinguishable nucleotide labels rather than a single radioactive signal. In an ideal experiment the first label is supplied for a known time interval and a second label for a subsequent interval. Newly synthesized regions become marked according to the interval during which they were copied. If one continuous fiber shows a central segment marked by the first label with segments of the second label extending outward on both sides, the pattern is compatible with bidirectional copying from a region that initiated during or before the first pulse. In contrast, an isolated sequence of first-label material followed by second-label material on one side records a single observed direction of fork progression. The first pattern is particularly informative when the two outward tracks are known to be contiguous and originate from the same local event.

A second-label region bounded by first-label regions on the two sides may indicate convergence of forks that approached from opposite directions. To see why, imagine two forks moving toward each other. During the first pulse each produces labeled DNA on its own side of the intervening interval. During the second pulse the forks extend farther inward, creating later-labeled segments nearer the convergence zone. The time order of the colors, rather than the apparent symmetry alone, supplies evidence of direction. An origin and a termination event can both give a symmetrical pattern, but their temporal ordering is opposite.

These interpretations require controls. The duration of the pulses must be known, the nucleotide labels must be distinguishable, and the conversion between measured fiber length and DNA length must be calibrated. DNA fibers may break during preparation. A single track with label on only one side could represent a broken bidirectional event rather than a genuinely unidirectional origin. A very short track may reflect a late initiation within the pulse rather than a slow fork. When evaluating a hypothetical dataset, ask whether track continuity, pulse duration, origin timing and detection thresholds have been specified. The measured length of newly synthesized DNA is useful only in conjunction with the interval over which it was synthesized.

One can also distinguish replication intermediates by methods that separate DNA molecules on the basis of their size and shape. A linear unreplicated DNA fragment and a branched fragment of comparable mass may behave differently during separation. In principle, an internal initiation event generates an intermediate with a bubble inside the fragment, whereas a fork entering from a neighboring region produces a Y-shaped intermediate. The basic inference is that the geometry of the intermediates can distinguish an origin within a fragment from passive replication by an outside fork. Detailed experimental protocols and specialist interpretation of complex intermediates are unnecessary for the present syllabus; the relevant principle is that molecular shape provides information independent of isotope composition.

Taken together, the historical studies show how the replication problem was separated into tractable questions. Isotope distribution answered the question of strand ancestry. Chromosome labeling extended the conclusion to eukaryotic chromatids. Physical images and fiber labeling addressed the progression of replication along DNA. Each technique was selected to make a particular hidden biological variable observable. No single one supplies every feature of the modern replication mechanism.

18. Bidirectional and unidirectional copying: comparing strategies quantitatively

Let a DNA molecule have total length L base pairs. Let v denote the rate of progression of an active replication fork, measured in base pairs of template traversed per unit time. A fork traversing v base pairs during one second synthesizes complementary DNA on both parental templates in that region. Thus its geometric progression is v base pairs per second, but the number of newly incorporated nucleotides associated with complete copying of that region is approximately 2v per second. The distinction prevents a frequent factor-of-two mistake when interpreting a rate described in base pairs rather than nucleotides.

In an ideal unidirectional replication event beginning at a suitable boundary, one active fork must traverse the entire length L. If the speed is constant and initiation and termination delays are ignored, the completion time is T = L/v. In an ideal bidirectional event on a circular molecule with two forks leaving the same origin, the two copied distances add to L. If both forks move at speed v and initiate together, they each traverse L/2, and the time is T = L/(2v). The advantage of two forks is a doubling of the total geometric coverage rate, not a change in the chemical direction of DNA synthesis.

For a hypothetical circular chromosome of 4.8 million base pairs and an equal fork speed of 800 bp per second, a unidirectional round would require 4,800,000/800 = 6,000 seconds or 100 minutes. Two simultaneously active forks would require 4,800,000/1,600 = 3,000 seconds or 50 minutes. The number of newly synthesized nucleotides is the same in both cases: complete duplication of one duplex requires approximately 9.6 million new nucleotide residues. Bidirectionality changes the time and spatial organization of synthesis, not the total amount needed to duplicate the original sequence.

If the two forks have unequal constant speeds v₁ and v₂, their covered distances after time T are v₁T and v₂T. On a circle they meet when the sum equals the circumference L, so T = L/(v₁+v₂). For example, if L = 1,200 kb, v₁ = 40 kb per minute and v₂ = 20 kb per minute, completion requires 20 minutes. The first fork travels 800 kb and the second travels 400 kb. The meeting point is not diametrically opposite the origin because the first fork covers twice as much DNA. A question that assumes a fixed termination barrier would require a modified model, since one fork might reach the barrier before the other.

A linear chromosome with a single internal origin requires slightly different reasoning. Suppose the origin divides the chromosome into a left interval of length a and a right interval of length b. The left fork cannot usually continue around the end and copy the right interval, as it might in a simplified circular geometry. If the two speeds are vL and vR, complete copying of the region requires the larger of a/vL and b/vR, neglecting chromosome-end processing. The first fork may finish well before the second. If a 300-kb linear molecule initiates 100 kb from its left end and both forks move at 2 kb per minute, the left arm requires 50 minutes and the right arm 100 minutes. The completion time is therefore 100 minutes, not 300/(2×2) = 75 minutes. The latter expression incorrectly assumes that the two forks remain equally productive until completion.

The optimal single-origin location under constant equal speeds would be the midpoint, because that placement makes the distances to the two ends equal. With unequal speeds, the origin position that balances the finishing times satisfies a/vL = b/vR and a+b=L. Substitution gives a = LvL/(vL+vR), and b = LvR/(vL+vR). This is a geometrical prediction, not a claim that real chromosomes choose their origins by solving an optimization equation. The useful biological interpretation is that a faster copying front can cover a longer arm within the same time.

Now consider a bidirectional bubble on a linear segment in which neither fork has yet reached an end or neighboring fork. At time t after initiation, its copied span is B(t) = (vL+vR)t if both forks have progressed at constant speeds. When vL = vR = v, the span grows at rate 2v. If a bubble has length 60 kb at 15 minutes after simultaneous initiation and both forks have equal speeds, each fork has moved 30 kb, giving v = 2 kb per minute. If one fork instead moves 40 kb and the other 20 kb, the total bubble length is unchanged but the origin lies asymmetrically within the bubble. The total span alone does not identify each individual fork speed.

A useful hypothetical pulse-label example follows. A continuous DNA fiber displays first-label and second-label tracks belonging to one known rightward fork. The second pulse lasted 20 minutes and the associated track is 30 kb long. The average geometric rate during that pulse is 30/20 = 1.5 kb per minute, or 25 bp per second because 1 kb = 1,000 bp and 1 minute = 60 seconds. A second fork produces a 42-kb track under the same pulse, giving 2.1 kb per minute. The ratio of the measured rates is 1.4. If the second track were part of two outward-moving forks instead of one, the computation would have to use the length attributable to each fork. Track geometry determines the denominator and the meaning of the rate.

The formulas are deliberately ideal. Real forks change speed, origins activate at different times and copying apparatus can pause. If speed varies during the experiment, track length divided by pulse time reports an average over the relevant period, not the instantaneous speed at the end. If a fork begins halfway through a pulse, using the entire pulse duration underestimates its rate. If the track terminates because it reaches another fork, the same problem occurs. Such considerations are not exceptions to arithmetic; they are limitations on the mapping from a biological observation to a mathematical variable.

19. Multiple origins and the completion of a large genome

Eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes are generally much larger than a bacterial chromosome. Copying from one origin per chromosome would often take too long relative to the available S phase. Multiple initiation events allow replication to proceed in parallel on different segments of the same DNA molecule. The organization is analogous to several teams working on different sections of a long road, provided that the finished regions meet without leaving gaps. The analogy concerns coverage in space and time, not the biochemical mechanism of the copying machinery.

Suppose an ideal chromosome of length L is partitioned into m identical nonoverlapping replicons, each with an origin at its midpoint and two equal-speed forks. If all origins activate simultaneously, the length assigned to each origin is L/m. Each fork traverses L/(2m), so T = L/(2mv). This equation gives a simple reason why increasing the number of simultaneously operating origins can greatly reduce completion time. It assumes equal partitions, equal constant speeds, no initiation delay and no unusual end effects. If any assumption fails, the time to finish is determined by the slowest remaining region rather than by the average partition alone.

Consider a hypothetical 60-Mb chromosome with forks progressing at 1.5 kb per minute. If one central origin initiates bidirectionally, its completion time would be 60,000 kb divided by 3 kb per minute, or 20,000 minutes. If 100 regularly spaced origins initiate together, the ideal time falls to 200 minutes. Each replicon covers 600 kb, and each fork traverses 300 kb. At the beginning of this idealized interval there can be 200 active forks, although their number declines when regions finish. The chromosome does not become 100 separate chromosomes: the different initiation events occur along one chromosome and their products ultimately form the two sister chromatids.

If a question asks for the minimum number of ideal simultaneous origins required to complete a genome by a specified time Tmax, the capacity calculation gives m ≥ L/(2vTmax). The minimum integer is obtained by rounding upward. For a 48-Mb molecule, v = 2 kb per minute and Tmax = 180 minutes, the ratio is 48,000/(2×2×180) = 66.666…, so at least 67 ideal origins are needed. This is a capacity lower bound rather than a measured biological origin count. Real initiation schedules, unequal inter-origin distances and fork stalls could require more events or a different arrangement.

The joining of neighboring bubbles explains why the number of active forks changes as S phase proceeds. Imagine three adjacent origins initiating bidirectionally on the same linear molecule. Immediately after initiation, six active forks can be present. The rightward fork from the first origin approaches the leftward fork from the second origin. When those two converge, they no longer constitute two active fronts, and the duplicated regions become contiguous. A similar event occurs between the second and third origin. Under this simplified sequence, the number of active forks decreases from six to four to two as the internal intervals complete. The two outermost forks can continue toward the terminal regions.

Counting bubbles and counting initiation events are therefore different measurements. After two neighboring bubbles merge, the visible duplicated region may appear as one larger continuous bubble even though two origins initiated its duplication. Conversely, the presence of an unactivated potential origin within a region does not add an active bubble. A snapshot of a replicating chromosome is a record of the current geometry, not a complete record of all initiation events that have occurred.

For two adjacent origins at positions 0 and D, assume both launch forks toward one another at equal speed v. If they activate together, the forks meet at D/2 after D/(2v). If the second origin fires Δ minutes later, and the first fork has not yet reached it, the first fork travels vΔ before the second starts. The remaining distance is D−vΔ. After the later initiation, the two forks meet after an additional (D−vΔ)/(2v) minutes. The meeting coordinate measured from the first origin is (D+vΔ)/2. This equation explicitly demonstrates why an effective replicon boundary can move toward a later origin.

For example, two origins are 100 kb apart, each fork moves at 2 kb per minute, and the second origin fires 10 minutes later. The first fork has already covered 20 kb when the second starts. The remaining 80 kb is copied at a combined rate of 4 kb per minute, requiring 20 more minutes. The meeting occurs 30 minutes after the first initiation, at coordinate 60 kb from the first origin. If the second origin waited 50 minutes or more, the first fork could reach its position before it fired. In an ordinary regulated system, the potential origin might then be passively replicated rather than initiate a redundant round in the same region.

This simple calculation captures a central distinction between the possible architecture of origins and their actual use. A chromosome can contain many sites capable of initiation but only a subset may be necessary in a particular S phase. Some origins can provide alternatives when copying elsewhere is delayed. The biological purpose of this arrangement is reliable completion of genome duplication under varying cellular conditions [8]. The detailed distinction between licensing an origin and activating it, and the mechanisms preventing inappropriate reinitiation, are developed in the following chapter.

20. Integrating chromosome duplication, variation and cell-cycle control

The geometry of replication should finally be connected to the fate of the cell. Copying DNA accurately is a necessary condition for faithful inheritance, but accurate synthesis over only part of the genome is insufficient. Every essential region must be represented in both chromosome copies before normal segregation. A stalled fork may leave a region unreplicated even if every newly synthesized base elsewhere is correctly paired. Conversely, a chromosome may be fully duplicated but contain a sequence error. Completion and fidelity are distinct dimensions of replication quality.

Cell-cycle control must coordinate initiation, completion and division. If a cell enters mitosis with substantial unreplicated DNA, mechanical separation of the chromosome copies can become difficult or damaging. If origins initiate repeatedly within a region that has already been copied, DNA content may increase without producing the balanced set of chromosome copies needed for inheritance. The mere observation of increased DNA quantity is therefore not evidence of properly controlled replication. A combination of DNA-content measurements, local replication markers and chromosome observations can help distinguish these conditions.

A conceptual comparison of organisms further clarifies the role of the replicon. E. coli is a useful single-chromosomal-origin model, but bacterial genomes are diverse. Some bacteria have more than one chromosome or linear chromosomes. Many archaea use multiple initiation origins on a chromosome, while the origin organization of eukaryotic chromosomes is generally more complex. The presence or absence of a nucleus does not by itself specify the number of replicons. Each system must be understood in terms of its chromosome architecture and initiation program.

The principles have practical significance in genetics and biotechnology. An independently maintained DNA construct must be able to obtain the replication functions needed for its persistence in a particular host; an appropriate origin or replication-control region is part of that requirement in ordinary plasmid systems. A construct that enters a cell but cannot replicate can become diluted during subsequent cell divisions, even if it initially expresses a gene. Likewise, measurements of DNA synthesis in growing cells are used to distinguish replicating from nonreplicating populations and to investigate defects that alter genome completion. These applications use the same distinction between material, location and time developed throughout the chapter. Detailed vector design and diagnostic procedures lie outside the current node.

The conceptual framework also provides a foundation for understanding mutations. An error in an initiation-control region can reduce or alter initiation from that region. An alteration in a factor needed for fork progression can instead slow copying after it has begun. These possibilities can produce similar reductions in overall DNA synthesis but different spatial patterns. Fewer initiation events with approximately unchanged fork speed suggests a different immediate explanation from unchanged initiation frequency with shorter tracks per unit time. Neither observation alone identifies a specific protein defect; it narrows the possible mechanisms and motivates further evidence.

A student can now separate four levels of a replication problem. Strand ancestry identifies the relationship between old and newly made DNA. The origin determines where a copying event begins. Forks and bubbles describe the geometry and progression of that event. The replicon identifies the functional unit of copying associated with initiation. Quantitative reasoning connects these levels to the number of molecules, amount of label, size of copied regions and time required for completion. The following chapter examines how initiation events are selected and organized at the molecular level. The established evidence for semiconservative replication remains the foundation on which that mechanistic account is built.

Revision and examination traps

  • Strand ancestry: write the actual daughter duplexes before predicting density. For HH → light, first generation HL only; later generations HL plus LL.
  • Use n≥1 for fHL=2^(1−n). The fraction of originally heavy chains is 2^−n. In mixed populations, fH=fHH+fHL/2.
  • Band position reports buoyant density; integrated area approximates DNA quantity with suitable calibration. Equal mean densities can conceal distinct distributions.
  • Heat denaturation distinguishes intact heavy/light chains from a uniformly mixed chain; compare denatured reference samples.
  • Taylor: label both sisters in the initial complete pulse, then one sister after the chase, apart from exchanges or imperfect labeling.
  • Origin is a location; fork is a copying boundary; bubble is a locally copied region; replicon is a functional unit of duplication.
  • Bidirectional does not mean 3′→5′ synthesis. Both new strands grow 5′→3′.
  • Circle: T=L/(v1+v2). Linear internal origin: T=max(a/vL,b/vR). Ideal m balanced origins: T=L/(2mv).
  • With equal inward speeds and delay Δ, the meeting coordinate is (D+vΔ)/2 if the second origin fires before passive copying.
  • More than one potential origin does not guarantee that each fires; snapshots of bubbles do not count historical initiation events.
