Extracellular Matrix: Composition, Organization and Cellular Roles

The molecular organization, physical properties and cellular functions of animal extracellular matrices.

1. The environment beyond the plasma membrane

An animal tissue is not simply a collection of cells arranged side by side. Consider a tendon, which must transfer the force generated by a muscle to a bone. Individual tendon cells cannot themselves form a continuous, strong cable spanning that distance. They produce extracellular material that can carry a substantial part of the load. In cartilage, by contrast, cells are embedded in a hydrated material that resists compression. An epithelium contains closely packed cells, but even there a thin extracellular supporting layer provides a distinct basal surface. These examples introduce the central problem of this chapter: how do cells build a material outside their plasma membranes that performs specialized functions and also changes how the cells behave?

The extracellular matrix, abbreviated ECM, is an organized network of extracellular macromolecules made by cells. In animal tissues it consists principally of proteins and carbohydrate-rich molecules, together with associated water and ions. Its composition varies according to tissue function. A matrix should not be equated with every substance outside the cell. Interstitial fluid contains small dissolved molecules that exchange with blood and cells, whereas the ECM includes relatively persistent macromolecular assemblies within which some of that fluid is held. A circulating hormone in the fluid may temporarily bind the matrix, but that does not turn every circulating hormone into a structural matrix component.

The plasma membrane separates the cytoplasm from the extracellular environment. Matrix proteins are usually delivered to the extracellular side, where they associate with one another and with cell surfaces. Thus the location where a matrix protein is translated and the location where it performs its mature biological role are often different. Cells can deposit new material, remove old material and reorganize existing networks. Unlike an inert artificial support, the matrix has a history. It reflects the activity of the cells that built it and the physical conditions under which assembly and remodeling occurred. A newly arriving cell can therefore encounter extracellular information left by other cells.

It is useful to distinguish cell adhesion to another cell from cell adhesion to a substrate. In cell–cell adhesion, the molecules on neighboring plasma membranes contribute directly to an attachment between cells. Cadherins provide a familiar example. In cell–matrix adhesion, a membrane receptor on a cell recognizes a ligand in an extracellular network. That ligand might be an adhesive glycoprotein or a collagen molecule. One network can bind many cells, permitting physical continuity between cells that do not actually touch. The categories are not mutually exclusive within a tissue: an epithelium uses cell–cell contacts laterally and cell–matrix contacts basally.

Imagine a sheet of cells placed over two substrates of identical chemical composition but different mechanical compliance. If a cell exerts traction on a substrate, a compliant material may deform relatively easily, whereas a stiff material may resist the same force. The cell's attachment sites and internal cytoskeleton experience different mechanical conditions. In an otherwise appropriately controlled setting, the cells can respond differently even without a change in soluble hormone concentration. The important general principle is that an external physical property can become biologically meaningful when a cell has a means of detecting or responding to it. The substrate itself need not be a living cell or a conventional secretory signaling organ.

The cell-facing surface of a matrix supplies chemical information too. An adhesive receptor may recognize a specific protein domain, an exposed amino acid sequence or a composite conformation. Which ligands are accessible depends on how proteins are assembled, their orientation, and whether surrounding material masks them. Two matrices with similar total collagen can differ in the distribution of accessible attachment sites, in fiber organization or in the proportions of their other constituents. As a consequence, a chemical assay reporting bulk concentration cannot by itself describe all the biological information available to a cell.

A further distinction is between an individual extracellular molecule and a matrix material. A purified collagen molecule has a characteristic molecular structure, but a tendon has mechanical properties that depend on many collagen molecules assembled over different length scales. Neighboring fibrils, their connections, their alignment and their interaction with hydrated material all matter. Conversely, an individual elastin segment has flexible regions, but elastic recoil of an artery requires a continuous organized network of elastic fibers. Tissue behavior cannot always be predicted by considering only the isolated constituent. The arrangement of constituents is part of the mechanism.

Water is not a trivial filler in this picture. Charged carbohydrate-rich structures influence local hydration and ion distribution. A hydrated matrix permits diffusion while resisting rapid fluid loss under load. Its porous architecture controls the space through which solutes move. A cell can obtain nutrients through the extracellular environment without having direct contact with a blood vessel, provided transport distances and tissue permeability permit adequate exchange. In a matrix-rich tissue such as articular cartilage, the characteristics of the material are therefore important not only for mechanics but also for cellular nutrition.

For an examination problem, begin by identifying the level at which an observation is made. A change in gene expression is a cellular response; a reduction in breaking force is a property of a tissue sample; a change in collagen triple helix stability concerns molecular structure. The levels are connected, but they are not interchangeable measurements. If cells stop producing a matrix component, the existing network may persist for some time. A defect in production may therefore precede a detectable mechanical defect. A successful rescue of secretion may similarly precede complete restoration of tissue architecture. This time difference is particularly important when interpreting developmental and genetic experiments.

2. Interstitial matrices and basement membranes

One broad classification distinguishes the interstitial matrix from basement membranes. The interstitial matrix occupies spaces between cells and other tissue structures. Fibroblasts in connective tissues are prominent producers, although they are not the only cell type that makes extracellular material. Interstitial matrices often contain fibrillar collagens, elastic fibers where appropriate, and a hydrated ground substance rich in proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans. Rather than being a uniform solution, the material contains interconnected fibers, crosslinks, water-filled spaces and molecules that attach cells to the network.

In loose connective tissue, an open architecture is compatible with the presence and movement of different cell types and the exchange of fluid. In a tendon, collagen bundles are strongly oriented along the principal direction of force. In the dermis, many collagen fibers have different orientations, allowing resistance to tension from different directions. These tissues differ in architecture despite sharing some molecular constituents. Thus identifying type I collagen in a sample may suggest a fibrillar load-bearing component, but identifying type I alone does not tell us whether the sample is tendon, dermis, bone or another collagen-rich tissue.

Basement membranes are thin specialized extracellular networks associated with cell surfaces. The basal lamina underlying an epithelial cell layer is a classic example. Similar structures surround individual muscle fibers and are associated with endothelial cells. Their position allows them to supply a defined cell-facing support and to separate or organize tissue compartments. The basement membrane is outside the plasma membrane; it is not a second phospholipid bilayer. It has neither the membrane topology of a cell nor the function of a selectively gated lipid barrier, even though it can influence which macromolecules move through a tissue interface.

The principal structural systems of many basement membranes are laminin assemblies and type IV collagen networks. Nidogens and heparan sulfate proteoglycans, including perlecan in many tissues, contribute interactions within the matrix. Other associated proteins differ with tissue and developmental stage. This differs from the arrangement of long load-bearing fibrils in a tendon. Type IV collagen retains terminal domains and forms a network rather than the classical banded fibrils associated with type I collagen. Laminins are multidomain heterotrimers whose assembly and surface-binding properties are well suited to organizing a sheet adjacent to cells.

Histological terminology needs care. Basal lamina often refers to the specialized cell-associated ECM identifiable by electron microscopy. In some histology conventions, basement membrane refers more broadly to this layer together with an associated connective tissue component identifiable by light microscopy. Authors do not use the terms with perfect uniformity. In a cell biology question concerning the molecular constituents of a basement membrane, collagen IV and laminin are generally the intended core components. A description that emphasizes the underlying connective tissue's reticular fibers may be using the broader histological convention. The experimental context resolves the terminology better than memorizing an absolute synonym rule.

Epithelial polarity illustrates why position matters. An epithelial cell has an apical surface oriented toward a lumen or exterior, lateral surfaces that contact neighboring cells, and a basal surface associated with underlying matrix. The basement membrane provides location-specific attachment cues and participates in maintenance of this organization. It does not determine polarity by itself in every tissue; junctions, trafficking and intracellular polarity proteins also contribute. When a matrix is experimentally disrupted, abnormal cellular organization may reflect loss of attachment, altered physical support or loss of spatial information. A change in polarity therefore need not imply that a particular soluble ligand was removed.

At the interface between capillary blood and the forming filtrate in the kidney, the glomerular basement membrane contributes to a specialized filtration barrier together with fenestrated endothelial cells and podocyte structures. The barrier depends on multiple interacting features, and it is not accurate to attribute all size and charge discrimination to one matrix molecule. Nevertheless, this example shows why a matrix that is only a thin sheet can have an essential physiological role. Changes in network assembly may compromise permeability even when cells remain present on both sides.

The boundary between the two matrix categories is an architectural distinction, not a claim that individual molecules always occur in only one location. Fibronectin can associate with developing or remodeling matrices in many contexts, and several collagen types can occur in specialized matrix structures. Type VII collagen, for example, contributes anchoring fibrils linking the epithelial basement membrane region to the underlying connective tissue in skin. This is useful because it illustrates how the mechanical connection between two different matrix compartments can require an additional specialized structure. One should not conclude that type VII is the main network-forming collagen of the basal lamina.

An image of a tissue can be interpreted by combining morphology with selective molecular labeling. A continuous band of laminin and collagen IV along the basal side of an epithelium is consistent with a basement membrane. Long bundles showing fibrillar collagen immunoreactivity in the underlying tissue are more consistent with an interstitial load-bearing network. A single stain for total carbohydrate or total protein would be much less informative. Even specific labeling establishes the location of a detected epitope, not automatically the continuity or strength of a functional network. Combining localization with perturbation and mechanical measurements strengthens the interpretation.

3. Collagen: molecular structure and tensile strength

Collagens constitute a large family of extracellular proteins that share regions organized as triple helices. The most familiar fibrillar collagens provide resistance to tensile loading. Tension is a pulling force that tends to elongate material; a collagen-rich tendon transmits such force between anatomical structures. Compression pushes material together and is resisted through additional mechanisms in many tissues. A collagen network can participate in resistance to compression indirectly, for example by restraining swelling of a hydrated cartilage matrix. The common description of collagen as a tensile component is therefore a statement about a major function rather than the exclusive role of every collagen in every tissue.

A collagen triple helix is formed from three polypeptide chains, often called collagen alpha chains. This terminology does not mean that each chain is a conventional alpha helix. In the characteristic triple-helical regions, each chain has an extended left-handed conformation resembling a polyproline II helix, and three chains wrap around one another into a right-handed superhelix. Their close packing constrains which amino acids can occupy the center. Glycine has only a hydrogen atom as its side chain and fits where a bulkier residue generally would not. The sequence commonly follows the repeating Gly–X–Y arrangement in triple-helical domains, with glycine at every third position in uninterrupted regions.

Proline and hydroxyproline occur frequently in the X and Y positions, respectively, though other residues also occur. Proline's cyclic structure restricts its backbone conformation. Hydroxyproline contributes to triple helix stability through effects on conformation and hydration, among other interactions. The helix is not stabilized by a conventional alpha-helical hydrogen-bond pattern within each chain. Instead, interactions between chains are crucial, with the packing permitted by small glycine residues helping establish the geometry of the structure. Understanding the sequence requirement explains why replacing a central glycine with a larger residue can be disruptive even if the total amount of protein produced is unchanged.

The historical development of the structural model is worth understanding. In the 1950s, G. N. Ramachandran and Gopinath Kartha interpreted X-ray diffraction patterns from collagen fibers and proposed triple-chain structural models. Their 1954 and 1955 reports were followed by refinements and other proposals, including work by Alexander Rich and Francis Crick in 1955. Diffraction supplied constraints on repeating distances and molecular geometry, rather than direct images of individual amino acids. A plausible model had to fit both the physical data and steric constraints of polypeptide chains. This example connects protein conformation from Unit 1 to the mechanics of a large extracellular assembly. The shared triple-helical principle is much more important here than memorizing competing historical helical pitches. [6–8]

A collagen molecule may contain three identical chains, or a defined combination of different chains. Type I collagen commonly contains two alpha1(I) chains and one alpha2(I) chain. Type II collagen commonly forms a homotrimer of alpha1(II) chains. The chain composition influences the structure and interactions of a collagen type. A mutation affecting one chain species may consequently alter some triple helices but not others. Depending on how chains assemble and whether abnormal chains are degraded, a heterozygous mutation can have effects more complex than a simple halving of normal collagen production.

Type I collagen is prominent in tendon, skin and the organic matrix of bone. Type II is a major fibrillar collagen of hyaline cartilage. Type III is associated with reticular fibers and occurs in tissues requiring flexible supporting networks, including blood vessel walls; it commonly coexists with other collagens. Type IV, in contrast, forms networks in basement membranes rather than classical fibrils. These categories provide a useful first approximation for questions about collagen function. They are not a complete list of collagen types. Several less abundant collagens regulate fibril assembly, attach networks to other structures or have specialized tissue distributions.

It is helpful to separate chain-level structure from molecular assembly. Three collagen chains form a triple-helical molecule. In fibrillar collagens, many molecules assemble in a staggered arrangement into fibrils. The molecular offset creates a characteristic banding pattern with an approximate axial periodicity of 67 nanometers in many mature fibrillar collagen preparations. Further association and organization of fibrils form fibers and bundles. The number of chains in the molecule and the spacing between bands in a fibril therefore refer to completely different levels of organization. A banded structure in an electron micrograph indicates supramolecular arrangement, not a repeated pattern of three individually visible alpha chains.

The direction of fibril alignment is mechanically significant. A bundle aligned with the pulling axis can carry force efficiently through its length. Loading perpendicular to the axis may instead depend more heavily on connections between fibrils and the surrounding matrix. As a consequence, a tendon is anisotropic, meaning its mechanical properties differ with direction. An isotropic material would have the same directional properties in the relevant measurement. Biological structures often occupy intermediate situations, with preferred but not perfectly uniform orientations. A section cut across a tendon can show circular fibril profiles even though longitudinal sections reveal long fibers; the different appearances do not imply distinct chemical compositions.

Collagen crosslinks help stabilize assemblies against intermolecular sliding. A strong interaction along a single triple helix is insufficient if molecules simply move past one another when a whole fibril is stretched. Intermolecular covalent crosslinks transmit stress between neighboring molecules. The spacing and extent of crosslinking influence strength and deformation; too much or poorly regulated crosslinking can also reduce flexibility. The distinction between molecular helix stabilization and intermolecular crosslinking predicts different effects of a defect in hydroxylation versus a defect in extracellular crosslink formation. A molecule can fold adequately yet assemble into a mechanically weakened network.

Denaturation disrupts the ordered triple-helical structure. Heating collagen under suitable conditions can produce gelatin, a mixture of denatured or partially hydrolyzed collagen-derived polypeptides that behaves differently from the intact fibrillar network. This comparison illustrates why total protein content alone is an inadequate indicator of matrix function. A material can contain collagen-derived amino acid sequences yet lack the native molecular organization required for tensile support. On the other hand, denatured collagen may expose different recognition sequences and have useful material properties of its own. Loss of the native function is not equivalent to absence of every biological interaction.

4. From secreted precursors to mature collagen assemblies

For fibrillar collagen, it is useful to follow a newly synthesized chain through processing and extracellular assembly. The cell must produce the chains, modify selected residues, coordinate trimer formation and export the precursor. It must then regulate assembly so that large fibrils form predominantly outside the plasma membrane rather than accumulating indiscriminately inside secretory compartments. This is a classic example of spatial separation between the synthesis of a protein and the formation of its mature macromolecular structure. A defect at any stage can reduce the final quantity or quality of the matrix.

Fibrillar procollagen chains enter the secretory pathway and undergo post-translational modifications. Certain proline residues are hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylases and selected lysine residues are hydroxylated by lysyl hydroxylases. These reactions are not performed on every proline or lysine in the protein. The relevant enzymes use oxygen, iron and 2-oxoglutarate; ascorbate supports maintenance of appropriate catalytic iron chemistry. Inadequate ascorbate can compromise hydroxylation and collagen stability. This explains why a nutritional defect can affect connective tissues without a mutation in the collagen genes themselves. It is also a direct link between micronutrient chemistry and tissue integrity.

Some hydroxylysine residues are glycosylated. Such modifications affect interactions and the later pattern of crosslinking in ways that differ among collagen types and tissues. The important distinction is that hydroxylation adds a hydroxyl group to an existing side chain, whereas glycosylation attaches a carbohydrate group. Neither change is the translation of a different genetically encoded amino acid. Hydroxyproline in collagen is predominantly generated after translation. Thus a question presenting hydroxyproline as though it had its own standard codon is testing confusion between ribosomal decoding and chemical processing of a completed or growing polypeptide.

The propeptide regions of fibrillar procollagen facilitate appropriate chain association and help prevent premature assembly into mature fibrils. The C-terminal propeptides contribute to chain selection and alignment. Once the three chains are associated, triple-helix formation proceeds from the C-terminal region toward the N terminus. This direction concerns propagation of folding along the associated chains, not the direction of ribosomal translation, which runs from the amino terminus toward the carboxyl terminus. Hydroxylation and related modifications normally precede completion of the triple helix because the enzymes need access to their substrates.

After secretion, appropriate extracellular proteinases remove the terminal propeptides of typical fibrillar procollagens. Processed collagen molecules can then assemble into fibrils. The terminal nonhelical telopeptide regions of fibrillar collagens are distinct from the large propeptides that are removed. They contribute to intermolecular interactions and crosslink formation. An experimental defect that prevents propeptide cleavage may therefore produce extracellular collagen precursor without normal fibrillar assembly. Detecting secreted collagen antigen would not prove that the tissue contains mature functional fibrils.

Lysyl oxidase and related enzymes contribute to extracellular covalent crosslink formation by oxidative deamination of selected lysine or hydroxylysine residues, generating reactive aldehyde groups. These can participate in subsequent condensation reactions to generate crosslinks. Lysyl oxidase is copper dependent, providing a second example of the relationship between micronutrients and connective tissue chemistry. Its substrate and reaction should not be confused with those of lysyl hydroxylase, which introduces hydroxyl groups during intracellular processing. In an exam, the name of the enzyme alone is insufficient: identify where it acts, the chemical change it makes and the biological structure that depends on that change.

Type IV collagen follows a different network-forming strategy. It has interruptions within its triple-helical regions and terminal domains that support association between molecules. Its noncollagenous C-terminal NC1 regions and N-terminal 7S regions participate in interactions that organize a meshwork. It does not require conversion into classical banded fibrils to become functional. The fibrillar procollagen cleavage sequence is therefore not a universal assembly scheme for the whole collagen superfamily. This is an important example of how a conserved structural element, the triple helix, can be combined with different molecular domains to produce distinct extracellular architectures.

Several kinds of perturbation can help distinguish steps. A substitution that disturbs triple helix packing might impair chain association or delay folding and secretion. A defect in a propeptide-processing enzyme may instead allow substantial secretion of an abnormal precursor. Inhibition of lysyl oxidase can permit deposition of collagen material but reduce the quality of intermolecular crosslinks. If all three conditions yield a weak tissue, the mechanical phenotype alone does not locate the defect. Assays of intracellular precursor, secreted precursor, mature collagen, fibril ultrastructure and crosslink-associated chemistry address different causal alternatives.

The role of ascorbate can be investigated conceptually with a matched cell culture comparison. Suppose fibroblasts of the same origin are maintained under conditions differing only in adequate versus inadequate ascorbate availability. A decrease in stable collagen accumulation in the deficient condition would be consistent with impaired collagen maturation. It would not by itself establish that a particular hydroxylated residue is absent. Measuring hydroxylation directly, comparing transcript and protein abundance, and restoring ascorbate in a rescue condition would narrow the explanation. This is a hypothetical teaching design, not a report of a specific historical experiment.

A final issue is the persistence of existing material. Mature collagen networks can survive much longer than many individual signaling molecules. If a cell's processing machinery is corrected, future collagen may be normal while the pre-existing abnormal network remains until it is remodeled. Conversely, a brief defect in collagen production may be buffered by existing matrix. The amount of ECM observed at one moment is therefore the accumulated result of synthesis, assembly, degradation and replacement. A snapshot of cellular transcription does not necessarily reveal the instantaneous mechanical condition of the tissue.

5. Elastin and reversible elastic deformation

Some tissues must stretch and then return toward their initial dimensions repeatedly. The elastic arteries expand when blood is ejected by the heart and recoil between beats; lung tissue also changes dimensions during ventilation. A purely rigid collagen cable would not provide the required reversible deformation over these cycles. Elastic fibers supply a different mechanical property. They allow a tissue to store part of the work of deformation and recover some of it when the external load decreases. Their behavior is related to, but not identical with, that of a metal spring or a simple rubber band.

Elastin is formed from the secreted precursor tropoelastin. Tropoelastin contains hydrophobic regions that favor association in water and lysine-rich regions that provide sites for crosslinking. During extracellular assembly, tropoelastin molecules associate and become covalently linked to form insoluble elastin. The network is associated with microfibrils containing fibrillin, which help organize elastic fiber formation. Fibrillin and elastin are distinct molecules; the former contributes the microfibrillar component and the latter provides much of the elastic recoil of mature elastic fibers. A defect affecting either can impair the integrated fiber by different mechanisms.

Stretching a flexible crosslinked polymer network tends to restrict the range of configurations that its chains can adopt. When the external force is released, thermal motion favors a return toward the larger set of accessible configurations. This entropic contribution to elastic recoil is especially helpful for understanding elastin, although hydration and intermolecular interactions also influence its behavior. The crosslinks keep the network connected so that local rearrangements of chains become a macroscopic recovery of shape. Without adequate connectivity, chains could rearrange irreversibly or slide away from their neighbors instead of restoring the original network.

The chemical crosslinks of elastin include desmosine and isodesmosine, derived from lysine residues in mature elastin. Lysyl oxidase contributes the oxidative chemistry that initiates these covalent links, as it does in collagen assemblies, but the molecular organization and characteristic crosslinks of the two proteins are not identical. Desmosine is commonly associated with mature elastin and can provide a useful biochemical indication of elastin turnover. It is not a universal marker of collagen crosslinking. An assay for elastin-derived fragments and an assay for total lysine would answer different questions about the state of a tissue.

A tissue can use collagen and elastic fibers in combination to achieve useful nonlinear mechanics. Under small or moderate deformation, elastic structures and the changing geometry of crimped collagen fibers can allow the material to extend relatively easily. With further stretching, more collagen fibers become taut and contribute greater resistance. The resulting increase in stiffness helps prevent excessive deformation. This interaction shows why describing collagen as strong and elastin as flexible is a useful starting point but not the entire mechanical explanation. In a composite network, the degree to which a component is recruited into load-bearing changes with strain.

A common confusion concerns the word elastic. Elasticity refers to the tendency of a material to recover deformation when a load is removed, provided the load does not produce permanent damage. Stiffness refers to resistance to deformation under an applied force. A material can be stiff and still be elastic over a small range; another material can deform easily and fail to recover because of irreversible rearrangement. The fact that elastin supports large reversible extension does not imply that all elastic materials must contain elastin. Collagen assemblies can also show recoverable deformation within a limited range.

A loss of elastin in an arterial wall could reduce the wall's ability to accommodate pulsatile changes in pressure and recover its geometry. The consequences depend on the remaining collagen, smooth muscle activity, wall thickness and other mechanical features. It is not scientifically sound to infer a specific pressure value from loss of elastin alone. Similarly, disruption of elastic fiber organization can affect lung recoil without requiring loss of the entire collagen network. In either case a useful biological question is whether the observed phenotype reflects altered elastin abundance, defective assembly, crosslink chemistry or subsequent degradation.

An experiment can distinguish elastic recoil from mere extensibility. Stretch equal-size specimens by the same controlled displacement, then remove the force and record the length as a function of time. A specimen that extends easily but retains a large permanent elongation is not demonstrating efficient elastic recovery. A second specimen might need a greater initial force yet return close to its original length. Measuring force during both loading and unloading also reveals energy dissipation. In a biological material, differences in water movement and loading rate can affect the outcome, so matching specimen geometry, hydration and deformation history is important.

Fibrillin-rich microfibrils also interact with molecules associated with growth factor regulation. This makes the elastic fiber system another example in which an extracellular assembly can have mechanical and cell-regulatory functions. Those functions should be experimentally distinguished. If a mutation in fibrillin changes both tissue mechanics and cellular signaling, a change in one does not automatically prove that it caused the other. A structurally abnormal network could influence both through partly separate pathways. The detailed molecular signaling of specific diseases is beyond the present scope, but the general reasoning is central to understanding why matrix proteins are more than structural fillers.
