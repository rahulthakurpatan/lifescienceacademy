1. The environment beyond the plasma membrane

An animal tissue is not simply a collection of cells arranged side by side. Consider a tendon, which must transfer the force generated by a muscle to a bone. Individual tendon cells cannot themselves form a continuous, strong cable spanning that distance. They produce extracellular material that can carry a substantial part of the load. In cartilage, by contrast, cells are embedded in a hydrated material that resists compression. An epithelium contains closely packed cells, but even there a thin extracellular supporting layer provides a distinct basal surface. These examples introduce the central problem of this chapter: how do cells build a material outside their plasma membranes that performs specialized functions and also changes how the cells behave?

The extracellular matrix, abbreviated ECM, is an organized network of extracellular macromolecules made by cells. In animal tissues it consists principally of proteins and carbohydrate-rich molecules, together with associated water and ions. Its composition varies according to tissue function. A matrix should not be equated with every substance outside the cell. Interstitial fluid contains small dissolved molecules that exchange with blood and cells, whereas the ECM includes relatively persistent macromolecular assemblies within which some of that fluid is held. A circulating hormone in the fluid may temporarily bind the matrix, but that does not turn every circulating hormone into a structural matrix component.

The plasma membrane separates the cytoplasm from the extracellular environment. Matrix proteins are usually delivered to the extracellular side, where they associate with one another and with cell surfaces. Thus the location where a matrix protein is translated and the location where it performs its mature biological role are often different. Cells can deposit new material, remove old material and reorganize existing networks. Unlike an inert artificial support, the matrix has a history. It reflects the activity of the cells that built it and the physical conditions under which assembly and remodeling occurred. A newly arriving cell can therefore encounter extracellular information left by other cells.

It is useful to distinguish cell adhesion to another cell from cell adhesion to a substrate. In cell–cell adhesion, the molecules on neighboring plasma membranes contribute directly to an attachment between cells. Cadherins provide a familiar example. In cell–matrix adhesion, a membrane receptor on a cell recognizes a ligand in an extracellular network. That ligand might be an adhesive glycoprotein or a collagen molecule. One network can bind many cells, permitting physical continuity between cells that do not actually touch. The categories are not mutually exclusive within a tissue: an epithelium uses cell–cell contacts laterally and cell–matrix contacts basally.

Imagine a sheet of cells placed over two substrates of identical chemical composition but different mechanical compliance. If a cell exerts traction on a substrate, a compliant material may deform relatively easily, whereas a stiff material may resist the same force. The cell's attachment sites and internal cytoskeleton experience different mechanical conditions. In an otherwise appropriately controlled setting, the cells can respond differently even without a change in soluble hormone concentration. The important general principle is that an external physical property can become biologically meaningful when a cell has a means of detecting or responding to it. The substrate itself need not be a living cell or a conventional secretory signaling organ.

The cell-facing surface of a matrix supplies chemical information too. An adhesive receptor may recognize a specific protein domain, an exposed amino acid sequence or a composite conformation. Which ligands are accessible depends on how proteins are assembled, their orientation, and whether surrounding material masks them. Two matrices with similar total collagen can differ in the distribution of accessible attachment sites, in fiber organization or in the proportions of their other constituents. As a consequence, a chemical assay reporting bulk concentration cannot by itself describe all the biological information available to a cell.

Water is not a trivial filler in this picture. Charged carbohydrate-rich structures influence local hydration and ion distribution. A hydrated matrix permits diffusion while resisting rapid fluid loss under load. Its porous architecture controls the space through which solutes move. A cell can obtain nutrients through the extracellular environment without having direct contact with a blood vessel, provided transport distances and tissue permeability permit adequate exchange. In a matrix-rich tissue such as articular cartilage, the characteristics of the material are therefore important not only for mechanics but also for cellular nutrition.

For an examination problem, begin by identifying the level at which an observation is made. A change in gene expression is a cellular response; a reduction in breaking force is a property of a tissue sample; a change in collagen triple helix stability concerns molecular structure. The levels are connected, but they are not interchangeable measurements. If cells stop producing a matrix component, the existing network may persist for some time. A defect in production may therefore precede a detectable mechanical defect. A successful rescue of secretion may similarly precede complete restoration of tissue architecture. This time difference is particularly important when interpreting developmental and genetic experiments.

2. Interstitial matrices and basement membranes

One broad classification distinguishes the interstitial matrix from basement membranes. The interstitial matrix occupies spaces between cells and other tissue structures. Fibroblasts in connective tissues are prominent producers, although they are not the only cell type that makes extracellular material. Interstitial matrices often contain fibrillar collagens, elastic fibers where appropriate, and a hydrated ground substance rich in proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans. Rather than being a uniform solution, the material contains interconnected fibers, crosslinks, water-filled spaces and molecules that attach cells to the network.

In loose connective tissue, an open architecture is compatible with the presence and movement of different cell types and the exchange of fluid. In a tendon, collagen bundles are strongly oriented along the principal direction of force. In the dermis, many collagen fibers have different orientations, allowing resistance to tension from different directions. These tissues differ in architecture despite sharing some molecular constituents. Thus identifying type I collagen in a sample may suggest a fibrillar load-bearing component, but identifying type I alone does not tell us whether the sample is tendon, dermis, bone or another collagen-rich tissue.

Basement membranes are thin specialized extracellular networks associated with cell surfaces. The basal lamina underlying an epithelial cell layer is a classic example. Similar structures surround individual muscle fibers and are associated with endothelial cells. Their position allows them to supply a defined cell-facing support and to separate or organize tissue compartments. The basement membrane is outside the plasma membrane; it is not a second phospholipid bilayer. It has neither the membrane topology of a cell nor the function of a selectively gated lipid barrier, even though it can influence which macromolecules move through a tissue interface.

The principal structural systems of many basement membranes are laminin assemblies and type IV collagen networks. Nidogens and heparan sulfate proteoglycans, including perlecan in many tissues, contribute interactions within the matrix. Other associated proteins differ with tissue and developmental stage. This differs from the arrangement of long load-bearing fibrils in a tendon. Type IV collagen retains terminal domains and forms a network rather than the classical banded fibrils associated with type I collagen. Laminins are multidomain heterotrimers whose assembly and surface-binding properties are well suited to organizing a sheet adjacent to cells.

Histological terminology needs care. Basal lamina often refers to the specialized cell-associated ECM identifiable by electron microscopy. In some histology conventions, basement membrane refers more broadly to this layer together with an associated connective tissue component identifiable by light microscopy. Authors do not use the terms with perfect uniformity. In a cell biology question concerning the molecular constituents of a basement membrane, collagen IV and laminin are generally the intended core components. A description that emphasizes the underlying connective tissue's reticular fibers may be using the broader histological convention. The experimental context resolves the terminology better than memorizing an absolute synonym rule.

Epithelial polarity illustrates why position matters. An epithelial cell has an apical surface oriented toward a lumen or exterior, lateral surfaces that contact neighboring cells, and a basal surface associated with underlying matrix. The basement membrane provides location-specific attachment cues and participates in maintenance of this organization. It does not determine polarity by itself in every tissue; junctions, trafficking and intracellular polarity proteins also contribute. When a matrix is experimentally disrupted, abnormal cellular organization may reflect loss of attachment, altered physical support or loss of spatial information. A change in polarity therefore need not imply that a particular soluble ligand was removed.

At the interface between capillary blood and the forming filtrate in the kidney, the glomerular basement membrane contributes to a specialized filtration barrier together with fenestrated endothelial cells and podocyte structures. The barrier depends on multiple interacting features, and it is not accurate to attribute all size and charge discrimination to one matrix molecule. Nevertheless, this example shows why a matrix that is only a thin sheet can have an essential physiological role. Changes in network assembly may compromise permeability even when cells remain present on both sides.

The boundary between the two matrix categories is an architectural distinction, not a claim that individual molecules always occur in only one location. Fibronectin can associate with developing or remodeling matrices in many contexts, and several collagen types can occur in specialized matrix structures. Type VII collagen, for example, contributes anchoring fibrils linking the epithelial basement membrane region to the underlying connective tissue in skin. This is useful because it illustrates how the mechanical connection between two different matrix compartments can require an additional specialized structure. One should not conclude that type VII is the main network-forming collagen of the basal lamina.

3. Collagen: molecular structure and tensile strength

Collagens constitute a large family of extracellular proteins that share regions organized as triple helices. The most familiar fibrillar collagens provide resistance to tensile loading. Tension is a pulling force that tends to elongate material; a collagen-rich tendon transmits such force between anatomical structures. Compression pushes material together and is resisted through additional mechanisms in many tissues. A collagen network can participate in resistance to compression indirectly, for example by restraining swelling of a hydrated cartilage matrix. The common description of collagen as a tensile component is therefore a statement about a major function rather than the exclusive role of every collagen in every tissue.

A collagen triple helix is formed from three polypeptide chains, often called collagen alpha chains. This terminology does not mean that each chain is a conventional alpha helix. In the characteristic triple-helical regions, each chain has an extended left-handed conformation resembling a polyproline II helix, and three chains wrap around one another into a right-handed superhelix. Their close packing constrains which amino acids can occupy the center. Glycine has only a hydrogen atom as its side chain and fits where a bulkier residue generally would not. The sequence commonly follows the repeating Gly–X–Y arrangement in triple-helical domains, with glycine at every third position in uninterrupted regions.

Proline and hydroxyproline occur frequently in the X and Y positions, respectively, though other residues also occur. Proline's cyclic structure restricts its backbone conformation. Hydroxyproline contributes to triple helix stability through effects on conformation and hydration, among other interactions. The helix is not stabilized by a conventional alpha-helical hydrogen-bond pattern within each chain. Instead, interactions between chains are crucial, with the packing permitted by small glycine residues helping establish the geometry of the structure. Understanding the sequence requirement explains why replacing a central glycine with a larger residue can be disruptive even if the total amount of protein produced is unchanged.

The historical development of the structural model is worth understanding. In the 1950s, G. N. Ramachandran and Gopinath Kartha interpreted X-ray diffraction patterns from collagen fibers and proposed triple-chain structural models. Their 1954 and 1955 reports were followed by refinements and other proposals, including work by Alexander Rich and Francis Crick in 1955. Diffraction supplied constraints on repeating distances and molecular geometry, rather than direct images of individual amino acids. A plausible model had to fit both the physical data and steric constraints of polypeptide chains. This example connects protein conformation from Unit 1 to the mechanics of a large extracellular assembly. The shared triple-helical principle is much more important here than memorizing competing historical helical pitches. [6–8]

A collagen molecule may contain three identical chains, or a defined combination of different chains. Type I collagen commonly contains two alpha1(I) chains and one alpha2(I) chain. Type II collagen commonly forms a homotrimer of alpha1(II) chains. The chain composition influences the structure and interactions of a collagen type. A mutation affecting one chain species may consequently alter some triple helices but not others. Depending on how chains assemble and whether abnormal chains are degraded, a heterozygous mutation can have effects more complex than a simple halving of normal collagen production.

Type I collagen is prominent in tendon, skin and the organic matrix of bone. Type II is a major fibrillar collagen of hyaline cartilage. Type III is associated with reticular fibers and occurs in tissues requiring flexible supporting networks, including blood vessel walls; it commonly coexists with other collagens. Type IV, in contrast, forms networks in basement membranes rather than classical fibrils. These categories provide a useful first approximation for questions about collagen function. They are not a complete list of collagen types. Several less abundant collagens regulate fibril assembly, attach networks to other structures or have specialized tissue distributions.

It is helpful to separate chain-level structure from molecular assembly. Three collagen chains form a triple-helical molecule. In fibrillar collagens, many molecules assemble in a staggered arrangement into fibrils. The molecular offset creates a characteristic banding pattern with an approximate axial periodicity of 67 nanometers in many mature fibrillar collagen preparations. Further association and organization of fibrils form fibers and bundles. The number of chains in the molecule and the spacing between bands in a fibril therefore refer to completely different levels of organization. A banded structure in an electron micrograph indicates supramolecular arrangement, not a repeated pattern of three individually visible alpha chains.

The direction of fibril alignment is mechanically significant. A bundle aligned with the pulling axis can carry force efficiently through its length. Loading perpendicular to the axis may instead depend more heavily on connections between fibrils and the surrounding matrix. As a consequence, a tendon is anisotropic, meaning its mechanical properties differ with direction. An isotropic material would have the same directional properties in the relevant measurement. Biological structures often occupy intermediate situations, with preferred but not perfectly uniform orientations. A section cut across a tendon can show circular fibril profiles even though longitudinal sections reveal long fibers; the different appearances do not imply distinct chemical compositions.

Collagen crosslinks help stabilize assemblies against intermolecular sliding. A strong interaction along a single triple helix is insufficient if molecules simply move past one another when a whole fibril is stretched. Intermolecular covalent crosslinks transmit stress between neighboring molecules. The spacing and extent of crosslinking influence strength and deformation; too much or poorly regulated crosslinking can also reduce flexibility. The distinction between molecular helix stabilization and intermolecular crosslinking predicts different effects of a defect in hydroxylation versus a defect in extracellular crosslink formation. A molecule can fold adequately yet assemble into a mechanically weakened network.

Denaturation disrupts the ordered triple-helical structure. Heating collagen under suitable conditions can produce gelatin, a mixture of denatured or partially hydrolyzed collagen-derived polypeptides that behaves differently from the intact fibrillar network. This comparison illustrates why total protein content alone is an inadequate indicator of matrix function. A material can contain collagen-derived amino acid sequences yet lack the native molecular organization required for tensile support. On the other hand, denatured collagen may expose different recognition sequences and have useful material properties of its own. Loss of the native function is not equivalent to absence of every biological interaction.

4. From secreted precursors to mature collagen assemblies

For fibrillar collagen, it is useful to follow a newly synthesized chain through processing and extracellular assembly. The cell must produce the chains, modify selected residues, coordinate trimer formation and export the precursor. It must then regulate assembly so that large fibrils form predominantly outside the plasma membrane rather than accumulating indiscriminately inside secretory compartments. This is a classic example of spatial separation between the synthesis of a protein and the formation of its mature macromolecular structure. A defect at any stage can reduce the final quantity or quality of the matrix.

Fibrillar procollagen chains enter the secretory pathway and undergo post-translational modifications. Certain proline residues are hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylases and selected lysine residues are hydroxylated by lysyl hydroxylases. These reactions are not performed on every proline or lysine in the protein. The relevant enzymes use oxygen, iron and 2-oxoglutarate; ascorbate supports maintenance of appropriate catalytic iron chemistry. Inadequate ascorbate can compromise hydroxylation and collagen stability. This explains why a nutritional defect can affect connective tissues without a mutation in the collagen genes themselves. It is also a direct link between micronutrient chemistry and tissue integrity.

Some hydroxylysine residues are glycosylated. Such modifications affect interactions and the later pattern of crosslinking in ways that differ among collagen types and tissues. The important distinction is that hydroxylation adds a hydroxyl group to an existing side chain, whereas glycosylation attaches a carbohydrate group. Neither change is the translation of a different genetically encoded amino acid. Hydroxyproline in collagen is predominantly generated after translation. Thus a question presenting hydroxyproline as though it had its own standard codon is testing confusion between ribosomal decoding and chemical processing of a completed or growing polypeptide.

The propeptide regions of fibrillar procollagen facilitate appropriate chain association and help prevent premature assembly into mature fibrils. The C-terminal propeptides contribute to chain selection and alignment. Once the three chains are associated, triple-helix formation proceeds from the C-terminal region toward the N terminus. This direction concerns propagation of folding along the associated chains, not the direction of ribosomal translation, which runs from the amino terminus toward the carboxyl terminus. Hydroxylation and related modifications normally precede completion of the triple helix because the enzymes need access to their substrates.

After secretion, appropriate extracellular proteinases remove the terminal propeptides of typical fibrillar procollagens. Processed collagen molecules can then assemble into fibrils. The terminal nonhelical telopeptide regions of fibrillar collagens are distinct from the large propeptides that are removed. They contribute to intermolecular interactions and crosslink formation. An experimental defect that prevents propeptide cleavage may therefore produce extracellular collagen precursor without normal fibrillar assembly. Detecting secreted collagen antigen would not prove that the tissue contains mature functional fibrils.

Lysyl oxidase and related enzymes contribute to extracellular covalent crosslink formation by oxidative deamination of selected lysine or hydroxylysine residues, generating reactive aldehyde groups. These can participate in subsequent condensation reactions to generate crosslinks. Lysyl oxidase is copper dependent, providing a second example of the relationship between micronutrients and connective tissue chemistry. Its substrate and reaction should not be confused with those of lysyl hydroxylase, which introduces hydroxyl groups during intracellular processing. In an exam, the name of the enzyme alone is insufficient: identify where it acts, the chemical change it makes and the biological structure that depends on that change.

Type IV collagen follows a different network-forming strategy. It has interruptions within its triple-helical regions and terminal domains that support association between molecules. Its noncollagenous C-terminal NC1 regions and N-terminal 7S regions participate in interactions that organize a meshwork. It does not require conversion into classical banded fibrils to become functional. The fibrillar procollagen cleavage sequence is therefore not a universal assembly scheme for the whole collagen superfamily. This is an important example of how a conserved structural element, the triple helix, can be combined with different molecular domains to produce distinct extracellular architectures.

Several kinds of perturbation can help distinguish steps. A substitution that disturbs triple helix packing might impair chain association or delay folding and secretion. A defect in a propeptide-processing enzyme may instead allow substantial secretion of an abnormal precursor. Inhibition of lysyl oxidase can permit deposition of collagen material but reduce the quality of intermolecular crosslinks. If all three conditions yield a weak tissue, the mechanical phenotype alone does not locate the defect. Assays of intracellular precursor, secreted precursor, mature collagen, fibril ultrastructure and crosslink-associated chemistry address different causal alternatives.

The role of ascorbate can be investigated conceptually with a matched cell culture comparison. Suppose fibroblasts of the same origin are maintained under conditions differing only in adequate versus inadequate ascorbate availability. A decrease in stable collagen accumulation in the deficient condition would be consistent with impaired collagen maturation. It would not by itself establish that a particular hydroxylated residue is absent. Measuring hydroxylation directly, comparing transcript and protein abundance, and restoring ascorbate in a rescue condition would narrow the explanation. This is a hypothetical teaching design, not a report of a specific historical experiment.

5. Elastin and reversible elastic deformation

Some tissues must stretch and then return toward their initial dimensions repeatedly. The elastic arteries expand when blood is ejected by the heart and recoil between beats; lung tissue also changes dimensions during ventilation. A purely rigid collagen cable would not provide the required reversible deformation over these cycles. Elastic fibers supply a different mechanical property. They allow a tissue to store part of the work of deformation and recover some of it when the external load decreases. Their behavior is related to, but not identical with, that of a metal spring or a simple rubber band.

Elastin is formed from the secreted precursor tropoelastin. Tropoelastin contains hydrophobic regions that favor association in water and lysine-rich regions that provide sites for crosslinking. During extracellular assembly, tropoelastin molecules associate and become covalently linked to form insoluble elastin. The network is associated with microfibrils containing fibrillin, which help organize elastic fiber formation. Fibrillin and elastin are distinct molecules; the former contributes the microfibrillar component and the latter provides much of the elastic recoil of mature elastic fibers. A defect affecting either can impair the integrated fiber by different mechanisms.

Stretching a flexible crosslinked polymer network tends to restrict the range of configurations that its chains can adopt. When the external force is released, thermal motion favors a return toward the larger set of accessible configurations. This entropic contribution to elastic recoil is especially helpful for understanding elastin, although hydration and intermolecular interactions also influence its behavior. The crosslinks keep the network connected so that local rearrangements of chains become a macroscopic recovery of shape. Without adequate connectivity, chains could rearrange irreversibly or slide away from their neighbors instead of restoring the original network.

The chemical crosslinks of elastin include desmosine and isodesmosine, derived from lysine residues in mature elastin. Lysyl oxidase contributes the oxidative chemistry that initiates these covalent links, as it does in collagen assemblies, but the molecular organization and characteristic crosslinks of the two proteins are not identical. Desmosine is commonly associated with mature elastin and can provide a useful biochemical indication of elastin turnover. It is not a universal marker of collagen crosslinking. An assay for elastin-derived fragments and an assay for total lysine would answer different questions about the state of a tissue.

A tissue can use collagen and elastic fibers in combination to achieve useful nonlinear mechanics. Under small or moderate deformation, elastic structures and the changing geometry of crimped collagen fibers can allow the material to extend relatively easily. With further stretching, more collagen fibers become taut and contribute greater resistance. The resulting increase in stiffness helps prevent excessive deformation. This interaction shows why describing collagen as strong and elastin as flexible is a useful starting point but not the entire mechanical explanation. In a composite network, the degree to which a component is recruited into load-bearing changes with strain.

A common confusion concerns the word elastic. Elasticity refers to the tendency of a material to recover deformation when a load is removed, provided the load does not produce permanent damage. Stiffness refers to resistance to deformation under an applied force. A material can be stiff and still be elastic over a small range; another material can deform easily and fail to recover because of irreversible rearrangement. The fact that elastin supports large reversible extension does not imply that all elastic materials must contain elastin. Collagen assemblies can also show recoverable deformation within a limited range.

A loss of elastin in an arterial wall could reduce the wall's ability to accommodate pulsatile changes in pressure and recover its geometry. The consequences depend on the remaining collagen, smooth muscle activity, wall thickness and other mechanical features. It is not scientifically sound to infer a specific pressure value from loss of elastin alone. Similarly, disruption of elastic fiber organization can affect lung recoil without requiring loss of the entire collagen network. In either case a useful biological question is whether the observed phenotype reflects altered elastin abundance, defective assembly, crosslink chemistry or subsequent degradation.

An experiment can distinguish elastic recoil from mere extensibility. Stretch equal-size specimens by the same controlled displacement, then remove the force and record the length as a function of time. A specimen that extends easily but retains a large permanent elongation is not demonstrating efficient elastic recovery. A second specimen might need a greater initial force yet return close to its original length. Measuring force during both loading and unloading also reveals energy dissipation. In a biological material, differences in water movement and loading rate can affect the outcome, so matching specimen geometry, hydration and deformation history is important.

Fibrillin-rich microfibrils also interact with molecules associated with growth factor regulation. This makes the elastic fiber system another example in which an extracellular assembly can have mechanical and cell-regulatory functions. Those functions should be experimentally distinguished. If a mutation in fibrillin changes both tissue mechanics and cellular signaling, a change in one does not automatically prove that it caused the other. A structurally abnormal network could influence both through partly separate pathways. The detailed molecular signaling of specific diseases is beyond the present scope, but the general reasoning is central to understanding why matrix proteins are more than structural fillers.

6. Glycosaminoglycans: charged polysaccharides in hydrated matrices

A fiber can carry tensile loads, but a tissue composed entirely of tightly packed fibers would have relatively little capacity to retain a large, mobile aqueous phase. Many extracellular matrices need both a supporting network and a hydrated environment. Glycosaminoglycans, often abbreviated GAGs, are long unbranched polysaccharides that make important contributions to the latter property. They are generally constructed from repeating disaccharide units. The identity of the sugars and the modifications made to them determine the chemical behavior of each class. Unlike an intracellular storage polysaccharide, their main role here is not to provide readily mobilized metabolic energy.

A typical glycosaminoglycan repeat contains an amino sugar such as N-acetylglucosamine or N-acetylgalactosamine together with a uronic acid or, in some cases, another sugar. Uronic acids contain a carboxyl group derived by oxidation of a terminal carbon of a monosaccharide. At physiological pH many of these groups are negatively charged. Many GAGs also contain sulfate groups that contribute additional negative charge. The resulting polymer is a polyelectrolyte, a macromolecule with numerous charged groups distributed along its length. Its interactions with water and dissolved ions are fundamentally different from those of an electrically neutral polysaccharide with a similar chain length.

Consider what happens when a large number of fixed negative charges are confined within an extracellular network. The local electrochemical environment favors an increased concentration of mobile positive counterions relative to the surrounding solution. The distribution of negative and positive mobile ions adjusts according to their chemical and electrical potentials. Water can enter the matrix in response to the resulting osmotic tendency, until other forces including the restraining tension of the surrounding network balance swelling. Hydration is therefore not simply a matter of carbohydrate chains attracting individual water molecules; it also involves collective effects of fixed charge, mobile ions and polymer confinement.

In an unconstrained solution a negatively charged polymer can expand because of electrostatic repulsion among its charged groups. Dissolved salts screen these interactions, reducing the effective range of repulsion. Inside a tissue, however, the polysaccharides are frequently confined or attached to larger structures. The equilibrium volume is determined by several contributions rather than by charge repulsion alone. If collagen fibrils restrain an otherwise expanding proteoglycan network, the balance creates a preloaded composite. Under compression, water tends to be displaced and mobile solute concentrations can change. The resulting stresses resist compression and contribute to the recovery of the tissue when the load is removed.

Hyaluronan, also called hyaluronic acid in many texts, is an important exception to several common descriptions of GAGs. Its repeat consists of glucuronic acid and N-acetylglucosamine. It is not sulfated and is generally not covalently attached to a core protein as a conventional proteoglycan side chain. Nonetheless, its carboxylate groups confer negative charge, and its long chains can contribute substantially to hydrated matrix organization. A student who equates all GAGs with sulfated side chains of proteins would fail to recognize hyaluronan even though it is a canonical example of the class.

Hyaluronan also differs in its route of synthesis. Hyaluronan synthases are membrane-associated enzymes that use nucleotide sugar precursors to extend the chain while extruding it toward the extracellular side. Many other GAG chains are instead assembled and modified on core proteins within the secretory pathway, particularly in the Golgi apparatus. This contrast explains why a general inhibitor of protein-linked GAG assembly need not eliminate every GAG in the extracellular environment. The distinction matters more than memorizing a long list of enzymes: it identifies a different causal dependence for the assembly of hyaluronan.

Chondroitin sulfate is common in cartilage and other connective tissues and contains sulfated N-acetylgalactosamine with glucuronic acid in its characteristic repeats. Dermatan sulfate is related to chondroitin sulfate but contains variable amounts of iduronic acid generated by epimerization, alongside other structural variation. These are not merely different names for identical polymers. The identity, spacing and conformation of charged groups influence the interactions available to them. Tissue composition and the biosynthetic modification of the polysaccharide determine its physical and biochemical properties.

Heparan sulfate contains glucosamine and uronic acid residues and has variable patterns of N- and O-sulfation as well as uronic acid composition. These structural differences permit selective association with numerous extracellular proteins. Heparin is a related, generally more highly sulfated GAG with a distinctive biological distribution, notably in mast cell granules. Its familiar anticoagulant activity does not imply that heparan sulfate in every basement membrane is acting primarily as an anticoagulant. The polymers share a broad chemical family but differ in their typical sulfation, localization and biological context.

Keratan sulfate provides another useful exception. Its repeat includes galactose and N-acetylglucosamine rather than a uronic acid. Sulfation supplies negative charges. Keratan sulfate is found, among other locations, in corneal and cartilage proteoglycans. Thus the rule that GAGs contain repeating disaccharides with an amino sugar is more robust at this level than the rule that all of them must contain a uronic acid. Recognizing the exceptions prevents an apparent conflict between textbook definitions and specific structures presented in examination diagrams.

Not all interactions involving GAGs can be predicted solely from their total charge. Two heparan sulfate chains with equal average sulfate content may display different sulfate arrangements along their lengths. A protein that contacts several groups in a particular spatial pattern can bind the two chains differently. A charge-based interaction may be weakened by ionic screening, whereas additional shape-dependent contacts can contribute specificity. This is why the ECM can simultaneously influence general material hydration and the localization of selected signaling proteins. The same broad class of polysaccharides has roles that range from physical water retention to selective molecular recognition.

The measurement of GAG content presents several interpretive challenges. A dye that binds negatively charged polymer groups can be useful for comparative assays, but differences in sulfation can alter the signal obtained for equal polymer mass. A decrease in staining could reflect loss of chains, a change in their modification or altered accessibility to the reagent. Identifying a particular GAG requires more selective evidence, such as sensitivity to an appropriate enzyme combined with compositional or immunochemical analysis. For a question involving loss of a charge-sensitive signal, do not infer a specific biosynthetic mutation until the experimental alternatives have been considered.

7. Proteoglycans: combining protein architecture with carbohydrate function

A proteoglycan consists of a core protein bearing one or more covalently attached glycosaminoglycan chains. The distinction from a conventional glycoprotein is based chiefly on the presence of these characteristic long repeating carbohydrate polymers, not on an absolute carbohydrate-to-protein ratio. A glycoprotein may have many oligosaccharides and a proteoglycan may contain only a few GAG chains. Nevertheless, a large cartilage proteoglycan can have a very high carbohydrate content. The side chains occupy much more space than would be suggested by the size of the isolated core protein, giving the whole assembly a large effective volume in water.

For many chondroitin sulfate and heparan sulfate proteoglycans, the growing carbohydrate chain is connected to a serine residue of the core protein through a characteristic linkage region containing xylose, two galactose residues and glucuronic acid. The subsequent addition of sugars directs formation of a particular GAG class. Sulfation and other modifications occur during chain elaboration. Not every proteoglycan linkage is identical; keratan sulfate may use other types of linkage. The central principle is that the protein sequence and the glycosylation machinery together determine the distribution and type of carbohydrate chains attached to the core.

Aggrecan is a prominent cartilage proteoglycan. Its core protein contains a region that can associate noncovalently with hyaluronan, and numerous chondroitin sulfate and keratan sulfate chains contribute to its large hydrated volume. Many aggrecan molecules bind along a long hyaluronan chain, with link proteins stabilizing the association. The result is a very large supramolecular aggregate with numerous charged side chains projecting into the surrounding water. The hyaluronan chain is not covalently attached to each aggrecan core, and the entire aggregate is not a single polypeptide. This hierarchical organization is central to understanding how cartilage generates a hydrated extracellular environment.

Aggrecan-rich material tends to swell, whereas the surrounding collagen fibrillar network restrains expansion. When a joint is loaded, part of the mechanical response reflects pressure within the fluid-containing matrix and resistance to its redistribution. Over time, fluid can move through the porous network, changing the balance between fluid-associated and solid-associated stresses. When loading is reduced, water can return. This composite explains why damage to cartilage proteoglycans can impair compressive function even before a dramatic reduction in total collagen becomes apparent. Conversely, if the collagen network fails, retaining a normal quantity of aggrecan need not preserve normal tissue geometry.

A small proteoglycan such as decorin illustrates a different use of the same architectural principle. Decorin associates with collagen fibrils and can influence fibril formation and organization. It carries far fewer GAG chains than aggrecan. Its core protein provides important interaction surfaces; the carbohydrate chain can also contribute to the properties of the assembly. Therefore an exam question asking about regulation of collagen fibril diameter or spacing is more likely to be testing a small fibril-associated proteoglycan than a giant cartilage aggregate. The answer follows from the location and scale of the proposed function rather than from assuming all proteoglycans have identical roles.

Perlecan is a large proteoglycan associated with many basement membranes. Its protein domains and GAG chains allow interactions with structural constituents and selected extracellular factors. A perlecan-containing basement membrane can provide a hydrated interface while retaining proteins whose local distribution affects cells. The role of perlecan differs from that of type IV collagen: it is not itself the characteristic triple-helical network-forming collagen. A reduction in heparan sulfate associated with perlecan may affect signaling factor presentation or permeability while leaving some laminin and collagen IV structures detectable.

Not all proteoglycans are freely deposited within extracellular material. Syndecans are transmembrane proteoglycans, and glypicans are attached to the outer face of the plasma membrane by glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchors. Their GAG-bearing extracellular regions interact with components of the surrounding environment. Such proteoglycans can influence the local presentation of ligands to other receptors or participate in cellular attachment. Their membrane association is an important distinction from aggrecan, which is chiefly part of a deposited cartilage matrix. Cell-surface and matrix proteoglycans thus connect the physical network with the membrane environment, without being interchangeable molecular objects.

Proteoglycan chains can act as local reservoirs for certain signaling proteins. If a diffusible factor has binding sites for a GAG, a matrix containing that GAG can alter the rate at which the factor travels or is cleared. In some systems, the interaction also helps assemble a ligand–receptor complex and promotes signaling. In other contexts, sequestration can reduce immediate access to receptors. It is therefore incorrect to adopt a universal rule that stronger matrix binding always increases or always decreases signaling. The answer depends on whether matrix association retains a functional local ligand pool, changes its availability, supports receptor interactions or prevents productive binding.

An experiment can distinguish the effect of the core protein from that of its attached GAG chains. Consider cells growing on otherwise comparable protein matrices. In one condition a proteoglycan is present in its normal form; in another its core protein is present but its relevant GAG chains are selectively reduced. A difference in factor retention suggests a contribution of the carbohydrate chains, provided core protein levels, matrix organization and cell viability remain comparable. A reduction in retention could also alter downstream responses without affecting the number of cell-surface receptors. Measuring ligand association separately from the cellular response helps identify the step that changed.

The biological cost of a matrix rich in fixed charges is that its organization must be maintained. Proteoglycans undergo turnover, and excessive cleavage can release parts of the molecules that previously remained associated with the network. A change in the length or number of charged side chains can alter swelling behavior even if the core proteins are still detectable. Restoring a normal core protein concentration may not immediately restore the correct carbohydrate modification or aggregate structure. This illustrates why the matrix must be treated as an assembled system whose functional integrity depends on both molecular composition and spatial organization.

8. Fibronectin: an adhesive bridge between cell and matrix

Fibrillar proteins and charged polysaccharides create a supporting material, but a cell also needs molecularly selective attachment sites within that material. Fibronectin is an important extracellular adhesive glycoprotein. It associates with a variety of other extracellular constituents and with selected cell-surface receptors. Its multidomain structure makes it well suited to bridge interactions. Rather than assuming that every part of the molecule performs the same function, it is useful to regard fibronectin as containing regions with different binding partners and different contributions to matrix assembly.

Fibronectin is commonly found as a dimer in which two large polypeptide chains are connected by disulfide bonds near their carboxyl termini. The subunits contain repeated structural modules conventionally described as type I, type II and type III fibronectin repeats. These module names refer to domains within fibronectin and must not be confused with types I, II and III collagen. Particular regions of fibronectin interact with collagen or gelatin, heparin-related GAGs, other fibronectin molecules and cell-surface receptors. This modular organization means that a mutation may disrupt one interaction without removing every other function of the protein.

A well-known cell-recognition sequence in fibronectin is Arg–Gly–Asp, abbreviated RGD using the one-letter amino acid symbols. It occurs in a type III module and is recognized by several integrins. A nearby synergy region in another module contributes to efficient binding by certain fibronectin receptors, especially alpha5 beta1 under suitable conditions. The existence of a short recognition sequence does not imply that all integrins bind RGD, or that any protein containing those three adjacent residues necessarily functions as a cell-attachment substrate. The sequence must be accessible and presented in a compatible chemical and structural context. Other receptor–ligand recognition mechanisms remain important.

The demonstration that small synthetic fibronectin fragments could reproduce cell-attachment activity was a major contribution to establishing the sequence-specific nature of some adhesive interactions. In 1984, Pierschbacher and Ruoslahti reported that attachment-promoting activity of fibronectin could be reproduced by small synthetic fragments. Subsequent work examined the activity of variants of the recognition region. The conceptual advance was that cells were not merely sticking indiscriminately to any large extracellular protein. Particular molecular features could be sufficient for defined attachment behavior under suitable assay conditions. [9–10] It was not a demonstration that all of fibronectin's functions are encoded by a three-residue peptide.

Why can a soluble recognition peptide sometimes decrease cell attachment while an immobilized version promotes attachment? A cell attached to a matrix requires a connection to an external structure that can withstand forces generated at its surface. A peptide immobilized on a substrate can supply a receptor-recognition site tied to that substrate. A soluble peptide may occupy some receptors without providing a mechanically connected attachment point. Under appropriate concentrations and receptor specificity, it can compete with binding to the immobilized ligand. The same chemical recognition motif may therefore have different functional outcomes depending on its presentation. This is a valuable example of the distinction between binding and adhesion.

Plasma fibronectin circulates in a soluble form and is synthesized largely by the liver. Cellular fibronectin is produced by many cell types and can assemble into fibrillar extracellular matrices. Alternative splicing generates fibronectin variants that differ in the inclusion of particular regions. The distinction between circulating and matrix-associated fibronectin is not equivalent to the distinction between entirely unrelated proteins. They are related forms with differing biological contexts and organization. In a wound, circulating fibronectin can become associated with the provisional extracellular material, while cells subsequently contribute new matrix constituents. The exact composition changes during repair.

Assembly of a fibronectin matrix is influenced by cells. Surface receptors attach to fibronectin and cytoskeletal forces can change how fibronectin molecules are organized and expose interaction regions. Association between molecules contributes to fibrillar assembly. This mechanism illustrates why simply secreting a soluble adhesive protein does not guarantee formation of a functional network. The cell participates in the organization of its substrate. The mature substrate then affects future attachment and cellular force generation. These reciprocal relationships make ECM assembly a dynamic process, not just spontaneous deposition of secreted material.

Fibronectin can provide routes for migration. A cell can establish attachment to the matrix, exert force through that attachment and release or reorganize contacts as its position changes. If attachment is too weak, the cell may fail to transmit useful traction. If attachment is very strong and cannot be released, movement can also be impaired. Thus migration can depend on a balance of adhesive formation and turnover. The exact relationship depends on the cell and matrix context, and the detailed receptor machinery is the subject of the next chapter. The essential ECM concept is that the spatial distribution and presentation of adhesive ligands shape the possible paths of cell movement.

Consider an experiment in which attachment is measured on surfaces coated with full-length fibronectin, an immobilized RGD-containing peptide, an otherwise comparable control peptide with an altered recognition motif, and an uncoated surface. If cells attach preferentially to fibronectin and the immobilized RGD peptide, the results support sequence-dependent attachment in the tested system. They do not establish that fibronectin and the peptide are functionally equivalent in every respect. The full-length protein has additional domains and can form assemblies that the small peptide cannot. Matching ligand density, accessibility, substrate chemistry and cell viability is important before attributing the result specifically to sequence recognition.

The interpretation becomes richer if soluble competitor is introduced. If soluble RGD reduces attachment on a fibronectin-coated surface but an unrelated peptide does not, the result is consistent with involvement of an RGD-recognizing receptor. If some attachment persists, several explanations are possible: not all receptors may be occupied, alternative fibronectin domains may be involved, or other deposited ligands may support adhesion. Adding a control for general toxicity and using an independent receptor-specific perturbation would help distinguish these possibilities. These are hypothetical assay designs informed by the historical finding, not reproduced historical raw data.

9. Laminin: molecular organization at a cell-facing sheet

Laminins are a family of large extracellular adhesive glycoproteins especially important in basement membranes. A laminin molecule contains one alpha, one beta and one gamma chain, making it a heterotrimer rather than a homotrimer or a fibronectin-like dimer. Different combinations of chain isoforms generate laminins with distinct distributions and interaction properties. A familiar schematic of certain laminins shows three shorter arms and one longer arm, with the long region formed by a coiled-coil association of the three chains. Not every laminin isoform has an identical complete cross-shaped architecture, but the schematic helps establish the positions of major interaction regions.

The long arm of a typical laminin ends in globular domains contributed by the alpha chain. These regions participate in binding selected cell-surface receptors and other molecules. Interactions between short-arm terminal regions of polymerization-competent laminins contribute to formation of an extracellular network. Different interactions are therefore spatially organized within the same molecule. A laminin variant unable to interact efficiently with a receptor might retain some ability to associate with other laminins, whereas a polymerization defect could disrupt network organization even if some receptor-binding regions remain functional.

It is important to distinguish binding to a cell from polymerizing within a matrix. The first establishes an interface between a molecular constituent and a receptor; the second connects matrix molecules to one another. Both can be necessary for building a stable cell-associated sheet. In many basement membrane assembly contexts, accumulation of appropriate laminins at cell surfaces increases their local concentration and favors network assembly. Additional matrix constituents become organized into or alongside that network. However, the details vary by tissue and laminin isoform. The useful general model is that cell attachment and extracellular polymerization cooperate.

Cell attachment to laminin may involve several types of receptors, including selected integrins and dystroglycan in appropriate tissues. These receptors do not all recognize an identical short motif. A broad claim that RGD is the only molecular basis of cell–ECM recognition is therefore incorrect. Many laminin-binding integrins recognize structural regions that depend on the organization of the laminin molecule, rather than the canonical fibronectin RGD sequence. In an experimental comparison of substrates, a cell that attaches to fibronectin but not laminin may lack appropriate receptor expression or activation for laminin, rather than lacking all adhesive capabilities.

Dystroglycan provides a useful additional example in muscle. Its extracellular component can interact with laminin and other ligands, and its membrane-associated component participates in a larger complex connecting the extracellular environment to intracellular structural elements. Appropriate glycosylation is important for some of its extracellular ligand interactions. The details of muscle membrane complexes belong to other curriculum topics, but this example demonstrates that integrins, although central matrix receptors, are not the only routes by which cells interact with extracellular material. A glycosylation defect can affect matrix attachment even if the quantity of the extracellular laminin is normal.

Laminin nomenclature can initially appear confusing. In a chain-based system a laminin designation such as laminin-111 identifies alpha1, beta1 and gamma1 chains. Laminin-332 identifies alpha3, beta3 and gamma2. The digits are not subunit copy numbers; each molecule contains one chain of each broad class. The nomenclature permits a student to infer which chain changes between two isoforms without memorizing every tissue distribution. For example, two forms sharing beta1 and gamma1 but differing in alpha chain may have different interactions mediated by alpha-chain regions while retaining some shared structural features.

Many epithelia depend on basement membrane attachment for stable organization. The extracellular binding functions of laminins help create a basal interface, while intracellular and membrane-associated structures transmit mechanical and regulatory consequences. At the muscle surface, laminin-associated connections help couple the extracellular network to cell structures subjected to repeated contraction. These examples emphasize that a laminin-rich sheet is not simply a flattened collagen bundle. It is an organized interface that combines matrix assembly with cell-surface recognition.

Laminin is also a useful substrate in cell culture because selected cells adhere or extend processes on appropriate laminin-containing surfaces. A positive response to laminin in culture is evidence about the tested cell population and experimental conditions. It should not be generalized to all neurons, epithelial cells or stem cells without further information. Differences in receptor repertoire, substrate presentation and soluble growth conditions can alter the result. A purified laminin coating may permit cell attachment but does not recreate the complete multicomponent architecture of a native basement membrane. This distinction limits the conclusions that can be drawn from simple coating experiments.

Imagine two mutant laminin proteins, one lacking a functional cell-recognition region and the other lacking an essential polymerization region. If both are secreted at normal levels, an antibody against a retained domain could report similar extracellular abundance. Nevertheless, the first mutation may weaken cell attachment and localization of laminin, while the second may impair development of a continuous extracellular network. These are not guaranteed outcomes for every laminin isoform; the predictions assume the specified functions are important in the chosen tissue. A combined analysis of surface association and network continuity would distinguish the mechanisms more effectively than total protein measurement.

10. Building a basement membrane: cooperating molecular networks

The main constituents of a basement membrane can be understood as cooperating molecular systems. Laminins can organize a network close to appropriate cell surfaces. Type IV collagen molecules associate through terminal domains to form a second network with substantial structural importance. Nidogens, perlecan and other associated molecules contribute connections and functions. These systems are not independent sheets that must always be stacked in a fixed order. They interact in a spatially organized composite. Different tissues use differing isoforms and associated proteins, adapting the general architecture to local functions.

Type IV collagen has a long collagenous region with interruptions of the repeating sequence. Its ends provide association sites different from the regions involved in classical fibrillar collagen maturation. At one end, NC1 domains from two protomers associate, producing a complex containing six NC1 domains. At the other end, associations involving four protomers contribute to 7S assemblies. These interactions connect elongated molecules into a network. In this terminology a protomer is one triple-chain collagen IV molecule; four protomers therefore contain twelve polypeptide chains, not four individual alpha chains. The molecular association level must be identified correctly before interpreting a structural diagram.

Basement membranes acquire stability through interactions among their constituents and through appropriate crosslink chemistry. Some connections in type IV collagen networks involve specialized covalent links, including sulfilimine bonds between NC1 domains. Such bonds are distinct from the common lysyl oxidase-initiated crosslinks emphasized for fibrillar collagen and elastin. For NET-level reasoning it is enough to recognize that different extracellular assemblies can employ different stabilization chemistry. Inferring the behavior of all collagens from the type I fibril pathway would ignore the specialized architecture of collagen IV. The precise enzymology of every crosslink is unnecessary for the present chapter.

Nidogen, historically also called entactin, is a basement membrane glycoprotein with binding interactions involving laminins and other matrix constituents. It can contribute to connections between network systems. Older schematic models sometimes present nidogen as the single indispensable bridge connecting a laminin layer to a collagen IV layer. Such a picture is useful as an introduction to molecular interactions but too absolute as a universal mechanism. Other connections and tissue-specific dependencies exist. A lack of one nidogen interaction does not necessarily eliminate every basement membrane in an organism. Conversely, an apparently assembled network may be functionally impaired in a tissue that depends strongly on that interaction.

Perlecan contributes both its core protein domains and carbohydrate chains to basement membrane organization. Agrin is another heparan sulfate proteoglycan associated with specialized basement membranes, with a well-known role in the neuromuscular synaptic environment. These molecules illustrate how a basement membrane can concentrate or present information near a particular cell surface. A specialized synaptic basal lamina is not merely an impermeable partition separating communicating cells. It provides a molecular environment that can help organize local interactions. The detailed mechanisms of neurotransmitter release and reception will be addressed in later nodes rather than repeated here.

The order of assembly can be explored through genetic perturbations. In several developmental contexts, laminin is crucial for initiating a basement membrane assembly at a cell surface, whereas type IV collagen contributes critically to later stability. This does not mean that every tissue follows a rigid universal chronological sequence with identical dependencies. The relevant evidence is that loss of one component can have different effects on initial localization and subsequent maintenance. If an early basement membrane-like structure is present in a mutant but fails when tissue stresses increase, a defect in stabilization is a plausible interpretation. If a structure never becomes organized at the relevant surface, an initiation or attachment defect becomes more plausible.

A controlled experiment could compare normal tissue with tissue deficient in a selected laminin or collagen IV component. Immunolabeling for several retained constituents would reveal their distribution, while electron microscopy could assess continuity and organization. Mechanical or permeability measurements could test the corresponding function. In interpreting such results, an absent epitope must not be mistaken for absence of the entire basement membrane. Loss of one antibody signal may result directly from deletion of its target even if other components persist. Conversely, the presence of several stains does not prove the correct molecular network organization. Different assays provide complementary evidence.

The epithelial–connective tissue interface illustrates the importance of connecting the basement membrane to deeper material. Anchoring structures involving type VII collagen contribute to physical continuity in skin. If the basement membrane is present but its connection to the surrounding connective tissue fails, an epithelial sheet may separate under mechanical stress. That phenotype differs mechanistically from the loss of lateral cell–cell cohesion, even though both can appear as tissue disruption. Identifying the plane of separation and the distribution of molecular markers helps locate the failure. This is an appropriate application of structural reasoning without requiring extensive clinical classification.

The distinction between basement membrane and interstitial matrix is particularly relevant when matrix components are removed experimentally. Degrading an interstitial fibrillar network may weaken mechanical support or change cell migration pathways. Disrupting laminin and collagen IV organization at a cell-facing boundary may compromise tissue polarity or the integrity of an interface. Broad-spectrum matrix degradation can produce both effects. A student should always ask which material was altered, where it was located, which interactions remain possible and what measured consequence follows. The words extracellular matrix alone do not specify a single mechanical or signaling outcome.

11. Physical reasoning: stress, strain, hydration and transport

The mechanical strength of a tissue cannot be compared fairly by measuring the force needed to stretch two samples if their sizes differ. A larger cross-sectional area may carry a greater force even when the material itself is no stronger. To separate the applied force from specimen geometry, engineering tensile stress is defined as force divided by the initial cross-sectional area. If force is expressed in newtons and area in square metres, stress is expressed in pascals. Engineering strain is the change in length divided by the initial length, and is dimensionless. It can be multiplied by one hundred to express percentage extension. These definitions are useful for small deformations of a specimen with an identifiable loading direction.

In a simple region where stress is approximately proportional to strain, the proportionality constant is the Young modulus, E = stress/strain. This is the slope of the stress–strain curve in that region and has the same units as stress. For a uniform specimen behaving linearly, the definition gives F/A = E times the change in length divided by initial length. Rearranging gives F = EA times the change in length divided by initial length. This relationship explains why structural stiffness, force divided by displacement, depends on geometry as well as material modulus. Two samples made of the same material may therefore need different forces for the same absolute elongation.

Consider a hypothetical collagen-rich tissue strip with initial length 20 mm and cross-sectional area 2 mm². A tensile force of 4 N extends it by 0.4 mm. Its engineering stress is 4 divided by 0.000002 square metres, which is 2,000,000 Pa or 2 MPa. The strain is 0.4 divided by 20, or 0.02, corresponding to 2 percent extension. The secant modulus from zero to this point is 2 MPa divided by 0.02, or 100 MPa. If a matched strip at the same stress extends by 0.8 mm, its strain is 0.04 and its secant modulus is 50 MPa. The second strip is more compliant in this particular comparison. The calculation alone cannot identify which molecular constituent changed.

Living tissues rarely behave as ideal linear elastic solids over their entire deformation range. In a collagen-rich material, a low-strain region may involve straightening and reorientation of initially crimped fibers. As more fibrils become aligned and taut, the stress can rise more rapidly for each additional increment of strain. In such a nonlinear curve, a tangent modulus is the local slope, whereas a secant modulus is the average slope between specified points. Comparing a low-strain tangent in one tissue with a high-strain tangent in another can be misleading. A quantitative question should specify the part of the curve being compared or supply enough data to identify it.

A tissue's ability to withstand high stress before failing is not identical to its stiffness. Ultimate tensile strength describes the maximum stress reached in a specified loading test. Extensibility concerns the amount of deformation a material can undergo. Toughness, in a simple tensile test, is related to the energy per unit volume absorbed before rupture and can be estimated from the area under the stress–strain curve. A material that reaches a high stress but breaks at very small strain need not absorb as much energy as a material that continues deforming substantially. These distinctions explain why multiple measured properties may change in different directions after an ECM perturbation.

The energy per unit initial volume supplied during loading is the integral of engineering stress with respect to engineering strain under the corresponding idealized uniaxial description. In a linear elastic region loaded from zero, this area is a triangle and equals one half times stress times strain. For the first illustrative strip at 2 MPa and strain 0.02, the energy density is 0.5 times 2,000,000 times 0.02, or 20,000 J per cubic metre. The energy density would be recoverable in a perfectly elastic model with no dissipation. Real tissues may return along a different unloading curve, making only part of the supplied work mechanically recoverable.

The difference between loading and unloading work is represented by the area enclosed by a hysteresis loop. In biological matrices, dissipation can arise from molecular rearrangement, internal friction and fluid movement. A sample that contains abundant elastin can still show hysteresis because other structures contribute to whole-tissue behavior. Time-dependent deformation at a maintained stress is called creep, whereas decline in stress at maintained deformation is called stress relaxation. These observations are compatible with viscoelastic behavior and, in fluid-containing tissues, can also reflect redistribution of water. The load history and observation interval must therefore be controlled when comparing specimens.

For hydration, an elementary mass balance is useful. If a sample has wet mass 50 mg and dry mass 10 mg after an appropriate drying measurement, its inferred water mass is 40 mg. Water constitutes 40/50 = 0.80, or 80 percent of wet mass. Water per unit dry mass is 40/10 = 4 mg per mg. Suppose another sample has wet mass 35 mg and dry mass 10 mg. Its water mass is 25 mg, water fraction about 71.4 percent, and water per dry mass 2.5 mg per mg. These are different ways to express hydration and should not be interchanged. The change in water fraction is about 8.6 percentage points, not a loss of 8.6 mg.

The ionic contribution to swelling can be introduced with an idealized Donnan model. Imagine a compartment containing immobile negatively charged groups at concentration C_f, in equilibrium with a large external solution containing a monovalent salt at concentration c_s. Assume ideal dilute solutions, free exchange of small ions and fixed charges that cannot cross the boundary. Electroneutrality inside gives c_+ − c_− = C_f, where c_+ and c_− are the internal mobile cation and anion concentrations. Equality of electrochemical potentials for the two ion species, when combined, gives c_+ c_− = c_s². The electrical potential factors cancel when the two equalities are multiplied.

Solving these relationships gives c_+ = [sqrt(C_f² + 4c_s²) + C_f]/2 and c_− = [sqrt(C_f² + 4c_s²) − C_f]/2. The sum of internal mobile ion concentrations is sqrt(C_f² + 4c_s²), exceeding the external sum 2c_s when C_f is positive. In the simple dilute ideal model the mobile-ion contribution to the osmotic pressure difference is RT times that concentration difference, with R the gas constant and T the absolute temperature. The term is not the complete swelling pressure of cartilage; polymer interactions, the mechanical constraint of the network and nonideal effects contribute to the real balance.

For a hypothetical matrix at 310 K with fixed charge concentration 0.10 mol/L and external monovalent salt concentration 0.15 mol/L, the internal ion sum is sqrt(0.01 + 0.09) = 0.3162 mol/L. The difference from the external sum is 0.0162 mol/L, or 16.2 mol/m³. Multiplying by 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and 310 K gives approximately 41.8 kPa as the ideal mobile-ion osmotic contribution. Raising external salt concentration to 0.30 mol/L while holding the fixed charge concentration constant reduces the difference to approximately 0.00828 mol/L and the ideal contribution to approximately 21.3 kPa. The tendency for salt to screen charge-related swelling follows from the model. In a freely swelling real matrix, C_f itself can change as the volume changes, so the fixed-volume comparison cannot be treated as a complete prediction of equilibrium tissue volume.

Transport through hydrated matrices is also affected by the physical environment. For a dilute solute with a constant effective diffusion coefficient D in a simple one-dimensional geometry, Fick's first law expresses flux density as J = −D dc/dx, where c is concentration and x is distance. If a concentration difference of 0.5 mol/m³ is maintained across a uniform layer 0.5 mm thick with D = 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²/s, the magnitude of the ideal steady-state flux is 2 × 10⁻⁷ mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The negative sign in the original law indicates movement toward decreasing concentration. The effective coefficient can be influenced by obstruction, molecular size and transient association with matrix molecules.

These simplified calculations teach the relationships between quantities rather than providing measured values for particular organs. An apparent change in permeability may arise from a change in diffusion coefficient, thickness or solute partitioning at an interface. Fluid flow can also transport solutes and is not represented by pure diffusion. Likewise, a matrix property measured at a large tissue scale is not necessarily equal to the local property experienced by an individual cell. Whenever an examination provides a simplified equation, identify what is held constant and whether the biological intervention changes any of the assumptions.

12. Matrix cues and cellular responses

A cell receives information from its extracellular surroundings through interactions that connect those surroundings to the plasma membrane. The matrix may contain molecules that bind receptors directly, retain soluble signaling factors, or provide resistance to deformation. These three influences can occur together. For example, an adhesive ligand can connect a cell to a material that also retains a growth factor and possesses a particular stiffness. Changing one molecular constituent may then alter several classes of information. The challenge is to determine which of these changes explains an observed cellular response.

Integrins are major cell-surface receptors for extracellular matrix proteins. They are transmembrane heterodimers whose extracellular regions bind appropriate ligands and whose intracellular connections can transmit forces and influence signaling. It is sufficient here to understand that an extracellular protein does not need to enter the cytoplasm to change intracellular behavior. Its interaction with a receptor can influence the organization of proteins on the cytoplasmic side. Detailed activation states, adaptor proteins and bidirectional signaling are reserved for 4.B.05, where the receptor rather than the extracellular ligand is the central subject.

A cell attached to a matrix often exerts traction through its attachment sites. On a very compliant surface, the substrate can move substantially in response to a given force; on a more resistant surface, the same force produces smaller deformation. The cell's own structural systems can respond to the resulting mechanical conditions. The conversion of mechanical information into a biochemical or cellular response is broadly called mechanotransduction. Matrix stiffness is not the only relevant feature: ligand mobility, stress relaxation, fiber orientation and the capacity of the matrix to be remodeled can also influence the forces that a cell experiences.

In an influential 2006 study, Engler, Sen, Sweeney and Discher examined how substrates with different elasticities influenced lineage-associated behavior of cultured mesenchymal stem cells. The study contributed to the evidence that mechanical features of a cell's surroundings can influence differentiation-related outcomes. [11] This observation does not establish an unconditional rule that a specified modulus always produces a specified cell type in every culture system. Soluble factors, ligand presentation, cellular state and experimental timescale can all modify the response. For examination purposes the key is to distinguish an experimentally observed environmental influence from a claim of universal mechanical determination of cell fate.

An experiment testing stiffness should therefore control surface chemistry. Suppose two gels of different moduli are coated with fibronectin. If the softer gel retains fewer accessible adhesive sites than the stiffer gel, a difference in cell spreading cannot be attributed uniquely to stiffness. Both ligand density and material mechanics changed. A stronger experiment varies stiffness while matching ligand presentation and measures that presentation independently. Complementary experiments vary ligand density at approximately constant stiffness. This two-factor logic allows a student to identify main influences and possible interactions, rather than merely selecting whichever explanation seems more familiar.

Spatially patterned matrix molecules can also direct cell organization. A gradient of immobilized adhesive ligands can provide spatial information to cells capable of responding to that gradient. Directed movement associated with a substrate-bound cue is often called haptotaxis, in contrast with chemotaxis toward or away from a gradient of soluble chemical concentration. The categories may overlap in natural tissues if a factor partitions between matrix-bound and soluble forms. Contact guidance refers to directional organization or migration along physical features such as aligned fibers. A migrating cell following aligned collagen without a chemical concentration gradient need not be undergoing chemotaxis.

Retention of soluble factors supplies a second mechanism of environmental regulation. A growth factor that binds a matrix-associated GAG may be enriched near a cell surface relative to its concentration in bulk fluid. This could sustain a local reservoir or contribute to receptor complex formation. Alternatively, if the binding interaction prevents the factor from reaching its receptor, immediate signaling can be reduced. Matrix degradation may release an associated factor and change its spatial availability. The outcome depends on the balance between retention, release, receptor binding and removal. It is not determined simply by whether more or less extracellular material is present.

A relevant example is the participation of heparan sulfate in selected fibroblast growth factor signaling systems. The carbohydrate chains can contribute to interactions among the growth factor and its receptor, depending on their structure and context. The underlying principle is that matrix-associated molecules can influence the effective presentation of a ligand rather than acting as the principal receptor themselves. A cell might have normal receptor abundance and still show reduced response when appropriate heparan sulfate interactions are disrupted. Conversely, adding excess soluble GAG fragments may produce outcomes that differ from restoring the correctly localized cell-associated material.

Matrix attachment also influences survival in many anchorage-dependent animal cells. Loss of appropriate substrate attachment can contribute to a form of apoptosis termed anoikis. This is not a universal fate of every detached cell: circulating blood cells, for example, are adapted to exist without continuous anchorage to a solid interstitial matrix. Other survival signals and the cell's developmental state are important. The concept connects extracellular organization to cell fate regulation without requiring repetition of the intracellular death pathways covered elsewhere. It also explains why viability controls are necessary when an experimental substrate fails to support attachment.

The physiological importance of matrix cues is especially clear during tissue organization. A newly positioned cell may encounter a matrix deposited by earlier cells, providing a pre-existing spatial pattern. It can also produce new components that change the environment for its neighbors. Thus cell–matrix communication is not necessarily a rapid one-to-one message. The extracellular network can preserve the influence of prior cellular activities over longer intervals. By combining adhesive, mechanical and biochemical cues, it links the behavior of cells across time and distance. This principle prepares the way for understanding the receptor machinery of the next node.

13. Matrix turnover, degradation and controlled remodeling

A stable tissue must maintain its extracellular structure, yet it must also adapt during growth, repair and changing mechanical conditions. These demands require controlled remodeling. Remodeling includes deposition of new constituents, cleavage or removal of existing ones, and reorganization of material that remains present. It is not synonymous with complete matrix destruction. During development, a temporary local change in a matrix may permit cells to move or permit a tissue boundary to change shape. During repair, provisional material can be replaced by a more mature network. The balance between persistence and change is essential.

Matrix metalloproteinases, commonly abbreviated MMPs, form a family of zinc-dependent proteolytic enzymes involved in remodeling extracellular substrates. Some are particularly effective against selected fibrillar collagens, while others act on denatured collagen, basement membrane constituents or a variety of other substrates. The family name does not imply that every MMP cleaves every protein. Substrate structure and accessibility are important. For example, efficient cleavage of an intact collagen triple helix places different demands on an enzyme from cleavage of an exposed polypeptide chain in gelatin. Exam questions often use that difference to distinguish collagenase-like and gelatinase-like activity.

Many MMPs are synthesized as precursors whose catalytic activity is restricted until activation. Their abundance, localization, precursor processing and inhibition can all affect the rate of extracellular proteolysis. Tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases, or TIMPs, contribute to regulation of several MMP activities. A measurement of MMP messenger RNA or total protein does not establish how much active enzyme is available at the relevant location. If a tissue produces abundant inactive precursor while maintaining inhibition of the active form, the net rate of degradation may remain low. The measurement needed depends on which step in the proposed mechanism is under investigation.

Other enzymes also participate in matrix turnover. Hyaluronidases can degrade hyaluronan, and specialized proteases can cleave proteoglycan core proteins. Members of the ADAMTS family contribute, for example, to aggrecan cleavage in cartilage contexts. Removing a proteoglycan core from an aggregate can release many attached GAG chains even if those polysaccharides have not each been chemically cleaved. In a similar manner, cleavage at one structural connection can disrupt the organization of a larger network. The enzyme's direct substrate and the eventual molecules lost from a tissue therefore need not be identical.

A biological distinction can be made between controlled degradation at a particular site and excessive widespread degradation. Local remodeling may allow a cell to pass through a dense matrix, while deposition elsewhere maintains the overall structure. If degradation becomes extensive and is not matched by restoration, mechanical and regulatory functions may be compromised. Excessive deposition can also be harmful because a highly rigid or densely organized matrix may restrict normal tissue behavior. The appropriate amount and organization depend on the tissue rather than on a universal goal of maximizing extracellular protein abundance.

A simple mass-balance model captures the relationship between synthesis and removal. Let M be the amount of a defined matrix component in a specified tissue region, with units of mass, let S be the deposition rate in mass per unit time, and let k be a first-order effective removal constant in inverse time. If these quantities and conditions are approximately constant, the model is dM/dt = S − kM. At steady state the derivative is zero, so M_ss = S/k. If deposition and removal both increase proportionally, the steady-state quantity can remain unchanged even though the turnover flux becomes larger. Thus identical total matrix abundance can conceal very different dynamic states.

For a hypothetical region with S = 2 mg/day and k = 0.10 day⁻¹, the steady amount in this model is 20 mg. If the removal constant doubles to 0.20 day⁻¹ without a change in deposition, the new steady amount is 10 mg. The existing material does not immediately decrease to 10 mg when the parameter changes. Starting from the earlier steady amount M₀, the solution is M(t) = M_ss + (M₀ − M_ss)e^(−kt). After 5 days at the new constant, e^(−1) is about 0.368, so the predicted amount is 13.68 mg. The model assumes a homogeneous pool and fixed parameters; it cannot capture separate fast and slow matrix fractions, changing accessibility, or feedback that adjusts deposition.

A related model applies to an individually labeled matrix pool in a pulse–chase experiment if the labeled material is removed with first-order kinetics and there is no further entry of label. The labeled amount L(t) then equals L₀e^(−kt). Its half-life is ln(2)/k. With k = 0.10 day⁻¹, this is approximately 6.93 days. If unlabeled deposition continues, total matrix mass may remain constant while the labeled fraction declines. This reasoning illustrates why pulse–chase evidence about turnover and a measurement of total amount are complementary rather than interchangeable. Biological labeling experiments require attention to recycling and to whether the label tracks intact material or retained fragments.

Matrix proteolysis may affect information as well as structure. Cleavage can expose a previously inaccessible binding region or release a matrix-associated signaling molecule. It can also remove an attachment ligand or destroy an organizing connection. In some contexts, fragments generated from larger matrix molecules have biological activity of their own. Consequently, a change in cellular behavior after protease treatment is not always explained solely by reduced mechanical support. Distinguishing release of a factor, loss of ligand and physical reorganization may require separate measurements and appropriate rescue experiments.

A wound provides an integrated example of controlled turnover. The earliest extracellular environment after injury can include a provisional clot-associated network that supports subsequent cell movement. Cells then deposit additional extracellular material, and the structure evolves as the wound closes and matures. A matrix with high synthesis at one time may later undergo extensive reorganization. Mature collagen arrangement and mechanical strength do not necessarily recover at the same rate as tissue coverage. This is why a visually closed wound cannot automatically be assumed to have regained its original load-bearing properties. The detailed inflammatory mechanisms and clinical management of wounds belong outside the present scope.

Remodeling experiments should discriminate loss of matrix from changes in the cells that normally make it. A reduction in deposited protein after exposure to a substance could reflect reduced cell number, impaired synthesis, disrupted secretion or increased degradation. Assays for viable cell number and newly synthesized material can help distinguish these alternatives. A selective enzyme activity assay can test the degradation hypothesis. Measuring fragments in the extracellular fluid may support cleavage, provided those fragments have a known origin and clearance is considered. The most defensible interpretation is the one supported by converging evidence rather than by a single change in total abundance.

14. Tissue-specific design, evolution and biological failure

The different matrix components are best understood through the tasks that particular tissues must perform. A tendon transfers directed tensile forces. Its oriented collagen-rich structure limits extension while permitting transmission of force. Hyaline cartilage must withstand repeated compression and permit joint motion with little friction. Its proteoglycan-rich hydrated matrix operates within a collagen II-containing framework. An elastic artery undergoes repeated changes in circumference, making elastic recoil and collagen-dependent limits to expansion important. These examples are not exclusive chemical definitions; they describe the functions achieved by the characteristic organization of each composite.

Bone illustrates another form of extracellular specialization. Type I collagen contributes the organic framework, while mineral deposition, predominantly in the form of calcium phosphate mineral related to hydroxyapatite, provides important resistance to deformation. A purely mineral structure and a purely organic collagen structure would not exhibit all the properties of native bone. The composite combines contributions from both phases. Defects in collagen organization can weaken bone even if mineral is present, while inadequate mineral deposition can impair stiffness despite an organic framework. This is another reason to avoid interpreting a tissue's function from a single chemical measurement.

The dermis contains an interstitial matrix with substantial collagen and elastic fibers, whereas the epidermis depends on a basement membrane-associated interface. Damage to the dermal collagen network may compromise tensile support, while alteration of elastic fibers may affect reversible deformation. Failure at the epithelial–matrix interface may instead produce separation of layers. These phenotypes illustrate three mechanically distinguishable roles within one organ. A specimen whose surface is fragile need not have a uniform reduction in every type of ECM protein. Identifying the layer and the kind of mechanical failure narrows the likely molecular mechanism.

In the cornea, extracellular organization contributes to an optical function as well as mechanical support. The arrangement and spacing of stromal collagen fibrils, their dimensions and the regulated hydration of the tissue are important for transparency. Proteoglycans contribute to the organization of this environment. Excessive swelling or disrupted organization can alter light scattering. A cornea rich in collagen is therefore not necessarily opaque; the arrangement of that collagen is crucial. Conversely, transparency cannot be predicted by measuring total collagen mass alone. This tissue makes especially clear that matrix architecture supports functions beyond simple strength.

Different cell populations also experience matrix material in different ways. Chondrocytes are embedded within a matrix rich in hydrated components; fibroblasts commonly maintain interstitial networks; many epithelial cells interact primarily with a basal surface and with neighboring cells. Endothelial cells experience basement membrane-associated cues in conjunction with fluid-derived mechanical signals at the luminal surface. Such distinctions influence which receptors are available, which parts of the matrix are contacted and how external forces reach the cell. The extracellular environment is therefore a local property, not a uniform substance with identical effects on all cell types in an organ.

Animal extracellular matrices can be compared with the plant cell wall at a functional level. Both are external to the plasma membrane and provide mechanical support while affecting cellular behavior. Plant primary cell walls typically rely on cellulose microfibrils within a matrix containing polysaccharides such as hemicelluloses and pectins; many secondary walls contain lignin. Animal collagen-rich ECMs and plant cellulose-rich walls therefore implement related physical tasks through different molecular building blocks. Plant turgor and wall mechanics form a distinct integrated system. The analogy does not imply that a plant wall is made of collagen, or that plant and animal adhesion receptors are interchangeable.

Bacterial cell walls and extracellular biofilm matrices offer further comparative examples. Peptidoglycan contributes mechanical support to many bacterial cell walls, whereas extracellular biofilm material may contain polysaccharides, proteins and extracellular nucleic acids. These extracellular materials can organize multicellular-like communities and affect the local environment, yet they are not structurally identical to vertebrate basement membranes. The broad evolutionary point is that extracellular construction has repeatedly become useful for protection, adhesion and organization. It would be incorrect to infer that the same molecular families implement those roles in all domains of life.

Collagen-like sequences and extracellular adhesion mechanisms have diversified across organisms, and basement membrane-associated proteins contribute to tissue organization in many animals. A conserved structural motif can be combined with differing accessory domains to support distinct assemblies. Collagen IV and fibrillar collagens demonstrate this principle within one protein family. Selection can act not only on a protein's molecular properties but also on where and when it is produced and how it is organized. For the present scope, comparisons should emphasize conserved functional principles without requiring memorization of every taxonomic distribution or reconstructing an unverified evolutionary chronology.

A loss-of-function change in a matrix gene can reduce the amount of the encoded component; a structurally abnormal protein may instead enter a network and interfere with its organization. These alternatives help explain why some collagen-related mutations can have a dominant effect despite the presence of a normal allele. A useful simplified example assumes random assembly of three identical-chain-class positions from equal pools of normal and mutant chains. The probability that a trimer contains only normal chains is (1/2)³ = 1/8. If every trimer with a mutant chain is dysfunctional, seven eighths of trimers would be affected under these assumptions. Actual collagen chain selection, degradation and the effect of a given mutation may violate the model, particularly for defined heterotrimers. The calculation is a teaching model, not a clinical prediction.

Matrix abnormalities need not be inherited. Defective nutrient-dependent collagen modification, excessive proteolysis or an altered balance of matrix deposition and removal can impair tissue function. A mechanically abnormal tissue may also alter the behavior of its resident cells, changing future deposition and remodeling. This feedback can reinforce an abnormal state. It does not imply that every change in matrix mechanics produces the same cellular response. The response depends on the receptors, local cell type and other influences present. The most useful examination answers identify the first altered step and trace its plausible consequences instead of naming a disease without explaining the underlying biology.

15. Experimental integration and reasoning across levels

The strongest evidence about an ECM constituent usually comes from combining approaches. A biochemical assay can identify or quantify a molecule. Imaging can reveal where it is located and whether an organized structure is present. Mechanical testing can reveal how the material behaves, and cellular assays can identify effects on attachment or signaling. Each measurement has limitations. A preparation with normal total protein but abnormal fiber organization may be mechanically weak. A preparation with an apparently continuous basement membrane can still show abnormal receptor binding if a relevant interaction domain is missing. Distinguishing these possibilities requires measurements designed around a specific causal question.

Decellularized extracellular preparations illustrate the difference between the contribution of cells and that of their matrix. If cells are removed while some organized ECM remains, the preparation can be used to examine the influence of retained extracellular material on subsequently introduced cells. A comparison between matrices deposited by different cell populations may reveal that the new cells respond differently. However, the removal process itself can alter matrix composition, mechanical properties or retained signaling factors. A useful control therefore assesses the remaining material rather than assuming that decellularization changes only the presence of living cells. The conceptual method is relevant to biotechnology without requiring a specialized laboratory protocol.

Suppose two cultures of the same epithelial cell type are placed on surfaces prepared from normal and altered extracellular matrices. Cells on the altered material spread poorly and show reduced survival. One possibility is loss of adhesive ligands; another is that the substrate has altered mechanical resistance; a third is that a matrix-associated growth factor is missing. Providing an appropriate immobilized adhesive ligand could test whether ligand availability contributes. Matching physical properties could address mechanical differences, and a soluble-factor rescue might address missing biochemical support. No single rescue proves every other property is normal; a rescue identifies a pathway capable of contributing to the observed phenotype.

A useful prediction follows when cells can adhere to purified fibronectin but cannot attach normally to a mutant tissue matrix. Because the cells retain attachment capability in one context, the defect may involve the tissue matrix's ligand availability or organization. However, the cells might still lack a receptor needed specifically for another matrix component. Testing attachment to purified laminin and collagen under comparable conditions would provide additional information. A reciprocal experiment with normal cells on mutant matrix and mutant cells on normal matrix can help distinguish primarily extracellular from primarily cellular defects. The interpretation depends on whether cells are capable of remodeling the test matrix during the observation period.

Mechanical measurements can be similarly combined with molecular perturbations. Imagine a matched set of cartilage specimens in which a selective treatment reduces aggrecan-associated GAG content while leaving the collagen network initially intact. The prediction is a reduction in fixed charge-associated swelling and a change in compressive behavior. A treatment disrupting the collagen framework could instead alter the restraint of swelling and structural integrity. Both interventions can impair cartilage function, but their effects on water content need not be identical. To test the mechanistic predictions, one would compare charge-related composition, hydration under specified conditions, and loading behavior over comparable timescales.

An apparent disagreement between two experiments may arise because they measure different aspects of the same system. A short compression test may emphasize fluid pressurization, whereas a long-duration test may reveal the behavior of a more equilibrated solid network. A rapid cell-attachment assay may primarily measure receptor–ligand binding, whereas a longer assay includes deposition of new material, cellular survival and changes in receptor expression. These are not necessarily contradictory outcomes. Before rejecting one interpretation, identify the relevant physical timescale and the biological processes that can occur during the measurement.

Histological images and immunostaining should be interpreted with similar care. If antibody staining for collagen IV is absent from a region, it could reflect absence of the targeted protein, alteration or masking of its epitope, or a technical staining failure. Controls for antibody specificity and preservation of tissue are necessary. Labeling laminin or another basement membrane constituent can help establish whether some part of the network remains. An ultrastructural image can then reveal whether the cell-associated layer is continuous. A correct inference describes what the specific marker shows and avoids equating a negative stain with complete absence of extracellular structure unless additional evidence supports that conclusion.

A quantitative experiment may present an apparent correlation between increasing matrix crosslink density and increasing tissue stiffness. Such a correlation is consistent with greater constraint on molecular rearrangement, but it does not prove that crosslinking alone caused the change. Samples with more crosslinks may also have altered fiber alignment or collagen concentration. A controlled manipulation that changes crosslink formation while preserving other relevant properties would strengthen causal inference. Conversely, excessive crosslink formation might increase stiffness while compromising other properties such as extensibility or remodeling capacity. The correct examination answer depends on which property is measured rather than on treating mechanical quality as a single scalar.

A matrix-associated growth factor can be studied through the distinction between total and free ligand. In a simplified preparation, if 80 of 100 arbitrary units of a factor are matrix-bound, 20 units remain free under the stated partition assumption. Another preparation retaining only 40 units has 60 free units if the total is still 100. It would be incorrect to conclude from this instantaneous calculation alone that signaling must triple: the free factor may be cleared rapidly, matrix binding may help form receptor complexes, and receptor activation may saturate. The calculation establishes a change in partitioning, whereas the biological response requires additional information about the mechanism.

The central thread running through these experiments is that matrix molecules have multiple roles that operate at different scales. Collagen sequence determines molecular folding, molecular assembly determines network architecture, architecture influences mechanical behavior, and surface interactions connect the network to cells. Proteoglycan charge influences hydration and can also affect protein localization. Fibronectin and laminin supply selective molecular recognition in different extracellular arrangements. Remodeling changes the composition and organization over time. A student who follows these causal links can reason from an unfamiliar perturbation to a plausible consequence without needing to memorize an exhaustive catalogue of matrix-related disorders. The next chapter will examine integrins in greater depth and explain how cells actively regulate the receptor connections that make this extracellular information biologically effective.

Part B — Conceptual questions

Original examination-style practice. These questions are not official previous-year questions.

1. Which description best distinguishes animal ECM from interstitial fluid?

  1. All molecules outside the plasma membrane
  2. A selectively permeable sheet composed mainly of extracellular lipids
  3. An organized extracellular macromolecular network associated with water and ions
  4. A pool of soluble secreted proteins without higher-order organization
Answer and explanation

C. An organized extracellular macromolecular network associated with water and ions — The defining feature is an organized macromolecular material. Extracellular fluid exchanges solutes with the network but is not identical to it. Neither a lipid bilayer nor the entire soluble extracellular compartment is ECM.

2. Which pair is characteristic of the core basement membrane architecture?

  1. Laminin and type IV collagen
  2. Elastin and type I collagen
  3. Aggrecan and type II collagen
  4. Cadherin and selectin
Answer and explanation

A. Laminin and type IV collagen — Laminin assemblies and collagen IV networks are characteristic, with associated nidogens and proteoglycans. The other pairs exemplify interstitial matrix components or cell–cell adhesion molecules.

3. Why is glycine especially frequent at every third position in uninterrupted collagenous domains?

  1. Its side chain forms strong hydrophobic contacts within the helix center
  2. It creates a conventional alpha helix in each chain
  3. Its side chain is the main substrate of lysyl oxidase-dependent fibril crosslinking
  4. Its small side chain permits close packing at the triple helix center
Answer and explanation

D. Its small side chain permits close packing at the triple helix center — Glycine has a hydrogen side chain and fits the restricted center. Collagen chains are not conventional alpha helices. Glycine has no hydrophobic side chain of appreciable size, and LOX acts principally on lysine-derived residues, not glycine.

4. Which collagen is the major characteristic fibrillar collagen of hyaline cartilage?

  1. Type IV
  2. Type II
  3. Type VII
  4. Type I
Answer and explanation

B. Type II — Type II contributes to the cartilage fibrillar framework. Type IV forms basement membrane networks; type VII helps anchor epithelia; type I predominates in tendon, bone and many other connective tissues.

5. Which event normally occurs in the extracellular space during maturation of typical fibrillar collagen?

  1. Cleavage of terminal procollagen propeptides
  2. Ribosomal synthesis of collagen alpha chains
  3. Initial proline hydroxylation in the ER
  4. Selection of procollagen chains by C-terminal propeptides
Answer and explanation

A. Cleavage of terminal procollagen propeptides — Propeptide processing occurs after secretion and supports fibril assembly. The other processes concern the intracellular secretory pathway.

6. What is the most direct chemical role of lysyl oxidase in fibrillar collagen assembly?

  1. Hydroxylation of proline in the ER
  2. Removal of all collagen propeptides
  3. Oxidative deamination of selected lysine or hydroxylysine residues
  4. Reduction of aldehydes to hydroxylysine in the secretory pathway
Answer and explanation

C. Oxidative deamination of selected lysine or hydroxylysine residues — The copper-dependent enzyme generates aldehydes that participate in crosslink formation. Prolyl hydroxylation and precursor cleavage are separate reactions.

7. Which feature distinguishes many mature elastic fibers from collagen fibrils?

  1. A network dominated by triple-helical molecules with NC1 terminal associations
  2. A crosslinked elastin-rich core associated with fibrillin-rich microfibrils
  3. Aggrecan core proteins with keratan sulfate chains
  4. A bundle of staggered triple helices with a periodic axial banding pattern
Answer and explanation

B. A crosslinked elastin-rich core associated with fibrillin-rich microfibrils — Elastin is formed from tropoelastin and is organized with fibrillin-rich microfibrils. NC1 associations characterize collagen IV; keratan sulfate-bearing aggrecan contributes hydration; staggered banded molecules characterize fibrillar collagen.

8. Which property is specifically indicated by substantial recovery of initial specimen length after unloading?

  1. Ultimate tensile strength
  2. High structural stiffness
  3. High dry-mass fraction
  4. Elastic recovery
Answer and explanation

D. Elastic recovery — Elastic recovery concerns reversible deformation. Ultimate strength is maximum stress, stiffness concerns resistance to deformation, and dry mass is a composition measure.

9. Which glycosaminoglycan is generally unsulfated and not covalently linked to a protein core?

  1. Heparan sulfate
  2. Hyaluronan
  3. Chondroitin sulfate
  4. Dermatan sulfate
Answer and explanation

B. Hyaluronan — Hyaluronan has charged carboxylates despite lacking sulfate. The other GAGs are commonly protein-linked and variably sulfated.

10. Which GAG lacks a uronic acid in its characteristic repeat?

  1. Hyaluronan
  2. Chondroitin sulfate
  3. Heparan sulfate
  4. Keratan sulfate
Answer and explanation

D. Keratan sulfate — Keratan sulfate contains galactose and N-acetylglucosamine, with sulfation. The other repeats contain uronic acid residues.

11. In a cartilage aggrecan aggregate, what directly binds the hyaluronan backbone?

  1. An aggrecan core region through noncovalent association stabilized by link proteins
  2. The C terminus of every collagen I chain through disulfide bonds
  3. Each GAG side chain by a separate covalent linkage to hyaluronan
  4. A collagen fibril through covalent links between its telopeptides and hyaluronan
Answer and explanation

A. An aggrecan core region through noncovalent association stabilized by link proteins — The aggregate contains numerous aggrecan cores associated with hyaluronan; their own GAG chains attach covalently to their cores. The side chains are not independently covalently joined to hyaluronan, and collagen telopeptides are not the defining aggregate attachment.

12. Which is a characteristic example of a basement membrane heparan sulfate proteoglycan?

  1. Tropoelastin
  2. E-cadherin
  3. Perlecan
  4. Type II collagen
Answer and explanation

C. Perlecan — Perlecan has a core protein and GAG chains and contributes to basement membrane organization. The others are an elastin precursor, cell–cell CAM and fibrillar collagen.

13. Which statement concerning the fibronectin RGD sequence is most accurate?

  1. It is required for every integrin–ECM interaction
  2. It is a universal site for covalent association with collagen fibrils
  3. It is a motif necessarily active even when buried within a folded protein
  4. It is a recognition motif for selected integrins, but recognition also depends on context
Answer and explanation

D. It is a recognition motif for selected integrins, but recognition also depends on context — RGD is an adhesive motif in fibronectin and other proteins. Not every integrin recognizes it. The motif mediates selective receptor recognition rather than universal covalent collagen attachment, and burial within a folded protein can prevent access.

14. A laminin-332 heterotrimer contains which chains?

  1. Three α3 and two γ2 chains
  2. α3, β3 and γ2
  3. Three β chains with no α chain
  4. α3, α3 and β2
Answer and explanation

B. α3, β3 and γ2 — The digits identify the α, β and γ chain isoforms in that order. They are not copy numbers; a laminin molecule contains one of each broad chain class.

15. Which collagen IV terminal association is correctly matched?

  1. NC1 association of four protomers as a typical junction
  2. 7S association of two protomers through their C-terminal regions
  3. NC1 association of two protomers
  4. NC1 cleavage into a classical 67 nm fibril as a universal requirement
Answer and explanation

C. NC1 association of two protomers — Two protomers provide six NC1 domains; 7S assemblies involve four protomers. Collagen IV forms a network rather than requiring the ordinary fibrillar collagen pathway.

16. Which matrix-associated cell behavior is best described as haptotaxis?

  1. Directed movement in response to an immobilized adhesive ligand gradient
  2. Directed movement exclusively along a soluble concentration gradient
  3. Orientation along aligned fibers without a ligand concentration gradient
  4. Random movement with a speed change but no directional bias
Answer and explanation

A. Directed movement in response to an immobilized adhesive ligand gradient — Haptotaxis concerns substrate-associated cues. Chemotaxis concerns soluble gradients, contact guidance concerns physical orientation cues, and a change in random migration speed without directional bias is not taxis.

17. What is the principal distinction between a proteoglycan and a typical adhesive glycoprotein such as fibronectin?

  1. All proteoglycans are exclusively carbohydrate with no protein
  2. All glycoproteins lack covalent carbohydrates
  3. A proteoglycan bears characteristic covalently attached GAG chains
  4. Every proteoglycan has exactly the same percentage of carbohydrate
Answer and explanation

C. A proteoglycan bears characteristic covalently attached GAG chains — The diagnostic structural feature is GAG attachment to a core protein; carbohydrate proportions vary. Glycoproteins can contain other covalent carbohydrate structures.

18. Which description of matrix metalloproteinases is correct?

  1. A single universal enzyme that cleaves all ECM molecules equally
  2. Copper-dependent enzymes that crosslink elastin
  3. Membrane synthases that polymerize hyaluronan
  4. A family of zinc-dependent proteases with regulated and differing substrate specificities
Answer and explanation

D. A family of zinc-dependent proteases with regulated and differing substrate specificities — MMPs catalyze proteolysis, but substrate preferences and activation differ. Lysyl oxidase and hyaluronan synthases perform distinct chemistry.

19. Which mechanism directly explains why a substrate can influence intracellular processes without entering the cytoplasm?

  1. All ECM proteins are transported intact to the nucleus
  2. Binding to surface receptors that communicate with intracellular structures
  3. The ECM replaces the lipid bilayer
  4. Extracellular collagen directly translates cellular mRNA
Answer and explanation

B. Binding to surface receptors that communicate with intracellular structures — Membrane receptors bridge the extracellular and intracellular environments. Internalization can occur for some molecules, but it is not required for the fundamental receptor-mediated effect.

20. Which comparison between plant cell walls and animal ECM is valid?

  1. Both provide external structural support but use substantially different molecular building blocks
  2. Both principally consist of fibrillar animal collagen I
  3. Both contain the same laminin-based basal lamina
  4. Both are lipid bilayers that actively pump ions
Answer and explanation

A. Both provide external structural support but use substantially different molecular building blocks — Cellulose-rich plant walls and animal collagen/proteoglycan matrices share broad mechanical roles but have different composition, assembly and associated receptors.

Part C — Analytical questions

Original examination-style practice. These questions are not official previous-year questions.

21. A tendon strip has an initial length of 20 mm, area 2 mm² and elongates 0.4 mm under 4 N. What is its secant modulus?

  1. 2 MPa
  2. 20 MPa
  3. 200 MPa
  4. 100 MPa
Answer and explanation

D. 100 MPa — Stress = 4/2 = 2 N/mm² = 2 MPa. Strain = 0.4/20 = 0.02. Modulus = 2/0.02 = 100 MPa. Stress alone is not modulus; 20 MPa and 200 MPa reflect incorrect strain or arithmetic.

22. Two strips of the same linear elastic material have identical length, but B has twice the cross-sectional area of A. Under the same force, what is B's elongation relative to A?

  1. One half
  2. Twice
  3. Equal
  4. One quarter
Answer and explanation

A. One half — From ΔL = FL/(EA), doubling area halves elongation when F, L and E are fixed. This geometry effect does not indicate a change in material modulus.

23. A cartilage specimen changes from 50 mg wet and 10 mg dry to 35 mg wet and 10 mg dry. What is its final water fraction of wet mass?

  1. 50%
  2. 80%
  3. 71.4%
  4. 250%
Answer and explanation

C. 71.4% — Final water mass = 25 mg; fraction = 25/35 = 0.714. The initial fraction was 80%; 250% describes water relative to dry mass rather than wet mass.

24. In an ideal monovalent Donnan model, fixed charge is 0.10 M and bath salt is 0.15 M. Which internal cation and anion concentrations satisfy both conditions?

  1. 0.15 M and 0.15 M
  2. Approximately 0.208 M and 0.108 M
  3. 0.10 M and 0.00 M
  4. 0.316 M and 0.216 M
Answer and explanation

B. Approximately 0.208 M and 0.108 M — The sum is √(0.10²+4×0.15²)=0.3162 M and difference 0.10 M, giving 0.2081 and 0.1081 M. Equal concentrations violate electroneutrality; the other pairs fail the product constraint.

25. Keeping fixed charge concentration and temperature constant, external salt is increased in the ideal Donnan model. What happens to the mobile-ion osmotic pressure excess?

  1. It increases linearly with salt concentration
  2. It decreases
  3. It remains exactly constant
  4. It becomes negative for every nonzero fixed charge
Answer and explanation

B. It decreases — √(C_f²+4c_s²)−2c_s decreases as c_s rises for positive fixed charge. Real unconstrained matrices may additionally change volume and fixed charge concentration.

26. A component is deposited at 2 mg/day and removed with k=0.10/day. Both parameters double. What happens at the new steady state?

  1. Mass doubles and flux stays fixed
  2. Mass halves and flux doubles
  3. Mass remains 20 mg but steady turnover flux doubles
  4. Both mass and flux stay fixed
Answer and explanation

C. Mass remains 20 mg but steady turnover flux doubles — Steady amount S/k = 20 mg in both conditions. At steady state removal equals deposition, which rises from 2 to 4 mg/day. Equal abundance does not imply equal turnover.

27. A labeled ECM pool follows first-order loss with k=0.10/day, with no recycling. Roughly what fraction of label remains after 13.86 days?

  1. 25%
  2. 50%
  3. 75%
  4. 10%
Answer and explanation

A. 25% — Half-life is ln2/0.10 ≈6.93 days; after two half-lives 0.5²=0.25 remains. This does not determine total unlabeled ECM mass.

28. Cells show normal collagen transcription and abundant secreted procollagen but few mature banded fibrils. Which direct defect is most consistent?

  1. Complete loss of transcription of all collagen genes
  2. Increased extracellular precursor secretion with normal cleavage and mature fibril assembly
  3. An early reduction in collagen transcription with no accumulated precursor
  4. Defective extracellular propeptide removal
Answer and explanation

D. Defective extracellular propeptide removal — The precursor is made and secreted but conversion to mature fibrillar collagen is impaired. The observed sequence places the defect downstream of production; the other options do not fit the direct processing pattern.

29. A mutation leaves procollagen secretion and extracellular cleavage intact but selectively lowers lysyl oxidase activity. Which outcome is most likely?

  1. Collagen can be deposited, but intermolecular crosslink-dependent stabilization is weakened
  2. No collagen chains can be translated
  3. Loss of intracellular proline hydroxylation is the necessary primary defect
  4. Propeptide cleavage necessarily stops because LOX acts as its cleavage enzyme
Answer and explanation

A. Collagen can be deposited, but intermolecular crosslink-dependent stabilization is weakened — LOX acts on extracellular crosslink chemistry. Upstream transcription, hydroxylation and cleavage can remain normal; LOX does not perform prolyl hydroxylation or procollagen cleavage.

30. An antibody detects similar laminin abundance in control and mutant tissue, but electron microscopy reveals discontinuous basal material. What is the best next inference?

  1. Basement membrane function must be normal
  2. A defect in organization is plausible despite preserved epitope abundance
  3. All laminin chains must be absent
  4. Normal staining proves that laminin receptor binding is unaltered
Answer and explanation

B. A defect in organization is plausible despite preserved epitope abundance — An antibody detects a retained epitope, not network continuity. Ultrastructure indicates an organization defect worth testing, but it does not identify a unique gene or eliminate other possibilities.

31. Cells bind immobilized RGD but detach when excess soluble RGD is added; unrelated soluble peptide has little effect. What is the strongest interpretation?

  1. The peptide must increase fibronectin deposition by stimulating its transcription
  2. All integrins in all tissues recognize RGD
  3. Soluble and immobilized peptides have identical mechanical functions
  4. Competition for an RGD-recognizing attachment interaction is supported
Answer and explanation

D. Competition for an RGD-recognizing attachment interaction is supported — The sequence-dependent competition is consistent with receptor recognition. Soluble ligand does not provide the mechanically anchored substrate; receptor specificity and toxicity controls would strengthen the inference.

32. Two substrates have different moduli and different accessible fibronectin densities. Cells spread more on the stiffer one. Which design best isolates stiffness?

  1. Increase both modulus and coating concentration again
  2. Compare total fibronectin mRNA only
  3. Compare substrates at different moduli with independently matched accessible ligand density
  4. Use different cell types on the two substrates
Answer and explanation

C. Compare substrates at different moduli with independently matched accessible ligand density — The original experiment confounds mechanics and ligand presentation. Matching accessible coating while varying modulus addresses the main alternative; other options preserve or introduce confounds.

33. Normal cells spread poorly on mutant ECM, whereas mutant cells spread normally on control ECM during a short matched assay. What is supported?

  1. A cell-intrinsic receptor defect is the only possible explanation
  2. All matrix components are absent
  3. A matrix-associated defect contributes to the phenotype
  4. Cell viability is necessarily unchanged without measuring it
Answer and explanation

C. A matrix-associated defect contributes to the phenotype — Reciprocal transfer localizes a contribution to the substrate in this assay. It does not establish which matrix component is responsible or exclude all cell-associated effects.

34. A cartilage treatment lowers retained aggrecan and leaves collagen initially intact. Which consequence is most directly expected?

  1. Reduced fixed charge-associated swelling tendency under matched conditions
  2. Complete loss of all type IV collagen
  3. Increased number of covalent collagen chains per molecule
  4. Increased hyaluronan sulfation as a necessary result
Answer and explanation

A. Reduced fixed charge-associated swelling tendency under matched conditions — Aggrecan loss removes many charged side chains from the retained material. Changes in tissue mechanics and hydration depend on the remaining network and bath conditions; the alternatives are not necessary consequences.

35. A matrix component initially at 20 mg is driven toward a new steady amount of 10 mg with k=0.20/day. What mass is predicted after 5 days?

  1. 10 mg
  2. 13.68 mg
  3. 17.36 mg
  4. 7.36 mg
Answer and explanation

B. 13.68 mg — M=10+(20−10)e^(−0.2×5)=13.68 mg. The steady amount is approached over time; the other values omit or misapply the exponential term.

36. In a simple random assembly model, each of three equivalent chain positions has an independent 50% probability of being normal. Only all-normal trimers function. What fraction functions?

  1. 1/2
  2. 3/8
  3. 7/8
  4. 1/8
Answer and explanation

D. 1/8 — Multiply independent probabilities: (1/2)^3=1/8. Seven eighths contain at least one mutant chain. Actual collagen heterotrimers may not meet the equal independent-position assumption.

37. A sample is compressed rapidly and then held at fixed deformation. Measured stress decreases with time. Which interpretation fits?

  1. Creep defined as strain increase at constant stress
  2. Immediate proof of complete collagen degradation
  3. An increase in equilibrium stress at fixed strain
  4. Stress relaxation, potentially involving fluid redistribution and viscoelasticity
Answer and explanation

D. Stress relaxation, potentially involving fluid redistribution and viscoelasticity — Declining stress at fixed strain is stress relaxation. Creep is a different loading protocol. The observation alone does not identify irreversible degradation.

38. A solute has effective D=2×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s across a 0.5 mm layer with a 0.5 mol/m³ concentration difference. What is the ideal flux magnitude?

  1. 1×10⁻¹⁰ mol m⁻² s⁻¹
  2. 2×10⁻¹³ mol m⁻² s⁻¹
  3. 2×10⁻⁷ mol m⁻² s⁻¹
  4. 4×10⁻⁴ mol m⁻² s⁻¹
Answer and explanation

C. 2×10⁻⁷ mol m⁻² s⁻¹ — Use |J|=DΔc/L. L=5×10⁻⁴ m; 2×10⁻¹⁰×0.5/(5×10⁻⁴)=2×10⁻⁷. The direction is from higher to lower concentration under these assumptions.

39. After a selective receptor-blocking treatment, cells no longer spread on laminin but spread normally on fibronectin. What is the most defensible conclusion?

  1. The blocked interaction contributes to laminin-dependent spreading in this context
  2. Every cell–matrix receptor has been inactivated
  3. The blocked interaction must be responsible for binding every collagen type
  4. The treatment proves that ligand availability was identical on the two substrates
Answer and explanation

A. The blocked interaction contributes to laminin-dependent spreading in this context — The substrate-selective effect is consistent with a specific interaction. The result does not establish all collagen binding, loss of all receptors or identical ligand availability on both substrates.

40. A tissue has increased MMP transcript but unchanged active enzyme assay and no increase in matrix fragments. What is warranted?

  1. Matrix degradation must have increased tenfold
  2. Higher transcript alone does not establish increased matrix degradation
  3. Total enzyme transcript is a direct measure of its active extracellular concentration
  4. Unchanged fragment abundance proves that no matrix turnover ever occurs
Answer and explanation

B. Higher transcript alone does not establish increased matrix degradation — Transcription, active enzyme, substrate accessibility and inhibition are distinct. The activity and fragment observations do not establish increased degradation; unchanged fragment abundance also cannot exclude balanced ongoing turnover.

Mixed-format examination practice

Original examination-style practice. These questions are not official previous-year questions.

41. Assertion–Reason: Collagen IV is a major basement membrane constituent. Reason: Its terminal association domains support a network rather than classical fibrils.

  1. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion
  2. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion
  3. The assertion is true but the reason is false
  4. The assertion is false but the reason is true
Answer and explanation

B. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion — The network-forming architecture is well suited to the sheet-like basement membrane; it helps explain the localization and structural role. The reason is not a claim that architecture is the only determinant.

42. Assertion–Reason: Hyaluronan can contribute to matrix hydration despite being unsulfated. Reason: Its glucuronic acid residues provide negatively charged carboxylates.

  1. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both are true, but the reason is unrelated
  3. The assertion is true but the reason is false
  4. The assertion is false but the reason is true
Answer and explanation

A. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion — Carboxylates contribute fixed negative charge without sulfate, supporting ion and water interactions. Sulfation is not a prerequisite for all GAG hydration functions.

43. Assertion–Reason: High collagen content alone proves high tensile strength. Reason: Fibril alignment and covalent crosslinks influence force transmission.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both are true but the reason is unrelated
  3. The assertion is true but the reason is false
  4. The assertion is false but the reason is true
Answer and explanation

D. The assertion is false but the reason is true — The reason supplies mechanisms that explain why collagen amount alone is insufficient. Organization, geometry, loading and defects influence the measured mechanical property.

44. Which statements are correct? I: Fibronectin is commonly a disulfide-linked dimer. II: Laminin contains one α, one β and one γ chain. III: Every integrin recognizes RGD. IV: Type IV collagen typically forms basement membrane networks.

  1. I and III only
  2. II and III only
  3. I, II and IV
  4. III and IV only
Answer and explanation

C. I, II and IV — Statements I, II and IV describe characteristic architectures. III is false because integrin recognition includes non-RGD mechanisms.

45. Which statements are correct? I: LOX is copper dependent. II: Selected proline residues are hydroxylated intracellularly. III: Every GAG is sulfated. IV: Fibrillar collagen propeptide cleavage occurs extracellularly.

  1. I and III only
  2. II and IV only
  3. I, II and IV
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

C. I, II and IV — LOX and hydroxylation/processing are distinct steps. Hyaluronan is unsulfated, so statement III fails.

46. Match 1 collagen II, 2 laminin, 3 elastin, 4 aggrecan to A hydrated cartilage proteoglycan, B elastic recoil, C cartilage fibrillar framework, D basement membrane adhesive glycoprotein.

  1. 1-D, 2-C, 3-A, 4-B
  2. 1-A, 2-B, 3-C, 4-D
  3. 1-C, 2-A, 3-D, 4-B
  4. 1-C, 2-D, 3-B, 4-A
Answer and explanation

D. 1-C, 2-D, 3-B, 4-A — Collagen II is fibrillar; laminin is a basement membrane glycoprotein; elastin contributes recoil; aggrecan retains hydrated GAG-rich material.

47. Match 1 prolyl hydroxylase, 2 lysyl oxidase, 3 hyaluronan synthase, 4 MMP to A extracellular proteolysis, B chain polymerization at membrane, C collagen hydroxylation, D initiation of covalent crosslink chemistry.

  1. 1-D, 2-C, 3-A, 4-B
  2. 1-C, 2-D, 3-B, 4-A
  3. 1-A, 2-B, 3-D, 4-C
  4. 1-B, 2-A, 3-C, 4-D
Answer and explanation

B. 1-C, 2-D, 3-B, 4-A — The four enzymes are distinguished by chemistry and location. The matrix degradation protease is not a biosynthetic hydroxylase or polymerase.

48. In the collagen IV diagram, a junction includes four protomers at their 7S ends. How many collagen alpha chains are represented?

  1. 12
  2. 4
  3. 7
  4. 6
Answer and explanation

A. 12 — Each protomer comprises three chains. Four protomers therefore contain 12 chains. Seven is a domain designation, not the number of chains.

49. Using the linear stress–strain approximation, a material reaches 2 MPa at strain 0.02. What is the loading energy density?

  1. 20 kJ/m³
  2. 40 kJ/m³
  3. 100 kJ/m³
  4. 0.02 J/m³
Answer and explanation

A. 20 kJ/m³ — The area under the linear curve is ½σε=0.5×2,000,000×0.02=20,000 J/m³. Full recovery additionally assumes negligible dissipation.

50. The remodeling graph shows a new steady matrix mass of 10 mg. Which curve feature best represents approach to steady state?

  1. A straight decrease that crosses zero at 10 days
  2. A decreasing curve flattening toward 10 mg
  3. An instantaneous vertical jump to zero
  4. A rising curve diverging to infinity
Answer and explanation

B. A decreasing curve flattening toward 10 mg — The first-order solution approaches the new equilibrium exponentially. A constant linear loss, instantaneous elimination and divergence contradict this model.

51. A factor has 100 total units; the matrix binds 80 in one sample and 40 in another. What is established about the second sample's free pool at that instant?

  1. It is twice as large and signaling necessarily doubles
  2. It is unchanged because total is equal
  3. It is three times as large, but signaling need not triple
  4. It is smaller because the matrix binds less
Answer and explanation

C. It is three times as large, but signaling need not triple — Free pools are 20 and 60 units. Receptor saturation, clearance and co-receptor presentation prevent inferring a proportional signaling response.

52. A matrix mutation decreases cell adhesion and increases water loss. Which investigation best distinguishes the two contributions?

  1. Measure only total DNA content
  2. Assume all effects arise from one receptor regardless of data
  3. Increase both matrix features simultaneously without separate controls
  4. Independently restore adhesive ligand presentation and fixed charge-associated hydration in matched experiments
Answer and explanation

D. Independently restore adhesive ligand presentation and fixed charge-associated hydration in matched experiments — Independent perturbation/rescue conditions allow the effects to be separated. A combined rescue may show that the matrix matters but cannot assign the specific contributions.

Examination traps and misconceptions

These distinctions frequently matter in questions where a familiar generalization is extended beyond its valid context. Identify the molecular class, location, measured variable or causal step before deciding whether the statement is correct.

  1. ECM equals all extracellular fluid. A matrix is an organized macromolecular network. Dissolved fluid components are not all matrix constituents. Fluid and matrix share the same extracellular location, which encourages confusion. Ask whether the component forms an organized macromolecular network.
  2. Basement membrane is a lipid bilayer. It is a cell-associated extracellular network, characteristically containing laminin and collagen IV. The word membrane suggests phospholipids. In a structure question, look for laminin and collagen IV, not lipid head groups.
  3. Every collagen makes banded fibrils. Type IV forms networks; different collagen architectures use distinct association domains. The common tendon example is overgeneralized. A network-junction diagram with NC1 and 7S sites identifies collagen IV.
  4. Collagen alpha chains are conventional α-helices. Individual chains in the triple-helical region have polyproline II-like conformations. The α-chain name resembles α-helix terminology. A question about chain conformation tests this difference.
  5. Hydroxyproline is directly encoded by a dedicated standard codon. Selected proline residues are hydroxylated after translation. Modified residues appear in protein composition lists. A biosynthesis question distinguishes genetic incorporation from post-translational modification.
  6. Lysyl hydroxylase and lysyl oxidase are the same enzyme. Hydroxylation occurs during intracellular processing; oxidative deamination supports extracellular crosslinking. The two names share the word lysyl. Localize the reaction and identify whether it adds hydroxyl groups or generates aldehydes.
  7. Collagen content determines mechanical behavior completely. Alignment, fibril structure, crosslinking, hydration and geometry matter. Bulk biochemical assays appear quantitative. Compare samples with equal collagen but different fiber alignment or crosslinking.
  8. Elastin and fibrillin are interchangeable. Elastin supplies much recoil; fibrillin-rich microfibrils organize elastic fibers. Both are present in elastic fibers. Predict whether a defect disrupts the scaffold or the crosslinked recoil component.
  9. More extensible means more elastic. Elasticity concerns recovery, while extensibility concerns deformation magnitude. Everyday usage conflates flexibility and elasticity. Use unloading data rather than extension alone to assess recovery.
  10. All GAGs are sulfated. Hyaluronan is unsulfated but contains charged carboxylates. Most commonly taught GAGs are sulfated. Identify hyaluronan from its unsulfated disaccharide.
  11. Every GAG contains a uronic acid. Keratan sulfate contains galactose and N-acetylglucosamine instead. A frequent textbook definition emphasizes uronic acids. Select keratan sulfate when the repeat instead contains galactose.
  12. Aggrecan is covalently joined to hyaluronan. The aggregate relies on noncovalent core–hyaluronan association, stabilized by link proteins. Aggregate drawings can look covalently joined. Identify the protein-to-GAG covalent linkage separately from core-to-hyaluronan association.
  13. Fibronectin RGD is the only matrix recognition sequence. Several integrins recognize RGD, but other recognition mechanisms exist. RGD is a famous motif and can be overgeneralized. In a receptor-specific assay, laminin recognition may remain after an RGD-dependent interaction is blocked.
  14. Laminin digits indicate numbers of chains. They specify alpha, beta and gamma isoforms, one chain of each class. The digits look like stoichiometry. Translate laminin-332 as α3β3γ2, not eight chains.
  15. Nidogen is the only possible link between basement membrane networks. Multiple interactions contribute and dependencies vary by tissue. Simple two-network diagrams emphasize a single bridge. A knockout that leaves some basement membranes intact tests the need for contextual interpretation.
  16. Matrix binding always enhances growth factor signaling. Sequestration can decrease immediate availability; retention or co-receptor interactions can increase local effects. Binding is often equated with activation. Compare free ligand, bound ligand and receptor response rather than assuming a fixed sign.
  17. MMP transcript equals proteolytic activity. Activation, inhibitors, substrate availability and localization matter. RNA abundance is often used as an activity surrogate. A precursor activation or inhibition experiment exposes the distinction.
  18. A constant amount of matrix means no turnover. Deposition and removal can balance while both remain substantial. Steady-state abundance can be mistaken for inactivity. Pulse–chase data distinguish ongoing turnover from a static pool.

Revision, active recall and mastery

The ECM integrates mechanical support, hydration, selective adhesion and regulation. Collagen and elastin provide different physical functions; GAGs and proteoglycans contribute charge-dependent hydration and selective interactions; fibronectin and laminin connect molecular networks to cell surfaces. Architecture converts molecular properties into tissue behavior, and remodeling changes the matrix over time.

High-yield relationships

  • Type I: tensile fibrils; II: cartilage fibrils; III: reticular fibrils; IV: basement membrane network.
  • Collagen processing: intracellular hydroxylation and folding → extracellular cleavage, assembly and crosslinking.
  • Elastin: tropoelastin and covalent crosslinks in a fibrillin-associated fiber.
  • Hyaluronan: unsulfated GAG; aggrecan: core with GAGs, associated with hyaluronan.
  • Fibronectin: modular dimer with selected adhesive motifs; laminin: αβγ heterotrimer.
  • Matrix properties depend on composition, arrangement, mechanics, ligand availability and turnover.

Active recall

  1. Distinguish interstitial matrix from basement membrane by architecture and location.
  2. Explain why glycine is required in the center of a collagen triple helix.
  3. Trace fibrillar collagen maturation from intracellular modification to extracellular crosslinking.
  4. Compare type I and IV collagen assembly and functions.
  5. Explain why elastin recoil requires both flexible chains and network connectivity.
  6. List the exceptions to sulfation and uronic-acid rules for GAGs.
  7. Sketch an aggrecan–hyaluronan aggregate and distinguish covalent from noncovalent interactions.
  8. Describe how fibronectin RGD activity differs for soluble and immobilized ligands.
  9. Distinguish laminin receptor recognition from polymerization.
  10. Explain how fixed charge and external salt influence ideal Donnan swelling.
  11. Derive the matrix steady-state amount and predict what happens after a change in removal.
  12. Design a reciprocal cell–matrix experiment to distinguish extracellular and cell-associated defects.

Student mastery checklist

  • Identify the major ECM components from an unfamiliar diagram.
  • Predict the effects of a change in collagen folding, cleavage or crosslinking.
  • Separate elastic recovery, stiffness and strength in a loading experiment.
  • Explain the role of proteoglycan charge and water in cartilage.
  • Evaluate attachment, localization and reciprocal transfer assays with controls.
  • Use stress, strain, simple transport and turnover equations with appropriate units and assumptions.
  • Distinguish matrix ligand functions from integrin receptor activation, the subject of 4.B.05.
