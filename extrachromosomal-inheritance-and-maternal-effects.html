1. Where hereditary information resides

A genetic cross is an experiment in which we follow information from parents to descendants. In the familiar case of a diploid nuclear locus, each parent contributes one of its two alleles through a gamete. The homologous chromosomes separate during meiosis, and fertilization restores the diploid state. This mechanism explains why a heterozygote typically transmits each allele to half of its gametes. It does not, however, explain every inherited difference. A eukaryotic gamete contributes more than nuclear chromosomes. Its cytoplasm contains membranes, proteins, RNA, and in many organisms organelles with their own genomes. Some of these components are inherited through routes that are quite different from chromosome segregation.

Consider two lines of a flowering plant that differ in the colour of their leaves. If their colour difference results from a single autosomal nuclear locus and dominance is complete, crossing a homozygous green plant with a homozygous white plant would normally produce the same F1 nuclear genotype whichever plant supplied the pollen. But if the chlorophyll defect resides in a plastid genome and only the egg transmits plastids, the exchange of parental roles changes the origin of nearly the entire plastid population in the offspring. This is a fundamentally different experimental prediction. The two crosses have the same expected nuclear genotype but not the same cytoplasmic genotype. In this chapter, the word maternal denotes the egg-producing parent rather than the parent providing care after fertilization.

An extrachromosomal genetic determinant is a heritable information-bearing element outside the host's ordinary nuclear chromosome complement. In eukaryotic genetics, mitochondrial and plastid DNA are the major examples. A cytoplasmically inherited symbiotic bacterium may also maintain its own genetic information and affect a host phenotype. The broader expression cytoplasmic inheritance describes transmission through the cytoplasm. It must not be treated as a synonym for every maternal influence on phenotype. A mother may produce a protein from her nuclear genome and load it into the egg, producing a maternal effect without transmitting a cytoplasmic DNA variant. The distinction depends on identifying what information persists across generations and where its genetic determinant is located.

Mendelian segregation is therefore not a general law about the distribution of every molecule in a cell. It describes the behaviour of chromosomal alleles under specified genetic conditions. A cell may contain dozens or thousands of organellar genome copies, and the number inherited by daughter cells can depend on sampling and replication. A maternally supplied protein, on the other hand, can be degraded during development. Its corresponding nuclear allele is still transmitted according to chromosomal rules. These differences allow us to classify unusual pedigrees without concluding prematurely that Mendel's principles have been disproved.

The first useful experiment is the reciprocal cross: one cross assigns phenotype A to the egg parent and phenotype B to the sperm or pollen parent; the second reverses those roles. Keep the strains, environmental conditions, age of the parents and the scoring stage comparable. A difference between the reciprocal F1 populations is an observation, not a complete explanation. X linkage, genomic imprinting, maternal-effect alleles, organelle inheritance, differential embryo survival and environmental maternal effects can all generate some form of reciprocal asymmetry. The task of the geneticist is to design a second test that distinguishes their predictions. The phenotype should be scored alongside nuclear markers and, wherever feasible, organelle markers.

There is also a distinction between a non-Mendelian phenotype ratio and a non-Mendelian transmission mechanism. A nuclear allele with incomplete penetrance may be passed on in ordinary proportions even though the fraction of affected individuals differs from the predicted genotype fraction. Conversely, an organelle variant may be inherited almost exclusively through eggs without producing symptoms in every recipient. Thus a phenotype count must always be interpreted relative to a model of the genotype and a model of how that genotype produces a phenotype. Keeping those two models separate prevents many common examination errors.

2. Mitochondrial and plastid genetic systems

Mitochondria perform several functions, including oxidative phosphorylation, and chloroplasts carry out photosynthetic reactions in green plant tissues. Both organelle classes possess DNA. This fact is consistent with the endosymbiotic origin of these organelles from bacterial ancestors. Their present genomes are much smaller in gene content than the genomes of free-living bacteria because many ancestral functions were lost or are now encoded by nuclear genes. The resulting relationship is not complete organelle autonomy but a partnership between different cellular genomes. Organelle gene expression depends heavily on nuclear-encoded proteins that are synthesized outside the organelle and imported into it.

Human mitochondrial DNA is a useful example because it is compact and well characterized. It is approximately 16.6 kilobases long and encodes 13 polypeptides of oxidative phosphorylation, 22 transfer RNAs and two ribosomal RNAs. The 13 polypeptides contribute seven subunits to complex I, one to complex III, three to complex IV and two to complex V. Complex II is encoded by nuclear genes. The numbers describe the human mitochondrial genome, not every mitochondrial genome in nature. Plants, fungi and protists may differ substantially in genome size, gene content, organisation and recombination behaviour. It is misleading to generalize human mitochondrial DNA structure to all eukaryotes.

A plant plastid genome usually retains genes contributing to its own transcription and translation systems and genes whose products participate in photosynthesis. For example, the plastid-encoded large subunit of Rubisco must associate with nuclear-encoded small subunits in typical flowering plants. Many proteins needed for chlorophyll synthesis, plastid division and protein import are also nuclear encoded. A pale leaf could therefore result from either a plastid mutation or a nuclear mutation that disrupts plastid function. The colour itself does not locate the causal gene. Reciprocal crosses and genotyping are required.

The word genome copy is important in understanding both systems. A mitochondrion can contain several DNA molecules in nucleoprotein structures called nucleoids, and a cell can contain many mitochondria or an interconnected mitochondrial network. One mitochondrion is not necessarily equivalent to one independent mitochondrial genome. Plastids likewise contain multiple genome copies. Nuclear autosomal loci normally have two allelic copies in an ordinary diploid cell before DNA replication, whereas an organellar locus may have a large population of copies. An organellar cell genotype is often described by the relative abundance of alternative sequence types rather than a single diploid genotype.

Organelle DNA replication is not constrained to the same one-copy-per-chromosome segregation process as nuclear meiosis. An organellar genome may replicate as cellular demands change, and organelles can divide, fuse or be degraded. Plastids are transmitted through pre-existing plastids rather than being assembled anew solely from the nuclear genotype in every generation. During ordinary cell divisions, the progeny cells receive a portion of the parental organellar population. These populations can change over time even when their nuclear genomes remain identical. Nuclear genes regulate many of the processes involved, so an apparently cytoplasmic transmission pattern is still subject to nuclear control.

The discovery of organelle DNA offered an explanation for earlier observations of unusual inheritance. Early twentieth-century plant crosses established patterns that did not follow the expected nuclear allele ratios. Later biochemical and microscopic evidence established the presence of nucleic acids in mitochondria and plastids, connecting an observable transmission pattern to a physical genetic substrate. The historical lesson is methodological: an inheritance pattern can suggest that information is located outside nuclear chromosomes, but the existence and location of a DNA determinant require additional evidence. Modern genetic analysis combines reciprocal crosses, organelle markers and functional measurements rather than relying on a single observation.

A nucleus containing functional mitochondrial maintenance genes cannot by itself restore an absent mitochondrial genome in an ordinary cell. The proteins may still carry out many essential mitochondrial functions, but the lost organellar DNA template is not reproduced merely because the nucleus carries the genes needed for replication. Similarly, nuclear and plastid products may be required together for normal photosynthesis. In an experimental cross, separating nuclear genotype from organellar genotype allows the investigator to examine these interactions. This concept becomes particularly clear in the yeast petite system discussed later.

3. Uniparental inheritance and reciprocal pedigrees

A uniparentally inherited organelle genome is contributed by one parent rather than both in the usual reproductive cycle. Maternal inheritance is one form; paternal inheritance is another. Biparental inheritance means that both parents contribute heritable organelle genomes. These terms describe the route of transmission and not the sex distribution of the resulting phenotype. An organelle allele can affect males and females even if it passes primarily through the egg lineage.

In humans and most familiar animal examples, mitochondrial DNA is predominantly maternally inherited. An egg is a large cell containing a substantial mitochondrial population. A sperm contains mitochondria associated with its motility, but paternal mitochondria or their DNA are normally excluded from sustained embryonic inheritance through mechanisms that vary among organisms. Numerical asymmetry at fertilization and elimination of paternal mitochondrial material both contribute to the outcome in relevant systems. Therefore it is incorrect to explain maternal inheritance simply by saying sperm never contain mitochondria. The relevant question is whether paternal genomes persist in descendants.

Take two homoplasmic parental lines for an illustrative cross: line M carries mitochondrial variant m and line W carries wild-type mitochondrial variant w. Assume exclusive maternal transmission and identical nuclear genetic backgrounds. An M egg fertilized by W sperm produces embryos with m mitochondrial genomes; a W egg fertilized by M sperm produces embryos with w genomes. In the first family, sons and daughters both inherit m, but only daughters normally transmit it to the next generation. In the second family, neither class receives m from the father. This reasoning predicts the distribution of mitochondrial genotype, not necessarily disease severity or viability.

A mitochondrial pedigree with strict maternal transmission can superficially resemble an X-linked pedigree because affected fathers might fail to transmit a phenotype to sons. The distinguishing test is to inspect daughters. An affected father with an X-linked dominant allele passes his X chromosome to all daughters, whereas a father carrying a strictly maternally transmitted mitochondrial variant does not ordinarily transmit it to daughters. For X-linked recessive inheritance, an affected father may pass the variant to carrier daughters without the phenotype. Molecular typing is therefore more decisive than simply looking for male-to-male transmission. A pedigree in which a mother transmits a variant to both sexes is consistent with mitochondrial inheritance but does not alone prove it.

For a homoplasmic mother under strict transmission, all offspring inherit the same mtDNA sequence at the locus being considered, barring mutation or exceptional transmission. Nevertheless, if a variant has incomplete penetrance, some offspring may not express a phenotype. If the mother is heteroplasmic, descendants can receive different proportions of the variant. The phrase all children of an affected mother are affected is too strong in both contexts. The genotype route and the clinical or physiological expression must be distinguished.

The maternal rule is species dependent. In some plants plastids are maternally transmitted, in others they are inherited from both parents, and paternal plastid inheritance is found in some taxa. Fungal mating systems may not resemble the anisogamous animal egg and sperm model. Budding yeast cells can contribute mitochondrial populations from both partners. Some organisms show exceptions to otherwise uniparental transmission, called paternal leakage when paternal genomes persist despite a predominantly maternal pattern. A small amount of paternal DNA detected immediately after fertilization is not sufficient to demonstrate heritable leakage; the evidence should show persistence or transmission rather than contamination or transient DNA.

A controlled reciprocal cross can distinguish an ordinary autosomal nuclear locus from a strictly uniparental organelle marker if the nuclear background is controlled. But it cannot distinguish a maternal-effect nuclear allele from a maternal organelle factor in a single generation, because both may make offspring resemble their egg parent. The distinction requires following the same F1 individuals when they become egg parents themselves, genotyping their nuclear loci, or directly tracing organellar sequences. 4. Chloroplast inheritance and Mirabilis jalapa

A green leaf contains chloroplasts that can synthesize chlorophyll and support photosynthesis. A white leaf region lacks sufficient functional chlorophyll-containing chloroplasts, and a variegated leaf contains regions with contrasting pigmentation. The four o'clock plant, Mirabilis jalapa, became a classical system for studying the transmission of plastid-associated leaf colour because differently coloured branches can occur on the same individual. Such a plant allows the experimenter to compare flowers arising from green, white and mixed branches rather than treating the whole plant as genetically uniform in the cytoplasm.

In the classical interpretation of Correns's crosses, the plastid population associated with the egg-bearing branch predicts offspring pigmentation. Pollen colour origin has little effect under the strictly maternal plastid transmission model used for this species. A green branch serving as seed parent produces green seedlings when its eggs contain functional plastids. A white branch serving as seed parent can produce white seedlings even when pollinated by a green branch. A variegated branch can produce green, white or variegated offspring because its eggs may sample different mixtures of plastids. The three classes are not expected to occur in a universal 1:2:1 or 3:1 ratio.

The white seedlings are deficient in photosynthetic capacity and commonly fail to survive beyond the support provided by stored reserves unless supplied with suitable external resources under experimental conditions. This creates an important problem in data interpretation. If a genetic cross is scored only after prolonged growth, white seedlings may have disappeared. The observed fraction of green seedlings then describes both the initial transmission distribution and survival after germination. To infer the plastid transmission mechanism, score pigmentation early and report whether the denominators refer to seeds, germinating seedlings or surviving plants. Selection can alter a phenotype ratio without changing the original transmission process.

Why does a variegated plant have sharply separated regions rather than an intermediate uniform colour? Imagine a cell containing two types of plastid: one with a functional genetic system for chloroplast development and one with a defect. During growth, daughter cells may inherit different samples. Subsequent rounds of plastid replication and cell division can produce lineages enriched for one type. If a descendant meristem cell becomes dominated by functional plastids, the tissue descended from it can appear green. A lineage dominated by defective plastids may appear white. Boundaries between cell lineages then become visible as sectors. The exact appearance depends on tissue development, plastid biology and how many functional plastids are needed to maintain chlorophyll.

This explanation is called vegetative or somatic segregation of cytoplasmic determinants. It differs from somatic recombination of nuclear alleles, though either can generate visible mosaics in particular systems. The nuclear genotype of two neighbouring cell lineages may be the same while their plastid populations differ. Conversely, not all variegation is caused by plastid DNA. Nuclear mutations, transposable elements, pathogens, environmental damage and developmental regulation can all alter leaf pigmentation. A reciprocal cross combined with plastid sequence analysis is needed before attributing a particular phenotype to plastid inheritance.

Historical comparisons emphasize why species identity matters. Correns's work on Mirabilis demonstrated maternal plastid transmission, while Baur's observations in Pelargonium supported contributions from both parents. A student who memorizes chloroplast inheritance as invariably maternal will fail questions constructed from the second system. The appropriate procedure is to identify the stated plastid transmission route, then calculate the descendant classes implied by that route. If both parents transmit plastids, heteroplasmy may originate directly through fertilization even when the parental lines are individually homoplasmic for different variants.

The design of a reciprocal plant cross must also separate plastid genotype from maternal sporophytic tissue. The seed coat is derived from maternal integuments and thus has the maternal nuclear genotype; the embryo is a new individual. A difference in seed-coat colour can therefore reflect the genotype of maternal tissue without indicating transmission of a plastid variant to the embryo. In Mirabilis, the relevant outcome is the pigmentation of the offspring seedling rather than the superficial appearance of maternal tissues surrounding the seed. Careful choice of the tissue scored is a general requirement of developmental and inheritance experiments.

5. Yeast petite mutants and mitochondrial genetics

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a particularly useful organism for investigating respiration-deficient phenotypes because it can obtain ATP by fermentation when an appropriate fermentable carbon source is available. A respiratory defect that would be catastrophic for many organisms can therefore be studied in living yeast. On a medium where normal colonies eventually benefit from respiratory metabolism, some respiratory-deficient mutants form small colonies, historically described as petites; the larger respiratory-competent colonies are described as grandes. Colony size is a screening phenotype rather than a direct measurement of mitochondrial DNA.

A respiratory-deficient isolate can arise through mutation of nuclear genes needed for mitochondrial respiration or through changes in mitochondrial DNA itself. Nuclear pet mutants can segregate according to chromosomal inheritance. Cytoplasmic petite mutants frequently carry extensively deleted mitochondrial genomes, designated rho-minus, or lack detectable mitochondrial DNA, designated rho-zero. Wild-type mitochondrial genomes are designated rho-plus. Specific mitochondrial point mutations can also impair respiration. These distinctions are genetic descriptions, not simply labels for colony appearance. A small colony can have a functional mitochondrial genome but a defective nuclear-encoded respiratory component.

Boris Ephrussi and colleagues studied the inheritance of petites and helped establish the cytoplasmic nature of the rho factor. Crossing a respiratory-deficient haploid with a compatible respiratory-proficient haploid creates a diploid in which nuclear alleles and mitochondrial populations can interact. If a recessive nuclear pet mutation is responsible and the other parent supplies a functioning nuclear allele, respiration can be restored in the diploid. Following meiosis, the nuclear allele may segregate two to two among four haploid products, assuming a single locus and reliable phenotypic scoring. This is a different prediction from the distribution of mitochondrial genome variants.

For a rho-zero petite crossed with a rho-plus partner, the wild-type partner supplies mitochondrial DNA. Provided the diploid's nuclear background permits maintenance and expression of that genome, the resulting lineage can become respiratory competent. A rho-minus petite retains a defective subset of the mitochondrial genome. Some rho-minus genomes are suppressive: after mating with rho-plus cells, the defective mitochondrial type becomes disproportionately represented in descendants, resulting in a high proportion of petites. Other defective genomes are neutral or only weakly suppressive. The terms neutral and suppressive describe outcomes in specified crosses, not the nuclear dominance relationship between two alleles.

A suppressive petite demonstrates why a defective genome need not disappear merely because it impairs the host cell. Different levels of selection can favour different outcomes. Within a cell, the propagation or transmission of one mitochondrial genome type can be biased relative to another; at the level of the whole organism or colony, respiratory competence may affect growth in particular environments. These two processes should not be conflated. The rate at which colonies grow on a particular substrate does not directly measure the replication rate of each mitochondrial DNA type.

To separate respiratory defects from general poor growth, compare growth on a fermentable carbon source with growth on a nonfermentable carbon source such as glycerol under appropriate aerobic conditions. A mutant that grows on the former but not the latter is consistent with a respiratory defect. A strain unable to grow on either medium may have an unrelated growth defect or be inviable. Include a known wild type and a characterized respiratory-deficient control. The experiment identifies a physiological requirement; it does not by itself locate the causal mutation. Crosses, nuclear segregation data and direct mitochondrial DNA analysis provide the genetic discrimination.

Unlike the typical human pedigree, yeast mating can mix mitochondrial genomes from both parents, and mitochondrial recombination can occur. The biparental contribution allows genetic interactions between mitochondrial markers to be studied. After the transient mixed state, vegetative propagation can produce lineages dominated by one genome class. Consequently, a single diploid culture may contain descendants of different respiratory competence. The time and conditions of scoring need to be specified when comparing experiments. A statement about yeast mitochondrial recombination should not be generalized into a claim that human mitochondrial genomes routinely recombine in the same way.

The most important lesson of the petite system is that cellular function arises from the interaction of two genetic compartments. Normal nuclear genes cannot rescue every mitochondrial DNA lesion, while a functional mitochondrial genome cannot overcome every nuclear defect. Observing respiratory deficiency tells us which biological process is impaired; genetic experiments identify where the relevant information resides. This distinction also applies to the analysis of mitochondrial disease and chloroplast phenotypes.

6. Heteroplasmy, replicative segregation and genetic drift

When all copies of an organellar genome in the defined sample carry the same sequence at a locus, the sample is homoplasmic at that locus. When two or more distinguishable organellar sequence variants coexist, it is heteroplasmic. The sample may be a cell, tissue, organism or organelle preparation. Always specify the sampling scale because a tissue containing a mixture of homoplasmic cells can yield a heteroplasmic bulk sequencing result even if no individual cell contains both variants. The reverse problem is also possible: a small tissue biopsy may not represent all tissues of an organism.

Let the number of mutant genome copies in a sample be M and the number of other genome copies be W. If these are the only two classes being counted, the mutant fraction is h = M/(M + W). A sample containing 240 mutant copies and 360 wild-type copies has h = 240/600 = 0.40, or 40 percent. This is a proportion of DNA copies, not a proportion of cells with a defect and not necessarily a proportion of mutant mitochondria. A cell can contain multiple DNA copies per mitochondrion, and proteins made from different genomes may contribute to a shared cellular function.

Suppose this cell divides, and the two daughter cells receive different samples of the genome population. If each copy is equally likely to enter either daughter and the initial distribution is well mixed, the expected mutant fraction of the two daughter cells is the parental fraction. However, the realized proportions can differ. Across many divisions, stochastic sampling produces lineages with increasing differences from one another even without a systematic change in the population mean. This is a form of genetic drift acting within a cellular or developmental lineage.

A simple model illustrates the relationship between sampling size and heteroplasmy. Assume that an effective population of n independent genome units is sampled with replacement from a large parental pool with mutant fraction h, and assume no selection, mutation or bias. The number K of mutant units in the sample follows a binomial distribution. The probability of exactly k mutant units is C(n,k)h^k(1-h)^(n-k). The mean number is nh and the variance is nh(1-h), because the total is the sum of n independent Bernoulli trials. For the descendant fraction K/n, the mean is h and the variance is h(1-h)/n. Its standard deviation is the square root of that expression. All quantities here are dimensionless proportions except the copy counts.

As a worked example, consider a parental fraction h = 0.30. If ten independent units are sampled, the standard deviation of the descendant fraction is sqrt(0.30 × 0.70 / 10) = approximately 0.145. If one hundred units are sampled, the standard deviation is approximately 0.046. Reducing the effective sample size increases the spread of possible descendant fractions without changing the expected fraction in the neutral model. One individual descendant may have a much higher or lower mutant fraction than the parent even though the model has no directional tendency.

The exact binomial assumptions matter. If a finite pool of distinct genome units is divided without replacement, the count in a daughter cell follows a hypergeometric rather than a binomial distribution under random allocation. If genomes are grouped within nucleoids or organelles, the units sampled may not be independent. If mitochondrial replication differs between variants, the distribution will depart from the neutral model. If the parental pool itself changes before division, the relevant h is the fraction at the stage of sampling. Thus the simple binomial expression is an explanatory baseline, not a universal formula for every organelle division.

Replicative segregation is the change in relative frequencies of organelle genome types through replication, partitioning and the growth of cellular lineages. Over repeated rounds, some lineages approach homoplasmy for one type while others approach homoplasmy for another. The process can occur without nuclear meiosis, which explains why genetically identical nuclear cell lineages may differ in their organelle genotypes. The term vegetative segregation is commonly used when describing plastid sorting in growing plant tissues.

It is useful to distinguish random drift from biased transmission. Imagine two hundred independent oocytes whose average mutant fraction is near 0.40, but whose values range from 0.05 to 0.85. Such variation can arise through sampling and does not by itself show selective amplification. If the distribution shifts systematically toward higher mutant fractions across multiple replicated experiments, a bias in replication, survival or selection becomes a possible explanation. Technical measurement bias and differing cell types must first be excluded. Variance and mean answer different biological questions.

7. Germline bottlenecks and tissue-dependent thresholds

During maternal mitochondrial transmission, siblings can differ in their heteroplasmy even when they have the same mother. A mitochondrial genetic bottleneck refers to a reduction in the effective number of independently segregating genetic units contributing to a descendant lineage. This may produce substantial variation in the variant fraction among developing oocytes or offspring. The effective population size is not necessarily identical to the total physical count of mitochondrial DNA molecules; replication of a subset of genomes, partitioning of nucleoids and developmental dynamics can contribute to the observed genetic behaviour.

Consider a simplified two-stage model. An oocyte precursor contains a mixed mitochondrial population. At a bottleneck stage, a small effective sample contributes to the subsequent population. If a sample happens to contain relatively many mutant units, replication of its members can generate a mature oocyte with a high mutant fraction. A second precursor might by chance sample fewer mutant units and generate a low-fraction oocyte. Amplification increases the number of genomes but does not restore the original parental fraction automatically. This is why a large number of genomes in the mature cell does not disprove a preceding genetic bottleneck.

With h = 0.5 and an effective sample of four independent units, the possible sampled fractions are 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1. Their probabilities are 1/16, 4/16, 6/16, 4/16 and 1/16 respectively. The probability of sampling only one genome type is 2/16 = 0.125. Under the same ideal assumptions with forty units, the two extreme outcomes together have probability 2(0.5)^40, far smaller. The calculation illustrates why a small bottleneck can expose variation. It does not estimate the actual mitochondrial bottleneck size in any particular organism.

A threshold effect describes the relationship between the proportion of a harmful mitochondrial variant and a measurable functional phenotype. For certain variants, a cell may retain sufficient respiratory capacity when the mutant fraction is low; after the fraction crosses a context-specific level, the remaining functional gene products may no longer meet cellular demand. This is an emergent property of gene expression, enzyme activity, bioenergetic reserve and tissue physiology. There is no universal percentage that defines the threshold for all mitochondrial variants or tissues.

The effect can be illustrated using an explicitly hypothetical tissue in which functional reserve supports normal measured activity until a mutant fraction reaches 0.60. A cell at 0.45 may score as phenotypically normal while a cell at 0.75 may score as impaired. A different tissue with greater energy demand, a lower copy number or a different nuclear background may cross its functional limit at a different fraction. Real dose-response curves may be gradual rather than a sharp binary transition. The numerical threshold in this example is a teaching assumption and must not be treated as a diagnostic cutoff.

Three distinct processes can now be separated. The germline bottleneck can change the distribution of variant fractions among offspring. Somatic replicative segregation can change fractions among tissue lineages within an offspring. The threshold effect translates a given molecular burden and cellular context into a functional outcome. A mother and child may differ in symptoms because their variant fractions differ, because tissue distributions differ, or because tissue physiology differs even at the same measured fraction. No single measurement establishes all three causal steps.

A further layer of complexity is selection. Highly damaging mitochondrial genomes can be selected against during transmission or within certain cellular lineages. Other genomes may increase in frequency through biased replication or other intracellular processes. An observed bottleneck is therefore not proof of purely random drift, and a difference in mutation burden between generations is not automatically evidence of directed adaptation. To distinguish drift from selection, compare observed frequency distributions with an explicit neutral expectation, use replicated lineages and consider the possibility that the most severely affected individuals were not available for sampling.

Human mitochondrial disease provides a biological context for these principles, but the aim here is genetic reasoning rather than clinical prediction. Some mitochondrial disorders are caused by variants in mtDNA; others result from nuclear genes needed for organelle maintenance or function. A representative mtDNA-associated disorder such as MELAS illustrates how altered mitochondrial translation and oxidative phosphorylation can affect multiple tissues. The fact that a disorder involves mitochondria does not automatically imply maternal inheritance. Locating its causal gene and examining its transmission remain essential steps.

8. Nuclear maternal effects and snail shell coiling

A maternal-effect gene is a gene whose expression in the egg-producing parent influences the phenotype of its offspring, often through RNA or protein deposited in the egg before fertilization. In the classical examples considered here, the gene itself is nuclear. Its alleles segregate according to ordinary Mendelian rules, but the phenotype scored in the offspring depends on the mother's genotype rather than directly on the offspring's genotype. This can produce a one-generation displacement between the genotype that determines a phenotype and the generation in which the phenotype is visible.

The biological basis is developmental timing. An egg is not simply an empty container waiting for a zygotic genome to begin transcription. It contains proteins, RNA, organelles and spatially organized determinants assembled during oogenesis. Early embryonic events may occur before the embryo can produce sufficient quantities of a particular gene product. If the mother's gene product is absent or altered during this window, a paternal wild-type allele inherited by the embryo may arrive too late to correct the relevant developmental event. The resulting phenotype can therefore depend on a gene expressed in the previous generation.

Shell coiling in the pond snail Lymnaea stagnalis provides a classical example. In a simplified single-locus model, D is the dominant allele for dextral coiling as determined by the egg parent's genotype, and d is the recessive allele associated with sinistral coiling. A mother with genotype DD or Dd produces dextrally coiled progeny; a mother with genotype dd produces sinistrally coiled progeny. This is a rule about the maternal genotype, not a rule stating that every Dd individual is itself dextral.

Cross a DD egg parent with a dd sperm parent. All offspring are Dd, and all are dextral because their mother was DD. Reverse the cross: dd egg parent with DD sperm parent. Again all offspring are Dd, but they are sinistral because their mother was dd. In both cases the F1 nuclear genotype is identical. The difference in phenotype follows the egg parent's genotype. If these F1 heterozygotes subsequently produce eggs, they produce dextral offspring irrespective of their own shell coiling direction in the simplified model.

Now self-fertilize an F1 Dd snail, assuming equal allele transmission and no differential survival. The F2 genotype ratio is 1 DD : 2 Dd : 1 dd. Every F2 individual is dextral because its egg parent was Dd. Among these dextral individuals, one quarter are dd and will produce sinistral offspring when acting as mothers. If each individual F2 mother contributes the same number of surviving offspring through self-fertilization, the aggregate F3 phenotype ratio is three dextral to one sinistral. Within each individual F3 family the expected phenotype is uniform under the complete maternal-effect model, although the nuclear genotypes within families can differ. The ratio describes families or equally weighted progeny, not an ordinary segregation of F3 phenotypes directly according to F3 genotype.

The pattern can appear surprising because a sinistral individual may produce dextral offspring and a dextral individual may produce sinistral offspring. The resolution is to write two columns for every generation: the individual's own genotype and the genotype of its mother. The first predicts the allele transmission from that individual; the second predicts its own shell phenotype. Never substitute shell orientation for nuclear genotype without additional evidence. In hermaphroditic snails, distinguish the egg-producing role from the sperm-producing role when interpreting an outcross.

Research on the molecular basis of snail chirality identified a maternal diaphanous-related formin gene. Formins regulate actin organization and can influence the earliest asymmetrical cellular events that precede the final left-right body pattern. An experimental study in 2019 used targeted disruption of Lsdia1 in Lymnaea stagnalis and linked loss of its normal maternal function to reversal of coiling in descendants. The important conceptual point is that a nuclear gene acting through the egg can determine an early developmental asymmetry. The shell is a late visible consequence of a much earlier event. It is not the shell structure itself that carries the causal hereditary information.

Drosophila maternal-effect genes provide a related example of developmental timing. Maternal bicoid RNA is localized in the egg and its translated product helps organize anterior development. Loss of the mother's functional contribution affects early embryonic patterning even when the embryo receives a wild-type allele from its father. The details of morphogen gradients and axial pattern formation belong primarily to developmental biology; here the example demonstrates why maternal genotype and embryonic genotype can be distinct predictors of phenotype.

A maternal-effect gene must also be separated from an environmental maternal effect. Maternal nutrition, temperature or provisioning can influence offspring growth without a causal heritable nuclear allele at the locus under study. Both effects may follow the maternal parent in a reciprocal experiment. To demonstrate a nuclear maternal-effect locus, track the alleles through generations while controlling environmental variables, or use genetic evidence linking altered gene function in the mother to the offspring phenotype. The observation of maternal resemblance is only the beginning of the analysis.

9. Cytoplasmic symbionts and differential embryo survival

Heritable cytoplasmic factors need not be mitochondria or plastids. Some intracellular symbionts have their own genomes and are passed from a host mother to her descendants through the egg. The host phenotype can therefore follow a cytoplasmic transmission route even though the causal genome belongs to another organism. A useful supporting example is Wolbachia, an intracellular bacterium found in many arthropods. Its transmission is commonly maternal, and some strains modify host reproduction in ways that change the frequency of infected and uninfected descendants.

In a simple model of unidirectional cytoplasmic incompatibility, an infected male crossed with an uninfected female produces reduced embryo survival, whereas an infected female carrying a compatible infection can produce viable offspring when crossed with either infected or uninfected males. Infected females can transmit the bacterium to their offspring. An infected male can influence the outcome of fertilization without ordinarily transmitting the bacterium through sperm. The incompatibility phenotype therefore cannot be explained by simply assuming that every effect of an infected father requires paternal genetic transmission of Wolbachia.

Imagine four crosses using infected and uninfected parents of the same host species and genetic background. In a compatible idealized system, uninfected female × uninfected male has high viability, infected female × uninfected male has high viability, infected female × compatibly infected male has high viability, and uninfected female × infected male has reduced viability. Different Wolbachia strains and host backgrounds can modify these patterns. The model should not be treated as a universal outcome for every natural infection.

The pattern also illustrates why a cross with no surviving offspring cannot be interpreted as though all offspring inherited an invisible genotype. The embryo viability difference alters which individuals remain available for scoring. To infer transmission, collect evidence at the relevant developmental stage and assay the symbiont directly. Crosses between treated and untreated lines can help separate infection status from host genotype, but any treatment may also alter other microbes or organellar function. Appropriate controls and restoration evidence matter when linking the effect to a particular symbiont.

The evolutionary interpretation follows from the difference in transmission route. A maternally transmitted symbiont is passed onward mainly by infected females. When uninfected females have reduced reproductive success in matings with infected males, infected females may gain a relative reproductive advantage. The mechanism can increase symbiont frequency in a host population even if infection carries a cost in other contexts. This is a simple example of how the transmission route of a genetic entity changes the selection pressures acting on it.

In an exam question, the phrase cytoplasmic inheritance may describe a symbiotic factor or a host organelle variant. If the phenotype disappears when the symbiont is removed and is restored when it is reintroduced, the evidence favours the symbiont hypothesis; if the phenotype instead tracks a sequence variant in mitochondrial or plastid DNA independently of infection, an organelle explanation becomes stronger. Neither phenotype resemblance nor a response to one nonspecific intervention alone proves causation. Distinguishing these models requires the combined logic of inheritance, molecular identity and functional evidence.

10. Distinguishing mechanisms with genetic evidence

Maternal inheritance, a nuclear maternal effect and genomic imprinting are easily confused because all may make reciprocal crosses differ. Their mechanisms are separate. In strictly maternal organelle inheritance, the determinant is an organelle DNA variant transmitted through the egg. In a nuclear maternal effect, a maternal nuclear genotype controls the developmental environment of the egg, while the causal nuclear alleles can be inherited from either parent. In genomic imprinting, the expression of an allele in the offspring depends on the parental origin of that allele; the relevant determinant remains associated with the offspring's nuclear locus and its parent-of-origin state.

Consider a simple imprinted autosomal locus where only the maternally inherited allele is expressed in a particular tissue. A heterozygous offspring with a functional maternal allele can have a different phenotype from a heterozygote with the same functional allele inherited paternally. Unlike the classical maternal-effect model, the identity of the allele inherited by the offspring from its mother matters directly; the other allele present in the mother's genotype need not determine the outcome. In a maternal-effect model, two siblings with different nuclear genotypes can display the same phenotype because both developed from eggs produced by the same heterozygous mother.

An experiment can exploit this distinction by crossing heterozygous mothers to homozygous fathers and genotyping offspring. In a complete dominant maternal-effect model, all offspring from a Dd mother have the same dextral phenotype even if half receive d from her. In an imprinting model with maternal allele-specific expression, the offspring inheriting different maternal alleles may show different phenotypes. In strictly maternal homoplasmic organelle inheritance, offspring from a homoplasmic mother also share the organelle marker, but their nuclear genotypes do not explain the maternal transmission of that marker. One generation is not always sufficient; molecular and pedigree evidence resolve the ambiguity.

A reciprocal backcross is especially useful for distinguishing nuclear and organellar effects. Suppose a hybrid receives organelle type O from its original egg parent. When it serves as the egg parent in repeated backcrosses to a second nuclear line, its nuclear ancestry becomes progressively enriched for that second line while its organelle lineage is retained under strict maternal transmission. After an F1 containing one half of its expected nuclear ancestry from the second line, one backcross gives an expected three quarters, and a second gives seven eighths. This expectation applies to neutral unlinked nuclear ancestry averaged across the genome; actual loci can depart from it through chance, linkage or selection. If a trait continues to track the retained organelle marker despite the replacement of much of the nuclear background, organelle causation becomes more plausible. Nuclear-organelle interactions may still alter the phenotype.

Molecular genotyping can test whether the putative organelle variant is present in the relevant tissue and co-transmitted with the trait. For mitochondrial sequencing, nuclear insertions of mitochondrial DNA can complicate measurements if reads are incorrectly assigned to the organelle genome. Low-level sequence signals may also reflect contamination, sequencing error or genuine heteroplasmy. A careful study therefore establishes measurement specificity and uses appropriate controls. At NET level the central principle is that detecting DNA with a mitochondrial-like sequence does not automatically prove that it is a transmitted mitochondrial genome.

Functional assays provide an independent evidence layer. For a respiratory-deficient yeast strain, substrate-dependent growth and respiration measurements evaluate function. For a plant, chlorophyll content, photosynthetic competence and plastid markers provide different types of information. A genotype association establishes a correlation; evidence that a specific genetic change alters function in a suitable background strengthens causal interpretation. A phenotype may persist when a marker is present because of another linked organelle variant, or it may disappear when a permissive nuclear background is changed. Genetic association and biochemical mechanism should not be treated as interchangeable.

The overall reasoning sequence is to define the scored phenotype, identify the expected nuclear genotype in reciprocal crosses, specify the potential cytoplasmic determinants, follow subsequent generations, test marker transmission and evaluate survival and environmental effects. Quantitative sampling models explain variation in organellar fractions; nuclear segregation predicts maternal-effect allele distributions; developmental and physiological models explain when those molecular differences become visible. This approach allows a student to answer an unfamiliar genetic cross by constructing the causal model rather than searching memory for a named exception.

11. Designing reciprocal crosses that distinguish inheritance models

A powerful way to understand an unexpected inheritance pattern is to start with the null hypothesis of ordinary autosomal transmission. Let a dominant functional allele A produce a green phenotype and a recessive allele a produce a pale phenotype. Assume both parental lines are homozygous, that the locus is not imprinted and that the environment does not alter the scoring of colour. In the cross AA egg parent by aa pollen parent, every egg contains A and every pollen nucleus contributes a. The reciprocal cross yields precisely the same Aa genotype. In each case, the dominant phenotype is expected in the F1. If green and pale reciprocal progeny are observed under well-controlled conditions, at least one of the model assumptions is false. The observation does not tell us which assumption has failed.

The investigator can build competing predictions without knowing the molecular identity of the determinant. Under strict maternal plastid inheritance, the first cross produces the maternal plastid type A-like and the second produces the a-like type even though nuclear genotype is Aa in both. Under a dominant maternal-effect nuclear model, the reciprocal F1 phenotypes would also follow the egg parents, but their nuclear genotypes remain Aa. Under an imprinting model with maternal allele expression, the first Aa genotype has the A allele inherited through the egg and the second has a inherited through the egg. They are equivalent in unordered genotype notation but not in parent-of-origin notation. Under sex linkage, the reciprocal outcomes depend on the sex chromosome system and on the sex of the offspring. A single reciprocal F1 experiment can therefore be consistent with several causal mechanisms.

The next generation provides greater resolution. Cross the reciprocal F1 individuals as egg parents to a common tester with genotype aa and a known organelle type. If the trait is a dominant maternal effect, both F1 mothers have genotype Aa, so their progeny have the functional phenotype in the idealized model, independent of whether individual progeny are Aa or aa. For a strictly maternal, homoplasmic plastid determinant, the reciprocal F1 individuals retain the distinct plastid populations inherited from their respective egg parents, and the two sets of progeny remain different. For a maternally expressed imprinted nuclear locus, each heterozygous F1 mother transmits either A or a with equal probability, so the offspring phenotypes can segregate in a one-to-one ratio. The predicted outcomes differ because the underlying information being tracked differs.

The choice of tester matters. If an imprinted locus is expressed from the paternal allele, crossing both F1 mothers to the same aa tester instead gives a common recessive phenotype rather than a one-to-one segregation. It would then be inappropriate to use that one cross as evidence for a maternal effect. Instead, exchange the parental roles, choose a heterozygous father, and record the allele origin in offspring. Testing imprinting requires knowing which parental allele is active; the statement that a gene is imprinted does not by itself specify whether the maternal or paternal copy is silenced.

The reciprocal comparison should include matched numbers of families, not merely matched numbers of individual descendants. In a maternal-effect experiment, offspring within one family share the genotype of the mother as their causal developmental influence. Counting a thousand siblings from one mother does not provide a thousand independent tests of maternal genotype. Independent mothers are the relevant replication units for that question. This is why examining multiple separately genotyped females is better than examining a very large single brood when asking whether the maternal genotype determines the phenotype. The same logic applies to a plant experiment in which many seeds are derived from a single branch and share a local plastid population.

An additional test is to separate the location of a molecular marker from its relationship to the phenotype. A plastid sequence marker that accompanies the pigmentation phenotype through repeated reciprocal crosses is evidence for a plastid-associated trait. Yet the marker could be a neutral sequence closely associated with another causal plastid variant. Establishing the causal change requires stronger molecular or functional evidence. The marker may also show a maternal pattern because it is transmitted through plastids regardless of whether the visible trait actually depends on it. Marker inheritance and trait inheritance should therefore be measured separately before assessing their association.

The distinction between maternal tissue and the embryo is especially important in plants. The outer tissues of a seed are not equivalent to its zygotic embryo. If a coloured maternal seed coat encloses embryos with both nuclear genotypes, the external colour does not necessarily indicate that the embryos inherited the maternal allele in every case. If the scoring stage is seedling leaf colour, the investigator must also consider which maternal reserves influence the first few days of growth. A study following embryos into seedlings can separate a transient developmental influence from a stable, self-propagating plastid determinant.

By setting out explicit predictions for several mechanisms, the experiment avoids the common strategy of assigning a label solely from a reciprocal F1 ratio. The most informative additional cross is the one for which the competing models predict different outcomes. An examination problem may provide only one small dataset, in which case the appropriate conclusion is that the data support one or more models and that a specific additional experiment is required. A scientifically justified answer is not always a unique gene location.

12. Organelle genome variants, markers and functional complementation

A genome variant is a difference in DNA sequence relative to a defined comparison sequence. It need not cause a loss of function. Organelle haplotypes consist of combinations of sequence variants carried by the same mitochondrial or plastid genome molecule or inherited genome lineage. A haplotype marker may be useful for identifying the parental origin of an organelle without altering respiration or photosynthesis. In contrast, a damaging variant may change the amino acid sequence of a protein, alter an RNA involved in translation, disrupt expression or interfere with genome maintenance. The distinction matters because a marker can report transmission even when there is no visible phenotype.

Suppose a mitochondrial protein-coding gene is altered so that the resulting polypeptide cannot participate normally in the assembly of a respiratory complex. At low variant fractions, a cell may still contain sufficient functional products from other genome copies. The functional respiratory complexes are molecular structures assembled from proteins, not intact genomes. A defective mitochondrial gene and a normal mitochondrial gene do not necessarily produce a simple dominant-recessive relationship because their products are made within a population of organelles and may interact with nuclear products. The effective distribution of functional proteins can depend on local synthesis, membrane association, turnover and organelle dynamics. Thus the use of classical diploid dominance terminology is often inappropriate for a mixed organelle genome population.

Mitochondrial transfer RNA variants can have a broader consequence than mutations in a single protein-coding sequence. A defective transfer RNA may impair translation of multiple mitochondrial messages because the same RNA species participates repeatedly in the synthesis of different proteins. The causal chain extends from altered RNA function to altered synthesis of organellar proteins, altered complex assembly, altered electron transfer or ATP production, and finally altered cellular physiology. A nuclear mutation in a protein required for mitochondrial translation may converge on a similar chain. The molecular position of the initial lesion determines the inheritance model; the shared physiological endpoint does not.

Functional complementation is the restoration of a biological activity when two genetic or cellular contributions together supply functions missing from either contribution alone. In yeast, two haploid nuclear mutants at different recessive loci may complement in a diploid because each partner provides a functional allele for the defective gene in the other. That logic differs from a mixture of mitochondrial genomes with complementary mutations. In the mitochondrial case, restoration could result from functional contributions from both populations, recombination that generates a competent DNA sequence, or other interactions. A restored respiratory phenotype does not uniquely establish which of these processes occurred.

Consider two respiratory-deficient yeast strains with different mitochondrial point mutations. One cannot synthesize a normal protein from gene X, while the other is defective at gene Y. If their descendants can respire after mating, the investigator should test whether the two mutant mitochondrial haplotypes remain present or whether recombinant mitochondrial genomes now contain functional versions of both genes. Tracking DNA markers before and after mating distinguishes a mixture-based explanation from an exchange-of-information explanation. The purpose of this example is not to derive a specialist mitochondrial map but to connect the restoration of function with the genetic information needed for that restoration.

The physical shape of DNA is not a reliable definition of an extrachromosomal determinant. Many textbook illustrations draw animal mitochondrial DNA as a circle, which is an accurate representation for the common human mitochondrial genome. Yet the organization of mitochondrial DNA differs widely among eukaryotic taxa. Similarly, the map of a typical plastid genome often shows a circular sequence with repeated regions, but the forms of DNA molecules actually present in cells can be more complex. Inheritance is determined by the location, replication and transmission of genetic information, not by whether a diagram can be drawn as a circle.

Because organelles depend on imported proteins, nuclear mutations can alter the fidelity or rate of organelle genome replication, the maintenance of genome copy number or the distribution of organelles between daughter cells. The primary mutation can therefore be nuclear while a secondary phenotype involves abnormal mitochondrial DNA populations. If such a nuclear mutation causes the loss of mitochondrial DNA in a cell lineage, restoring the nuclear allele alone may not immediately restore the missing mitochondrial template. A cross providing both normal nuclear function and intact mitochondrial DNA may be needed to restore respiration. This is an important qualification to the simple expectation that the normal nuclear allele should always complement a recessive respiratory defect.

In a plant, the same principle can be understood through an example involving Rubisco. The large and small subunits come from different genetic compartments. A mutation in either compartment can impair the assembled enzyme. A change in one plastid-encoded component may have different functional consequences in two different nuclear genetic backgrounds. Such nuclear-organelle interactions can make a trait follow the cytoplasmic genotype imperfectly. To establish which determinant is responsible, compare the same plastid haplotype in different nuclear backgrounds and different plastid haplotypes in the same or closely matched nuclear background. This comparison tests genetic interaction rather than assuming that an organellar phenotype has a single independent cause.

13. Plant transmission experiments and plastid sorting

Consider an illustrative experiment using a variegated seed parent whose plastid lineages are sampled during gamete formation. The investigator records 40 green, 20 white and 40 variegated seedlings from 100 germinated seeds. These values are invented for reasoning practice, not historical observations of Mirabilis. The simplest interpretation is that a fraction of eggs carried predominantly functional plastids, another fraction carried predominantly defective plastids, and a third fraction carried mixed populations capable of generating sectors. No universal ratio is implied by those counts. If the same experiment produced 70 green seedlings in a different maternal branch, that difference could reflect a different local plastid composition rather than a new nuclear allele.

Now suppose the investigator waits until all surviving plants establish new leaves. Twenty white seedlings and ten severely affected variegated seedlings die, while every green seedling survives. The surviving counts are 40 green and 30 variegated. Green seedlings originally represented 40 out of 100 germinants but now represent 40 out of 70 survivors, or about 57.1 percent. The transmission process has not been altered retrospectively; the population available for scoring has changed. In exam problems, a denominator of surviving offspring must be recognized as conditional on survival. It may yield a different ratio from the ratio at fertilization or germination.

A second sample tests whether the pollen source affects the embryo plastid genotype. Use flowers of the same green maternal branch, and pollinate one group with green pollen and another group with pollen from a variegated branch containing a distinct molecular plastid marker. If all seedlings from both groups carry the maternal marker at a validated detection limit, the result supports maternal transmission in that cross. However, the experiment must not claim that paternal inheritance is impossible at every frequency. If the marker assay can detect paternal variants only above a given fraction of genome copies, paternal leakage below that limit could go undetected. If only a small number of offspring are examined, rare events may also be missed.

This reasoning is useful when comparing two plant species. In a species with biparental plastid transmission, cross a homoplasmic green line carrying plastid marker G with another homoplasmic line carrying marker P. If the embryo receives both G and P plastids, its initial cytoplasm can be mixed regardless of whether its nuclear genotype is heterozygous at a marker locus. Later vegetative segregation can produce individual tissue regions with different G:P ratios. Depending on the molecular function of the markers, these regions may or may not differ visibly. A mixed embryo genotype and a variegated mature plant are related possible observations, but they are not interchangeable definitions.

Paternal plastid inheritance gives another distinct prediction. Suppose the pollen-producing parent contributes the plastid genomes that persist in embryos, while plastids from the egg are excluded from sustained inheritance. Reciprocal crosses of two homoplasmic lines would then produce the paternal plastid marker in each cross. The paternal rule should be applied to the organelle type explicitly stated in the problem. A species can have different transmission rules for plastids and mitochondria. Knowing the plastid transmission mode does not determine the mitochondrial transmission mode automatically.

What mechanisms can produce these different routes? In plants with a strongly maternal pattern, plastids may be excluded from the cell lineage that forms sperm, or plastid DNA may be degraded during pollen development. Different taxa use different combinations of these processes. In a biparental system, exclusion or degradation is incomplete or absent to a degree that allows contributions from both parental lines. Paternal transmission requires that the male gamete's plastid genetic material survives and contributes to subsequent development. The molecular details need not be identical for all species that produce the same phenotypic inheritance pattern. Experimental evidence of both plastid exclusion and genome degradation in flowering plants supports a mechanism-based rather than purely descriptive account of inheritance.

The distinction between plastid number and genome copy number is relevant to variegation. A cell that contains ten plastids of two different visual classes may not contain the same number of genome copies per plastid. Measuring the fraction of defective plastids microscopically and measuring the fraction of variant DNA by sequencing can therefore give different quantities. Both can be scientifically useful, provided the measured variable is clearly named. A plastid that appears green can also contain a mixture of genome types if sufficient functional gene expression is available. The phenotype at organelle, cell, tissue and whole-organism scales must be interpreted separately.

For a breeding application, an organelle-associated trait can be retained by using the appropriate parent in repeated crosses. In a maternally transmitting species, a desired plastid genotype usually follows the seed parent. In a paternally transmitting system it follows the pollen parent. Yet the final phenotype still depends on a compatible nuclear background. The biological lesson is that crossing strategies need to account for both genome compartments. A practical genetics question may ask which parental role preserves a plastid trait while introducing nuclear alleles from a second line; the answer follows from the experimentally specified transmission mode, not from an assumption that every cytoplasmic trait is maternal.

14. Quantitative models of organelle segregation

A neutral sampling model becomes especially informative when the question asks for the probability of an extreme descendant fraction. Take a parental population containing 25 percent of variant m. An effective sample of eight independent units is drawn from a large well-mixed pool. The chance that none of the units carries m is (0.75)^8, which equals approximately 0.1001. The chance that every unit carries m is (0.25)^8, about 0.0000153. The asymmetry arises because the parental population contains more w than m. It would be incorrect to assign equal probabilities to the two homoplasmic outcomes merely because two alternatives exist.

The probability that at least one sampled unit carries m is one minus the probability of sampling none. For this example, it equals about 0.8999. This event is not the same as crossing a phenotypic threshold. If a hypothetical phenotype requires a sampled mutant fraction of at least 0.5, at least four of the eight units must be m. The probability is the sum of the binomial probabilities for k equal to four, five, six, seven and eight. These values are approximately 0.0865, 0.0231, 0.00385, 0.000366 and 0.0000153, giving a combined probability of about 0.1138. Most sampled lineages carry at least one mutant unit, but far fewer reach the stated threshold. This calculation separates transmission of a variant from expression of its phenotype.

A different situation occurs when an original finite pool is divided into two complementary daughter populations. Suppose there are exactly six units, three mutant and three wild type, and three are allocated to daughter A without replacement. Daughter B receives the remaining three. There are C(6,3) = 20 equally probable unordered choices for daughter A if all units are equally likely to be selected. The probability that A receives no mutant is C(3,0)C(3,3)/20 = 1/20. The probability that A receives exactly one mutant is C(3,1)C(3,2)/20 = 9/20. The probabilities of two and three mutants are 9/20 and 1/20. If A receives one mutant, B receives two, and if A receives three mutants, B receives none. Their fractions are negatively correlated because they share a fixed parental pool.

The binomial and hypergeometric calculations answer different questions. In the first, the effective sampling is independent from a large parental source. In the second, removal of one unit changes the composition available for subsequent draws. Students often recognize that the probability distribution must be different but apply binomial coefficients without adjusting for the finite pool. Read whether a question specifies sampling with replacement, a very large pool, or a partition of a fixed number of units. The assumptions are part of the model, not minor details that can be ignored after a formula is memorized.

It is possible to derive a simple expression for the tendency toward homoplasmy under repeated idealized sampling. Define H = 2h(1-h), the probability that two independently drawn units from a population carry different sequence types. After a neutral sampling event of size n, the expected descendant fraction remains h, but the expected square of the descendant fraction equals h squared plus h(1-h)/n. Substituting into 2 times the expected value of h' minus h' squared gives E(H') = H(1-1/n). Thus expected diversity within a descendant population decreases by a factor of 1-1/n per independent sampling generation in this model. Repeating the calculation for t cycles yields H multiplied by (1-1/n)^t, when n remains constant. No new variant has to arise for diversity to decrease within lineages.

This result does not contradict the increase in differences between lineages. If every lineage begins at h = 0.5, the within-lineage diversity is initially maximal. Some lineages later become mostly m and others mostly w, so their within-lineage diversity falls while differences among lineages increase. At the limiting idealized state each lineage may be homoplasmic, even though the collection of lineages still contains both sequence types. Genetic diversity depends on the organizational level at which it is measured. This same distinction appears in population genetics, but here it operates among organelle populations and cellular lineages.

Effective sampling size can also be estimated from an illustrative variance. Suppose a large set of independent offspring has mean heteroplasmy 0.4 and variance 0.012 in fraction-squared units, while a neutral one-step binomial approximation is assumed. Rearranging Var(h') = h(1-h)/n yields n = 0.4 × 0.6 / 0.012 = 20. This value is an effective parameter for the simplified model; it is not a direct count of mitochondrial DNA molecules present in the precursor cells. Measurement noise can inflate observed variance, selection can alter the distribution, and multiple developmental events can be collapsed into a single effective parameter.

One should not assume that an observed heteroplasmy variance uniquely identifies a bottleneck size unless the sampling and developmental assumptions are defensible. For example, if technical measurement error contributes 0.004 to the observed variance of 0.012 and is independent of biological variation, the estimated biological variance would be 0.008. The corresponding neutral estimate becomes 0.24/0.008 = 30. Ignoring the measurement component would incorrectly suggest a stronger bottleneck. This is a general lesson in quantitative inference: a fitted parameter inherits the assumptions of the measurement model used to obtain it.

15. From variant frequency to physiological expression

A simple threshold model is often introduced as a graph in which normal function is maintained until a mutant fraction reaches a critical value. Its usefulness lies in explaining how a molecular burden can be present without an obvious phenotype. The model is not a universal description of the response curve. Enzyme activity may decline gradually as mutant fraction increases, whereas a tissue-level function remains almost normal until a reserve capacity is exhausted. Alternatively, impaired assembly of one component can produce a nonlinear change in the amount of functional enzyme. The functional phenotype depends on the molecular lesion, the total amount of protein and the physiological demand placed on the tissue.

Consider two illustrative cell populations with the same mutant fraction of 0.6. Population A has 1000 copies of mitochondrial DNA per cell; population B has 200. If the remaining copies are wild type, the expected counts of wild-type genomes are 400 and 80 respectively. Even if all other properties were equivalent, the same fractional burden could correspond to different numbers of functional templates. The actual relationship between template count and protein output is not necessarily proportional, because transcription and translation can be regulated and mitochondrial components turn over. Nevertheless, the example demonstrates why heteroplasmy fraction and total mitochondrial DNA copy number are distinct biological measurements.

A bulk tissue measurement can obscure variability among cells. Imagine a tissue composed of two equal-sized cell populations, each with the same number of mitochondrial DNA copies per cell. One population has heteroplasmy 0.2 and the other 0.8, so the average fraction is 0.5. A second tissue in which every cell has heteroplasmy 0.5 also has a bulk average of 0.5. If a hypothetical cellular impairment occurs above 0.7, half the cells in the first tissue and none of the cells in the second tissue are impaired. The same tissue-level fraction can therefore be compatible with different physiological states. This problem becomes more complicated if cell types differ in mitochondrial abundance, because the bulk fraction is weighted by DNA copy number rather than simply by the number of cells.

The physiological readout also has a threshold of its own. A tissue may continue to function normally if a sufficient fraction of its cells performs the required task. Conversely, a small group of specialized cells may be essential for a particular organ function, so a defect limited to those cells can have a large effect. A threshold observed at the whole-organ level is therefore not necessarily the same threshold measured for an enzyme in an isolated cell. The examiner may ask why a patient or experimental organism with a given bulk heteroplasmy can show a different phenotype from another with the same measured value. The appropriate answer invokes tissue distribution, functional demand, variant identity and nuclear background rather than one universal percentage.

Three mitochondrial complexes may be impaired by a variant affecting mitochondrial translation, yet a measurement of complex II activity may remain relatively preserved because its subunits are nuclear encoded. This pattern can suggest a problem with mitochondrial gene expression. It is not a definitive diagnosis because nuclear genes involved in mitochondrial translation can produce similar defects, and secondary physiological changes can affect multiple complexes. Compare the results against appropriate controls and ask which mechanistic explanation predicts the combination of affected and spared functions. A useful functional assay narrows the hypotheses; direct genetic evidence is still needed to locate the primary mutation.

A representative contrast is between an mtDNA variant affecting mitochondrial transfer RNA and a nuclear mutation affecting a mitochondrial DNA replication protein. In the former, the variant may show maternal transmission and heteroplasmy. In the latter, the primary allele can show autosomal inheritance, even though it may cause changes in mitochondrial DNA copy number or multiple mitochondrial genome deletions. Both can impair energy metabolism. One cannot infer the inheritance route simply from the appearance of altered mtDNA in affected tissues. The primary molecular defect and its consequences must be separated.

This reasoning also helps interpret an apparent skipped generation. Suppose a woman carries a mitochondrial variant but has no detectable symptoms at the time she is examined. Her offspring may nevertheless inherit the variant, and a child may have a higher burden or a more susceptible tissue distribution. A maternal lineage with variable symptoms is therefore not inconsistent with mtDNA transmission. At the same time, incomplete penetrance occurs in nuclear inheritance as well. An apparently skipped generation is not unique evidence for a mitochondrial bottleneck. Pedigree information should be interpreted in conjunction with variant measurements and an explicit model of phenotype expression.

The simplest exam strategy is to translate each numerical statement into its corresponding variable. Is the value a fraction of mitochondrial DNA copies, a percentage of affected cells, an enzyme activity relative to controls, a percentage of individuals with a phenotype, or a probability of transmission? These quantities are not interchangeable. If a problem gives only a mitochondrial variant fraction and asks for an exact probability of a clinical phenotype without a defined threshold or model, the data are insufficient. A mechanistic answer identifies what additional measurements would be necessary rather than assigning a probability based on an unrelated example.

16. Interpreting yeast crosses and respiratory growth data

The petite example becomes particularly useful when experimental results must be interpreted without knowing in advance whether the lesion is nuclear or mitochondrial. Suppose a haploid strain P produces small colonies on glucose and fails to grow on glycerol, while a compatible wild-type strain G grows on both carbon sources. These are qualitative observations in a constructed teaching example. The first inference is that P may have impaired respiratory competence. The investigator must not immediately call it a rho-minus mutant, because numerous nuclear genes are required for respiration and the observed growth pattern is shared by different genotypes.

Assume P and G mate to produce a diploid with normal growth on glycerol. The restored phenotype is compatible with recessive nuclear complementation, because the wild-type partner could provide a functioning allele. It is also compatible with restoration of mitochondrial genomes from G in a rho-zero strain. Thus the diploid result alone cannot distinguish these explanations. Examine the progeny of meiosis and determine whether respiratory deficiency segregates with a nuclear marker. In the simple nuclear model, a complete tetrad contains two spores carrying the defective allele and two carrying the normal allele. If all four spores contain functional mitochondrial genomes and environmental conditions permit scoring, a corresponding two-to-two growth pattern is expected.

A cytoplasmic petite crossed to a wild-type partner can yield a different result. Imagine the diploid is able to respire and all four spores from a complete tetrad are respiratory competent. This is consistent with a rho-zero parent's lack of mitochondrial DNA and acquisition of a functional mitochondrial population from the rho-plus partner. It may also be consistent with a nuclear model involving unusual selection or an unrecognized experimental detail, so the absence of a two-to-two pattern alone should not be treated as a definitive molecular diagnosis. Direct examination of mitochondrial DNA and nuclear genotypes strengthens the inference.

Suppose instead that an independently characterized rho-minus strain produces a high fraction of respiration-deficient descendants after mating with a rho-plus strain. The term suppressive describes that experimental transmission outcome. It is tempting to conclude that the defective genome must encode a dominant inhibitor of respiration. This conclusion does not follow. Preferential propagation of the defective mitochondrial genome, or differential maintenance of genome types, can cause it to outcompete functional genomes without encoding a conventional dominant nuclear inhibitor. At the cellular level, there may be insufficient functional mitochondrial information remaining after sorting. At the colony level, growth depends on available substrates and the ability to compensate by fermentation.

For a quantitative example, suppose 100 independent descendant colonies are scored after the cross of a rho-minus line with a rho-plus partner. Eighty are respiration deficient and twenty are competent. A parallel wild-type by wild-type control produces three respiration-deficient colonies among 100. The hypothetical observed fraction of petites is 0.80 in the test and 0.03 in the control. This large difference supports a suppressive effect associated with the tested strain. It does not establish that exactly 80 percent of the mitochondrial DNA molecules in the original zygotes were defective. The measured unit is a colony, not a DNA molecule. A colony can descend from a cell whose mitochondrial population changed before scoring.

The medium used to score a phenotype can determine which types are observable. If cells are plated directly on a strictly nonfermentable carbon source, respiratory-deficient descendants may fail to form colonies and disappear from the denominator. In that case, the recovered population is enriched for respiratory-competent cells and cannot directly reveal the total petite fraction among original descendants. A permissive growth condition followed by a comparison of growth phenotypes is more informative for counting both classes. Conceptually, selection and screening are different: selection enriches cells capable of growth under a particular condition, while screening evaluates differences among cells that have already been recovered.

The classical yeast system also shows why an organism's ecological and metabolic flexibility matters for genetic analysis. Saccharomyces cerevisiae can remain viable after loss of its mitochondrial DNA when supported by fermentation, although it still requires important mitochondrial functions that do not depend on mtDNA-encoded oxidative phosphorylation products. Not every yeast species shares this capacity, and not every organism tolerates severe mitochondrial genome loss. Thus a petite phenotype and its recovery conditions should not be treated as universal properties of eukaryotes. The biological model is valuable because it provides an experimentally tractable situation in which nuclear and mitochondrial inheritance can be separated.

When interpreting an unfamiliar yeast problem, follow the evidence in sequence. First determine whether growth depends on respiratory capacity. Second ask whether mating restores function. Third inspect segregation among meiotic products and the composition of mitochondrial genomes. Fourth consider the possibility of preferential mitochondrial genome propagation or a bias introduced by the scoring medium. This sequence keeps the interpretation tied to measurable observations. It also connects directly to the next Academy subunit, where complementation tests and functional classification of mutations are developed in greater detail.

17. Maternal-effect inheritance through several generations

The one-generation displacement of a maternal effect is best mastered by explicitly tracking genotypes across crosses. In the classical snail model, use D for a functional maternal allele and d for a recessive maternal allele. The offspring phenotype is dextral when the egg parent's genotype contains D, and sinistral when the egg parent is dd. The embryo's own genotype controls which alleles it can contribute as a future parent but does not alter the shell direction already determined by its mother's contribution. This rule applies throughout the following hypothetical crosses.

Start with a dd egg parent and a DD sperm parent. The F1 offspring are all Dd and sinistral. Cross one of these F1 snails, now acting as egg parent, to a dd sperm parent. The F2 genotypes are half Dd and half dd, but the F2 phenotypes are all dextral. If the two F2 genotypes later act as egg parents and are separately crossed with DD sperm parents, the Dd egg parent produces dextral progeny, while the dd egg parent produces sinistral progeny. All offspring of the dd mother in this final cross are Dd. They carry D but display the phenotype determined by the absence of D in their mother's genotype.

A useful extension is to distinguish the phenotype ratio among offspring from the ratio among mothers. Suppose four F2 mothers have genotypes DD, Dd, Dd and dd, and each produces a brood of twenty successfully scored offspring. Three mothers produce all dextral offspring and one produces all sinistral offspring. The aggregate result is sixty dextral and twenty sinistral offspring, or 3:1. However, if the dd mother produces only five surviving offspring and the other mothers produce twenty each, the observed ratio becomes sixty to five, or 12:1. The genetic model has not changed. Unequal brood sizes weight maternal families differently, making a phenotype ratio among individuals differ from a ratio among mothers.

This distinction is especially important when an examiner supplies a pedigree with differently sized families. A complete maternal-effect model predicts the phenotype within each family from the egg parent's genotype. It does not generally predict a fixed overall ratio unless the distribution of maternal genotypes and their contributions to the sampled progeny are known. The expectation of 3:1 in a selfed heterozygote sequence is not the same as a conventional 3:1 monohybrid F2 result, even though the numbers can coincide. In the maternal-effect case the characteristic ratio is displaced by one generation and depends on the way broods are combined.

Now consider a snail of unknown nuclear genotype that is itself dextral. Under ordinary dominance, one would restrict its possible genotypes to DD and Dd. Under maternal-effect inheritance, the unknown snail could also be dd because its mother's genotype may have contained D. When the snail serves as the egg parent in a cross, all its offspring are sinistral if it is dd, whereas all are dextral if it carries D. A brood of dextral progeny alone does not distinguish DD from Dd. Genotyping or testing the transmission of d into later generations is required. The phenotype of the mother and the phenotype she produces are two different pieces of evidence.

A paternal test can be equally revealing. A sinistral Dd snail that contributes sperm to a DD egg parent can transmit d to about half the progeny while all the resulting offspring are dextral. The sperm parent's own sinistral phenotype has no direct control over their shell orientation. The allele d is nevertheless inherited and may influence later broods when it occurs in a homozygous egg parent. This shows why a nuclear maternal-effect allele is not confined to the maternal lineage. It can move through paternal transmission while its phenotypic effect appears only when expressed in the appropriate maternal role.

The distinction from an X-linked gene becomes clear in an analogous animal with separate sexes. A maternal-effect allele located on an autosome can be inherited by both sons and daughters; a son may pass it to his daughter even though he does not provision eggs. When the daughter becomes a mother, her genotype can influence her offspring. An mtDNA variant with strict maternal transmission, in contrast, is ordinarily not passed by the son to his daughter. The ability of a paternal line to transmit the causal nuclear allele, even when phenotypically silent in that parental role, is a key diagnostic feature.

Maternal-effect genes can also be recessive lethal in the offspring phenotype when the required egg product is absent. In such a system, a homozygous mutant mother may lay embryos that fail to develop despite receiving a wild-type allele from the father. Such lethality changes which genotypes can reach adulthood and act as future mothers. It would be wrong to transfer the viable snail coiling F3 calculation directly to a lethal Drosophila example. Always determine whether the phenotype permits descendants to contribute to the next generation before applying multigeneration ratios. The general maternal-effect logic is shared, but the viability assumptions determine the expected pedigree.

18. Classical evidence, controls and alternative interpretations

The historical significance of extrachromosomal inheritance is that phenotypic transmission patterns first challenged an overly narrow interpretation of nuclear chromosome inheritance, and later molecular observations clarified the mechanism. Correns and Baur demonstrated that the plastid-associated phenotypes of different plant systems need not follow the same parental transmission rule. Ephrussi's work on yeast respiratory-deficient mutants highlighted a cytoplasmic determinant that could be distinguished from nuclear respiration genes. The presence of organelle-specific DNA later supplied a physical substrate for these genetic observations. This sequence of discoveries illustrates how genetics can predict the existence of a hereditary determinant before the determinant has been characterized biochemically.

To understand what a cross proves, compare an observation with the prediction that would be expected if an alternative hypothesis were true. In the Mirabilis example, a simple autosomal pigmentation gene with complete dominance predicts the same F1 phenotype in reciprocal crosses of true-breeding parental lines. A consistent difference between the two reciprocal F1 groups challenges that hypothesis. If plastid markers also follow the maternal parent and the pigment pattern persists through subsequent crosses despite changes in nuclear ancestry, the organelle model gains additional support. Still, a molecular marker merely located in the plastid is not itself proof that the particular sequence change causes white tissue. The evidence should be assembled at several levels.

Modern studies of plastid inheritance provide examples of how mechanisms can be tested rather than inferred from the final inheritance pattern alone. If male gametes exclude plastids, microscopy at appropriate developmental stages can reveal their absence from the generative lineage. If plastids enter the relevant lineage but lose their DNA, an assay targeting plastid genomes can reveal a decline in DNA abundance even when plastid structures remain visible. Distinguishing these two mechanisms matters because either one can result in few or no paternal plastid genomes persisting in the offspring. Observing a maternally inherited marker does not identify the particular exclusion mechanism.

A similar logic applies to paternal mitochondrial transmission in animals. Detecting paternal mitochondria in a sperm cell establishes their presence in the gamete. Detecting paternal mtDNA immediately after fertilization may establish an initial contribution under some circumstances. Neither observation establishes persistent inheritance. To claim paternal transmission of a mitochondrial genome, one must distinguish persistent organellar DNA from transient material and from nuclear DNA segments derived from mitochondrial sequences. Research on nuclear mitochondrial DNA insertions shows why apparent paternal mitochondrial haplotypes require careful interpretation. The standard human mitochondrial inheritance model remains predominantly maternal, with exceptional claims demanding stronger evidence than ordinary nuclear sequence inheritance.

The snail maternal-effect model provides a different type of experiment. Classical multigeneration crosses establish that the shell phenotype depends on the genotype of the mother. A candidate gene identified by genetic association can be evaluated using targeted changes to its function and careful analysis of the resulting early embryonic phenotype. The 2019 Lsdia1 study linked disruption of the nuclear gene to reversed coiling in the offspring. The mechanistic conclusion is stronger than a simple association of a marker with shell phenotype because alteration of the candidate gene changes the predicted developmental outcome. Appropriate comparison of genetic backgrounds and independent evidence of early chirality connect the nuclear gene with the developmental process.

The bicoid example helps distinguish the transfer of a gene product from the transfer of an active organelle genome. In the fly, localization of maternal RNA during egg formation establishes a spatial source of Bicoid protein in the embryo. A mother lacking the required activity fails to provide the usual anterior determinant. The location of the RNA in the egg is direct mechanistic evidence for a maternal contribution; it is not evidence that the bicoid gene itself resides in cytoplasmic DNA. The gene remains nuclear. Its product is present in the cytoplasm because the mother expressed the gene before fertilization. Later embryonic expression of other nuclear genes responds to that inherited developmental environment.

Controls are required whenever a treatment changes a maternal or cytoplasmic factor. In a symbiont experiment, removal of bacteria might simultaneously change host health, other microorganisms or mitochondrial translation. Comparing treated and untreated individuals is useful but insufficient if the treatment has several effects. A stronger design includes matched host genetic backgrounds, a control treatment, direct measurement of infection status, and, where feasible, a restoration comparison. Similarly, altering incubation conditions during a plastid inheritance experiment may affect both gamete survival and genome transmission. Data on surviving progeny should be accompanied by evidence on the developmental stage at which the relevant difference arises.

The central skill for CSIR-NET analytical questions is to distinguish what has been observed from what is inferred. If the observation is a maternal pattern in one reciprocal cross, the defensible inference is that an ordinary symmetric nuclear model may not explain the result. If the observation includes allele segregation in later generations and phenotype tracking the maternal nuclear genotype, a maternal-effect model is supported. If the observation includes stable inheritance of a plastid or mitochondrial marker independent of nuclear segregation, a cytoplasmic genetic model is supported. If the evidence includes direct functional perturbation of the causal determinant with appropriate controls, the mechanistic claim becomes stronger. This graduated reasoning is more reliable than treating a single characteristic pattern as conclusive evidence for a mechanism.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. All reciprocal differences prove cytoplasmic inheritance

Correct concept: X linkage, imprinting, maternal effects and survival can also cause asymmetry. Why it is tempting: A reciprocal F1 result is often treated as a definitive label. Exam trap: An exam may offer both maternal effect and mitochondrial inheritance. Reasoning: Compare later crosses and molecular markers.

2. All mitochondrial proteins are encoded by mtDNA

Correct concept: Most mitochondrial proteins are nuclear encoded. Why it is tempting: The organelle's own genome is mistaken for a complete cellular genome. Exam trap: A question asks where a complex II subunit gene resides. Reasoning: Separate gene location from protein destination.

3. Mitochondrial inheritance affects only daughters

Correct concept: Both sexes can receive maternal mtDNA; daughters generally transmit it. Why it is tempting: The direction of transmission is confused with expression. Exam trap: A pedigree contains affected sons who have unaffected children. Reasoning: Track receipt and onward transmission separately.

4. Every child of a symptomatic mitochondrial mother must be symptomatic

Correct concept: Heteroplasmy, tissue distribution and penetrance affect expression. Why it is tempting: Genotype transmission is confused with phenotype. Exam trap: A question gives a heteroplasmic mother with variable offspring. Reasoning: Do not assign symptom probabilities without a defined model.

5. Sperm contain no mitochondria

Correct concept: Sperm commonly contain mitochondria, but paternal genomes generally fail to persist in typical maternal systems. Why it is tempting: The shorthand 'egg supplies mitochondria' is taken literally. Exam trap: A question shows sperm midpiece mitochondria. Reasoning: Distinguish gamete contents from persistent inheritance.

6. Plastids are always maternally inherited

Correct concept: Transmission can be maternal, paternal or biparental. Why it is tempting: Mirabilis is memorized as a universal rule. Exam trap: A conifer or Pelargonium cross specifies a different route. Reasoning: Use the route specified for the species.

7. A variegated branch gives a 3:1 ratio

Correct concept: Plastid populations can sort into different classes with variable proportions. Why it is tempting: Students apply monohybrid ratios to every cross. Exam trap: Seedling classes have unequal counts that do not fit Mendelian ratios. Reasoning: Model plastid sampling and survival instead.

8. Every white leaf is a plastid mutation

Correct concept: Nuclear genes and environment can also disrupt chloroplast function. Why it is tempting: The site of the phenotype is confused with the site of the mutation. Exam trap: A nuclear mutant has chlorosis. Reasoning: Use cross and genotyping evidence.

9. All petite mutants are cytoplasmic

Correct concept: Nuclear pet mutations may produce respiratory deficiency. Why it is tempting: Colony size is assumed to identify a genome compartment. Exam trap: A petite segregates 2:2 in tetrads. Reasoning: Test nuclear segregation and mtDNA.

10. Rho-zero and rho-minus are interchangeable

Correct concept: Rho-zero lacks mtDNA; rho-minus retains altered genomes. Why it is tempting: Both are respiratory deficient and look alike. Exam trap: A cross restores respiration from a rho-plus partner. Reasoning: Distinguish absence from altered DNA.

11. Heteroplasmy equals the percentage of abnormal cells

Correct concept: It is a fraction of genome copies in a defined sample. Why it is tempting: Bulk tissue and cell counts are conflated. Exam trap: Two tissues have the same mean but different cellular distributions. Reasoning: State the denominator and organizational scale.

12. A bottleneck necessarily shifts mean heteroplasmy

Correct concept: Neutral sampling increases variance without changing the expected mean. Why it is tempting: Variance and directional change are conflated. Exam trap: A smaller effective n is offered as a cause of a directional shift. Reasoning: Use mean and variance separately; test selection.

13. Every mtDNA disease has a universal 60% threshold

Correct concept: Functional thresholds depend on variant, tissue and context. Why it is tempting: Illustrative numbers are memorized as biological constants. Exam trap: An exam gives differing thresholds for two tissues. Reasoning: Use the stated model only.

14. A dominant maternal-effect allele determines the carrier's own shell

Correct concept: The egg parent's genotype determines the offspring's shell in the model. Why it is tempting: Ordinary dominance is substituted for maternal action. Exam trap: A dextral dd mother produces sinistral offspring. Reasoning: Write individual and maternal genotypes in separate columns.

15. The F2 of selfed Dd snails segregates 3:1 for shell phenotype

Correct concept: All F2 are dextral under a complete dominant maternal effect; genotype is 1:2:1. Why it is tempting: Standard Mendelian phenotype ratios are applied without the lag. Exam trap: The problem follows F1 to F3. Reasoning: Derive each brood from its mother's genotype.

16. A maternal-effect nuclear allele cannot be paternally transmitted

Correct concept: The nuclear allele can move through sperm, while its action is maternal. Why it is tempting: Maternal effect is equated with maternal organelle inheritance. Exam trap: A father passes d to a daughter who later produces a mutant brood. Reasoning: Track allele route independently of timing of expression.

17. Imprinting and maternal effect are identical

Correct concept: Imprinting concerns parental origin of the offspring allele; maternal effect concerns the parent's genotype and egg product. Why it is tempting: Both can produce reciprocal differences. Exam trap: A heterozygous mother produces phenotype segregation or uniformity. Reasoning: Test whether transmitted allele or total maternal genotype predicts the outcome.

18. Cytoplasmic incompatibility proves paternal bacterial transmission

Correct concept: An infected male can alter embryo viability without stably transmitting bacteria. Why it is tempting: Influence on offspring is equated with transmission. Exam trap: A cross of an infected male with an uninfected female has low survival. Reasoning: Measure infection directly and separate viability from inheritance.

CSIR-NET Part B

1. Which component is the extrachromosomal genetic determinant in a typical mitochondrial inheritance cross?

  1. A mitochondrial DNA sequence variant
  2. A nuclear allele for mitochondrial import
  3. A transient maternal protein
  4. A cytosolic ATP molecule
Answer and explanation

A. The determinant is a sequence in mtDNA. Nuclear import genes remain chromosomal, while ATP and a deposited protein are not independently replicating organelle genomes.

2. Which set correctly describes the standard human mitochondrial genome?

  1. 22 proteins, 13 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs
  2. 13 proteins, 2 tRNAs, 22 rRNAs
  3. 37 respiratory proteins
  4. 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs
Answer and explanation

D. The 37 human mtDNA genes encode 13 oxidative phosphorylation proteins, 22 tRNAs and two rRNAs. They do not encode all mitochondrial proteins.

3. Which oxidative phosphorylation complex lacks mtDNA-encoded subunits in humans?

  1. I
  2. III
  3. II
  4. IV
Answer and explanation

C. Complex II is nuclear encoded. Human mtDNA supplies subunits of I, III, IV and V.

4. What does homoplasmy at a specified mitochondrial locus mean?

  1. All mitochondria have exactly one DNA molecule
  2. All sampled mtDNA copies share the same sequence at that locus
  3. A cell has no mitochondrial genome
  4. All tissues show identical respiratory activity
Answer and explanation

B. Homoplasmy describes sequence uniformity at the locus in a defined sample, not copy number or physiological activity.

5. Under strict maternal mtDNA transmission, who can receive a variant from a homoplasmic mother?

  1. Daughters only
  2. Both sons and daughters
  3. Sons only
  4. Neither if the father lacks it
Answer and explanation

B. Egg-derived mitochondria enter embryos of both sexes. Transmission onward normally occurs through daughters, which is different from receiving the variant.

6. The classical reciprocal crosses of Mirabilis demonstrated primarily which pattern?

  1. Maternal plastid transmission
  2. Paternal plastid transmission
  3. Autosomal dominant leaf colour
  4. Y-linked leaf colour
Answer and explanation

A. Offspring plastid-associated colour generally followed the egg-bearing branch; pollen origin did not determine the classical result.

7. Why can a variegated Mirabilis branch yield different seedling colours?

  1. Its pollen always contains three nuclear alleles
  2. Meiosis creates three sex chromosomes
  3. Every white chloroplast becomes a nucleus
  4. Different eggs can receive different plastid populations
Answer and explanation

D. A mixed population can be sorted during cell and gamete development, producing eggs with different proportions of functional and defective plastids.

8. Which term denotes a yeast cell without mitochondrial DNA?

  1. rho-plus
  2. rho-minus
  3. rho-zero
  4. Dd
Answer and explanation

C. Rho-zero lacks mtDNA; rho-minus retains severely altered/deleted mtDNA; rho-plus denotes the wild-type mitochondrial genome.

9. What does a suppressive cytoplasmic petite do in an appropriate cross?

  1. Always segregates 2:2 at a nuclear locus
  2. Never has mtDNA
  3. Produces an excess of respiration-deficient descendants relative to a neutral cross
  4. Cannot mate with any yeast
Answer and explanation

C. Suppressiveness is defined by the excess petite outcome after mating with a respiratory-proficient partner; it is not conventional nuclear dominance.

10. Which is the best definition of mitochondrial heteroplasmy?

  1. Two homologous nuclear chromosomes
  2. Two or more sequence variants in the specified mitochondrial genome population
  3. The presence of both chloroplasts and mitochondria
  4. The presence of two organelles
Answer and explanation

B. Heteroplasmy concerns sequence variants of organellar DNA. The sample may be a cell, tissue or individual.

11. What is a mitochondrial genetic bottleneck?

  1. A reduction in effective segregating genome units that increases variance among lineages
  2. A universal 50% phenotypic threshold
  3. An obligatory deletion of all mtDNA
  4. A change in chromosome number
Answer and explanation

A. A bottleneck concerns the effective number of genetic units transmitted or amplified; it need not equal the physical genome count.

12. A threshold effect directly concerns which relationship?

  1. Nuclear crossing over versus map distance
  2. Pollen number and flower colour only
  3. Allele transmission through Y chromosomes
  4. Mutation burden and functional phenotype in a defined context
Answer and explanation

D. A threshold describes the burden at which a functional deficit becomes apparent in a particular context; it is not itself a transmission probability.

13. In the classical snail maternal-effect model, what predicts an individual's shell coiling?

  1. Its own D/d genotype alone
  2. Its sperm parent's phenotype
  3. Its mitochondrial copy number
  4. Its egg parent's D/d genotype
Answer and explanation

D. The maternal nuclear genotype sets the relevant egg environment and hence offspring coiling.

14. Which is true of the causal nuclear allele of a maternal-effect gene?

  1. It is always mitochondrial
  2. It never follows Mendelian segregation
  3. It may be inherited through sperm or eggs
  4. Only females possess it
Answer and explanation

C. Maternal expression is distinct from allele transmission. Autosomal maternal-effect alleles can pass through either parent.

15. What is the distinctive basis of genomic imprinting?

  1. Loss of all paternal chromosomes
  2. The origin of the allele inherited by the offspring affects its expression
  3. Only the mother's total genotype matters in all cases
  4. Every organelle allele is maternally inherited
Answer and explanation

B. Imprinting affects expression of the offspring's inherited allele according to parental origin; maternal-effect genes act through the parent's genotype.

16. Which organism is a classical supporting example of a maternally transmitted cytoplasmic symbiont?

  1. Wolbachia in arthropods
  2. Rubisco in mitochondria
  3. A human nuclear chromosome
  4. Bicoid protein in an adult fly
Answer and explanation

A. Wolbachia is an intracellular bacterium with its own genome and can pass via the female germline.

17. Under the simple model of unidirectional Wolbachia incompatibility, which cross has reduced embryo viability?

  1. Uninfected female × compatible infected male
  2. Compatible infected female × uninfected male
  3. Uninfected female × uninfected male
  4. Compatible infected female × compatible infected male
Answer and explanation

A. The infected male by uninfected female cross is the characteristic incompatible combination in this model. Other strains and hosts may differ.

18. Vegetative segregation can occur during which process?

  1. Only nuclear meiosis
  2. Only fusion of sperm and egg
  3. Only nuclear crossing over
  4. Ordinary growth and mitotic cell division
Answer and explanation

D. Organelle genome populations can be partitioned and replicated during mitotic growth; nuclear meiosis is unnecessary.

19. Why is sperm mitochondrial presence not proof of paternal mtDNA inheritance?

  1. Sperm never have DNA
  2. Every sperm mitochondrial genome is nuclear DNA
  3. The paternal mitochondrial material may be eliminated and fail to persist
  4. Eggs contain no mitochondrial genomes
Answer and explanation

C. Heritable transmission requires persistence of the paternal genome in the offspring rather than its transient presence in gametes or embryos.

20. Which observation alone is insufficient to establish cytoplasmic inheritance?

  1. Direct stable organelle marker transmission across generations
  2. A reciprocal F1 phenotype difference
  3. A mitochondrial DNA variant co-transmitted in a controlled cytoplasmic replacement study
  4. Independent molecular evidence of an organelle genome
Answer and explanation

B. A reciprocal asymmetry also occurs in sex linkage, maternal effects, imprinting and differential viability. Additional tests are necessary.

21. A homoplasmic mother has mtDNA marker M; the father has W. Under strict maternal transmission and no mutation, what markers do their son and daughter carry?

  1. Son W, daughter M
  2. Both M
  3. Both W
  4. Son M, daughter W
Answer and explanation

B. Both receive egg-derived M. The son is normally unable to transmit M to his own offspring, but his receipt of M is not in doubt.

22. A woman has h = 0.3. Under a neutral independent-sampling model, n falls from 100 to 25. What happens to the variance of descendant h?

  1. It quadruples
  2. It halves
  3. It doubles
  4. It stays unchanged
Answer and explanation

A. Variance equals h(1−h)/n. The denominator becomes one quarter of its original value, so variance rises fourfold; the standard deviation doubles.

23. A mitochondrial sample has 150 mutant and 350 wild-type genomes. What is h?

  1. 0.15
  2. 0.43
  3. 0.70
  4. 0.30
Answer and explanation

D. The denominator is 500 total genomes; 150/500 = 0.30. Dividing by only wild-type genomes gives an incorrect ratio.

24. For h = 0.5 and independent n = 3, what is the chance that a sample contains only mutant units?

  1. 1/2
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/8
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

C. All three independent draws must carry the mutant, so multiply 0.5 three times. The probability of either homoplasmic class would instead be 1/4.

25. A pool contains two mutant and two wild-type units. Two units are sampled without replacement. What is the probability of sampling exactly one mutant?

  1. 1/2
  2. 1/3
  3. 2/3
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

C. There are C(4,2)=6 pairs and 2×2=4 mixed pairs. The probability is 4/6=2/3, not the binomial 1/2.

26. In a neutral model h = 0.4 and the variance of descendant fractions is 0.006. What is the one-step effective n?

  1. 20
  2. 40
  3. 60
  4. 100
Answer and explanation

B. Rearrange the variance equation: 0.4×0.6/0.006=40. This effective value need not equal physical copy number.

  1. 0.5904
  2. 0.0016
  3. 0.4096
  4. 0.8
Answer and explanation

A. The complementary event of no mutant has probability 0.8^4=0.4096. Subtract from 1 to obtain 0.5904.

28. Two equal-cell-number tissues with equal mtDNA copy count per cell both have mean h = 0.5. Tissue A has equal groups at h = 0.1 and 0.9; tissue B is uniformly 0.5. With impairment only above 0.7, what fractions of cells are impaired?

  1. A 0%, B 50%
  2. Both 50%
  3. Both 0%
  4. A 50%, B 0%
Answer and explanation

D. Only A's high-burden group exceeds the stated threshold. Equal means do not imply equal distributions or phenotypes.

29. A cross yields 30 green, 30 white and 40 mixed seedlings. All white seedlings die before late scoring. What fraction of survivors are green if the other seedlings survive?

  1. 30%
  2. 70%
  3. 50%
  4. 42.9%
Answer and explanation

D. Thirty green seedlings remain among 70 survivors. 30/70≈0.429; the original 30% was among all germinants.

30. A homoplasmic green plastid line is crossed as pollen parent to a homoplasmic white line. Strict maternal plastid transmission and adequate early scoring are assumed. What is the expected early phenotype?

  1. Green
  2. All variegated
  3. White
  4. 3:1 green:white
Answer and explanation

C. The seed parent contributes white plastids. A green paternal plastid type is not transmitted under the stated assumption.

31. Two homoplasmic plant lines carry plastid markers A and B. Both markers are found in a validated embryo plastid fraction. Which model is favoured?

  1. Strict maternal inheritance regardless of evidence
  2. Biparental plastid contribution
  3. A recessive nuclear maternal effect alone
  4. No DNA transmission
Answer and explanation

B. Both distinct parental organelle markers in the embryo support biparental contribution. Persistence should be followed to establish stable inheritance.

32. A petite × grande yeast diploid grows on glycerol, and its complete tetrads repeatedly show two competent and two deficient spores tracking a nuclear marker. What model is best supported?

  1. A simple nuclear respiration locus
  2. Strict maternal mitochondrial transmission
  3. Only rho-zero inheritance
  4. A threshold with no genetic effect
Answer and explanation

A. Repeated 2:2 segregation associated with a nuclear marker fits a chromosomal locus. Diploid rescue alone would not distinguish a nuclear mutation from acquisition of mtDNA.

33. A characterized rho-zero yeast mates with rho-plus; the diploid and all four meiotic progeny respire. What is a plausible explanation?

  1. The functional mtDNA was supplied by the rho-plus partner
  2. The rho-zero genome dominated
  3. Nuclear alleles never segregate
  4. Fermentation requires mtDNA
Answer and explanation

A. The rho-zero parent has no mtDNA to contribute; the rho-plus parent supplies a functional mitochondrial population.

34. A test cross involving a characterized rho-minus line yields 90 petites out of 100 descendant colonies versus 4 of 100 in controls. What can be concluded?

  1. 90% of original mitochondrial DNA was mutant
  2. Every offspring inherited a recessive nuclear allele
  3. All petites are rho-zero
  4. The line is consistent with a strong suppressive phenotype
Answer and explanation

D. The measured unit is a colony. The excess relative to controls supports suppressiveness but does not quantify initial mutant DNA fraction.

  1. DD dextral
  2. Dd dextral
  3. Dd sinistral
  4. dd sinistral
Answer and explanation

C. All inherit D from sperm and d from egg, but coiling is determined by the dd mother's genotype.

  2. All dextral, genotypes 1:2:1
  3. 3:1 dextral:sinistral within its brood
  4. All DD
Answer and explanation

B. Its Dd genotype determines the egg environment. Mendelian segregation produces DD:Dd:dd in 1:2:1, while all offspring are dextral.

  1. All Dd dextral
  2. All Dd sinistral
  3. All dd sinistral
  4. Half dextral and half sinistral

  1. 1/7

  4. 1/2

39. Reciprocal F1 groups both have Aa nuclear genotypes. When crossed as egg parents to aa testers, both produce entirely dominant-phenotype broods despite segregating Aa and aa offspring. Which model best fits among the options?

  1. Ordinary zygotic complete dominance
  2. Maternal allele expression at an imprinted locus with no maternal effect
  3. A Y-linked gene
  4. Complete dominant maternal effect
Answer and explanation

D. The mother's Aa genotype predicts the phenotype for every offspring, including aa. Ordinary dominance and maternal allele-specific imprinting predict segregation in this cross.

40. An F1 hybrid contains 50% of its expected neutral nuclear ancestry from recurrent line B. After three backcrosses to B using the hybrid lineage as egg parent, what is the expected B ancestry and maternal organelle type?

  1. 75%, organelles always B
  2. 87.5%, organelles lost
  3. 93.75%, original maternal type retained under strict maternal transmission
  4. 100%, original type always lost
Answer and explanation

C. The non-B fraction halves at each backcross: 0.5→0.25→0.125→0.0625. Thus B ancestry is 93.75% while the maternal organelle type is retained.

41. A mother has heteroplasmy h=0.25. In two idealized independent samples of n=8 units, a lineage is classified as impaired if at least four units are mutant. Which is closest to the probability that exactly one of the two lineages is impaired?

  1. 0.013
  2. 0.114
  3. 0.202
  4. 0.886
Answer and explanation

C. For a single lineage, sum binomial terms for k=4 to 8: p≈0.1138. Exactly one of two independent lineages has probability 2p(1−p)≈0.202. The value 0.013 approximates both impaired; 0.114 is the single-lineage probability.

42. Three tissues contain 100, 400 and 500 mtDNA copies respectively, with heteroplasmies 0.8, 0.2 and 0.6. The samples are pooled. What is the measured heteroplasmy, and why is it not the arithmetic mean of the three fractions?

  1. 0.533; each tissue contributes equally
  2. 0.46; it is weighted by copy count
  3. 0.60; the largest sample dominates completely
  4. 0.36; divide by wild-type copies
Answer and explanation

B. Mutant copies are 80+80+300=460, and total copies are 1000. The pooled fraction is 0.46. The unweighted mean 0.533 assumes equal copy contributions; neither the largest sample alone nor the wild-type denominator is appropriate.

43. Reciprocal F1 groups from pure AA and aa lines are all Aa but differ in phenotype. Each F1 is tested as egg parent to aa, and both sets produce 1:1 dominant:recessive phenotypes; the phenotype tracks the maternal allele inherited by each offspring. Which explanation best fits these findings?

  1. Maternal allele-specific expression at the nuclear locus
  2. Complete dominant nuclear maternal effect
  3. Strict homoplasmic maternal organelle inheritance
  4. Ordinary complete dominance explains the entire experiment
Answer and explanation

A. The original reciprocal asymmetry is not explained by ordinary zygotic dominance. The two F1 mothers have the same total genotype, so a complete dominant maternal effect predicts uniform dominant broods, and strict homoplasmic maternal organelle inheritance predicts persistence of reciprocal differences. Tracking the inherited maternal allele favours maternal allele-specific expression.

44. Two yeast strains have identical nuclear marker sets. A petite crossed with a rho-plus tester yields respiration-competent diploids, but 75% of descendants later become petite; a neutral control yields 2%. The petite initially contained deleted mitochondrial genomes. Which further result best supports preferential propagation of the defective genome rather than a dominant nuclear inhibitor?

  1. A larger number of mitochondria per cell without genotyping
  2. A 2:2 nuclear segregation pattern for a dominant inhibitor
  3. A smaller original colony only
  4. An increase in defective mtDNA fraction preceding loss of respiration in descendant lineages
Answer and explanation

D. The temporal association of expansion of the defective genome with loss of respiratory function supports biased mitochondrial propagation. A nuclear segregation pattern would favour a different hypothesis; morphology and total organelle number do not directly identify competing genomes.

45. For a heteroplasmy dataset with mean 0.4, observed between-offspring variance 0.018 and independent measurement-error variance 0.006, calculate the neutral one-step effective n after correcting measurement error. Which interpretation is valid?

  1. n≈13; it is an exact count of oocyte mtDNA molecules
  2. n=40; subtract measurement error from the mean
  3. n=60; variance always increases the physical number of genomes
  4. n=20; it is an effective model parameter
Answer and explanation

D. The corrected biological variance is 0.018−0.006=0.012. Then n=0.4×0.6/0.012=20. The uncorrected estimate is about 13.3; neither value directly counts physical molecules. Noise is subtracted from variance, not mean.

46. A Dd mother produces 40 dextral F2 snails, ten each of DD and dd and twenty Dd. All F2 act as mothers with DD sperm donors, but dd mothers each contribute half as many scored offspring as other mothers. What fraction of the F3 individuals are sinistral?

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/2
  3. 1/7
  4. 1/8
Answer and explanation

C. The ten dd mothers have weight 5 ordinary brood equivalents, whereas the thirty D-bearing mothers have weight 30. The fraction is 5/(30+5)=1/7. One quarter is the proportion of dd mothers rather than the weighted proportion of F3 offspring.

47. A variegated seed parent gives 50 green, 25 white and 25 mixed seedlings under pollen source X. Under pollen source Y, the initial distribution is similar, but white survival is 0 and mixed survival is 40%, while all green seedlings survive. Which comparison is appropriate for testing whether pollen changes plastid transmission?

  1. Compare the late green fraction of X with the early green fraction of Y
  2. Compare early embryo or seedling plastid genotype frequencies under both pollen sources, separately measuring survival
  3. Conclude Y gives paternal green inheritance because surviving Y seedlings are mostly green
  4. Count only the surviving white seedlings
Answer and explanation

B. Initial transmission must be measured at a comparable early stage, before differential survival distorts the denominator. In the hypothetical Y group 50 green and 10 mixed survive, so the late green fraction is 83.3%, despite unchanged initial proportions. The survival result is not evidence of paternal plastid transmission.

Assertion–reason and State SET-style application

48. Assertion–reason: An affected father does not normally pass a strictly maternally transmitted mtDNA variant to his daughter. Reason: Under this model, the daughter's persistent mtDNA originates from her egg parent.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. The reason describes the actual transmission rule and explains why neither sex of offspring normally receives the father's mtDNA variant.

49. Assertion–reason: A heteroplasmic mother must have identical mutant fractions in every child. Reason: Organelle genome sampling can alter fractions among lineages.

  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Both true
  3. Both false
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

A. Sampling and the germline bottleneck can produce differences among siblings, so the assertion is false.

50. Assertion–reason: A nuclear maternal-effect allele may pass through an unaffected paternal line. Reason: Its allele transmission and its phenotypic action through the egg parent are distinct.

  1. Both true, reason does not explain
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both true, reason explains assertion
Answer and explanation

D. The father can transmit a nuclear allele even though he does not supply the maternal gene product controlling this phenotype.

51. Which additional observation best distinguishes persistent paternal mtDNA from a nuclear mitochondrial DNA insertion?

  1. More unrelated nuclear markers alone
  2. A larger sperm cell
  3. A validated localization of paternal haplotype within mitochondria rather than nuclear DNA
  4. A more severe phenotype
Answer and explanation

C. Physical genomic compartment and sequence context distinguish transmitted mtDNA from a nuclear insertion that can mimic it.

52. A cell contains 100 mutant and 100 wild-type mtDNA copies. Another contains 100 mutant and 900 wild-type copies. What differs?

  1. Only mutant copy number
  2. The mutant fractions, 0.5 versus 0.1
  3. Both mutant copy number and h are identical
  4. Neither cell is heteroplasmic
Answer and explanation

B. The mutant counts are identical, but total counts differ, producing different mutant fractions.

53. A plant experiment detects 0 paternal-marker seedlings in 20 offspring. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. Paternal leakage is mathematically impossible
  2. The data support maternal inheritance at the assay's sampling and detection limits
  3. All pollen had no plastids
  4. Exactly 5% of paternal genomes were inherited
Answer and explanation

B. A small negative dataset supports but cannot prove universal absence; rare transmission and assay sensitivity remain relevant.

54. In a hypothetical Wolbachia cross, an infected father with an uninfected mother gives low embryo survival; reversing roles restores survival. What extra measurement is best to identify a symbiont mechanism?

  1. Direct infection status in matched crosses and appropriate removal/restoration controls
  2. Host chromosome count only
  3. Colour of the host's wings only
  4. mtDNA copy number alone
Answer and explanation

A. The pattern is compatible with incompatibility but identifying the symbiont requires infection-specific evidence and controlled comparisons.

55. For h=0.5 and n=10 independent units, what is the expected within-lineage diversity H' after one neutral sampling step if H=2h(1−h)?

  1. 0.50
  2. 0.05
  3. 0.10
  4. 0.45
Answer and explanation

D. H=0.5 and E(H')=H(1−1/n)=0.5×0.9=0.45; variation among lineages can increase at the same time.

56. Two reciprocal crosses differ in phenotype but have indistinguishable autosomal genotypes at a candidate locus. What should be concluded before further tests?

  1. Maternal organelle inheritance is proven
  2. Imprinting is excluded
  3. A reciprocal asymmetry exists, with several mechanisms still possible
  4. The phenotype is necessarily environmental
Answer and explanation

C. A genotype's parental origin, a maternal nuclear product, a cytoplasmic genome or environmental differences can produce this pattern.

57. A mutant plant has white plastids, but a functioning nuclear allele restores green colour in an otherwise matched cross; organelle sequences are unchanged. What inference is best?

  1. All white leaves are caused by plastid DNA mutations
  2. Plastids lack DNA
  3. Nuclear function can influence plastid phenotype; further causal tests are appropriate
  4. The nuclear allele is mitochondrially transmitted
Answer and explanation

C. The restoration shows that nuclear genotype matters for plastid function. It does not automatically locate every contributing variant.

Revision and mastery

Organelle genomes can segregate vegetatively, and their transmission through gametes depends on the species and organelle. Heteroplasmy measures genome-copy fractions, while the phenotype depends on cellular and tissue context. A nuclear maternal effect is a temporal separation between the genotype that supplies an egg product and the offspring phenotype. Reciprocal and multigeneration crosses help discriminate these mechanisms from imprinting and sex linkage.

Essential terminology

Heteroplasmy
Coexistence of distinct organelle genome variants in a defined sample.
Homoplasmy
Uniform sequence at the specified organelle locus in the sampled population.
Replicative segregation
Frequency changes among genome variants through replication and partitioning.
Bottleneck
Reduced effective number of transmitted genetic units, potentially increasing variance among lineages.
Maternal effect
Offspring phenotype influenced by the egg parent’s genotype and gene products.
Paternal leakage
Persistence of paternal organelle genomes in a normally predominantly maternal system.
