1. Beyond the chromosome: what makes DNA a replicon?

A bacterial cell may contain a large chromosome and several much smaller DNA molecules. When the cell divides, its chromosome must be copied because it carries genes required for ordinary cellular existence. The smaller molecules present a different problem. They may encode functions useful in a particular environment but absent from many other members of the species. Nevertheless, when a lineage retains such a molecule for hundreds of generations, its persistence cannot be explained merely by its initial presence. Every generation consumes some of the supply of molecules by distributing them between daughters. Continued inheritance therefore requires production of new copies and successful allocation of those copies. These two processes, replication and segregation, are the foundations of the biology of extrachromosomal replicons.

A replicon is a unit of DNA whose copying is controlled by an initiation system. Its boundaries are determined by the region copied from a functional origin under the relevant replication program. A small bacterial plasmid with one origin is a straightforward example: activating that origin normally results in copying the complete plasmid. A mammalian nuclear chromosome, in contrast, contains many origins and can be understood as containing numerous replication units. Two different meanings of independence must be kept separate. A plasmid can initiate replication from its own origin, independently of initiation at the bacterial chromosomal origin, yet still depend on bacterial polymerases, nucleotide metabolism and cellular energy. It is autonomous as an initiation unit, not autonomous as an organism. Even an element that encodes its own polymerase relies on an environment containing the precursors and conditions required by the enzyme.

The molecular distinction is between the replicator and the initiator. A replicator is a DNA sequence or collection of sequences necessary for regulated initiation. It acts in cis, meaning that its activity is associated with the molecule on which it resides. An initiator is a molecule, often a protein, that acts on the replicator. Many initiators can diffuse within the cell and therefore act in trans. Some origins instead depend on RNA molecules produced locally, and local synthesis can make a diffusible molecule function largely on the DNA from which it arose. An origin is more than an arbitrary stretch of sequence: it is a site where biochemical events convert intact parental DNA into a substrate that can accept new nucleotides. The distinction predicts an important genetic result. A defective initiator gene may be complemented by a functional gene elsewhere, whereas deleting the only required origin usually cannot be rescued just by placing another origin on an unrelated molecule.

Consider two otherwise identical circular DNA molecules. One has a functional origin and an appropriate initiator; the other lacks its origin. Both may initially be detected inside a cell. At the next division, either molecule may be inherited by a daughter. After repeated divisions, however, the nonreplicating molecule is diluted out of the expanding lineage, even if no enzyme destroys it. If the original molecule is integrated into a chromosome, chromosomal replication can preserve the inserted sequence without requiring the original plasmid origin. In such a case the sequence persists, but not as an independently copying plasmid. This distinction is the reason a genetic marker alone is insufficient evidence of plasmid replication. The physical form of the DNA and the source of its copying must also be established.

A plasmid is typically a separately maintained genetic element, most often double-stranded DNA in bacteria. Plasmids are frequently circular, but linear plasmids also occur. Their size ranges widely, and the distinction between a very large plasmid and a secondary chromosome can become biologically complicated. The usual textbook contrast is functional: the primary chromosome carries a large complement of core genes, while a plasmid often carries accessory genes and has an independent maintenance system. These are useful tendencies rather than universal definitions. Some secondary replicons contain essential functions, and certain plasmids become indispensable to a host under particular conditions. Neither length nor circular geometry alone provides a reliable classification.

Extrachromosomal DNA is not restricted to bacterial cytoplasm. Eukaryotic cells also maintain mitochondrial DNA, and photosynthetic eukaryotes maintain plastid DNA. These are organellar genomes whose location outside the nucleus distinguishes them from nuclear chromosomes. They should not be treated as merely large bacterial plasmids: they constitute part of the hereditary system of their host cell and encode selected organellar functions. Viral DNA may also exist as a separate replicating molecule inside a host, although viruses depend on infection or another association with cells rather than ordinary division as independent cells. In each case the question is not simply where DNA is located but how its own copying is initiated, executed and coordinated with the biological system that contains it.

It is useful to distinguish stable replicons from extrachromosomal DNA fragments. A fragment produced by breakage, an excised DNA segment without an active origin, or a newly entered DNA molecule that fails to establish replication may be extrachromosomal in location without being a self-maintaining replicon. Conversely, the same sequence may be an autonomous replicon in one state and part of a chromosome in another state. An experiment must therefore distinguish the presence of a molecule from its persistence and persistence from demonstrable independent initiation. This prevents a common error in interpreting a short observation period as proof of long-term maintenance.

Historically, the vocabulary emerged from genetic observations in bacteria. Joshua Lederberg introduced the term plasmid in 1952, before modern molecular characterization of the many elements now placed in that category. Jacob, Brenner and Cuzin subsequently articulated the replicon model, separating a genetic element's ability to serve as a substrate for replication initiation from the factors that trigger that event. These ideas were powerful because they converted inheritance patterns into testable molecular predictions. Genetic observations that independently inherited elements could be retained or lost became questions about which DNA sequences were required for initiation and which gene products controlled their activity. The modern plasmid concept and its terminology developed through further genetic and biochemical evidence rather than appearing fully formed in a single experiment.

Three experimental outcomes are especially informative. First, a DNA element might remain present during proliferation as a molecule of its original size and structure. This supports stable maintenance but does not by itself identify its initiation mechanism. Second, an origin alteration might prevent DNA synthesis while a control alteration elsewhere has no effect. This localizes a required sequence, provided both molecules have comparable initial entry and detection. Third, an initiator defect might be rescued by functional initiator supplied separately. This supports a role for a trans-acting product, although rescue must be interpreted in light of any effects on host growth. The logic of combining physical evidence, sequence-specific perturbation and functional complementation will recur throughout the chapter.

2. Plasmid architecture and the requirements for stable maintenance

A simple map of a plasmid divides its sequence into regions with different functions. At minimum, a replicating plasmid must possess a way to begin DNA synthesis and obtain the machinery that completes it. A regulatory region determines how often new initiation events occur. Additional regions may promote partition, resolve multimers or encode functions that alter the host phenotype. Such maps are circular in many familiar illustrations, but the map represents sequence order and does not necessarily describe the physical conformation of the molecule in a living cell. A tightly supercoiled circular DNA molecule may appear compact rather than ring-shaped; the circular map preserves the fact that the end of the sequence joins its beginning.

The origin of vegetative replication, often denoted oriV, must be distinguished from the origin of transfer, oriT, found in transferable plasmids. The former participates in copying DNA for intracellular maintenance. The latter is recognized during DNA transfer between cells. A transferable molecule can undergo strand cleavage and replacement synthesis associated with transfer, but the presence of oriT does not by itself ensure ordinary vegetative replication in a recipient. This distinction explains why transfer competence, replication competence and stable inheritance are separable properties. A plasmid may replicate without transferring, may require other functions to transfer, or may fail to persist after arriving in a host whose replication machinery is incompatible with its origin system.

Many plasmid origins contain binding sites for an initiation protein encoded by the plasmid. The protein recognizes particular bases or DNA structures rather than binding to every position with equal probability. Repeated binding sites, called iterons in several plasmid systems, may help assemble an initiation complex and also participate in copy-number regulation. Other sequences provide a region that can be opened, enabling priming. Because base stacking, local sequence, supercoiling and protein binding all influence opening, it is inadequate to explain origin activation solely by counting hydrogen bonds. AT-rich regions often facilitate opening, but the complete origin is a coordinated recognition and assembly site, not merely any sequence rich in adenine and thymine.

Not every plasmid encodes a dedicated initiator protein. ColE1-family replicons illustrate initiation through a locally transcribed RNA precursor that becomes a primer for DNA synthesis. This immediately reveals why the term origin should not be confused with a promoter. A promoter directs transcription initiation; an origin directs DNA replication initiation. A transcription event can be an essential step at a replication origin, but not every promoter produces an RNA capable of establishing DNA replication. The fate of the RNA depends on its sequence, its interaction with the DNA template and its processing. The specificity of an RNA-dependent origin lies in this chain of events.

The energy for polymerization is provided by the incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates. Their incorporation releases pyrophosphate, and cellular pyrophosphate hydrolysis favors the overall forward reaction. DNA polymerases extend a pre-existing hydroxyl group at the growing 3′ end, making the new strand in the 5′ to 3′ direction. A replisome may additionally require ATP-dependent helicase activity, nucleotide-dependent clamp loading, topological relaxation and primer processing. Consequently, the origin is necessary but not sufficient. A correct initiation event cannot yield a complete daughter molecule if elongation is blocked or if the necessary precursors are unavailable. This is why a replication defect may arise from a host mutation even when all plasmid DNA sequences remain intact.

Host range is the set of hosts in which an element can be maintained under specified conditions. It reflects multiple barriers rather than a single recognition reaction. A plasmid may encode its own initiator but still require a host helicase, polymerase or transcriptional system capable of interacting with that initiation complex. It must also operate in the host's physiological environment and avoid rapid loss. The inability to detect a plasmid after many generations in a new host may therefore reflect failure of initiation, defective elongation, unstable segregation, or reduced survival of plasmid-containing cells. Determining which mechanism is responsible requires observations of replication intermediates and lineage behavior, rather than a single final count of cells carrying a marker.

Plasmid maintenance differs from the synthesis of an RNA transcript or a protein. The product of a transcription event is normally not a new copy of the DNA sequence that directs subsequent transcription. A successfully replicated plasmid, however, contributes additional origin-containing molecules to the very population that can initiate future replication. Without regulation, this positive feedback could produce disproportionate accumulation of DNA and impose a cost on the host. Conversely, if initiation is too infrequent relative to cellular proliferation, copy number falls. Stable plasmids solve the problem by coupling initiation probability to the abundance of molecules or to other features of cellular growth. Copy-number control is thus a feedback problem, not just an enzyme-speed problem.

Segregation is distinct from copying because DNA molecules do not automatically occupy different future daughter cells simply by being synthesized. Replicated circular duplexes may remain topologically linked or physically clustered. Topoisomerases can separate linked molecules, recombination-associated resolution systems can convert multimers into independently segregating units, and partition systems can position copies. A plasmid with a high abundance of separate copies can tolerate imperfect positioning more readily than one with only a few copies, but actual intracellular distributions are not necessarily random. Experimental interpretation must distinguish the number of origin sequences, the number of full genome equivalents and the number of physical particles that can independently segregate.

3. Theta replication: opening a duplex and copying around a circle

The first major strategy used by circular double-stranded replicons is theta replication. The name refers to an intermediate whose outline can resemble the Greek letter theta: an unreplicated part of the parental circle connects two junctions that are also connected by the two growing daughter duplexes. It is not a special chemical reaction different from ordinary DNA polymerization. Instead, it is a geometric arrangement of template exposure and fork progression. A student who understands the architecture of a bacterial chromosome replication fork already possesses most of the molecular vocabulary required to understand this mechanism.

Imagine an intact closed circular duplex with one origin. An initiation complex recognizes the origin and destabilizes a short region of double-stranded DNA. The separation creates two single-stranded templates with opposite orientations. In a conventional bidirectional theta system, two replication forks are established at the edges of the origin region and move away from one another along opposite arcs of the circle. Helicases enlarge the accessible region of template, single-strand binding proteins reduce reannealing and inappropriate secondary structures, primases generate suitable primers, and polymerases extend the resulting 3′ ends. The detailed proteins vary with the plasmid and host, but the direction of nucleotide addition is conserved.

At either fork, the parental strand oriented 3′ to 5′ in the direction of movement permits leading-strand synthesis in the same overall direction as that fork. The opposite parental strand requires lagging-strand synthesis in segments. A primer is placed near the newly exposed region, extended away from the fork, and later replaced or processed so that adjacent DNA fragments can be joined. This coordination explains an apparent puzzle: both daughter strands become complete even though polymerases share a single chemical direction of synthesis. It is the orientation of templates and the arrangement of initiation events, not reversal of polymerase chemistry, that accommodates antiparallel DNA.

At a pair of bidirectional forks, a parental strand that serves as the leading-strand template at one fork serves as the lagging-strand template at the other. To verify this, draw one parental strand as clockwise 5′ to 3′. A fork moving clockwise encounters that strand in the wrong orientation for continuous 5′ to 3′ daughter synthesis, while the fork moving anticlockwise encounters the same strand in the 3′ to 5′ direction. The two forks thus use opposite leading templates. This relationship is a valuable exam strategy: label template polarity first, then infer synthesis directions, rather than trying to memorize which side of a diagram is the leading strand.

Some theta replicons use only one progressing fork. The origin may produce a replication bubble with one boundary that remains near the initiation region while the other advances around the molecule. There are still duplex replication intermediates rather than a long displaced tail of single-stranded DNA. The term theta therefore does not mean that every origin launches two active forks. Directionality must be inferred from evidence about the positions and movement of active junctions. One cannot simply count the two boundaries of a DNA bubble and conclude that both move at the same speed.

Because parental strands are intertwined, moving a replication fork around a covalently closed circle changes topological constraints. Without strand passage or an equivalent relaxation mechanism, separation at one position tends to create overwinding ahead of the fork or intertwining behind it. Topoisomerases resolve these constraints by transiently breaking and rejoining DNA strands. Type I topoisomerases act through transient single-strand cleavage, whereas type II enzymes pass a duplex through a transient double-strand break and can resolve DNA catenanes. In bacterial systems, DNA gyrase is important for controlling supercoiling, and topoisomerase IV is especially important for decatenation. The contribution of each enzyme depends on the particular organism and replication system.

As replication approaches completion, the remaining unreplicated arc becomes shorter. In a bidirectional system the two forks converge; in a unidirectional system the advancing fork returns to the completion region. Processing of residual primers, sealing of nicks and physical separation of daughter duplexes complete the production of two closed circles. Each daughter duplex normally contains one parental and one newly synthesized strand. The daughter circles may still need to be disentangled if they are catenated. Replication completion is therefore not identical to physical separation. An experiment that counts completed DNA sequence copies but ignores topology could report a successful replication event even when inheritance remains impaired.

The distinction between DNA geometry and the molecular history of its strands is fundamental. A theta intermediate can be drawn as three arms between two branch points. Counting visible arms gives information about structure, not necessarily about whether all arms are old or new. Each replicated duplex consists of an old template paired with a newly made strand; the unreplicated duplex contains both old strands. Under the idealized situation in which half of a circular genome has been copied, the total amount of DNA in the intermediate is one and a half genome equivalents, not two. There is still only one connected replicating structure until termination and resolution. A two-dimensional image must therefore be interpreted with a distinction between molecule count, mass and sequence copy count.

Classic evidence that helped establish this way of thinking came from John Cairns's 1963 autoradiographic study of bacterial chromosomes. Radiolabel incorporated into DNA made long DNA molecules visible indirectly through photographic tracks. The observed circular and branched patterns supported a model in which a circle was copied progressively rather than broken into many independently duplicated pieces. This evidence concerned a bacterial chromosome, not every plasmid, but it supplied a physical language in which related circular replication intermediates could be recognized. The presence of an intermediate is not by itself a measurement of how rapidly its forks move. Comparing stages or differently labeled newly synthesized regions is required to infer direction and kinetics.

A direct contrast with a rolling circle is possible before learning the latter in detail. If one observes a circular duplex with an internal duplicated arc and no extended displaced tail, theta replication is a plausible interpretation. If one observes a circle with a long extending strand or duplex tail, a displacement-based mechanism becomes plausible. However, branch structures can arise from recombination, incomplete processing or molecule breakage. A reliable conclusion combines topology with strand composition, origin dependence and the time order of intermediates. A single attractive drawing is never sufficient evidence to identify a mechanism in an unfamiliar system.

4. Distinct routes into theta replication

Although theta replication has a recognizable geometry, its origins can establish replication through different molecular routes. One useful comparison is between a protein-dependent origin such as that of many iteron-controlled plasmids and an RNA-dependent origin represented by ColE1. Both ultimately require DNA synthesis from an accessible 3′ hydroxyl group, yet they create that substrate differently. This comparison shows why the geometry of an elongating intermediate should not be used to infer the identity of the initiation factor.

For a representative protein-dependent theta plasmid, origin-binding protein accumulates in the cell, binds specific origin elements and promotes local DNA opening with assistance from host factors. A host helicase can then be recruited, primers generated and the replicative polymerase assembled. Iterons provide a recognizable platform for origin binding, while an adjacent unwinding region supports exposure of template. The precise order and role of host proteins differ among plasmid families. It is therefore safer to learn the common logic, recognition, opening, priming and fork assembly, rather than attribute the exact bacterial chromosomal oriC mechanism to every plasmid.

In the ColE1 family, transcription from a particular promoter produces RNA II, which can interact with the DNA template at the replication origin. A stable RNA–DNA hybrid is formed as the RNA anneals to its complementary DNA strand. RNase H participates in processing the RNA to form a usable primer terminus. DNA polymerase I extends that primer, and host machinery subsequently establishes continued replication. The striking feature is that a dedicated plasmid-encoded Rep initiation protein is not required for the canonical ColE1 system. This is a clear exception to a statement that is otherwise tempting in textbook summaries: plasmid initiation is often controlled by a Rep protein, but it is not universally dependent on one.

The initial RNA–DNA hybrid is sometimes called an R-loop. In this structural arrangement the RNA pairs with one DNA strand while the other DNA strand is displaced locally. The hybrid stabilizes a particular arrangement near the origin, allowing RNA processing to generate the substrate for DNA polymerase. An ordinary messenger RNA generally separates from the DNA template and does not serve this role. RNA II must form the correct structure and persist in the correct location. Therefore, transcription alone is not proof that replication initiation has occurred. The experimental distinction would require detection of the processed primer or its conversion into an extending DNA product.

RNA I provides negative regulation in ColE1-type systems. It is a short antisense RNA whose sequence is complementary to a region of RNA II. Interaction between the two RNAs interferes with the conformational pathway required for RNA II to form the productive RNA–DNA hybrid. In simple terms, RNA I intercepts the primer precursor before it reaches the state that supports replication. When plasmid abundance rises, the cell produces more copies of RNA I, increasing the likelihood of such inhibition and reducing the average initiation probability per plasmid. The Rop or Rom protein, where present, facilitates the inhibitory RNA interaction. Neither RNA I nor Rop should be described as a DNA polymerase inhibitor acting at every replication fork; the central control is directed at formation of a competent initiation substrate.

This regulatory system predicts different effects for changes in different regions. If RNA II loses the ability to establish an origin hybrid, successful initiations decline even if RNA I is absent. If RNA I is produced normally but can no longer recognize the appropriate RNA II structure, inhibition may weaken and copy number may increase, assuming the rest of the origin remains functional. A defective host primer-processing activity may reduce productive initiation despite normal transcription. The direction of the phenotype depends on which biochemical step fails. One should not interpret every mutation near an origin as a simple reduction in replication: removing a negative regulatory element can have the opposite effect from damaging the priming element itself.

The genetic analysis of ColE1 copy-number variants provides a way to separate feedback from basal priming. Suppose a mutation changes an antisense RNA without affecting the complementary origin sequence, and another mutation changes the target RNA region. If the first change reduces inhibition of the original target, while a compensatory target change restores inhibition, the sequence specificity of the interaction is supported. Such compensation is more informative than the observation that an altered plasmid simply reaches a high abundance. A high abundance could also result from changed degradation, host growth or segregation. Measurements of RNA interaction and initiation intermediates help connect the genetic effect to a direct molecular mechanism.

In iteron-containing plasmids the feedback mechanism can involve limiting availability of active initiator, origin titration or inhibitory pairing of origin complexes, sometimes termed handcuffing. In a titration model, a finite pool of active initiator is distributed among many binding sites, reducing the free fraction as origin abundance increases. In a handcuffing model, interactions between protein-bound origin regions can reduce productive initiation. Different systems use different combinations of autoregulation, active-state switching and origin interactions. A diagram showing origin pairing should therefore be treated as a model for appropriate examples, not a universal mechanism shared by all theta plasmids.

In both regulatory examples, increasing the number of replicons changes the probability of future replication. The controlled variable is average initiation activity, not necessarily the rate at which polymerase adds nucleotides once a fork is established. This distinction matters in experiments. If the duration of replication of each individual molecule is unchanged but the number of initiation events decreases, an initiation-control model is supported. If initiation frequency is unchanged but completion takes longer, altered elongation or resource availability becomes more plausible. An observed fall in final copy number can arise from either mechanism, making time-resolved measurements essential for causal interpretation.

The naturally occurring 2-micron plasmid of budding yeast is a useful eukaryotic comparison. This nuclear circular DNA uses a replication origin recognized by the yeast replication machinery, and ordinary steady-state copying occurs during S phase. Plasmid-encoded Rep proteins and a partition locus contribute to stable segregation, while a recombination-dependent amplification system can help restore copy abundance after loss. Thus regulation of a separate DNA element can be coordinated with the nuclear cell cycle without integrating the element into a chromosome. This also illustrates why high physical copy abundance and active partition are not mutually exclusive. The detailed recombinase reaction is developed later in the site-specific recombination chapter.

5. Rolling-circle replication: nicking and strand displacement

The second principal geometry of circular replication begins in a different way. Instead of establishing a conventional replication bubble by opening a duplex and placing a new primer on an exposed template, a rolling-circle system can cleave one strand at a defined site. The intact complementary strand remains circular and serves as a continuous template. Cleavage creates a free hydroxyl group at the 3′ end of the nicked strand. DNA polymerase extends that end, copying the intact circle while displacing the downstream portion of the nicked strand. The parental strand that is displaced can become a template for synthesis of its complementary strand. This approach provides a way to generate both unit-length products and, in suitable systems, longer products containing repeated genome sequences.

It is important to understand the geometry of the nick. It is a break in one phosphodiester backbone, not a double-strand break. The uncut strand preserves the circular continuity of the template and the nicked strand has two chemically distinguishable ends. In many rolling-circle plasmids a plasmid-encoded Rep protein recognizes a double-strand origin, abbreviated dso, and a catalytic tyrosine participates in cleavage. The reaction produces a free 3′-OH and a covalent linkage between the protein and the 5′ phosphate of the cleaved strand. The details differ among initiator families, but the two products of cleavage explain the asymmetry that distinguishes this mode of replication.

At the initiation stage the origin DNA may adopt a locally distorted or partly single-stranded configuration that permits recognition and cleavage. The nick is positioned so that synthesis proceeds around the intact circular template. Host DNA polymerase extends the 3′-OH, with helicase and single-stranded DNA-binding activities assisting the displacement and stabilization of the outgoing strand in representative systems. Because the growing DNA replaces material that previously paired with the intact template, an increasing length of the original nicked strand becomes exposed. The 5′ end of this displaced region is the end produced during initiation. The 3′ end being extended remains at the moving junction. This distinction prevents confusing the displaced parental tail with the growing daughter strand.

One can visualize the process by imagining the sequence of the intact circle as positions 1 to L. After initiation, polymerase copies positions sequentially around the ring. When it reaches the original origin region again, a complete genome length has been synthesized. For a unit-length rolling-circle plasmid, termination requires recognition of the regenerated origin and appropriate cleavage and joining reactions. These yield a double-stranded circular product and a displaced single-stranded circular product, each representing a genome unit in the simplified model. The displaced circle must acquire a complementary strand before it becomes an ordinary duplex plasmid. Some Rep proteins participate in both initiation and termination, explaining why a mutation may affect cleavage at the beginning, closure at the end, or both.

The origin for synthesis of the complementary strand is often distinct from the double-strand origin. In many rolling-circle plasmids a single-strand origin, abbreviated sso, contains sequences that form a structure when exposed as single-stranded DNA. Host machinery can recognize this configuration and generate a primer for second-strand synthesis. The relevant polymerase then extends the primer around the single-stranded circle. RNA primer processing and DNA ligation convert the product to a completed duplex. The term single-strand origin refers to the state of the DNA substrate on which this second phase starts; it does not mean that ordinary plasmid replication creates DNA from an RNA genome.

The separation between first-strand and complementary-strand synthesis has major experimental consequences. Suppose a hypothetical alteration leaves double-strand-origin recognition and nicking intact but prevents efficient use of the single-strand origin. First-strand copying and displacement can still occur, so newly synthesized DNA may initially be detectable. The displaced single-stranded molecules, however, may accumulate because conversion to duplex products is inefficient. Stable plasmid abundance can fall despite continuing first-strand synthesis. A measurement of total nucleotide incorporation could underestimate the severity of this defect because it does not distinguish productive genome completion from synthesis of incomplete intermediates.

A different outcome is expected if the cleavage function of the initiator fails. The free 3′-OH that normally starts rolling-circle synthesis is not generated, so the defect acts before displacement. If the nick is made normally but the initiation protein cannot support proper termination, extended or abnormal products may accumulate. If polymerase is inhibited after cleavage, nicked molecules may exist without long products. These are separate phenotypes. They show why experiments that only score the presence or absence of a DNA marker cannot localize the defect. An analysis must distinguish cleavage, extension, displacement, termination and second-strand completion.

The common diagram of rolling-circle replication shows a circular structure with a long tail, sometimes compared with the Greek letter sigma. The tail can initially be single-stranded and may become double-stranded if complementary synthesis accompanies or follows displacement. In phage systems in which the machinery continues around the circle repeatedly, the tail can contain multiple head-to-tail genome copies. A DNA molecule of this kind is called a concatemer. Concatemers and plasmid multimers can have similar sequence repetition, but their biological origins need not be the same. A concatemer produced by ongoing rolling-circle synthesis is an elongating replication product, whereas a circular dimer produced through recombination between plasmid molecules is a distinct structural outcome.

The chemistry does not violate semiconservative replication. In the simplified plasmid case, one original DNA strand remains in the circle and serves as the template for a new complementary strand. The other original strand is displaced, circularized and copied. After second-strand synthesis the two duplex products each possess an original strand and a newly synthesized complement. What differs from theta replication is the timing and arrangement of synthesis. In a continuously elongating phage system the strand histories of individual repeated genome units become more complex, so it is better to trace a defined stretch of template than to assume every packaged genome contains one complete original parental strand.

Rolling-circle replication should also be distinguished from strand displacement as a broader phenomenon. DNA polymerase may synthesize a new strand while displacing a pre-existing strand in several replication and repair settings. Mammalian mitochondrial replication, for example, can proceed by an asynchronous strand-displacement mechanism that starts from an RNA-derived primer rather than the characteristic nick of a rolling-circle plasmid. Thus the presence of displaced DNA is not sufficient to identify rolling-circle initiation. The defining combination in the textbook plasmid example is a circular template, origin-specific cleavage producing a primer end, extension with displacement and appropriate termination or repeated copying.

Some bacterial plasmids from Gram-positive species provide established rolling-circle examples, while certain small single-stranded DNA bacteriophages also use a rolling-circle process at part of their replication cycle. The mechanism is economical because a nick in a pre-existing DNA strand supplies a primer end, but the economy of initiation does not remove the need for second-strand synthesis, topological management or host enzymes. Single-stranded intermediates can form secondary structures and may be vulnerable to nucleases. Efficient stabilization and complementary-strand conversion are consequently important components of the biological strategy, not optional details.

6. Copy number as a regulated population of molecules

Copy number ordinarily means the number of copies of a replicon in a specified cellular unit, but that unit must be made explicit. It may mean the number per cell, per chromosome equivalent, per nucleus or per organelle. These denominators are not interchangeable. A cell undergoing multifork chromosomal replication may contain more than one copy of a chromosomal reference locus. A culture may contain cells of different sizes, and the same average plasmid-to-chromosome ratio can arise from different distributions among cells. When an exam question gives a copy number without qualification, inspect its measurement definition before using the number in a calculation.

A plasmid is frequently described as low-copy or high-copy. These terms refer to an approximate range of abundance under particular conditions, not immutable numbers built into the DNA. The F plasmid is a familiar example of a low-copy maintenance strategy; ColE1-family replicons provide examples of higher copy abundance. Copy number can change with origin sequence, regulatory mutations, host physiology and growth conditions. It can also fluctuate among individual cells. The biologically important distinction is that a small number of molecules makes stochastic loss during segregation potentially serious, whereas many independent copies provide greater redundancy. High abundance may also increase the cost of maintaining DNA and expressing its genes.

Begin with a simple deterministic model. Let N be the mean number of intact plasmid genome equivalents per cell at a standardized cell-cycle stage. Suppose every copy present at that stage generates an average of r additional completed copies before division. Neglect DNA degradation and assume symmetric division. Immediately before division the expected total is N(1+r). The expected number in each daughter is then N(1+r)/2. For a stable mean across generations, N(1+r)/2 must equal N. For nonzero N, cancellation yields r=1. This does not mean each individual plasmid must replicate exactly once. One copy may replicate more than once while another fails to initiate, so long as the average rate of completed synthesis balances dilution in the stated model.

If r remains constant at 0.8 in that model, the mean decreases by a factor of 0.9 each generation. Starting from 20 genome equivalents per cell, the expected abundance after five divisions is 20 times 0.9 raised to the fifth power, or approximately 11.81. If r=1.2, the mean increases by a factor of 1.1 and reaches approximately 32.21 after five divisions. These are hypothetical population averages, not predictions for an exact individual cell. An individual may contain twelve or thirty-two molecules, but cannot contain a fractional physical molecule. Fractional averages summarize variation across a population. In real systems feedback changes r as N changes, so constant-r extrapolation is only a teaching approximation over a limited range.

The need for feedback becomes clearer if one imagines two daughter cells that receive different numbers of plasmids. If all plasmids replicate at the same fixed rate regardless of abundance, the relative difference between the two lineages may persist, and fluctuations can eventually generate cells that carry none. Negative feedback can correct deviations: a cell with fewer copies may permit a higher probability of initiation per copy, while a cell with too many may inhibit future initiation. This regulation does not replace partition because a daughter with zero copies lacks the template required for correction. Feedback is a recovery mechanism for nonzero deviations; partition and redundancy help prevent complete loss in the first place.

Consider a continuous approximation in which n(t) is the average number of genome equivalents per cell, k(n) is the per-copy net synthesis rate in inverse time, and mu is the cellular growth rate in inverse time. Assume balanced growth, negligible degradation and enough cells for a smooth average. An equation describing the competition between replication and dilution is dn/dt = [k(n) − mu]n. At a positive steady state n*, k(n*) must equal mu. If k(n) decreases as n increases, a small upward perturbation makes synthesis slower than dilution, and a small downward perturbation makes synthesis faster. The equality of rates establishes the equilibrium; their dependence on abundance determines whether the equilibrium is stable.

A numerical illustration uses a purely hypothetical linear feedback relation k(n)=0.60−0.02n per hour within the local range of interest. If the cellular growth rate is 0.30 per hour, the positive steady abundance is found from 0.60−0.02n*=0.30, yielding n*=15. At n=10, k=0.40 per hour, so dn/dt is positive; at n=20, k=0.20 per hour, so it is negative. The coefficient 0.02 has units of inverse hour per genome equivalent per cell in this model. Extrapolating the equation to arbitrarily large n would predict a negative synthesis rate, which is biologically inappropriate. This reveals a limitation of the linear approximation rather than a new biological mechanism. The model is useful near the operating range and must be replaced or bounded elsewhere.

Increased copy abundance has consequences beyond DNA replication. Each additional genome consumes nucleotides and polymerase time, and transcription from additional gene copies can consume RNA synthesis and translation resources. A host carrying an expensive replicon may divide more slowly than a host that lacks it. A population-level decrease in plasmid frequency could therefore occur even when replication and segregation function reasonably well, if plasmid-free lineages expand more rapidly. Conversely, a plasmid that supplies a useful function in a given environment may increase the relative growth of cells carrying it. The molecular processes of replication and segregation must be distinguished from selection acting on whole cells.

Gene dosage offers an example of both benefit and cost. If each plasmid molecule carries one copy of a particular gene, a cell with twenty plasmid copies has twenty copies of that gene, subject to completion and structural integrity. It does not follow that it produces exactly twenty times as much functional protein as a cell with one gene copy. Promoters can differ in activity, transcriptional resources may be limiting, RNA and protein degradation affect abundance, and some proteins are assembled into complexes whose other subunits become limiting. The simplest proportional relationship holds only if expression per gene remains constant and downstream resources are not saturated. In Part C reasoning, gene copy number is a potential explanatory variable, not a direct substitute for gene expression.

A feedback model also explains why changes in replication origin and maintenance genes can have distinct time courses. If initiation is reduced, the cellular DNA concentration may decline through dilution even though all existing copies remain structurally intact. If partition is disrupted, the mean abundance among plasmid-bearing cells might remain high while the fraction of cells carrying the plasmid decreases. If host growth changes, plasmid abundance per cell can change because the dilution demand changes. Monitoring abundance both per total population and per retaining cell, while separately measuring cell growth, helps discriminate these possibilities. A single population average is particularly misleading when a substantial fraction of cells has already lost the replicon.

7. Segregation, copy redundancy and the probability of loss

Even perfect chemical copying would be insufficient if all daughter molecules remained in the same half of a dividing cell. Segregation addresses the spatial side of inheritance. In low-copy plasmids, active partition systems commonly include a centromere-like DNA site, a protein that binds that site and an energy-dependent positioning component. In a representative type I partition system, ParB recognizes a parS region and works with the ATPase ParA to help distribute copies. Type II systems provide another example in which an actin-like ATPase can generate a filament-based mechanism. The essential lesson is that position and movement can be regulated independently of the chemistry of nucleotide polymerization.

Terminology can create confusion here because bacterial chromosomal segregation and plasmid partition often use related protein families. A plasmid parS site is a recognition sequence involved in spatial inheritance, not necessarily the site where DNA replication initiates. Deleting a partition site can allow copies to be synthesized but impair their reliable allocation. Conversely, deleting the replication origin can abolish production of new copies while leaving a partition site structurally intact. A double phenotype involving both reduced abundance and reduced inheritance does not prove that replication and partition are the same molecular process. The two processes must be assayed separately.

To understand why abundance matters, consider a simplified random segregation model. Let N be the number of distinct, freely segregating plasmid molecules present immediately before cell division. Assume each molecule independently enters the left or right daughter with probability one-half, there is no active positioning, and neither daughter receives new DNA during the partition event. The probability that a specified daughter receives no plasmid is the product of N probabilities of one-half, or (1/2)^N. For N greater than zero, the outcomes in which all copies enter the left daughter or all enter the right daughter are mutually exclusive. Consequently, the probability that either daughter is plasmid-free is 2(1/2)^N, equal to 2^(1−N). These formulas have different interpretations and should not be interchanged.

For two independently segregating copies, the probability that a specified daughter receives none is one-quarter, while the probability that the division produces one empty daughter is one-half. For ten copies, the probabilities are 1/1024 and 1/512, respectively. A population in which all cells contain ten independent copies at the time of division is thus much less vulnerable to random loss than a population with two. However, the model excludes plasmid clustering, active segregation, degradation and growth differences. It establishes a baseline against which observed losses can be compared; it is not a claim that every biological plasmid undergoes independent Bernoulli allocation.

The expected copy number in either daughter in this model is N/2. The variance of the binomial distribution is N/4. The standard deviation is therefore the square root of N divided by two, and the relative standard deviation, defined as the standard deviation divided by the mean, is 1/sqrt(N). For N=4, the expected daughter count is two and the standard deviation is one, giving a relative standard deviation of 50 percent. For N=100, the expected count is fifty and the standard deviation is five, giving ten percent. Absolute fluctuation increases with N, but relative fluctuation decreases. This distinction explains why high-copy redundancy can stabilize relative abundance even when physical counts vary.

A multimer illustrates a limitation of treating genome equivalents as independent molecules. Imagine a cell containing eight plasmid genome equivalents. If all eight are separate circles, random segregation predicts a probability of 1/256 that a specified daughter receives none. If the same eight genome equivalents are organized as two physical particles, each carrying four genome units and segregating as a whole, the corresponding probability becomes 1/4 under the simplified model. A measurement that counts origin sequences would report eight copies in both cases, yet the inheritance predictions differ by a factor of sixty-four. Resolution systems that separate multimeric molecules can therefore improve stability without increasing the number of DNA bases synthesized.

One can extend the reasoning to a heterogeneous population. If half of dividing cells have two independent copies and half have eight, the mean predivision copy count is five. The average probability that a specified daughter receives no copies is one-half times 1/4 plus one-half times 1/256, approximately 0.12695. Substituting the mean N=5 into the random model instead gives 1/32, or 0.03125. The difference is substantial because the loss probability is a nonlinear function of copy number. Cells with few copies contribute disproportionately to loss. An exam question giving a population distribution requires averaging the probabilities, not averaging the copy counts and then applying the formula.

A further simple model considers retention over multiple generations. If a retaining lineage has a constant probability q of producing a plasmid-free randomly selected daughter, and plasmid-free cells never regain the plasmid, the fraction remaining after g generations along randomly followed daughter lineages is (1−q)^g. This assumes the loss probability does not change and there is no selection or reacquisition. For q=0.02 and g=20 the fraction is approximately 0.668, so about two-thirds retain the replicon in this idealized lineage model. A measured final percentage cannot generally establish a molecular loss rate unless the assumptions about growth and reacquisition are justified. If plasmid-free cells divide faster, the population fraction can decrease more rapidly than the simple lineage calculation predicts.

Active partition changes the distribution of copies, rather than guaranteeing that every chemical replication event succeeds. A hypothetical cell that starts division with two copies can be compared with a partition-defective cell containing the same two copies. Under random allocation the chance that both daughters obtain a copy is one-half. An active positioning system can raise that probability by placing the copies in different prospective daughter compartments. An appropriate experiment would compare daughter-cell inheritance at matched predivision copy counts, rather than compare two strains with unknown or different abundance. This design isolates spatial distribution from the compensating effect of additional molecules.

Some plasmids also have maintenance systems that alter the survival or growth of cells that lose the element. Such systems affect the observed prevalence of plasmid-bearing cells but are not themselves DNA replication or physical segregation mechanisms. In interpreting a population assay, loss of a replicon and subsequent survival of the plasmid-free daughter are two separate events. Counting only surviving cells can hide the actual frequency of segregation failure. This is why lineage observation, measurements of DNA abundance and population growth should be distinguished conceptually. The three measurements answer complementary questions about a replicon's stability.

8. Plasmid incompatibility and competition for maintenance

Two different plasmids may enter the same cell and both begin replication, yet fail to remain together stably over many generations. Plasmid incompatibility describes such a failure of stable coexistence under conditions where the individual elements can be maintained. It often arises because the plasmids share a replication-control system or a partition system that cannot reliably distinguish one from the other. The defining phenomenon concerns inheritance of two replicons together, not a direct chemical attack on one plasmid by another.

Imagine two versions of a replicon, A and B, each carrying a different neutral identifying sequence but using the same origin regulation. A copy-number controller responds to the total abundance of A plus B because it recognizes a common regulatory signal. If the cell contains seven A molecules and three B molecules and the total target abundance is ten, feedback may respond as if the system is perfectly balanced. Yet the controller does not detect that B is becoming rare. Random differences in initiation and segregation can further reduce the B fraction, eventually producing a cell containing only A. The total copy number may remain at its usual level throughout this process. The controller maintains the combined pool, not the identity of each participating plasmid type.

Replication incompatibility can therefore be understood as a lack of independent feedback channels. If two plasmids use different sufficiently independent regulatory systems, each can respond to its own abundance. A decline in B may stimulate B replication without necessarily stimulating A. In the shared system, by contrast, an increase in A can increase the common inhibitor and suppress the initiation of both types. Even if both replicons are chemically capable of copying, the minority type receives no preferential correction. Under persistent random fluctuations the two identities may drift, while total copy abundance remains regulated. This is an important example in which a stable aggregate masks an unstable composition.

In ColE1-type systems, cross-recognition of antisense RNA and the corresponding primer precursor can provide a molecular basis for shared regulation. If RNA I produced by either element interferes with productive RNA II formation on both, the inhibitory signal depends on the combined abundance. Alterations that change recognition specificity can affect incompatibility and copy control together, although the relationship depends on the precise mutation and on other maintenance components. In protein-dependent plasmids, common initiator recognition or origin interactions can likewise create shared regulation. The molecular implementation differs, but the principle of indistinguishable control signals is the same.

Partition incompatibility offers a separate route. Two plasmids may possess different replication origins yet share spatial allocation components that interact with the same type of partition site. The shared apparatus can then distribute DNA particles without reliably ensuring each daughter receives both identities. Therefore, demonstrating distinct origins does not by itself guarantee compatibility. Likewise, detecting a common marker or sequence in two plasmids does not prove that they share a maintenance system. A compatibility assessment requires the actual behavior of replication and partition determinants in the host under the conditions being considered.

The classical incompatibility group is an operational classification based on stable coexistence behavior and underlying shared control determinants. Members of an incompatibility group often exhibit mutual instability in the absence of conditions that artificially maintain both. This classification is not equivalent to plasmid size, gene function, transfer ability or host range. Two plasmids of different size can be incompatible; two similarly sized plasmids can be compatible. Incompatibility does not mean that the elements can never be found together in a single cell. Transient coexistence may be common, and selection that favors retention of both elements can alter the observed population.

Consider a hypothetical experiment with two distinguishable neutral plasmid markers, A and B. Each plasmid alone is maintained in almost every cell over twenty generations. When both enter the same cells, the combined plasmid genome count remains near ten, yet the fraction of cells with both identities falls progressively. Some lineages retain A and lose B; others show the reverse. A second pair with independently regulated origins remains jointly present at much higher frequency under matched conditions. The observations support shared-control incompatibility in the first pair, but do not distinguish replication control from partition unless additional data reveal the molecular mechanism. If the loss is always biased against one identity, differences in replication efficiency, partition or host fitness may also be involved.

It is instructive to calculate an idealized single-division probability. Suppose a cell contains five independently segregating A molecules and five independently segregating B molecules. For a specified daughter the chance of receiving at least one A is 1−1/32, and the same holds for B. If allocations of A and B are independent, the probability of receiving both is (31/32)^2, equal to 961/1024 or approximately 93.85 percent. If the composition changes to nine A and one B while total abundance remains ten, the chance becomes (511/512)(1/2), approximately 49.90 percent. Total abundance is unchanged but reliable inheritance of both identities falls dramatically. This toy example illustrates why total copy regulation does not solve the compatibility problem.

Incompatibility is sometimes confused with exclusion during entry into a cell. Entry exclusion can reduce the probability that an additional related plasmid enters a cell that already contains an element. Maintenance incompatibility operates after entry, as a problem of continued replication and segregation of the two identities. An experiment in which a second plasmid is never detected immediately after attempted entry cannot establish maintenance incompatibility by itself. An experiment in which both are initially detectable, replicate and then segregate from one another over subsequent generations is more directly relevant. The distinction between entry and maintenance is analogous to distinguishing infection of a host from intracellular viral DNA replication.

The important practical lesson is that a replicon's identity is determined partly by its maintenance circuitry. Two molecules can have different genes yet appear equivalent to a common controller. Conversely, two molecules carrying similar genes can behave independently if their initiation and partition systems are sufficiently distinct. This explains why the origin and associated control regions are central to the biology of plasmid-based genetic tools. Detailed construction methods belong to the methods unit; here the point is the causal relationship between the recognition of molecular identity, feedback stability and inheritance.

9. Plasmids, episomes and changes in replication context

A plasmid is not necessarily an episome in the classical sense. The term episome was introduced to describe a genetic element capable of existing as an independently maintained unit and, in an alternative state, as an integrated component of the host chromosome. In modern discussions the word is also sometimes used more broadly for extrachromosomal DNA, particularly in eukaryotic virology. For examinations dealing with classical bacterial genetics, the important distinction is the alternative integrated state. The F factor of Escherichia coli illustrates why the distinction was biologically useful. It may be maintained as a separate plasmid or become integrated into the bacterial chromosome.

Integration changes the source of replication of the element. When an F factor is free, its own origin and maintenance functions govern plasmid copying and partition. When the factor is integrated into a chromosome, its DNA is physically part of a much larger molecule. Chromosomal replication copies the integrated sequence as replication forks traverse that region. It is no longer necessary for the element to produce a separate daughter circle in each generation in order for its sequence to be inherited. Although the integrated element still contains much of its original sequence, the boundaries of the actively copied unit may differ from those of the free plasmid. Replicon identity is therefore a functional property of the current molecular state, not merely a name permanently attached to a DNA sequence.

Classical experiments with F-associated inheritance and bacterial recombination helped reveal that a transferable genetic element could influence transmission of chromosomal markers. The high-frequency recombination state, or Hfr state, is associated with an integrated F factor. Detailed genetic mapping belongs to the inheritance unit, but the replication connection is crucial here. A marker encoded by the F sequence might be inherited reliably either because a free plasmid replicates and segregates or because an integrated sequence is copied along with the chromosome. Detecting the marker in daughter cells alone does not discriminate the two alternatives. The physical relationship between marker DNA and chromosome DNA must be established.

In the case of certain temperate bacteriophages, the corresponding distinction is between an integrated prophage and an independently replicating viral genome. Bacteriophage lambda can become integrated into the E. coli chromosome. During lysogenic growth, the viral DNA can be inherited along with the host chromosome. When a productive lytic program occurs, the viral DNA can be copied as a separate DNA replication system. The transition requires excision and regulation, topics treated elsewhere in this unit. Here the relevant contrast is between a quiescent chromosomal copy of an element and active replication that increases the amount of extrachromosomal viral DNA.

A useful hypothetical test separates molecular form and initiation requirement. Suppose a mutation specifically abolishes a plasmid origin without affecting the host chromosome or the marker gene. When the element is free, its persistence is impaired as the host divides. If a counterpart containing the same origin defect is integrated into a chromosome, the marker can continue to be inherited, provided the chromosome itself replicates normally and the integrated sequence does not impair viability. The result would support the conclusion that the integrated sequence depends on chromosomal copying rather than its own autonomous origin. It would not demonstrate that the origin mutation had been repaired.

The distinction between integration and autonomous replication also helps classify other mobile genetic elements. A DNA transposon can change its genomic location through transposition. Some transposition mechanisms generate an additional copy of the element, but this does not mean that the transposon is maintained as an autonomous plasmid. An element that depends on chromosomal replication for ordinary inheritance and on occasional transposition for relocation is mechanistically different from a plasmid that initiates its own maintenance replication. In the same way, a DNA circle created by excision is not automatically a plasmid. Its stability depends on whether it possesses a working replication and inheritance system in that state.

Linear plasmids are an additional reason to avoid defining plasmids by shape. A circular duplex avoids conventional linear chromosome ends, but a linear replicon must have a strategy for preserving its terminal sequences. Some linear DNA elements use terminal proteins or special end structures. Their mechanisms differ according to organism and are not adequately described by simply drawing a circular theta intermediate. The general replicon framework still applies: a functional initiation system must generate new DNA and the product must be completed and retained. Circularity is one way of organizing a DNA template, not a requirement of the definition of a replicon.

10. Mitochondrial DNA: a separate genome within a dependent organelle

The evolution of mitochondria from bacterial ancestors helps explain why eukaryotic cells contain a DNA replication system outside the nucleus. Modern mitochondria retain genomes that encode a subset of the components required for organellar function. Most of the proteins required by mitochondria, including many involved in DNA metabolism, are encoded in the nucleus, synthesized on cytosolic ribosomes and imported into the organelle. Consequently, mitochondrial DNA replication is physically separate from nuclear DNA replication without being genetically or metabolically independent of the nucleus. The concept of autonomous initiation must again be separated from complete biochemical self-sufficiency.

Human mitochondrial DNA provides a useful standard example. The canonical genome is a circular double-stranded molecule of approximately 16.6 kilobase pairs. It encodes thirteen polypeptides that contribute to oxidative phosphorylation complexes, as well as two ribosomal RNAs and twenty-two transfer RNAs. The genome is present in multiple copies, organized into structures called nucleoids that include DNA-associated proteins. The number of mitochondrial genome copies per cell varies with cellular identity and physiological state. It is not equal to the number of mitochondria because each organelle or connected mitochondrial region can contain multiple genome copies, while the distribution of nucleoids is heterogeneous.

The two strands are commonly called the heavy strand and the light strand. These names refer to differences in base composition and buoyant density rather than to the mass of a complete mitochondrion. The heavy strand is relatively rich in guanine in human mitochondrial DNA. The origin called OriH is associated with initiation of synthesis of a new heavy strand, whereas OriL is associated with initiation of synthesis of a new light strand. A common diagrammatic mistake is to label OriH as a site at which the original heavy strand is necessarily the template. In strand terminology, the origin name identifies the newly synthesized strand in the conventional model, and a newly synthesized heavy strand is complementary to a parental light strand.

The central catalytic enzyme for mammalian mitochondrial genome copying is DNA polymerase gamma, often written Pol γ. Its catalytic subunit works with accessory subunits that promote efficient DNA synthesis. TWINKLE acts as a mitochondrial replicative helicase, and mitochondrial single-stranded DNA-binding protein, mtSSB, helps stabilize accessible single-stranded template. The mitochondrial RNA polymerase, POLRMT, can participate in primer generation. Additional enzymes process primers, resolve DNA topology and seal interruptions. Many of these proteins are encoded by nuclear genes. A mutation in a nuclear gene can thus cause a mitochondrial DNA maintenance defect even if the mitochondrial genome sequence itself was initially normal.

The classic mammalian strand-displacement model begins in the major noncoding control region at or near OriH. An RNA-derived primer supplies a 3′ terminus, and Pol γ extends the new heavy strand on the parental light-strand template. As synthesis proceeds, the parental heavy strand is displaced. TWINKLE helps expose template ahead of the moving machinery, and mtSSB can associate with the displaced strand. The displacement creates an asymmetry in the timing of strand synthesis: the newly made heavy strand can extend a considerable distance before synthesis of its complementary partner begins. This is an asynchronous replication model rather than a conventional pair of simultaneous leading- and lagging-strand reactions at every fork.

In the standard description, progression around roughly two-thirds of the genome exposes the OriL sequence in the displaced parental heavy strand. Once single-stranded, this region can adopt a stem-loop structure associated with primer initiation. An RNA primer is produced and extended so that a new light strand is synthesized on the parental heavy-strand template in the opposite direction around the genome from heavy-strand synthesis. Both polymerases still synthesize their products from 5′ to 3′. The opposite directions on the genomic map arise from the opposite orientations of the parental templates. When the two syntheses finish and the products are processed and joined, the result is two daughter duplex genomes.

The timing difference has important implications. In the idealized strand-displacement model, different regions of the parental heavy strand spend different lengths of time displaced before being copied into duplex DNA. A region exposed early may remain in a displaced state for a longer interval than one exposed shortly before light-strand synthesis begins. Protein binding and, in alternative models, association with RNA can protect displaced template. The duration of single-strand exposure and the protective mechanism influence vulnerability to damage and formation of secondary structures. However, these relationships should not be presented as a single universally established explanation for every mitochondrial mutation pattern. Sequence context, DNA repair, selection and alternative replication mechanisms can also contribute.

The mitochondrial D-loop must be distinguished from a fully productive replication fork. A displacement loop is formed where a short third DNA strand occupies part of the control region and displaces one strand of the parental duplex. In mammalian mitochondria, short 7S DNA can form such a structure when initiation is followed by early termination. The resulting local three-stranded region is not equivalent to a completed extra mitochondrial genome. Detecting D-loop DNA or a short nascent strand shows local synthesis and structural displacement, but does not by itself prove that a full round of genome replication has been completed. This is a classic distinction between initiation-associated products and productive elongation.

Likewise, strand displacement in mitochondria does not make the standard mammalian replication mechanism identical to rolling-circle replication. The initial priming event differs, a dedicated cleavage of the parental strand is not the defining start, and the delayed second-strand origin leads to completion of two duplex genomes rather than necessarily producing a long concatemeric tail. Both mechanisms can expose a parental strand while synthesis proceeds, but the presence of that shared physical feature does not erase differences in origin chemistry and product formation. The terms should be applied according to the complete mechanism rather than one visual resemblance.

The classical model is an important teaching framework, but evidence has also supported other mitochondrial replication models, including strand-coupled synthesis and models in which RNA is associated with the displaced template during replication. Differences in organisms, cells and methods can influence the observed replication intermediates. A model is evaluated by asking what structures it predicts, how those structures respond to enzymes that distinguish DNA and RNA, and how the positions of initiation and newly synthesized strands are distributed. The correct examination approach is to recognize the standard mammalian strand-displacement model while avoiding universal claims about every mitochondrial genome. Yeast and plant mitochondrial DNA can differ markedly in organization and replication.

A simple numerical illustration makes the asynchronous timing concrete. Assume a hypothetical circular genome of 16,500 base pairs, with OriL exposed after synthesis across 11,000 bases, and suppose the heavy-strand synthesis rate is constant at 550 nucleotides per minute. The modeled delay before light-strand initiation is 11,000 divided by 550, or 20 minutes. Completion of heavy-strand synthesis alone would take 16,500 divided by 550, or 30 minutes from its initiation. If light-strand synthesis begins at minute 20 and copies its entire 16,500-nucleotide template at 750 nucleotides per minute, it requires 22 minutes and finishes at minute 42. In this simplified model, duplex completion cannot occur before minute 42, neglecting processing. These illustrative speeds are not measured human mitochondrial replication rates. The calculation isolates the effect of asynchronous initiation and different elongation durations.

Changes in the mitochondrial replication machinery can be interpreted at different levels. If Pol γ activity is reduced, nascent DNA synthesis may decline and total mitochondrial genome abundance may eventually fall. A defect in helicase activity may slow fork progression or generate incomplete replication products. A problem in RNA primer production can affect initiation. The physiological consequences may become apparent in oxidative phosphorylation because selected subunits and organellar translation components are encoded by mitochondrial DNA. However, a decline in respiratory capacity does not uniquely identify a replication defect: transcription, translation, assembly and membrane function can also fail. A molecular diagnosis requires evidence for the altered replication step rather than only the downstream energetic phenotype.

Copy-number variation in mitochondria also has to be interpreted with attention to scale. Suppose one hypothetical cell contains 120 mitochondrial structures, each with an average of four genome equivalents, yielding 480 genome equivalents. Another cell contains sixty structures with an average of eight genome equivalents, also yielding 480. These cells have equal mitochondrial genome abundance but different organellar distributions. In a real mitochondrial network, even counting the number of distinct organelles can be difficult because fusion and fission continuously change connectivity. Mitochondrial genome abundance per cell, nucleoid abundance and mitochondrial mass are distinct measurements. None is a universally reliable substitute for the others.

Replication of mitochondrial DNA is not simply another phase of nuclear chromosome S phase. In many mammalian cellular contexts mitochondrial DNA replication can continue outside nuclear S phase, including in nondividing cells that must maintain organellar genomes. The organellar genome population is regulated according to cellular needs and turnover rather than universally undergoing a single synchronous round before each cell division. Yet mitochondrial abundance may still change with proliferation, development and metabolic conditions. The relevant question is how synthesis balances turnover and dilution over time. This provides a useful parallel to plasmid copy-number homeostasis without implying that the molecular control systems are the same.

11. Plastid genomes and diversity of organellar DNA replication

Chloroplasts and other plastids also possess genomes inherited from a bacterial endosymbiotic ancestor. The plastid genome of many land plants is conventionally represented as a circular map containing a large single-copy region, a small single-copy region and two inverted repeat regions. The repeated segments often contain ribosomal RNA genes. This organization is useful for understanding gene order and sequence relationships, but the map is not sufficient evidence that every physical DNA molecule in a plastid is a covalently closed circle. Direct observations of plant organellar DNA have revealed mixtures of circular, linear and branched forms.

The distinction between a genome map and a physical DNA molecule is especially important in plant organelle biology. A complete reference sequence may be assembled into a circle because its sequence relationships close on themselves, even if much of the DNA in living organelles is organized as larger branched or linear structures. A circular map describes sequence continuity at the level of a genome representation. A microscopic or biochemical observation describes molecular conformation and connectivity in a specific sample. When those observations appear to differ, it is not necessary to discard one or the other. They can be answering different questions about the same hereditary material.

Several replication mechanisms have been proposed for plant organellar genomes. Textbook descriptions have included origin-associated initiation and structures interpreted in terms of strand displacement or bubble formation. Recombination-dependent DNA replication has also been proposed, particularly in light of abundant branched and linear DNA forms and the ability of homologous DNA segments to associate. In a simple recombination-dependent model, a homologous DNA end pairs with another DNA molecule and provides or helps establish an extendable primer end. Copying can then proceed from that DNA interaction. The resulting structures need not be restricted to simple circular monomers. Detailed strand-exchange mechanisms belong to the later recombination nodes, but the conceptual connection is worth establishing here.

The distinction between proposed mechanisms and established general facts matters. Nuclear-encoded proteins participate in plastid DNA maintenance, and plastid genomes can be present in multiple copies. DNA synthesis depends on appropriate polymerases and accessible template. The precise contribution of particular initiation and recombination pathways is less uniformly resolved across plant species and developmental stages than the core enzymology of a conventional bacterial replication fork. Therefore, a statement that all chloroplast genomes use a single universal theta mechanism or a single universal rolling-circle mechanism is not justified. The examination-relevant understanding is that organellar DNA represents another extrachromosomal replication system with distinctive and diverse molecular organization.

Plant organelles also illustrate the coupling of replication and organelle development. A young tissue that produces numerous new plastids may need to increase the total supply of plastid DNA. A differentiated tissue may have a different balance of genome synthesis, degradation and plastid turnover. Even if the genome sequence is the same, abundance and molecular forms may differ. This means that a replication intermediate observed in one developmental context should not be assumed to dominate at every stage of plant growth. Comparisons of DNA synthesis require matched tissue conditions or explicit consideration of developmental differences.

Genome repeats provide a useful quantitative trap. Suppose a plastid reference genome has a single-copy gene X and a gene Y represented twice because it lies in an inverted repeat. If there are fifty complete genome equivalents, the idealized cell or organelle sample contains fifty copies of X and one hundred copies of Y. Measuring Y without correcting for its repeated representation would overestimate genome equivalents by a factor of two compared with X. If rearranged genomes or subgenomic molecules are present, even a single-copy reference locus may not fully represent complete genome abundance. The calculation shows why DNA sequence counts and complete replicon counts must be distinguished in organellar systems as well as in plasmids.

Compare plastid replication with mammalian mitochondrial replication. Both occur outside the nucleus, both rely extensively on nuclear gene products, and both must maintain a population of DNA molecules within dynamic organelles. Their ancestral relationship to bacteria helps explain some shared molecular principles. However, human mitochondrial DNA is commonly represented by a small circular genome and a standard asynchronous strand-displacement model, whereas plant plastid DNA displays more complex physical organization and may involve multiple replication mechanisms. Neither organellar genome should be treated as an ordinary bacterial chromosome simply because of endosymbiotic ancestry. Modern cellular integration, genome reduction or rearrangement, and nucleus-dependent protein supply have transformed the biological context of replication.

Organelle inheritance is connected with replication but is not explained solely by it. If one cell contains two sequence variants of an organellar genome, their relative abundance may change through unequal copying, degradation, partition and selection. The term heteroplasmy describes the coexistence of different mitochondrial DNA variants within a biological sample or individual; a related mixture can occur in plastid genomes. A change in variant frequency does not necessarily indicate a higher intrinsic polymerization rate of one genome. Differential survival of organelles or cells and nonrandom partition may also contribute. Detailed inheritance patterns are assigned to the genetics unit; for this chapter, the useful connection is that multiple-copy replication systems create a population-level problem of maintaining both total abundance and genetic composition.

12. Viral DNA replicons: matching a copying strategy to the viral genome

Viruses illustrate how the general chemical requirements for DNA synthesis can be satisfied in dramatically different biological settings. A virus does not simply divide into two daughter cells. Instead, during a productive infection, viral genetic material is copied within a suitable cellular environment and genome copies may become incorporated into newly assembled particles. The viral genome may have a different physical form inside a particle from its actively replicating form inside a cell. Thus a linear viral genome can become circular before copying, and a single-stranded viral genome can generate a double-stranded intermediate. Understanding the transition from the entering genome to the replicative form is essential for choosing a correct mechanism.

Bacteriophage lambda provides a classical illustration of changes in replication geometry. Its genome in the mature particle is linear double-stranded DNA with complementary single-stranded cohesive ends. These ends can associate after the DNA reaches the bacterial interior. Sealing the backbone discontinuities produces a circular DNA molecule. The physical transition from a linear sequence in a particle to a circle inside a cell allows the circular form to be replicated without ordinary linear end-completion problems. This circularization does not by itself increase DNA copy number. It reorganizes the substrate from which copying can subsequently occur.

During the early productive replication phase of lambda, circular molecules can be copied through theta-type intermediates. Viral origin recognition functions, including the O protein, participate in establishing initiation, and viral P protein helps recruit bacterial helicase machinery. Other essential activities are supplied by the host. The detailed regulation of the viral developmental program is beyond the present chapter, but the biochemical division of labor is instructive. Viral genes help define which DNA molecules initiate copying, while host replication proteins supply major catalytic and structural functions. Dependence on cellular proteins does not make the viral genome a mere passive segment of the host chromosome; the viral origin defines its own replicating template during productive replication.

Later in the classical lambda productive program, replication produces long concatemeric DNA through rolling-circle-type replication. The advantage of a concatemer in this biological context is that it provides repeated genome-length units as substrates for genome packaging. Each repeat contains the information needed for one mature genome. A packaging-associated cleavage system recognizes appropriate sites in the repeated DNA and generates unit-length DNA with the required ends. Synthesis of the long DNA and cleavage into genome units are different processes. If DNA synthesis were normal but packaging-associated cleavage failed, long products could still accumulate without a corresponding production of mature genome-containing particles.

The transition from theta-type replication to production of concatemers illustrates that a replicon need not use one fixed geometry throughout its biological existence. Early amplification can increase the number of circular templates, while later replication creates long substrates appropriate for packaging. The precise molecular control of this transition has been investigated experimentally and should not be reduced to a simplistic universal switch. Classical kinetic studies of lambda DNA distinguished the early multiplication of circular molecules from later extension of rolling-circle structures. The important conclusion for examinations is the association of early theta intermediates and later concatemeric products in this representative system, not an assumption that all DNA viruses follow the same sequence.

A hypothetical numerical example distinguishes concatemer length from product count. Let a model viral genome be 40 kilobase pairs, and let a continuous double-stranded DNA product contain five complete head-to-tail repeats, for a total length of 200 kilobase pairs. If the product contains all required processing sites and is partitioned perfectly into genome-length units, the theoretical number of units is five. This is not the actual yield of infectious particles. Cleavage, packaging, particle assembly and other biological requirements may lower the number of viable products. If a 210-kilobase product contains only five complete repeats and an additional incomplete segment, one cannot simply round the quotient 210/40 up to six. The amount of information in a complete genome and the physical number of complete genome units constrain the interpretation.

The single-stranded DNA bacteriophage phiX174 illustrates a different starting substrate. A single-stranded viral genome cannot undergo conventional theta replication as a double-stranded circle immediately upon entry. It first acquires a complementary DNA strand, using host activities to establish a double-stranded replicative form. This form permits both expression of viral information and further DNA copying. Viral gene A protein participates in origin-specific nicking and rolling-circle replication of the replicative form. The strand produced or displaced in the relevant replication phase can ultimately supply single-stranded viral genomes. The cycle therefore includes a double-stranded intermediate even though the genome in the mature viral particle is single-stranded.

This example helps resolve an apparent paradox. If a virus carries only one DNA strand, how can it use ordinary DNA-dependent RNA polymerase to produce transcripts from a normal double-stranded promoter? Formation of a complementary strand can generate the necessary double-stranded DNA context. Equally, producing a DNA complement is a replication event but does not automatically create a second genome-containing viral particle. The viral system must produce the correct strand type and perform additional steps to generate mature progeny. The distinction among genome synthesis, replicative form and packaged genome is more informative than a broad classification of the virus as single-stranded.

Consider what would be observed if complementary-strand synthesis of an entering phiX174-like genome were blocked. The entering single-stranded molecules might remain detectable briefly, yet the double-stranded replicative form would be reduced and subsequent amplification impaired. In a different conceptual perturbation, the initial double-stranded form could accumulate if later nick-dependent replication were impaired. These distinct structural phenotypes identify different stages of the copying pathway. They also demonstrate why the presence of a viral genome at the beginning of an observation is not evidence that productive DNA replication has taken place.

Not all viral DNA replication can be reduced to either theta or rolling-circle initiation. Adenovirus supplies a textbook example of protein-primed replication of linear double-stranded DNA. A viral precursor terminal protein serves as a primer through covalent attachment of the first nucleotide, and a viral DNA polymerase extends the growing strand. The protein primer provides a solution to the problem of initiating synthesis at the end of a linear template without relying on a conventional upstream nucleic-acid primer. The essential lesson is that initiation requires a suitable chemical group for the first linkage, but the way this group is provided varies. Once a nucleotide is linked and extension proceeds, subsequent nucleotide addition obeys the ordinary directionality of DNA synthesis.

Some small double-stranded circular DNA viruses provide still other examples of theta-like replication using a combination of virus-specific initiation functions and cellular replication proteins. Simian virus 40, commonly used as a classic model of eukaryotic DNA replication, uses its large T antigen to recognize its origin and promote DNA unwinding and works with host replication machinery. A circular genome and bidirectional fork movement make its replication geometry comparable in broad outline to theta replication of bacterial circles, but the protein machinery reflects the eukaryotic host environment. This example reinforces that similar geometries can be implemented by different sets of enzymes.

RNA viruses are outside the central scope of this DNA replication chapter. Retroviruses, which pass through a DNA intermediate, use reverse transcription at an important stage of their cycle. Reverse transcription differs from DNA-dependent DNA replication because an RNA template initially directs DNA synthesis. Although integrated retroviral DNA may subsequently be copied as part of a chromosome, it is misleading to classify the entire replication cycle as ordinary plasmid theta replication. The appropriate classification follows the relevant substrate and stage, not simply the fact that DNA is detectable somewhere in the life cycle.

Biochemical studies of phiX174 provided direct support for the initiation role of gene A: purified gene A product was shown to introduce a strand-specific interruption in the replicative form, and studies of replication with cellular components established its participation in DNA copying. These observations connect a genetic requirement to a defined chemical event rather than merely associating the presence of the gene with viral DNA accumulation. Cleavage alone is insufficient evidence of completion; evidence for the extended strand and the completed circular product addresses subsequent steps.

13. Quantitative reasoning across replication geometries

The geometry of an extrachromosomal replicon influences how much template each progressing fork must traverse. Consider a circular duplex of length L base pairs with one active origin and two forks that begin at the same time and advance at constant speeds v1 and v2 measured in base pairs per minute. If the forks travel along opposite directions around the circle and copying is complete when the total distance equals L, the completion time T satisfies v1T+v2T=L. Hence T=L/(v1+v2). If only one fork advances, T=L/v under the equivalent assumptions. These equations describe the time required to traverse the template and do not include initiation delays, fork pauses, primer processing or physical decatenation.

For a hypothetical 12,000-base-pair circle with two forks moving at 600 and 400 base pairs per minute, completion occurs after 12 minutes, since the combined speed is 1,000 base pairs per minute. The two forks travel 7,200 and 4,800 base pairs, respectively. They do not meet opposite the origin because their speeds differ. In a separate unidirectional model with one 600-base-pair-per-minute fork, traversal requires 20 minutes. These values do not imply that introducing a second origin necessarily yields the same effect as introducing a second fork. The position and firing time of multiple origins determine how much each fork travels. The derivation applies to the specified single-origin system.

In theta replication, the physical length of duplex regions changes as parental DNA is copied. Let f be the fraction of the initial genome sequence that has already been replicated on both strands. The initial parental molecule contains L base pairs, and an additional fL base pairs of daughter DNA are synthesized when both complementary strands over that fraction have been completed. The total duplex DNA content is therefore L(1+f) base pairs, neglecting short single-stranded regions and primer effects. If a circle of length 8,000 base pairs is 75 percent replicated, the total DNA content of the connected intermediate is equivalent to 14,000 base pairs. There is not yet necessarily a pair of separate 8,000-base-pair molecules. DNA mass and number of physically distinct molecules tell different stories.

Rolling-circle products are quantified differently. For a first-strand polymerase that synthesizes at v nucleotides per minute for a time t, the amount of newly synthesized first-strand DNA is vt nucleotides under a constant-speed approximation. If the circular template contains L nucleotides, the quotient vt/L gives the number of template lengths traversed. The number of complete repeats is the integer part of that quotient when synthesis is uninterrupted from an appropriate starting position. Additional complementary-strand synthesis is needed before a displaced single-stranded product becomes fully duplex. For example, a hypothetical 6,000-nucleotide circle copied at 500 nucleotides per minute for 30 minutes yields 15,000 newly synthesized nucleotides, corresponding to two complete template lengths and one-half of a third. A measurement in nucleotides must not be labeled as base pairs unless duplex formation is established.

These examples illustrate a broader principle about molecular quantities. A single genome equivalent of a double-stranded DNA circle of L base pairs contains approximately 2L nucleotide residues. Replication of one intact duplex into two intact duplexes requires incorporation of approximately 2L new deoxyribonucleotide residues, ignoring replacement events and primer processing details. For a 5,000-base-pair plasmid, producing an additional duplex copy therefore involves approximately 10,000 newly incorporated deoxyribonucleotide residues. A reaction that has incorporated only 5,000 residues could have copied one full strand or partially copied both strands. Without structural or strand-specific information the incorporation count cannot discriminate those possibilities.

DNA dosage can also be inferred from a mass ratio, subject to explicit assumptions. If a cell has one complete 4,000,000-base-pair chromosome and twenty complete 5,000-base-pair plasmids, the plasmid DNA contributes 100,000 base pairs and the total cellular DNA represented by these molecules is 4,100,000 base pairs. Assuming similar average mass per base pair, plasmid DNA accounts for approximately 2.44 percent of the total. A mass fraction is not equal to the fraction of molecules. The twenty small plasmids greatly outnumber the single chromosome in physical copy number, despite accounting for a small proportion of DNA mass. Cells with multiple chromosomes or replication intermediates require a modified denominator.

Sequence-specific quantification introduces its own assumptions. A hypothetical measurement reports a plasmid marker to chromosomal reference ratio of 12. If each complete plasmid contains one marker and each cell has one reference locus copy, the inferred average abundance is twelve plasmid genome equivalents per cell. If the same cells actually contain two copies of the reference locus on average, the inferred abundance is twenty-four. Neither calculation guarantees that the plasmids are structurally intact or separate particles. Fragments carrying the marker, integrated sequences and tandem repeats could contribute to the signal. The most defensible interpretation reports the directly measured ratio and then states the conditions under which it estimates complete plasmid copies.

When quantitative amplification is used as a conceptual measurement, an ideal doubling of amplified product per cycle relates a difference in threshold cycle to a ratio of starting template abundance. Under matched efficiencies and calibration, a difference of three cycles corresponds to an eightfold difference in starting quantity. If the plasmid marker reaches the threshold three cycles earlier than a single-copy reference, the idealized ratio is eight marker copies per reference copy. In real measurements, primer efficiency, sample quality and calibration matter; threshold differences cannot be used uncritically. The method estimates target sequence abundance rather than the physical topology or inheritance competence of the DNA.

14. Experimental discrimination and integration of the major strategies

Mechanistic evidence is strongest when different measurements address different features of the same replication process. Physical visualization can reveal a branched circle or a tail. Sequence-specific measurements can determine which regions are present at increased abundance. Label incorporation can identify newly synthesized DNA. A controlled perturbation can connect a particular origin or protein to a change in replication. None of these alone necessarily establishes the entire mechanism. Their combination can distinguish a structural intermediate that participates in copying from an unusual DNA form that accumulates for an unrelated reason.

Consider a hypothetical time-resolved dataset. At the first observation, a preparation contains closed circular molecules. At an intermediate observation, many molecules contain an internal duplicated arc and two branch points. At a late observation, the predominant products are separate duplex circles of the original length. The data are consistent with theta replication. A second preparation starts with similar circles, but early products include nicked circles and extended single-stranded tails, followed later by displaced circles and completed duplex products. This sequence of structural states is consistent with rolling-circle replication. The order of appearance is important: a nick detected after all products have accumulated could instead be caused by sample handling, whereas a nick associated with initiation and followed by extension supports a role in the mechanism.

Electron microscopy and other structural approaches can be interpreted using the same caution. A replication bubble is a physical structure in which DNA has been duplicated over a region. A recombination intermediate may also have branch points, and a broken DNA molecule can display an unusual contour. To distinguish these cases, one asks whether new DNA label is present in the expected arms, whether structures depend on the relevant origin and whether the branch points progress over time. A static image cannot reveal by itself the polarity of synthesis or whether a structure leads to viable daughter molecules. Those conclusions require complementary evidence.

Electrophoretic mobility is another useful conceptual tool. DNA molecules of the same sequence length can migrate differently because of their topology. A supercoiled circular plasmid, an open circular plasmid containing a nick, and a linear DNA molecule of the same length often occupy different positions in a gel. The exact order depends on experimental conditions, so one should avoid a universal claim about which topology always migrates fastest. A linear DNA size marker calibrates linear molecules, not necessarily the apparent mobility of a compact circular form. A change in band position can therefore reflect topology rather than changed genome length. Conversely, a double-length product can arise through recombination or replication; a length measurement alone does not establish its origin.

Two-dimensional DNA gel analysis offers a standard example of evidence linking molecular mass with geometry. In an idealized analysis of a defined DNA fragment, replication intermediates can separate according to size and structural shape. Fragments containing an internal active origin may produce a bubble-like pattern; fragments through which a single fork passes can display a Y-like pattern. The presence or absence of a pattern depends on the position of the origin relative to fragment boundaries and on the abundance of intermediates. A Y pattern does not necessarily mean that the entire genome has only one replication fork. It may simply mean that one fork is traversing the particular DNA fragment being examined. The inference must match the scale of the measurement.

Genetic complementation provides a different kind of evidence. Suppose a hypothetical replicon contains an origin O and an initiator gene I. Mutation of I prevents new DNA synthesis, but a functional I gene supplied separately restores synthesis. Mutation of O is not rescued by the same source of functional I. The pattern is consistent with a diffusible initiator acting on a required local DNA sequence. However, control experiments must show comparable initial DNA amounts, cell viability and host replication. If the origin mutation destabilizes the entire DNA molecule or prevents detection of the marker, the apparent lack of complementation could have another explanation. Good experimental reasoning separates the observation from the interpretation and identifies a plausible alternative.

In organellar DNA studies, a decrease in abundance is likewise insufficient to locate a defect. If newly synthesized DNA falls before existing genome abundance falls, a defect in synthesis becomes plausible. If nascent synthesis is near normal but pre-existing genomes disappear more rapidly, increased turnover becomes plausible. If the average organellar DNA per cell falls because a population contains more cells with low organelle mass, the observed change may reflect cell composition. Normalization to a nuclear reference and an independent measure of organellar content can help distinguish these levels, but each normalization has assumptions. No single ratio universally establishes the activity of a specific DNA polymerase.

The diverse mechanisms can now be organized around a shared set of questions. What is the template at the moment of initiation? What event provides an extendable primer end? Which strand or strands are copied at the moving junction? How is the product completed and physically separated? What ensures that enough copies persist in the appropriate biological setting? For theta plasmids, these questions lead to duplex opening, priming, fork movement, circle completion and feedback with segregation. For rolling-circle plasmids, they lead to nick-dependent initiation, displacement, termination and complementary synthesis. For mammalian mitochondrial genomes, they lead to RNA-associated initiation, temporally separated strand synthesis and maintenance of multiple nucleoids. For viruses, the answers depend on the replication form and on the relationship between genome synthesis and particle production.

These questions also clarify the relationship to the next chapter on replication fidelity. The current chapter has concentrated on generating enough complete copies and recognizing distinct mechanisms of initiation and progression. But a complete physical copy can still contain an incorrect nucleotide. Increasing replication frequency may increase the number of copying events without changing the error probability per nucleotide, and a stable copy-number controller does not prove that every copy is genetically accurate. The next logical step is to ask how polymerases select correct nucleotides, how proofreading removes some mistakes, and how mismatch repair corrects errors that escape the initial copying reaction. Quantity, molecular completeness, inheritance and sequence fidelity are related but distinguishable properties of a replication system.

16. Part C — Analytical and experimental reasoning

All numerical data and experimental cases below are hypothetical educational examples. Explain the assumptions before solving.

C1. A 15 kb circular replicon has two forks moving at 500 and 750 bp/min. Predict completion time and meeting positions.

Answer and reasoning

12 min; 6 kb and 9 kb from the origin on opposite arcs. T=15,000/(500+750)=12 min. The two distances sum to the genome length. Equal-distance convergence would require equal speeds.

C2. A 9 kb circle uses one active theta fork at 450 bp/min. A second model uses two equal forks at the same speed. Compare traversal times.

Answer and reasoning

20 min versus 10 min. One fork traverses all 9,000 bp. Two forks cover 900 bp/min together. Neither result includes initiation or product processing.

C3. A connected theta intermediate derived from a 10 kb circle contains 16 kb duplex-equivalent DNA. Find the copied fraction.

Answer and reasoning

60%. L(1+f)=16 kb, so f=16/10−1=0.6. It does not mean 1.6 physically independent plasmids.

C4. A continuous strand-displacement polymerase copies a hypothetical 4 kb circular template at 300 nt/min for 30 min. How many complete lengths and residual nucleotides?

Answer and reasoning

Two complete lengths plus 1,000 nt. Total synthesis=9,000 nt; divide by 4,000. These are nucleotides, not necessarily base pairs.

C5. A 7 kb duplex plasmid gives one additional complete duplex. Approximate number of new DNA nucleotide residues?

Answer and reasoning

14,000. Both parental strands are copied over 7,000 positions. A single new strand would supply only half this count.

C6. A culture has 20 genome equivalents per cell. Each copy yields an average 0.6 additional copies per generation, with equal partition in the mean. After three generations?

Answer and reasoning

10.24 equivalents per cell. The per-generation multiplier is (1+0.6)/2=0.8. 20×0.8³=10.24; it is a mean, not an individual count.

C7. For k(n)=0.8−0.025n per hour and growth rate 0.3 per hour, find the positive steady copy abundance and response at n=16.

Answer and reasoning

20; abundance increases at n=16. Set k=0.3: n=0.5/0.025=20. At 16, k=0.4, greater than dilution. This is a local model.

C8. A cell has six independent copies before division. Probability a specified daughter receives none? Probability either daughter is empty?

Answer and reasoning

1/64 and 1/32. The first probability is 2⁻⁶; the mutually exclusive empty-left and empty-right outcomes sum to 2⁻⁵.

C9. For random allocation of 16 copies, calculate mean and standard deviation of the number in a specified daughter.

Answer and reasoning

Mean 8, standard deviation 2. Binomial N=16, p=0.5: mean Np=8, variance Np(1−p)=4. Standard deviation is 2.

C10. Eight genome equivalents are either eight separate molecules or four linked pairs that segregate as units. Compare empty-daughter probabilities.

Answer and reasoning

1/256 versus 1/16; sixteenfold difference. The relevant N is the number of independent units, not sequence equivalents. The four particles produce a 16-fold higher modeled risk.

C11. Half of cells have one independent copy and half have five. What is the mean probability that a specified daughter lacks the plasmid?

Answer and reasoning

17/64, or 26.5625%. Average the conditional probabilities: (1/2)(1/2)+(1/2)(1/32)=17/64. Substitution of mean count 3 would incorrectly yield 1/8.

C12. A lineage loses a plasmid with probability 0.1 per generation. With no reacquisition and equal growth, what fraction remains after four generations?

Answer and reasoning

65.61%. Retention is 0.9⁴=0.6561. Population selection or changing loss rates would violate this simple model.

C13. A cell has three A copies and two B copies independently allocated. Chance a specified daughter receives both identities?

Answer and reasoning

21/32, or 65.625%. P(A present)=7/8, P(B present)=3/4; multiply under independence. The total number five alone is insufficient.

C14. A plasmid marker to chromosome reference ratio is 15. Cells average two reference copies. The plasmid marker is present twice per intact plasmid. Estimate genome equivalents.

Answer and reasoning

15 per cell. There are 15×2=30 marker copies per cell; divide by two markers per complete plasmid. Fragmentation or integration would complicate inference.

C15. One 3 Mb chromosome and 30 copies of a 10 kb plasmid are complete. What fraction of represented DNA mass is plasmid?

Answer and reasoning

About 9.09%. Plasmids contribute 300 kb out of 3,300 kb, assuming equal mass per bp. Molecule fraction is a different quantity.

C16. In an ideal quantitative amplification assay a marker threshold occurs four cycles earlier than the reference. With equal doubling efficiencies, estimate the starting ratio.

Answer and reasoning

16-fold. Each cycle corresponds to a factor of two. The ratio 2⁴ estimates sequence abundance, not physical plasmid number without further assumptions.

C17. In a hypothetical organellar strand-displacement model, the second origin is exposed after 9 kb at 450 nt/min. The first 15 kb strand takes the same speed; second-strand speed is 600 nt/min. Earliest completion time?

Answer and reasoning

45 min after initial start. Delay=20 min; first strand=33.33 min; second=25 min after its start, ending at 45. The later completion constrains duplex completion.

C18. A plastid single-copy locus is detected 80 times and an inverted-repeat locus 160 times. What is the simplest genome-equivalent interpretation?

Answer and reasoning

80 complete equivalents under the intact-genome assumption. The repeat marker is present twice per genome. Equal corrected estimates are consistent with intact molecules, but do not prove topology.

C19. A 180 kb hypothetical concatemer contains full 35 kb repeats from its starting boundary. Maximum complete units?

Answer and reasoning

Five with 5 kb residual. 180/35=5 remainder 5. Particle yield can be lower and requires appropriate processing and packaging.

C20. Mutant I is rescued by supplying an initiator gene in trans; mutant O is not. Initial entry and host growth match. Which assignment is supported?

Answer and reasoning

I is a trans-acting initiator defect; O is a required local origin defect. Rescue distinguishes diffusible activity from a cis sequence under the specified controls. It does not identify every participating host protein.

C21. A ColE1-like origin produces normal RNA II transcripts, but origin hybrids and nascent DNA are low. Name two distinct hypotheses and a discriminating observation.

Answer and reasoning

Defective RNA II hybrid formation or excessive RNA I inhibition; compare RNA II hybrid formation under matched inhibition and primer precursor interaction. Normal transcription excludes only a major transcription deficit. A processing defect is another alternative if hybrids form normally but usable primer is absent.

C22. A rolling-circle sample accumulates displaced circular ssDNA while duplex products decline. Cleavage and first-strand synthesis remain normal. Which stage is implicated?

Answer and reasoning

Second-strand initiation or completion. The first-strand steps occur; conversion of ssDNA to duplex is impaired. This is not evidence of a complete block at dso cleavage.

C23. Two distinct-origin plasmids individually persist, but when co-resident their total copy number remains normal and one identity is lost. Both use the same partition machinery. Which interpretation is favored?

Answer and reasoning

Partition incompatibility is plausible. Independent origins do not exclude shared segregation. Matched copy counts and daughter distributions would help distinguish it from residual replication cross-control.

C24. In cells carrying a neutral plasmid, DNA synthesis per retained cell is unchanged, but cells without plasmids expand faster. Explain a falling population retention fraction.

Answer and reasoning

Selection can change prevalence independently of molecular synthesis. Relative growth changes population composition. A retention fraction is not a direct measurement of initiation rate or segregation loss.

C25. A gel shows two bands from a DNA preparation. The two forms become the same length when interpreted as linear molecules. What is a plausible explanation?

Answer and reasoning

Different topological forms of equal sequence length. Open circular and supercoiled forms can differ in mobility. Band migration alone is not a reliable molecular length estimate.

C26. A mapped fragment produces a Y pattern on a replication-intermediate gel while another fragment produces a bubble pattern. What follows?

Answer and reasoning

The former is consistent with fork passage; the latter supports initiation inside that fragment. The Y pattern does not establish that the whole replicon has one fork. The analysis has fragment-level resolution.

C27. Statements: (I) All rolling-circle replication yields infectious virions. (II) A displaced strand may later become duplex. (III) Both theta and rolling-circle DNA synthesis proceed 5′→3′. (IV) All theta systems have two moving forks. Which are correct?

Answer and reasoning

II and III only. I confuses a replication geometry with viral assembly; IV excludes unidirectional theta incorrectly.

C28. Match A: RNA I; B: ParB; C: TWINKLE; D: terminal protein. Functions: 1 mitochondrial helicase; 2 antisense inhibition; 3 protein priming; 4 centromere-like site binding.

Answer and reasoning

A–2, B–4, C–1, D–3. Each pair identifies a distinct level of replicon maintenance. A primer inhibitor, partition protein, helicase and protein primer are not interchangeable.

C29. Statements: (I) A free F element can initiate its own maintenance replication. (II) Integrated F sequence can be copied by chromosomal forks. (III) An integrated marker proves an active separate plasmid. (IV) Integration and replication are identical processes. Which are correct?

Answer and reasoning

I and II only. III confuses sequence presence with autonomous form; IV confuses DNA rearrangement with DNA synthesis.

C30. A mitochondrial sample has normal nuclear DNA synthesis, reduced new mtDNA incorporation, unchanged initial mtDNA count and later mtDNA depletion. What inference is best supported and what is not established?

Answer and reasoning

Reduced mitochondrial synthesis is supported; a specific Pol γ mutation is not established. The time order fits reduced synthesis followed by depletion. Primer formation, helicase, polymerase or substrate limitations could explain it. Independent turnover and organelle-mass measurements would strengthen the conclusion.

Examination traps

  1. Autonomy does not mean self-sufficient enzymes.
  2. A circular reference map does not prove every DNA molecule is circular.
  3. An origin is not automatically a promoter or an oriT.
  4. A theta bubble does not imply two equally moving forks.
  5. A nick is not a double-strand break; the 3′ end is the extension site.
  6. A displaced strand is not the newly growing strand in the typical nick-based first round.
  7. The single-strand origin is distinct from the double-strand origin.
  8. Genome equivalents are not independently segregating particles.
  9. Probability that either daughter is empty is not the probability that a specified daughter is empty.
  10. High total copy number does not guarantee joint maintenance of two identities.
  11. Distinct origins do not rule out partition incompatibility.
  12. A chromosomal marker can persist without autonomous replication.
  13. OriH names the newly synthesized H strand, not the H template.
  14. A short mitochondrial D-loop product is not a complete new genome.
  15. Viral genome amplification is not equivalent to infectious-particle yield.
  16. Nucleotide counts are not base-pair counts for single-stranded products.
