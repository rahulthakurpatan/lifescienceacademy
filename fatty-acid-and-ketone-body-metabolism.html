1. Why fatty acids are both fuel and building material

A person who has not eaten overnight continues to supply ATP to the heart, liver and skeletal muscles. A germinating oil-rich seed can develop before its leaves perform enough photosynthesis to support growth. Both observations point toward a feature of fatty acids: they contain a long chain of relatively reduced carbon atoms that can donate electrons when oxidised. The cell does not obtain energy simply because the chain is hydrophobic. It obtains energy because transferring electrons from reduced carbon to suitable acceptors is thermodynamically favourable, and the energy released can be captured through cellular respiration.

Fatty acids also solve a different problem. Every growing cell must make new membrane lipids, and a fed organism may receive more carbon than it immediately needs for ATP production. The same chemical class can therefore serve as an energy reserve or a structural precursor. The biological challenge is to choose the direction of metabolism at the right time, place and rate. If a cell actively synthesised a chain and immediately oxidised the newly made chain, it would consume ATP and reducing power without achieving useful net work. The enzymes that make and degrade fatty acids are consequently distinct, occupy different compartments and communicate through regulatory metabolites.

The chemistry of fatty acids introduced in Node 1.B.02 provides the necessary starting point. A fatty acid has a carboxyl group and a hydrocarbon chain. Carbon 1 is the carboxyl carbon, carbon 2 the alpha carbon and carbon 3 the beta carbon. The terminal methyl carbon is the omega carbon. A label such as 16:0 means sixteen total carbons including carbon 1 and no carbon–carbon double bonds. Oleate is 18:1 cis-Δ9, meaning a cis double bond between carbons 9 and 10, counted from the carboxyl end. Omega numbering starts at the other end, so oleate is also an omega-9 fatty acid. These conventions are not interchangeable: during beta oxidation the carboxyl end shortens while the terminal end remains unchanged until the last cycle.

Compare glucose and a fatty acid at the level of elemental composition. Glucose already contains numerous carbon–oxygen bonds, whereas a saturated fatty acid contains mostly carbon–hydrogen bonds. Oxidising the latter to carbon dioxide and water transfers more electrons per unit mass. In food-energy terms, fat supplies approximately 9 kcal per gram and carbohydrate approximately 4 kcal per gram; these are nutritional approximations, not the number of ATP molecules made by a cell. Neutral triacylglycerol is also stored with relatively little associated water, unlike hydrated glycogen. This makes lipid stores particularly useful for extended energy supply, while glycogen offers a rapidly mobilised source of glucose.

Three earlier concepts are essential for following this chapter. Acetyl-CoA is a two-carbon activated acyl group attached through a thioester bond to coenzyme A. The citric acid cycle can oxidise this group, yielding reduced electron carriers, and oxidative phosphorylation uses those electrons to support ATP formation. NADH and NADPH differ by an additional phosphate on the latter, but both carry reducing equivalents. Enzymes distinguish them, and cells generally use NADH in energy-producing oxidation and NADPH in reductive biosynthesis and antioxidant defence. The distinction is functional rather than an absolute chemical rule. We will use it to understand why fatty acid synthesis is not beta oxidation run backwards.

Our focus is mammalian metabolism unless explicitly stated otherwise. Plant and microbial comparisons clarify important exceptions, but the detailed metabolism of complex lipids, cholesterol and lipid transport belongs to the next chapter. The goal here is to follow individual fatty acid carbons, activated acyl groups, electron carriers and ketone bodies through the network, and to predict the outcome when one of those routes is perturbed.

2. From lipid stores to activated fatty acyl-CoA

Before a fatty acid can be oxidised, it must be available to the cell and converted into a chemically useful form. In the fed state, triacylglycerol delivered in lipoproteins can be hydrolysed by lipoprotein lipase at capillary surfaces, allowing fatty acids to enter tissues. During fasting, adipose tissue instead releases fatty acids from intracellular triacylglycerol. The principal sequential reactions involve adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) and monoacylglycerol lipase. Each removes an acyl group from a glycerol-based lipid, ultimately producing fatty acids and glycerol. This is hydrolysis of ester bonds, not oxidation of the hydrocarbon chain, and it does not itself generate NADH or ATP.

Released long-chain nonesterified fatty acids are carried in the aqueous blood by albumin. They are often called free fatty acids because they are not esterified to glycerol, but they are not freely soluble in blood in large amounts. Albumin binding is reversible, allowing tissue uptake while limiting the concentration of unbound hydrophobic molecules. Cellular entry depends on chain length, diffusion and protein-assisted uptake. The released glycerol has a separate metabolic fate: the liver can phosphorylate it to glycerol 3-phosphate and oxidise it to dihydroxyacetone phosphate, which can contribute to glucose formation. Therefore, even if the fatty acid portion of a fat cannot yield net glucose in a mammal, the glycerol portion can.

Inside or at the surface of intracellular compartments, fatty acyl-CoA synthetases attach coenzyme A to a fatty acid. Different isoforms show chain-length and localisation preferences. Activation creates an acyl thioester, R–CO–SCoA, that can be recognised by oxidation or biosynthetic enzymes. This is a commitment to an activated pool, not necessarily a commitment to oxidation: activated fatty acids can also be incorporated into membrane and storage lipids.

The reaction proceeds in two chemical stages. First the fatty acid carboxyl group reacts with ATP to form an acyl-adenylate intermediate (acyl-AMP) and pyrophosphate (PPi). Next the thiol group of coenzyme A attacks the activated acyl group, producing fatty acyl-CoA and releasing AMP. Cellular inorganic pyrophosphatase hydrolyses PPi to two orthophosphate ions. By removing a product, this strongly favours overall activation and prevents appreciable reversal under normal cellular conditions.

Net reaction: Fatty acid + CoA–SH + ATP + H₂O → fatty acyl-CoA + AMP + 2 Pi (proton and ionic conventions omitted).

Why is this reaction assigned a cost of two ATP equivalents rather than one? Only one ATP molecule is consumed directly, but ATP is converted to AMP rather than ADP. Restoring AMP to the ATP pool requires the equivalent of two phosphoryl-transfer steps. The energetic accounting counts high-energy phosphoryl bonds, not simply molecules bearing the name ATP. An exam question that begins with free palmitate must subtract two ATP equivalents; one that begins with palmitoyl-CoA already activated must not subtract them a second time.

Activation does not make the fatty acid more oxidised. Its carbon skeleton and number of double bonds are unchanged. Instead, the reaction makes acyl transfer easier by forming a thioester. This is analogous to the useful chemical activation of acetyl groups encountered in pyruvate oxidation. CoA is derived from pantothenic acid (vitamin B5); its sulfhydryl group is the reactive part that carries the acyl chain. The same fatty acid may be activated in different compartments depending on whether it is to be stored, modified or degraded. To understand mitochondrial oxidation we must now solve the transport problem created by this activation.

3. The carnitine shuttle: crossing a selective mitochondrial membrane

The major site of mammalian fatty acid oxidation is the mitochondrial matrix, where the enzymes of beta oxidation lie near the citric acid cycle and the respiratory chain. Yet an activated long-chain fatty acid formed outside the matrix cannot simply cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. Coenzyme A is large and charged, and the inner membrane maintains separate matrix and cytosolic CoA pools. A transport mechanism must move the acyl group without allowing unrestricted passage of CoA.

Carnitine is a small molecule with a hydroxyl group that can form an ester with a fatty acyl group. The carnitine shuttle uses this ester as a temporary transport form. Its three functional components act in sequence. The first, carnitine palmitoyltransferase I (CPT I), is associated with the outer mitochondrial membrane, with its catalytic site accessible from the cytosolic side. It transfers the long-chain acyl group from CoA to carnitine. The reaction releases CoA outside the matrix and produces acylcarnitine. CPT I is not the carrier across the inner membrane; it prepares the substrate for that carrier.

The second component is carnitine–acylcarnitine translocase (CACT) in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It transports acylcarnitine inward in exchange for free carnitine moving outward. Exchange helps sustain the pool of carnitine available on each side. The third enzyme, carnitine palmitoyltransferase II (CPT II), is located on the matrix-facing side of the inner membrane. CPT II transfers the incoming acyl group from carnitine to matrix CoA, regenerating fatty acyl-CoA in the compartment containing beta-oxidation enzymes. Free carnitine can return through the translocase. Thus the chain has crossed the barrier, but the CoA molecule originally used for activation has not.

No additional ATP hydrolysis is directly assigned to the CPT I–CACT–CPT II cycle in conventional ATP-yield calculations. The acyl group is passed between thioester and oxygen ester forms. The cost of ATP to AMP during initial activation has already been included. The process depends on membrane organisation and available pools of CoA and carnitine, and it should not be confused with an ATP-driven membrane pump.

Chain length matters. Short- and many medium-chain fatty acids can enter mitochondria without obligatory use of the long-chain carnitine shuttle and can be activated within the matrix. The exact dependence varies with chain length, tissue and species. Very-long-chain fatty acids are frequently shortened first in peroxisomes; this route is considered later. A transport defect therefore need not affect every fatty acid in the same way. This is a powerful diagnostic principle: if mitochondria oxidise octanoate but not palmitate, their respiratory chain is unlikely to be completely inactive, and an upstream long-chain-specific transport or oxidation step becomes a candidate.

CPT I is an important control point because malonyl-CoA, an intermediate in fatty acid biosynthesis, inhibits it. When the cytosolic cell is making fatty acids, high malonyl-CoA can restrict their entry into mitochondrial oxidation. This control connects two pathways before either can waste large amounts of resources in simultaneous opposing fluxes. The physical separation of CoA pools is therefore not just an obstacle; it creates an opportunity for metabolic regulation.

4. Beta oxidation: the four reactions that shorten a chain

A saturated fatty acyl-CoA now lies inside the matrix. It is not cleaved randomly. The reaction sequence acts on the first few carbons next to the thioester, transforms the beta carbon into a carbonyl group and then breaks the bond between the alpha and beta carbons. The first two carbons leave as acetyl-CoA, and the remaining chain is two carbons shorter. Because the remaining fragment retains an activated thioester, it can immediately enter another cycle. The term beta oxidation refers to the oxidation at carbon 3, not the removal of a single beta carbon.

The original chemical insight was that fatty acids lose two-carbon fragments. Franz Knoop's early twentieth-century experiments used fatty acids bearing a terminal phenyl group and found different urinary products according to whether the starting chain had an odd or even number of carbons: odd phenyl-substituted chains yielded hippurate (the glycine conjugate of benzoate), whereas even chains yielded phenaceturate (the glycine conjugate of phenylacetate). If one-carbon units were removed sequentially without the two-carbon restriction, this stable parity distinction would not be expected. This supported stepwise shortening by two carbons from the carboxyl end rather than arbitrary fragmentation. Later biochemical studies with isolated enzymes and activated substrates established the intervening oxidation, hydration, oxidation and cleavage reactions. The modern pathway explains both the parity observation and why fatty acids supply respiratory electrons.

Reaction 1 — FAD-dependent dehydrogenation

Acyl-CoA dehydrogenase removes hydrogen from the alpha and beta positions, introducing a trans double bond between them. The product is trans-Δ2-enoyl-CoA, not a cis unsaturated fatty acid of the kind usually present in membrane lipids. The enzyme-bound FAD accepts the reducing equivalents. The thioester carbonyl makes the adjacent chemistry accessible, and the enzyme fixes the orientation needed for the trans product.

Acyl-CoA dehydrogenases exist in forms that prefer different chain lengths, including very-long-, long-, medium- and short-chain substrates. Their overlapping specificities allow a single chain to be processed as it becomes shorter. The electron pair accepted by FAD is transferred through electron-transferring flavoprotein (ETF) and ETF:ubiquinone oxidoreductase to ubiquinone in the respiratory chain. It does not enter through complex II, although it joins the ubiquinone pool at an energetically similar level. In textbook ATP accounting this step is represented as one FADH₂ equivalent, with an approximate yield of 1.5 ATP.

FAD is suited to this initial dehydrogenation; unlike NAD-dependent oxidation of an alcohol, forming a carbon–carbon double bond in this substrate is not readily coupled to NAD⁺ under the same conditions. Enzyme-bound flavin and subsequent electron transfer provide the necessary redox pathway. The enzyme itself must be reoxidised for another molecule to react.

Reaction 2 — stereospecific hydration

Enoyl-CoA hydratase adds water across the trans-Δ2 double bond. Hydrogen is added at the alpha carbon and hydroxyl at the beta carbon, giving L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA. No molecular oxygen is added and no ATP is made in this step. Water provides the elements required to turn the beta carbon into an alcohol that can undergo the next oxidation.

The L configuration is crucial because the following dehydrogenase recognises this stereochemistry. Some substrates in alternative pathways contain double bonds in different positions or configurations. Their failure to fit this hydratase is the reason auxiliary enzymes are required for oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. Stereospecificity is not a decorative feature of the pathway; it defines which intermediates can proceed.

Reaction 3 — NAD⁺-dependent dehydrogenation

L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase oxidises the beta hydroxyl group to a keto group. NAD⁺ accepts a hydride and is reduced to NADH, while a proton is released to the surrounding medium. The product, 3-ketoacyl-CoA, has carbonyl groups at positions 1 and 3 with carbon 2 between them. This arrangement makes cleavage of the alpha–beta bond chemically feasible.

NADH usually transfers electrons to complex I of the respiratory chain and gives a conventional average of about 2.5 ATP under coupled conditions. The reaction depends on the availability of NAD⁺. When the mitochondrial NADH/NAD⁺ ratio becomes very high, for example if electron transfer is restricted, this oxidation becomes less favourable. A complete enzyme pathway is not enough to guarantee flux: cofactor recycling by the rest of the metabolic network is essential.

Reaction 4 — thiolytic cleavage

Beta-ketothiolase (acyl-CoA acetyltransferase) uses a second CoA–SH to cleave the bond between carbons 2 and 3. This is thiolysis, because a thiol participates in bond cleavage. It differs from hydrolysis, in which water would be the attacking reagent. The original thioester-containing two-carbon fragment becomes acetyl-CoA. The beta carbon, previously oxidised to a keto group, becomes the new carbonyl carbon of the shortened acyl-CoA. The incoming CoA carries that remaining chain.

For a sixteen-carbon palmitoyl-CoA, the products of the first cycle are acetyl-CoA and a fourteen-carbon myristoyl-CoA. Subsequent cycles give chains of twelve, ten, eight, six and four carbons. When a four-carbon acyl-CoA completes one final cycle, both resulting fragments contain two carbons, so two acetyl-CoA molecules emerge from that last cycle. There is no additional cycle needed to oxidise acetyl-CoA by beta oxidation; the citric acid cycle is responsible for its further oxidation when conditions permit.

The four reactions can be summarised for a saturated even-chain acyl-CoA of at least four carbons:

Acyl-CoA (Cₙ) + FAD + NAD⁺ + H₂O + CoA–SH → acyl-CoA (Cₙ₋₂) + acetyl-CoA + FADH₂ equivalent + NADH + H⁺.

Here the FAD notation represents electron transfer through enzyme-bound flavin and ETF rather than accumulation of freely diffusible mitochondrial FADH₂. The net reaction neither requires oxygen as a direct chemical substrate nor forms ATP directly. Nevertheless, continued mitochondrial beta oxidation ordinarily depends on respiration because the reduced cofactors must be reoxidised. This distinction between direct and indirect oxygen requirement is essential when interpreting experiments involving anoxia or respiratory inhibitors.

Long-chain substrates in mammalian mitochondria are often handled by a membrane-associated trifunctional protein providing hydratase, hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase and thiolase activities; shorter chains can use matrix enzymes. This arrangement improves handling of hydrophobic intermediates. The underlying four-reaction chemistry is the same, and the shortening process continues until the chain has been converted to the appropriate terminal product.

5. Deriving ATP yields rather than memorising numbers

The energetic contribution of fatty acid oxidation is distributed across three processes. First, every ordinary beta-oxidation cycle transfers one electron pair to the ubiquinone pool through ETF and one to NAD⁺. Second, each acetyl-CoA can be oxidised through the citric acid cycle. Third, ATP or GTP generated by substrate-level phosphorylation and ATP produced by the respiratory chain are combined into a common ATP-equivalent ledger. The initial activation cost must then be subtracted if the starting substance was a free fatty acid.

For a saturated even-chain fatty acid with n carbons, all carbons eventually appear in n/2 acetyl-CoA molecules. The number of cycles is one less than the number of acetyl units: n/2 − 1. This follows directly from the final cleavage, which yields two acetyl-CoA molecules. Each cycle gives one NADH and one flavin-linked electron pair. Using the conventional P/O values of 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FAD-linked pair, the combined value per beta cycle is 4 ATP equivalents.

In one turn of the citric acid cycle, one acetyl-CoA gives three NADH, one FAD-linked electron pair and one GTP (or ATP equivalent). The yield is therefore 3 × 2.5 + 1 × 1.5 + 1 = 10 ATP equivalents. This assumes that the citric acid cycle runs to completion and that the respiratory chain is coupled. No ATP is counted twice: the beta-cycle cofactors were made before acetyl-CoA entered the citric acid cycle, while the three NADH and one flavin pair in the latter are generated during its oxidation.

Net ATP = 4(n/2 − 1) + 10(n/2) − 2 = 7n − 6. The last term represents activation of the free fatty acid. The formula is valid only for saturated even-chain fatty acids fully oxidised in the mitochondrial system under the specified P/O convention. If activation has already occurred, the result is 7n − 4. Unsaturation, odd carbon numbers, peroxisomal shortening and incomplete oxidation require separate adjustments.

For palmitate (16:0), eight acetyl-CoA are formed by seven cycles. Seven NADH give 17.5 ATP equivalents and seven FAD-linked pairs give 10.5, totalling 28 from beta oxidation. Eight acetyl-CoA contribute 80. Gross yield = 108; net yield = 106 after activation. The corresponding aggregate reduced-carrier count before oxidative phosphorylation is 31 NADH (7 + 24), 15 FAD-linked pairs (7 + 8) and 8 GTP. These provide 77.5 + 22.5 + 8 − 2 = 106, an independent cross-check of the same calculation.

A free stearate molecule (18:0) gives nine acetyl-CoA and eight cycles. The ledger is 90 + 32 − 2 = 120 ATP equivalents. Adding two carbons to a saturated even chain increases the yield by 14 ATP: one extra beta cycle supplies 4, and one additional acetyl-CoA supplies 10. A free laurate molecule (12:0) gives six acetyl-CoA and five cycles, yielding 60 + 20 − 2 = 78. The difference between laurate and palmitate is 28, exactly as predicted by two extra cycles and two extra acetyl units.

Older examination material may use 3 ATP per NADH and 2 per FADH₂. Under those assumptions one acetyl-CoA yields 12 ATP equivalents and one beta cycle yields 5; free palmitate yields 8 × 12 + 7 × 5 − 2 = 129. Neither 129 nor 106 should be selected without reading the convention in the question. The same carbon and electron counts support both answers; what differs is the estimated coupling efficiency.

ATP yields are approximate even with stated P/O values. Proton leak, membrane transport and tissue-specific respiratory coupling alter the amount of ATP actually captured. Oxidative phosphorylation is not an integer machine that deposits a fixed number of ATP molecules for every individual electron pair. The calculation represents an average energetic equivalence and is particularly useful when comparing substrates under matched assumptions.

An independent oxygen-balance check is possible. Complete combustion of palmitic acid C₁₆H₃₂O₂ has the overall equation C₁₆H₃₂O₂ + 23 O₂ → 16 CO₂ + 16 H₂O. In the biochemical ledger, 31 NADH and 15 flavin-linked pairs donate 46 electron pairs. Reduction of one O₂ to water consumes four electrons, or two pairs. The theoretical oxygen requirement is therefore 46/2 = 23 O₂, agreeing with the combustion equation. This agreement checks electron balance without using an ATP yield assumption. It does not imply that all oxygen consumption in a cell is due to palmitate, or that every oxygen molecule passes through one specific enzyme.

6. Odd-chain fatty acids and the propionyl-CoA exit

Not every fatty acid contains an even number of carbons. Odd-chain fatty acids occur naturally in some foods and microbial products. They enter the same four-reaction pathway and repeatedly lose two-carbon units. Once the chain reaches five carbons, the last beta cycle cleaves it into one two-carbon acetyl-CoA and one three-carbon propionyl-CoA. Propionyl-CoA is not a suitable substrate for another ordinary beta-oxidation cycle. It is therefore routed through three specialised reactions that connect the remaining carbon atoms with the citric acid cycle.

Propionyl-CoA carboxylase first adds bicarbonate-derived carbon at the alpha carbon of propionyl-CoA, producing D-methylmalonyl-CoA. The enzyme requires biotin (vitamin B7) and ATP. Biotin acts as a carrier of activated carbon dioxide in this carboxylation, while ATP is converted to ADP and Pi. Unlike fatty acid activation, this reaction costs one ATP equivalent. The product is a four-carbon compound but its carbon skeleton is branched.

Methylmalonyl-CoA epimerase then interconverts D- and L-methylmalonyl-CoA. This step does not change the molecular formula or oxidation state; it establishes the stereochemistry needed for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. The mutase uses adenosylcobalamin, a coenzyme derived from vitamin B12, to rearrange the carbon skeleton to succinyl-CoA. No extra ATP or NADH is directly consumed by the epimerase or mutase reactions. The rearrangement uses radical chemistry, but the detailed radical steps are not required to predict its metabolic products.

Succinyl-CoA is a citric acid cycle intermediate. It can proceed through succinyl-CoA synthetase, succinate dehydrogenase, fumarase and malate dehydrogenase to oxaloacetate. This sequence yields one GTP, one FAD-linked pair and one NADH, corresponding to 5 ATP equivalents under the modern convention. Subtracting the ATP required for propionyl-CoA carboxylation gives a net 4 ATP equivalents for this particular endpoint. It is important to state the endpoint because oxaloacetate remains and not all propionyl-derived carbon has yet been oxidised to CO₂. The value is a standard exam ledger for conversion to oxaloacetate, not an assertion that three propionyl carbons have been completely combusted in those four reactions.

Consider free heptadecanoate (17:0). Seven beta cycles release seven acetyl-CoA and one propionyl-CoA, since 7 × 2 + 3 = 17. The beta cycles yield 28 ATP equivalents, acetyl groups contribute 70, and the propionyl-to-oxaloacetate sequence contributes 4 net. Activation costs 2. The stated ledger gives 28 + 70 + 4 − 2 = 100 ATP equivalents. If a question instead ends at propionyl-CoA, the last contribution is omitted. A student must not use 7n − 6 for odd chains because the final fragment is not acetyl-CoA.

The propionyl pathway has an important consequence for glucose metabolism. Mammals cannot convert acetyl-CoA into a net gain of oxaloacetate through their ordinary citric acid cycle: acetyl entry is accompanied by two decarboxylations over the cycle, and pyruvate dehydrogenase is effectively irreversible. In contrast, conversion of propionyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA adds a new intermediate to the pool. This is an anaplerotic reaction, meaning it replenishes cycle intermediates. Oxaloacetate derived from that pool can contribute to gluconeogenesis. Odd-chain fatty acids thus contain a potentially glucogenic terminal portion, even though their acetyl-CoA portions remain subject to the same restriction as even-chain fatty acids.

Biotin deficiency or a defect in propionyl-CoA carboxylase interferes before methylmalonyl-CoA formation. A defect in methylmalonyl-CoA mutase or inadequate functional cobalamin causes accumulation of methylmalonate-related metabolites instead. These observations link vitamin chemistry to pathway localisation. They also illustrate why two vitamin-dependent enzymes in the same sequence cannot substitute for one another: one performs carboxylation and the other a skeletal rearrangement.

7. Unsaturated chains: why double-bond position changes the energy ledger

The enzymes of beta oxidation are designed around a precise sequence of intermediates. A naturally occurring cis double bond can obstruct this sequence when shortening brings it near the carboxyl end. The first reaction normally produces a trans-Δ2 double bond; the hydratase needs that particular geometry and position. A pre-existing cis bond elsewhere in the chain does not automatically satisfy the requirement. Auxiliary enzymes convert unsuitable double-bond arrangements into the standard intermediate.

Oleate (18:1 cis-Δ9) illustrates the simpler case. After three ordinary cycles the chain is twelve carbons long and its original double bond has moved from Δ9 to cis-Δ3 in the shortened chain. This change in numbering occurs because each cycle removes two carbons from the carboxyl end. The cis-Δ3-enoyl-CoA cannot directly undergo the hydration reaction. Enoyl-CoA isomerase moves the double bond to the Δ2 position and changes its configuration to trans. The product can now enter the hydration step, bypassing the acyl-CoA dehydrogenase reaction for that cycle.

The total carbon yield is still nine acetyl-CoA, and the chain undergoes eight shortening cycles. The NADH-producing and cleavage reactions still occur eight times, but only seven FAD-linked pairs are generated by the initial dehydrogenase. Relative to stearate, oleate therefore yields 1.5 fewer ATP equivalents under the modern convention. Its net yield is 120 − 1.5 = 118.5 ATP equivalents. An existing double bond represents carbon that is already partially oxidised; fewer reducing equivalents are available. The isomerase does not itself consume ATP or NADPH in this example.

Polyunsaturated fatty acids may create a second type of obstacle. A pre-existing double bond at an even-numbered position can produce a conjugated 2,4-dienoyl-CoA intermediate when the ordinary acyl-CoA dehydrogenase introduces a trans-Δ2 bond. The normal hydratase cannot process the conjugated arrangement efficiently. NADPH-dependent 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase converts it into a trans-Δ3-enoyl-CoA, which enoyl-CoA isomerase then converts to trans-Δ2-enoyl-CoA. The standard hydration, NAD-dependent oxidation and thiolysis can resume.

For linoleate (18:2 cis-Δ9,12), three ordinary cycles expose cis-Δ3,6-dienoyl-CoA. Isomerase permits another shortening, leaving a cis-Δ4 double bond in the ten-carbon chain. The ordinary dehydrogenase generates the 2,4 conjugated intermediate; reductase uses NADPH, then isomerase restores the trans-Δ2 intermediate. That cycle did generate the flavin-linked pair, unlike the earlier isomerase-only cycle. Thus for this conventional route one FAD-linked pair is bypassed and one NADPH is consumed relative to stearate, not automatically one bypassed pair for each original double bond. If an exercise assigns an opportunity cost of 2.5 ATP equivalents per NADPH, the comparative ledger is 120 − 1.5 − 2.5 = 116. If NADPH cost is not specified, report the cofactor consumption separately. NADPH is not normally oxidised by complex I as if it were an identical matrix NADH molecule; the numerical equivalence is an explicit simplifying convention.

The exact auxiliary steps depend on double-bond position and arrangement. For a complex unsaturated chain, tracing the remaining carbon number and Δ position after each cleavage is more reliable than memorising a universal subtraction rule. Carbon accounting and redox accounting answer different questions: all eighteen carbons may still yield nine acetyl units, but the number of donated or consumed electron pairs changes.

In addition to being important fuels, unsaturated fatty acids determine the physical properties of membrane lipids and supply precursors for signalling molecules. Their biosynthetic origin will be discussed after the oxidation pathways. The cell must be able to oxidise these structurally useful chains without requiring an entirely new degradation cycle for each possible pattern of double bonds. A small set of auxiliary enzymes achieves that flexibility.

8. Peroxisomal, alpha and omega oxidation: necessary exceptions

Mitochondria are not the only organelles able to shorten fatty acids. Peroxisomes contain a related beta-oxidation system that is especially important for very-long-chain substrates and some branched or unusual lipids. Their first oxidative reaction differs from the mitochondrial one in the destination of electrons. Peroxisomal acyl-CoA oxidase transfers electrons from its reduced flavin directly to molecular oxygen, forming hydrogen peroxide. Catalase then converts hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen or participates in related detoxifying reactions. Energy from this initial electron transfer is not captured through the mitochondrial ubiquinone pool and does not generate the usual 1.5 ATP equivalents locally.

The remaining peroxisomal reactions use hydration, NAD-dependent oxidation and thiolysis to shorten the chain. Peroxisomes lack the ordinary mitochondrial respiratory chain, so reduced cofactors and shortened acyl products must be handled through other routes. Shortened products may be transferred to mitochondria for more extensive oxidation. Acetyl units can also be exported through appropriate metabolic forms. For this reason a mitochondrial ATP formula cannot be blindly applied to a very-long-chain substrate that undergoes initial peroxisomal processing. The chain lengths and destination of reducing equivalents must be specified.

A second exception arises when a methyl branch blocks the beta carbon. Phytanic acid, a branched fatty acid derived from phytol-related sources, contains a methyl group at carbon 3. The usual conversion of carbon 3 to a keto group and subsequent cleavage cannot proceed in the standard way with that substitution. Peroxisomal alpha oxidation first hydroxylates carbon 2 of phytanoyl-CoA and subsequently removes one carbon from the carboxyl end. The resulting pristanic acid has its first methyl branch in a position compatible with a specialised beta-oxidation route after appropriate activation and stereochemical handling. This explains why loss of the alpha-oxidation machinery causes phytanic acid accumulation in Refsum disease. The one-carbon removal is the defining feature of alpha oxidation; it is not an alternative spelling for beta oxidation.

A third route, omega oxidation, begins at the terminal methyl group rather than the carboxyl end. Microsomal cytochrome P450-dependent enzymes in the endoplasmic reticulum hydroxylate that terminal carbon using oxygen and reducing equivalents. Subsequent oxidation of the alcohol to an aldehyde and then a carboxyl group yields a dicarboxylic acid. The product can undergo further shortening, including peroxisomal beta oxidation. Omega oxidation is normally a relatively minor route but can become more prominent when mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation is impaired. Urinary dicarboxylic acids can therefore reveal altered lipid metabolism, although they alone do not identify one specific enzyme defect.

Plants use peroxisomal beta oxidation prominently, including in specialised seed peroxisomes called glyoxysomes. During germination, acetyl-CoA from stored fat can support the glyoxylate cycle, which bypasses the decarboxylating reactions of the citric acid cycle through isocitrate lyase and malate synthase. The resulting four-carbon intermediates can contribute to carbohydrate formation. Mammals do not possess this complete glyoxylate-cycle pathway. Thus the statement that fatty acids cannot produce net glucose must always be restricted to the relevant carbon type and organism: even-chain acetyl units cannot yield net glucose through ordinary mammalian pathways, but plants and many microorganisms possess a different metabolic solution.

These exceptions show that oxidation is a family of chemical strategies rather than a single universal ATP formula. The location of a reaction, the position of a branch or double bond and the final electron acceptor determine the pathway outcome.

9. Building fatty acids: citrate export and the source of reducing power

When carbohydrate is plentiful and energy demand is satisfied, a cell can redirect carbon to fatty acid synthesis. Glucose is converted through glycolysis to pyruvate, which enters mitochondria and is oxidatively decarboxylated by pyruvate dehydrogenase to acetyl-CoA. Yet the main mammalian fatty acid synthase operates in the cytosol. Matrix acetyl-CoA cannot freely traverse the inner mitochondrial membrane. Just as oxidation required a transport solution for long-chain acyl groups, synthesis requires a transport solution for two-carbon acetyl groups.

The cell solves this problem using citrate. Citrate synthase combines mitochondrial acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate, forming citrate. When citrate is available in excess of immediate oxidative needs, it can leave the matrix through a mitochondrial citrate transport system. In the cytosol, ATP-citrate lyase cleaves citrate in the presence of CoA and ATP to regenerate acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate. The ATP expenditure is the price of making a cytosolic activated acetyl group from the transported citrate.

The cytosolic oxaloacetate cannot simply diffuse back into the mitochondrial matrix. Cytosolic malate dehydrogenase can reduce it to malate using NADH. Malate can return to the mitochondrion, or cytosolic NADP-dependent malic enzyme can oxidatively decarboxylate malate to pyruvate, making NADPH and releasing CO₂. Pyruvate can return to the matrix. Pyruvate carboxylase can then regenerate oxaloacetate using bicarbonate and ATP. This branch links acetyl export with the production of reducing power, but its energetic cost must be recognised. The two pathways for malate have different cofactor and ATP consequences.

The oxidative pentose phosphate pathway provides another major source of cytosolic NADPH. As established in Node 1.E.05, oxidation of glucose 6-phosphate to ribulose 5-phosphate produces two NADPH per passage and releases one CO₂. Cytosolic NADP-dependent isocitrate dehydrogenase can also contribute in appropriate tissues. The relative importance of these sources varies with the organism, tissue and metabolic state. It is not generally correct to claim that every NADPH needed for synthesis is generated exclusively by one pathway.

Why does synthesis require NADPH instead of NADH? The chemistry needs a reducing hydride donor, but separate cofactor pools support separate metabolic objectives. In many cells the NADPH/NADP⁺ ratio is kept relatively high, favouring reduction, while the NAD⁺/NADH balance supports oxidation of fuels. Enzyme specificity lets reductive biosynthesis continue without directly competing for the same cofactor pool used by mitochondrial energy metabolism. The phosphate on NADPH is a recognition feature, not a source of extra transferable electrons.

This stage supplies cytosolic acetyl-CoA and NADPH but does not yet form the growing hydrocarbon chain. The next challenge is to create carbon–carbon bonds in a favourable direction. The solution is to invest ATP in a temporary carboxyl group that is later released during chain extension.

10. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase and the role of malonyl-CoA

Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) converts a two-carbon acetyl group to a three-carbon malonyl group. The reaction uses bicarbonate, ATP and biotin. This is the committed carboxylation step of de novo fatty acid synthesis: the product serves as the activated two-carbon donor after decarboxylation during chain extension.

Acetyl-CoA + HCO₃⁻ + ATP → malonyl-CoA + ADP + Pi.

The chemistry proceeds through two coordinated catalytic events. First, ATP activates bicarbonate and enables transfer of a carboxyl group to biotin, which is covalently attached to a lysine residue on the enzyme. The biotin-bearing domain moves the activated carboxyl group to the carboxyltransferase active site. Second, the carboxyl group is transferred to the methyl carbon of acetyl-CoA, forming malonyl-CoA. Biotin is regenerated rather than consumed. This mobile cofactor links two reaction centres, a strategy that also appears in other biotin-dependent carboxylases such as pyruvate carboxylase and propionyl-CoA carboxylase.

Why add carbon dioxide if the final fatty acid contains only acetyl-derived carbons? A direct condensation between two activated acetyl groups is not sufficiently favourable to sustain repeated biosynthetic extension under cellular conditions. Malonyl-CoA contains an additional carboxyl group that can be lost as CO₂ during condensation. Decarboxylation helps generate a reactive carbon nucleophile and provides a thermodynamic driving force for formation of the new carbon–carbon bond. ATP was invested earlier at ACC, and the energy of that investment supports the later condensation. The temporary bicarbonate carbon is not retained in the final chain.

The regulation of ACC affects more than the rate of synthesis. Mammals express ACC1, associated largely with provision of malonyl-CoA for lipogenesis, and ACC2, often associated with regulation of mitochondrial fatty acid entry through its local malonyl-CoA pool. These functions overlap in a tissue-dependent manner. Citrate is an allosteric activator of ACC and promotes its active organisational state; long-chain acyl-CoA products such as palmitoyl-CoA act as feedback inhibitors. Low energy availability activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which phosphorylates ACC and decreases its activity. Insulin-associated signalling in the fed state favours ACC activation and increased lipogenic capacity, including changes in enzyme expression. The precise hormonal effects vary with tissue and physiological context.

The link with CPT I provides a second reason to monitor malonyl-CoA. When ACC activity is high, malonyl-CoA serves as a substrate for chain extension and also inhibits mitochondrial entry of long-chain fatty acyl groups. When ACC is inhibited, less malonyl-CoA is made, allowing CPT I activity to increase if other requirements are met. Malonyl-CoA decarboxylase can also influence this pool by converting malonyl-CoA back to acetyl-CoA. A signal at a single metabolite can therefore coordinate carbon allocation between biosynthesis and degradation.

ACC and fatty acid synthase are different enzymes. ACC uses ATP to make malonyl-CoA but does not perform repeated chain extension. Fatty acid synthase uses malonyl-derived carbon and NADPH to perform extension but does not directly substitute for ACC. In an experimental preparation where ACC is specifically inhibited, externally supplied malonyl-CoA can allow synthesis to continue if the synthase and other substrates are functional. Such an experiment separates the role of making the extender from the role of using it.

11. Fatty acid synthase: how a molecular assembly line makes palmitate

Mammalian cytosolic fatty acid synthase (FAS, often called FASN) is a large multifunctional type I enzyme that operates as a homodimer. Its catalytic domains perform a sequence of acyl transfer, condensation, reduction, dehydration and reduction. An acyl carrier protein domain (ACP) carries intermediates between these catalytic sites. ACP contains a flexible 4′-phosphopantetheine arm with a terminal thiol derived from pantothenic acid. Acyl groups form thioesters with this thiol, allowing them to move between active sites without diffusing away as free acids.

The synthase also contains a ketosynthase (KS) active-site cysteine thiol. The two thiol attachment sites play different roles. The KS cysteine holds the growing chain just before condensation, while ACP carries the malonyl group and then carries the product through subsequent transformations. Malonyl/acetyltransferase (MAT) loads acetyl and malonyl groups from their CoA forms onto the ACP system. The acetyl primer is transferred to KS; malonyl is loaded on ACP. Free CoA is released during these loading reactions.

The acetyl primer is the initial two-carbon fragment. Each malonyl group is an extender that supplies two additional retained carbons after loss of its extra carboxyl carbon. The distinction between primer and extender is indispensable for isotope questions. The acetyl primer ultimately supplies the omega end of the chain, whereas the last malonyl unit supplies the two carbons nearest the carboxyl end. During extension the new carbon atoms are added at the carboxyl end, not at the terminal methyl end.

In the first condensation, ketosynthase combines the acetyl group on KS with the malonyl group on ACP. Decarboxylation of malonyl enables a carbon–carbon bond to form, generating four-carbon acetoacetyl-ACP and releasing CO₂. The growing chain is now attached to ACP. The product resembles the 3-ketoacyl intermediate of beta oxidation, but it is not carried on free CoA and the enzyme system is directed toward reduction rather than cleavage.

The beta-keto group must be reduced to produce a saturated fatty acid. Beta-ketoacyl-ACP reductase (KR) transfers reducing equivalents from NADPH to the carbonyl group, forming D-3-hydroxyacyl-ACP. This stereochemical product is different from the L-hydroxyacyl-CoA encountered in mitochondrial beta oxidation. The distinction demonstrates that biosynthesis is not merely the same set of enzymes working in reverse. Separate proteins recognise different carrier molecules and intermediate configurations.

The dehydratase (DH) removes water from the hydroxyacyl intermediate to form a trans-Δ2-enoyl-ACP. At this stage the chain has a carbon–carbon double bond, but this is a temporary biosynthetic intermediate rather than the final unsaturation pattern of the fatty acid. Enoyl-ACP reductase (ER) uses a second NADPH to reduce the double bond, producing saturated butyryl-ACP. One elongation round has therefore transformed a two-carbon primer into a four-carbon saturated acyl chain using one malonyl group and two NADPH.

For the next round the butyryl group is transferred to the KS cysteine and ACP is reloaded with another malonyl group. Condensation gives a six-carbon keto intermediate; the two reduction reactions and dehydration then yield hexanoyl-ACP. The cycle repeats with the chain increasing by two carbons each time. The growing chain remains tethered to the enzyme, allowing the individual catalytic domains to operate as a coordinated assembly line.

After seven extension rounds the product is palmitoyl-ACP, containing sixteen carbons. The thioesterase domain hydrolyses the terminal thioester to release free palmitate and regenerate the ACP thiol. Palmitate is the principal product of mammalian FAS; specialised tissues and enzyme variants can produce other chain lengths. The final product is free palmitate rather than palmitoyl-CoA, so additional activation may be needed if it is to be elongated or esterified into complex lipids.

Many bacteria and plastids use type II fatty acid synthesis, where the catalytic activities are largely separate proteins and ACP is a distinct carrier. The same broad chemical logic of condensation and reduction applies, but the protein organisation is different. Plant plastid synthesis is an important contrast to mammalian cytosolic synthesis. Mammalian mitochondria also possess a specialised fatty acid synthesis system for particular cellular needs; the major palmitate-producing pathway studied here is the cytosolic type I system. Avoid confusing enzyme organisation with the direction of the chemical reactions.

12. Stoichiometry, ATP cost and tracing newly synthesised carbon

Palmitate contains sixteen carbons. One acetyl-CoA supplies the primer, and seven malonyl-CoA supply fourteen retained carbons. Because every malonyl-CoA is produced from one acetyl-CoA, the total carbon requirement is eight acetyl-CoA. Formation of seven malonyl groups by ACC costs seven ATP molecules, each converted to ADP and inorganic phosphate. Each extension round consumes two NADPH, so seven rounds require fourteen NADPH.

The overall FAS reaction can be expressed as:

Acetyl-CoA + 7 malonyl-CoA + 14 NADPH + 14 H⁺ → palmitic acid + 8 CoA–SH + 7 CO₂ + 14 NADP⁺ + 6 H₂O.

Seven dehydration reactions release seven water molecules, but hydrolytic release of palmitate from ACP consumes one. Six water molecules therefore appear in the combined equation. Loading of eight acyl groups releases eight CoA molecules. The additional seven carbon atoms provided by bicarbonate are released as CO₂ during the seven condensation reactions. In the conventional combined biochemical equation that cancels the temporary inorganic carbon pool (acid/base species and protons depend on the chosen convention), the net synthesis is often written:

8 acetyl-CoA + 7 ATP + 14 NADPH + 14 H⁺ → palmitic acid + 8 CoA–SH + 7 ADP + 7 Pi + 14 NADP⁺ + 6 H₂O.

The carbon and cofactor ledger is invariant despite alternative choices of bicarbonate, CO₂ and proton notation. A frequent error is to count sixteen acetyl-CoA molecules because the product has sixteen carbons. Acetyl-CoA already carries two carbons. Another error is to count eight rounds rather than seven. The initial two-carbon primer is loaded without a chain extension; seven two-carbon additions complete the chain.

The seven ATP cost refers to formation of malonyl-CoA from already available cytosolic acetyl-CoA. If the problem begins with eight mitochondrial acetyl groups exported through citrate and cleaved by ATP-citrate lyase, eight additional ATP molecules are required for cleavage, giving fifteen direct ATP equivalents for these two sets of reactions. Further costs can arise from the route chosen to recycle oxaloacetate, especially pyruvate carboxylase in the malic enzyme branch. Do not add a universal extra eight ATP unless that route and the assumed carbon flux are specified. Similarly, the opportunity cost of NADPH is not fixed by the synthesis equation; it depends on how the cell generates NADPH and what would otherwise happen to the carbon and electrons.

Suppose a simplified cell obtains all fourteen NADPH from independent single passages of the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway. Each passage produces two NADPH, so seven glucose 6-phosphate molecules are required for those passages, yielding seven CO₂ and seven ribulose 5-phosphate molecules. This does not imply that seven glucose molecules are consumed solely to make one palmitate under all circumstances; nonoxidative rearrangements and carbon recycling can change glucose requirements, and glucose must also supply acetyl carbon and energy. The question must specify which pathways are included in the balance.

Carbon labelling reveals which parts of the reaction remain in the product. If only the acetyl primer is uniformly labelled with two carbon-13 atoms and all malonyl extenders are unlabelled, the final palmitate carries label at carbons 15 and 16, numbered from its carboxyl end. If only the last malonyl extender is labelled in its two acetyl-derived carbons, the label appears at carbons 1 and 2. If only bicarbonate is labelled in an idealised closed reaction without isotope recycling, the newly added bicarbonate-derived carbon is released during condensation and is not retained in palmitate. In real cells, labelled inorganic carbon can enter other carboxylation reactions and eventually recycle into metabolic precursors; a whole-cell tracer experiment therefore requires caution before making an absolute inference.

Compare the oxidation and synthesis ledgers. Complete oxidation of free palmitate yields about 106 ATP equivalents, whereas synthesis requires at least seven ATP and fourteen NADPH from cytosolic acetyl-CoA. These are not simple inverses with equal and opposite ATP numbers. Oxidation sends the acetyl units into the TCA cycle and respiratory chain; synthesis starts with activated acetyl units, consumes reducing equivalents, and stores energy in reduced carbon. Comparing pathways fairly requires matching starting and ending chemical states, including the activation status and fate of cofactors.

13. Elongation, desaturation and essential fatty acids

Palmitate provides a useful starting point but is not the only fatty acid needed by a cell. Membranes require chains of different lengths and degrees of unsaturation, and some signalling lipids are derived from longer polyunsaturated chains. The cell modifies newly made or dietary fatty acids through elongation and desaturation, mainly in the endoplasmic reticulum in mammals.

Elongation generally begins with a fatty acyl-CoA, rather than the ACP-tethered intermediate of cytosolic FAS. ER elongases add two carbons from malonyl-CoA by decarboxylative condensation. Subsequent reduction, dehydration and reduction produce a longer acyl-CoA using NADPH. Thus palmitoyl-CoA can give stearoyl-CoA (18:0) in one extension and a twenty-carbon chain in another. Different elongases favour different chain lengths and unsaturation patterns. The principle is familiar from the synthase reaction, although the proteins and carrier form differ. The cost of producing the malonyl extender must again be included if calculating the total synthesis expenditure.

Desaturation introduces a double bond into an existing chain. In mammalian ER membranes, stearoyl-CoA desaturase introduces a cis double bond at Δ9; it can convert stearoyl-CoA to oleoyl-CoA and palmitoyl-CoA to palmitoleoyl-CoA. The reaction requires molecular oxygen and a chain of electron-transfer components, commonly including NADH-dependent cytochrome b5 reductase and cytochrome b5. Electrons from the acyl substrate and the electron donor ultimately reduce oxygen to water. The introduction of the double bond is an oxidation of the fatty acyl chain, not an NADPH-dependent hydrogenation.

The difference between Δ and omega notation clarifies nutritional requirements. Stearate (18:0) desaturated at Δ9 gives oleate (18:1 omega-9). Mammals lack the Δ12 and Δ15 activities needed to convert oleate into linoleic acid (18:2 Δ9,12, omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (18:3 Δ9,12,15, omega-3). These two fatty acids must normally be supplied in the diet and are called essential fatty acids. The term does not mean the cell cannot modify them. Mammalian elongases and other desaturases can convert them to longer polyunsaturated derivatives, although conversion efficiency is variable and may be limited.

Linoleate can be converted through Δ6 desaturation, elongation and Δ5 desaturation to arachidonic acid (20:4 Δ5,8,11,14; omega-6). Alpha-linolenate can give longer omega-3 fatty acids, including EPA and through further steps DHA. Elongating a chain at the carboxyl end shifts the Δ position of a pre-existing double bond by two, while leaving its omega family unchanged. For example, elongation of a C18 Δ9 chain gives a C20 Δ11 chain, still omega-9. The terminal end has not moved relative to the existing double bond.

Polyunsaturation changes membrane packing because cis double bonds introduce bends and reduce efficient chain alignment. The physical consequences were introduced in lipid chemistry; here we can explain the metabolic origin of the structural variation. Different pathways adjust chain length and double-bond placement according to cellular requirements. Eicosanoid production from arachidonate and the assembly of phospholipids from these chains belong to the next node, where the fatty acid products will be followed into complex lipids.

14. Reciprocal control: choosing between oxidation and synthesis

The cell now has two chemically distinct pathways that act in opposite directions. Their regulation must match the physiological state. Consider a liver cell after a carbohydrate-rich meal. Insulin signalling promotes glucose utilisation and storage, and ample carbohydrate provides pyruvate and acetyl-CoA. Citrate availability and lipogenic gene expression support cytosolic acetyl-CoA production and ACC activity. Malonyl-CoA rises, increasing substrate supply for fatty acid synthase and restricting long-chain fatty acid import through CPT I. The direction of net carbon flow shifts toward synthesis and storage. Insulin also suppresses adipose lipolysis, reducing the delivery of fatty acids for hepatic oxidation.

Now consider fasting. Insulin falls and counter-regulatory signals favour mobilisation of stored fuels. Catecholamine-dependent cAMP and protein kinase A signalling in adipose tissue helps mobilise triacylglycerol; glucagon acts prominently on the liver, while direct glucagon effects on human adipose lipolysis are not the main mechanism to assume. Circulating fatty acids become available to tissues. In liver, the fasting signalling state and, where activated, AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of ACC reduce malonyl-CoA production. CPT I inhibition is relieved. More long-chain fatty acids can reach the matrix, and beta oxidation supplies ATP and acetyl-CoA.

AMPK is especially useful for understanding local energy regulation. A high AMP or ADP relative to ATP reflects lower energy availability and promotes AMPK activation. Phosphorylation of ACC decreases its activity. This reduces ATP-consuming lipogenesis while favouring access to oxidative fuel. In contracting skeletal muscle this helps match substrate selection to ATP demand. The response is conditional: dephosphorylation of ACC is not the only determinant of oxidation, and abundant malonyl-CoA is not the only possible limitation. Oxygen supply, carnitine, CoA availability, electron transport capacity and substrate delivery remain essential.

Control also operates within the matrix. High NADH relative to NAD⁺ can restrain hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase. A high acetyl-CoA/CoA ratio can limit thiolytic cleavage and other CoA-dependent processes. Even when CPT I permits more substrate entry, the cycle will slow if the respiratory chain cannot reoxidise NADH or ETF-linked electrons. This is why an inhibitor of oxidative phosphorylation can suppress fatty acid oxidation although the inhibitor may not bind a beta-oxidation enzyme at all.

Short-term regulation involves phosphorylation, allosteric signals and substrate availability. Longer-term regulation changes the abundance of metabolic enzymes. Insulin and nutrient-responsive transcription factors such as SREBP-1c and ChREBP favour expression of lipogenic enzymes in appropriate tissues. PPAR-alpha contributes to induction of fatty acid oxidation and ketogenic capacity during fasting. These names are useful because they distinguish changes occurring over seconds or minutes from changes requiring altered gene expression. The detailed signalling cascades belong to the cell-signalling unit; the examination-level metabolic consequence is that both immediate activity and longer-term capacity change with nutrient availability.

Reciprocal regulation reduces unnecessary ATP and reducing-power expenditure, but it does not imply that synthesis and oxidation can never occur simultaneously anywhere in an organism. Different tissues perform different tasks, and metabolic pathways may have basal or overlapping fluxes. A growing tissue can make membrane lipids while another tissue oxidises stored fatty acids. Even within a cell, subcellular pools and regulated cycling can support lipid turnover. The crucial claim is that the cell limits uncontrolled simultaneous opposing flux under a given metabolic state, not that all activity of one pathway becomes exactly zero.

15. Ketogenesis: exporting surplus acetyl carbon as water-soluble fuel

During fasting, beta oxidation supplies the liver with acetyl-CoA at a high rate. The citric acid cycle can oxidise acetyl-CoA only when an adequate pool of intermediates is available and the redox and energy state permits continued flux. Fasting liver must also maintain blood glucose for tissues that depend on it. Oxaloacetate and related intermediates can be diverted toward gluconeogenesis, while high mitochondrial NADH favours reduction of oxaloacetate to malate and can restrain oxidative cycle reactions. Although oxaloacetate is regenerated during normal cycle operation and is not simply consumed by each acetyl-CoA, competition for its pool and altered redox state can restrict the rate at which new acetyl groups enter the cycle.

An accumulation of acetyl-CoA creates a further problem. Much of the available CoA can become tied up as acetyl-CoA, limiting the free CoA required for continuing fatty acid oxidation. The liver solves these linked problems by converting acetyl groups into ketone bodies. This pathway takes place primarily in liver mitochondria and releases CoA for reuse. Its products are relatively water-soluble and can circulate to extrahepatic tissues as an alternative fuel. Ketogenesis is therefore not a failure of metabolism; under ordinary fasting conditions it is a coordinated adaptation to changing fuel availability.

The three compounds conventionally called ketone bodies are acetoacetate, D-beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone. Acetoacetate is a four-carbon beta-keto acid; beta-hydroxybutyrate is its reduced four-carbon hydroxy acid. Despite its name, beta-hydroxybutyrate does not contain a ketone functional group. Acetone is a neutral three-carbon ketone produced by decarboxylation of acetoacetate. These chemical differences explain their different physiological roles: the first two are usable oxidative fuels, whereas acetone is mainly exhaled or otherwise eliminated/metabolised through minor routes.

The first step of ketogenesis is reversible thiolase condensation of two acetyl-CoA molecules to form acetoacetyl-CoA, releasing free CoA. This resembles the reverse of the final cleavage reaction in beta oxidation. In the next step mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase (HMGCS2) adds a third acetyl-CoA and uses water to produce 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA), a six-carbon intermediate, releasing CoA. This reaction is a key committed control point in hepatic ketogenesis. It is distinct from cytosolic HMG-CoA synthase (HMGCS1), which supplies a related intermediate for cholesterol biosynthesis.

Mitochondrial HMG-CoA lyase then cleaves HMG-CoA into acetoacetate and acetyl-CoA. The third acetyl group introduced in the synthase step is balanced by an acetyl-CoA released in the lyase step, so the net carbon requirement for acetoacetate formation is two acetyl units, not three. Summing the core reactions gives:

2 acetyl-CoA + H₂O → acetoacetate + 2 CoA–SH (protonation conventions omitted).

Acetoacetate may be exported or reduced by mitochondrial D-beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase, using NADH and H⁺ to form D-beta-hydroxybutyrate and NAD⁺. This reaction links the ratio of the two circulating fuels to the mitochondrial redox state. A highly reduced NADH-rich matrix generally favours beta-hydroxybutyrate formation. Acetoacetate can also spontaneously decarboxylate to acetone and CO₂. Acetone does not enter the main ketolysis pathway used by acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate.

Ketogenesis directly produces no ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation. Its value lies in exporting oxidisable carbon and regenerating CoA. Beta-hydroxybutyrate formation also reoxidises NADH in the hepatic matrix, but the reducing power is not destroyed: the receiving tissue can recover an NADH when it oxidises beta-hydroxybutyrate back to acetoacetate. The liver has effectively packaged both acetyl carbon and, in the reduced ketone, one electron pair into transportable fuel.

Why not simply send acetyl-CoA into the bloodstream? CoA-bound metabolites are large, charged and normally confined to intracellular compartments. The ketone bodies solve the transport problem by moving the carbon in smaller soluble molecules. Unlike long-chain fatty acids, their utilisation does not require the carnitine shuttle. During extended fasting the brain increases ketone utilisation, helping spare glucose and reducing the need to generate glucose from amino acid carbon skeletons. The adaptation develops over time and is not an immediate elimination of the brain's glucose requirement.

16. Ketolysis and the energetic value of ketone bodies

Ketone bodies circulate to tissues with the machinery needed to oxidise them. Heart, skeletal muscle, renal cortex and, during sustained fasting, the brain can use them as oxidative fuels. Transport across cell membranes occurs through monocarboxylate transport systems. The relevant enzymes are mitochondrial because the endpoint is acetyl-CoA oxidation through the citric acid cycle. Mature mammalian red blood cells lack mitochondria and therefore cannot perform ketolysis even when ketone body concentrations are high.

The first reaction depends on which ketone arrives. D-beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase oxidises D-beta-hydroxybutyrate to acetoacetate while reducing NAD⁺ to NADH. Acetoacetate itself bypasses this step. The resulting acetoacetate is still a free organic acid and must be activated before it can be cleaved to acetyl-CoA.

Extrahepatic mitochondria use succinyl-CoA:3-oxoacid CoA transferase (SCOT, also called OXCT1 or thiophorase) to transfer the CoA group from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate. The products are succinate and acetoacetyl-CoA. This reaction activates acetoacetate without direct ATP hydrolysis. It is energetically economical compared with an ATP-to-AMP activation, but it is not free: succinyl-CoA is diverted from the succinyl-CoA synthetase step of the citric acid cycle, so one GTP or ATP equivalent that could otherwise have been obtained is forgone. This is an opportunity cost, not the consumption of a GTP molecule by SCOT.

Thiolase then adds CoA–SH and cleaves acetoacetyl-CoA into two acetyl-CoA molecules. These can enter the citric acid cycle. The liver has abundant ketogenic enzymes but normally lacks significant SCOT activity for the principal mitochondrial ketolysis pathway, which is why it exports ketone bodies rather than oxidising them through this route. This difference is a form of tissue specialisation. A liver can oxidise fatty acid-derived acetyl-CoA directly, but it does not ordinarily reclaim its own exported ketone bodies via SCOT.

Using the modern ATP convention, acetoacetate yields two acetyl-CoA worth 20 ATP equivalents, minus one for the foregone succinyl-CoA synthetase reaction. Net yield is approximately 19 ATP equivalents per acetoacetate. D-beta-hydroxybutyrate supplies one extra NADH at its initial oxidation, worth 2.5 ATP equivalents, giving approximately 21.5. The two values refer to utilisation of already delivered ketone bodies, with sufficient oxygen and cycle function. They do not subtract the ATP used earlier to activate a fatty acid in the producing tissue or add the ATP previously produced there by beta oxidation. Whole-organism energy accounting must avoid attributing the same electron pair to both liver and receiving tissue.

For example, four acetoacetate molecules provide eight acetyl-CoA. Oxidising these acetyl units yields 80 ATP equivalents, and four SCOT transfers cost four foregone GTP, giving 76. Four D-beta-hydroxybutyrate instead yield four additional NADH, or ten extra ATP equivalents, for a total of 86. If an exercise explicitly uses older P/O ratios, acetoacetate yields 23 and beta-hydroxybutyrate 26 under that convention. If the question asks only about direct ATP synthesis during ketolysis before acetyl oxidation, the answer is zero; the ATP values are downstream energetic equivalents.

This accounting also illustrates why ketones cannot replace every function of glucose. They provide acetyl groups and, for beta-hydroxybutyrate, reduced cofactor. They cannot support net glucose formation through ordinary mammalian acetyl-CoA metabolism, and cells lacking mitochondria cannot use them for ATP production. Glucose also supplies pentose phosphates, glycolytic intermediates and glycosylation precursors. A fuel that replaces part of oxidative ATP demand does not necessarily replace all biosynthetic functions of another fuel.

17. From normal fasting to metabolic failure: interpreting regulation and redox

A healthy fasting response normally increases lipid oxidation and ketone production in a coordinated manner. As glucose intake stops, glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis help maintain blood glucose. Fatty acids released by adipose tissue support liver and muscle ATP demand, while ketone bodies become increasingly available to the brain and other oxidative tissues. Some insulin remains present in ordinary fasting, limiting uncontrolled adipose fatty acid release. Ketone production and peripheral consumption can approach a regulated balance.

Ketone body concentration is determined by both production and utilisation. A rise in blood beta-hydroxybutyrate does not on its own tell us the rate of hepatic ketogenesis, because extrahepatic uptake and oxidation can also change. A high beta-hydroxybutyrate/acetoacetate ratio often indicates a relatively reduced hepatic mitochondrial NADH/NAD⁺ environment. Under simplified equilibrium conditions at fixed pH, the ratio depends on the NADH/NAD⁺ ratio through the reversible beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase reaction. In whole blood the relationship is approximate because transport, peripheral consumption and time-dependent changes modify it. Students should distinguish a useful redox indicator from a direct measurement of mitochondrial NADH.

In severe insulin deficiency, unrestrained mobilisation of adipose lipid and increased hepatic beta oxidation can produce ketone bodies faster than tissues can oxidise or eliminate them. Acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate contribute to acid loading because they are organic acids that dissociate at physiological pH. Bicarbonate buffering can be overwhelmed, resulting in ketoacidosis. This is not the same as regulated nutritional or fasting ketosis. Acetone can contribute a distinctive breath odour but is not the principal acid responsible for the metabolic disturbance. The purpose of this comparison is to understand the mechanism, not to diagnose disease from a single laboratory value.

The opposite pattern occurs when a person cannot adequately oxidise fatty acids. During fasting the liver needs ATP and reducing equivalents to support gluconeogenesis and acetyl-CoA to activate pyruvate carboxylase and supply ketogenesis. An inherited block in medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (MCAD) can reduce both the supply of hepatic ATP and the formation of ketone bodies from fatty acids. A characteristic biochemical consequence during fasting is hypoketotic hypoglycaemia: low glucose with inappropriately low ketones. Medium-chain acylcarnitines and dicarboxylic acid metabolites can accumulate. The exact pattern depends on the defect, residual activity and metabolic state.

A carnitine transport or CPT defect can similarly restrict use of long-chain fatty acids, but the location of the block affects which acylcarnitines accumulate and whether medium-chain substrates bypass the defect. A deficiency of SCOT instead impairs consumption of ketone bodies by extrahepatic tissues; hepatic production can remain intact. These examples demonstrate a general analytical rule: low product concentration can result from impaired production or increased removal, while high concentration can result from accelerated production or impaired removal. The rest of the biochemical profile is required to locate a bottleneck.

The metabolic coupling to gluconeogenesis also explains a subtle distinction. Fatty acid oxidation supplies much of the energy required to make glucose during fasting, and its acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase. But providing energy for glucose synthesis is not the same as supplying the carbon atoms of glucose. Lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acid carbon can contribute directly, while ordinary even-chain fatty acid acetyl groups do not give net glucose. The propionyl-derived terminal fragment of odd-chain fatty acids is an exception. During extended fasting, ketone utilisation in the brain decreases glucose demand and can indirectly reduce protein breakdown. These connections bring together the main pathways studied in the preceding carbohydrate chapters and the current lipid chapter.

18. Experimental reasoning: distinguish transport, oxidation and synthesis defects

Biochemical experiments are most informative when their substrates and measured outputs are clearly distinguished. The Knoop parity experiments provided evidence for two-carbon chain shortening, but they did not themselves identify the four modern enzymes. Isolated mitochondrial studies permit more precise questions because substrates can be selected to enter the pathway at different points. In a preparation with functional membranes, supplying long-chain fatty acyl-CoA together with carnitine requires CPT I to make acylcarnitine, whereas supplying preformed palmitoylcarnitine bypasses CPT I. Assume equal mitochondrial protein, intact membranes, adequate ADP, oxygen, CoA, carnitine where required and a functional citric acid cycle. These are constructed teaching data, not measurements from a published experiment. The control has high respiration with both long-chain substrates. Preparation X oxidises palmitoyl-CoA plus carnitine poorly but responds nearly normally to palmitoylcarnitine. This localises the likely limitation to the CPT I-dependent entry step or a process specifically required for that first substrate. It does not prove a particular gene mutation; differences in substrate access, membrane preparation or enzyme inhibition could produce a similar pattern.

Preparation Y shows low oxygen consumption for both long-chain inputs but normal octanoate-supported respiration. CPT II or translocase impairment could fit, as could a long-chain-specific dehydrogenation defect. The medium-chain substrate bypasses much of the long-chain import system but still requires downstream cofactors and respiration. Additional measurement of acylcarnitine species or enzyme activity would discriminate the alternatives. Preparation Z gives low rates with all three fatty substrates and with succinate. The result is compatible with a more general respiratory or coupling problem, although the precise respiratory step is not identified by these data. One result cannot be interpreted in isolation from suitable controls.

An analogous strategy can distinguish synthesis defects. In a cytosolic system supplied with acetyl-CoA, ATP, bicarbonate and NADPH, inhibition of ACC should reduce malonyl-CoA formation and therefore palmitate synthesis. Adding malonyl-CoA can rescue synthesis if FAS is intact. A direct FAS inhibitor will not be overcome merely by supplying more malonyl-CoA, though competitive inhibition can show concentration-dependent effects. Measuring both malonyl-CoA concentration and fatty acid product gives better localisation than measuring palmitate alone.

The early study by McGarry, Mannaerts and Foster (1977) connected malonyl-CoA to suppression of hepatic fatty acid oxidation and ketone formation. The logic of this finding is especially instructive: a biosynthetic intermediate can regulate the competing degradation route at its transport step. In a simplified experiment, adding malonyl-CoA should inhibit respiration supported by palmitoyl-CoA plus carnitine more strongly than respiration supported by palmitoylcarnitine, because the latter bypasses CPT I. The inference requires otherwise matched conditions and does not mean malonyl-CoA universally inhibits every fatty acid oxidation enzyme.

Tracer experiments provide a different kind of evidence. If carboxyl carbon 1 of a saturated fatty acid is labelled, the first beta-oxidation cleavage places that label in the carbonyl carbon of acetyl-CoA. The label does not necessarily appear immediately as carbon dioxide in the first turn of the TCA cycle. The initial decarboxylations preferentially release carbons that were already present in oxaloacetate, while the acetyl carbons become distributed through the cycle and can be released in subsequent turns. A student who confuses acetyl-CoA formation with immediate CO₂ release will misinterpret the timing of label recovery.

Together these approaches teach three kinds of reasoning: substrate bypass locates a functional block, product and cofactor measurements distinguish reactions, and carbon labelling tests the fate of specific atoms. The methods are useful at CSIR-NET Part C level without requiring specialist laboratory protocols.

19. Part B: original conceptual and assertion–reason questions

Choose the best answer under the stated mammalian conditions. Reveal the explanation only after considering each alternative.

1. Why does activation of a free fatty acid cost two ATP equivalents?

A. Two ATP molecules are hydrolysed directly; B. ATP becomes AMP and PPi is hydrolysed; C. CPT I consumes ATP; D. Thiolase consumes GTP.

Show answer and reasoning

B. ATP is converted to AMP, and restoring the ATP pool requires two phosphoryl transfers. A counts molecules incorrectly; C and D assign ATP expenditure to reactions that do not directly consume it.

2. Which enzyme transfers a long-chain acyl group to carnitine on the cytosolic side?

A. CPT I; B. CPT II; C. CACT; D. ACC.

Show answer and reasoning

A. CPT I makes acylcarnitine. CPT II regenerates matrix acyl-CoA, CACT transports the ester, and ACC makes malonyl-CoA.

3. Which intermediate directly precedes thiolytic cleavage?

A. trans-Δ2-enoyl-CoA; B. L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA; C. 3-ketoacyl-CoA; D. Malonyl-CoA.

Show answer and reasoning

C. The beta carbonyl enables cleavage. A and B must first undergo hydration or oxidation; D is a biosynthetic extender.

4. The first mitochondrial beta-oxidation electron pair reaches ubiquinone through:

A. Complex II directly; B. ETF and ETF:ubiquinone oxidoreductase; C. Cytochrome c directly; D. NADPH oxidase.

Show answer and reasoning

B. ETF relays electrons from acyl-CoA dehydrogenase. Complex II is succinate dehydrogenase; cytochrome c lies downstream of ubiquinone, and NADPH oxidase is unrelated.

5. Which cofactor is essential for methylmalonyl-CoA mutase?

A. Biotin; B. PLP; C. Adenosylcobalamin; D. Thiamine pyrophosphate.

Show answer and reasoning

C. B12 supports the skeletal rearrangement; biotin is required in the preceding carboxylation, PLP commonly transfers amino groups, and TPP participates in decarboxylation and carbon transfer.

6. Which statement about malonyl-CoA is correct?

A. It activates CPT I; B. It is an oxidation product of acetyl-CoA; C. It donates two retained carbons during FAS condensation and inhibits CPT I; D. Its bicarbonate-derived carbon is retained in palmitate.

Show answer and reasoning

C. It supplies an extender and links synthesis to import regulation. A reverses the regulatory sign; B mistakes carboxylation for oxidation; D ignores decarboxylation.

7. Which hydroxy intermediate is characteristic of mammalian FAS?

A. L-3-hydroxyacyl-CoA; B. D-3-hydroxyacyl-ACP; C. D-lactate; D. Acetoacetate.

Show answer and reasoning

B. FAS uses ACP and the D hydroxy intermediate. A is the ordinary mitochondrial beta-cycle intermediate; C and D belong to other pathways.

8. Which fatty acid is essential in the human diet?

A. Palmitate; B. Stearate; C. Oleate; D. Linoleate.

Show answer and reasoning

D. Mammals lack the Δ12 desaturase needed to make linoleate from oleate. A is the main FAS product, B can be elongated from A, and C can be made by Δ9 desaturation.

9. Assertion: The liver normally exports ketone bodies rather than using them through the major mitochondrial ketolysis route. Reason: Hepatocytes lack significant SCOT activity.

Choose: A. Both true, reason explains assertion; B. Both true, reason does not explain; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Show answer and reasoning

A. SCOT is required to transfer CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate. Its absence explains why the major pathway cannot operate normally in liver.

10. Assertion: Mitochondrial beta oxidation generally needs oxygen for sustained flux. Reason: Oxygen is a direct substrate of mitochondrial enoyl-CoA hydratase.

Choose: A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Show answer and reasoning

C. Respiratory recycling of NADH and flavin-linked electrons depends on oxygen under ordinary aerobic conditions. Hydratase adds water, not molecular oxygen.

11. Assertion: Beta-hydroxybutyrate is a ketone body. Reason: It contains a ketone functional group.

Choose: A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Show answer and reasoning

C. Its classification reflects metabolic origin and fate; chemically it contains a hydroxyl and carboxyl group, not a ketone.

20. Part C: original quantitative and analytical questions

Use 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 per flavin-linked electron pair unless another convention is stated. These are original examination-style problems, not claims of past-year questions.

1. A free saturated C20 fatty acid is completely oxidised. Calculate the ATP yield at 2.5 and 1.5 P/O.

Show answer and reasoning

Ten acetyl-CoA and nine cycles are formed. 10 × 10 + 9 × 4 − 2 = 134 ATP equivalents. The already activated C20 acyl-CoA would yield 136. The final cleavage produces two acetyl units.

2. How many NADH and flavin-linked pairs are produced before oxidative phosphorylation in complete oxidation of free C12:0? Check O₂ consumption.

Show answer and reasoning

Five beta cycles and six TCA turns give NADH = 5 + 18 = 23; flavin pairs = 5 + 6 = 11; GTP = 6. Total electron pairs = 34, requiring 17 O₂. ATP = 23 × 2.5 + 11 × 1.5 + 6 − 2 = 78. Combustion C₁₂H₂₄O₂ + 17 O₂ → 12 CO₂ + 12 H₂O confirms oxygen balance.

3. A textbook reports 129 ATP for palmitate. Under which assumption is this correct?

Show answer and reasoning

Seven beta cycles give 7 × (3 + 2) = 35; eight acetyl-CoA yield 8 × 12 = 96. Subtract two for activation: 129. Modern 2.5 and 1.5 yields 106; neither convention changes the carbon or electron count.

4. A free saturated C15 fatty acid is oxidised with the propionyl fragment taken as far as oxaloacetate. Calculate the ledger.

Show answer and reasoning

Six beta cycles release six acetyl-CoA and one propionyl-CoA. Beta cycles yield 24, acetyl-CoA 60, propionyl conversion 5 − 1 = 4, activation −2: total 86 ATP equivalents. This is an oxaloacetate endpoint, not complete oxidation of that terminal fragment.

5. After four standard beta cycles of a C20 cis-Δ11 fatty acyl-CoA, what double-bond position remains? What is the next adjustment?

Show answer and reasoning

Removing eight carbons leaves C12 cis-Δ3. Enoyl-CoA isomerase changes it to trans-Δ2, bypassing one flavin-linked dehydrogenation. Relative to saturated C20, the net yield is 134 − 1.5 = 132.5 for the free acid.

6. An exercise specifies linoleate oxidation via one bypassed dehydrogenation and one NADPH-dependent reductase. It values NADPH at 2.5 ATP equivalents. Calculate the net yield.

Show answer and reasoning

The C18 saturated baseline is 120. Subtract 1.5 for the bypass and 2.5 for the stated NADPH cost: 116. Do not subtract a second FAD-linked pair solely because there are two original double bonds; follow the specified conjugated-diene route.

7. Mitochondrial palmitoyl-CoA plus carnitine supports respiration at 12% of control. Palmitoylcarnitine supports 94%. Which is the best candidate: CPT I, CPT II, complex III or thiolase?

Show answer and reasoning

CPT I. Providing acylcarnitine bypasses the first transferase, but still requires CPT II, thiolase and the respiratory chain. Those downstream defects would generally not be rescued by this substitution. The observation is consistent with an upstream block, not proof of a specific mutation.

8. A peroxisome performs two initial beta cycles of a very-long-chain substrate. Why is subtracting exactly three ATP from a mitochondrial ledger only a partial correction?

Show answer and reasoning

Two acyl-CoA oxidase reactions transfer electron pairs to oxygen, not ETF–ubiquinone, so the usual 2 × 1.5 = 3 ATP contribution is absent. But NADH transfer, product export and possible reactivation also affect the ledger; the full ATP yield needs their fates.

9. A cell makes three palmitate molecules from cytosolic acetyl-CoA. How many acetyl-CoA, ATP and NADPH are required by ACC and FAS?

Show answer and reasoning

Each palmitate uses eight acetyl-CoA, seven ATP and fourteen NADPH. Three require 24 acetyl-CoA, 21 ATP and 42 NADPH. If all acetyl groups arrive through ATP-citrate lyase, another 24 ATP are used; recycling costs depend on route.

10. Only the first acetyl primer is labelled at both carbons during palmitate synthesis. Where is label found?

Show answer and reasoning

Carbons 15 and 16, the omega end. Successive malonyl additions occur at the carboxyl end. Labelling the two retained carbons of the final malonyl unit instead marks carbons 1 and 2.

11. ACC inhibition lowers malonyl-CoA from 10 to 2 arbitrary units while cellular ATP falls. Predict effects on FAS flux and CPT I, other factors equal.

Show answer and reasoning

FAS has less extender substrate, so synthesis tends to fall. Lower malonyl-CoA relieves CPT I inhibition, so long-chain fatty acid entry may increase. Actual oxidation also depends on respiratory capacity, substrate and cofactor supply; a lower ATP level alone does not guarantee a particular rate.

12. In a cytosolic synthesis assay, acetyl-CoA plus ATP gives low product but adding malonyl-CoA restores product. Which reaction is more likely limited?

Show answer and reasoning

The production of malonyl-CoA by ACC, because bypassing it restores downstream chain extension. A complete loss of FAS, NADPH availability or thioesterase function would not generally be rescued by malonyl-CoA alone. Appropriate matched controls are essential.

13. Elongate 18:1 cis-Δ9 by two carbons at its carboxyl end. Give the new Δ position and omega family.

Show answer and reasoning

The product is 20:1 cis-Δ11, because original carbon 9 becomes carbon 11. Omega position = 20 − 11 = 9, unchanged. The existing double bond does not move relative to the terminal methyl group.

14. A peripheral tissue completely oxidises 5 acetoacetate and 3 D-beta-hydroxybutyrate molecules. Calculate ATP equivalents.

Show answer and reasoning

Eight ketone molecules give 16 acetyl-CoA worth 160 ATP. Eight SCOT transfers bypass eight GTP: 152. Three beta-hydroxybutyrate produce three additional NADH worth 7.5, total 159.5 ATP equivalents.

15. A liver mitochondrial sample has a high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio. Which ketone form tends to increase relative to the other, and why?

Show answer and reasoning

D-beta-hydroxybutyrate relative to acetoacetate. The reversible dehydrogenase consumes NADH in the reductive direction. This prediction assumes comparable pH and substrate conditions; circulating ratios are not exact direct measures of matrix NADH.

16. After prolonged fasting, a subject has low blood glucose, inappropriately low ketone bodies and medium-chain acylcarnitine accumulation. Which pathway class best explains this?

Show answer and reasoning

Impaired mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, compatible with MCAD deficiency. Reduced oxidation lowers both ATP support for gluconeogenesis and acetyl-CoA supply for ketogenesis. SCOT deficiency would instead impair ketone utilisation and could raise ketones. The profile is a biochemical clue, not a definitive clinical diagnosis.

17. A scientist proposes that three acetyl-CoA must be consumed for every acetoacetate formed, based on HMG-CoA synthase. Identify the carbon balance error.

Show answer and reasoning

Two acetyl groups form acetoacetyl-CoA, and a third is added to make six-carbon HMG-CoA. HMG-CoA lyase releases one acetyl-CoA along with four-carbon acetoacetate. Net consumption is two acetyl-CoA; the third is balanced by release.

18. An oil seed converts stored fatty acid carbon into carbohydrate, whereas a mammalian hepatocyte cannot obtain net glucose from palmitate acetyl groups. Explain the difference.

Show answer and reasoning

The seed uses the glyoxylate cycle, bypassing the two decarboxylating TCA reactions and allowing net four-carbon intermediate production. Mammals lack this complete route and PDH is effectively irreversible. Fatty acid oxidation can still supply energy for gluconeogenesis; glycerol and odd-chain propionyl carbon can contribute carbon.

21. Consolidation: a carbon, cofactor and compartment map

The chapter can now be reconstructed from three questions. First, where is the carbon? A fatty acid begins as an unesterified chain, becomes an activated acyl-CoA and is progressively shortened at its carboxyl end. Even chains yield acetyl-CoA; odd chains also yield propionyl-CoA, which enters the intermediate pool as succinyl-CoA. Unsaturated chains retain the same carbon accounting but need auxiliary enzymes when double-bond geometry prevents ordinary processing. In the synthetic direction acetyl-CoA supplies the primer and malonyl-CoA the extenders. Ketogenesis converts a net two acetyl units into a four-carbon exportable fuel; ketolysis recovers two acetyl units.

Second, where do the electrons go? The first mitochondrial beta-oxidation reaction transfers electrons via flavin and ETF to ubiquinone; the second oxidative reaction transfers electrons to NAD⁺. The respiratory chain recycles the acceptors and generates ATP. Peroxisomal acyl-CoA oxidase instead passes electrons to oxygen to make hydrogen peroxide. Fatty acid synthesis consumes two NADPH per extension. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is more reduced than acetoacetate, so its oxidation in a receiving tissue makes an additional NADH. These redox differences explain why equal carbon yields need not imply equal ATP yields.

Third, where is the pathway operating? Long-chain mitochondrial import is controlled by the carnitine shuttle, whereas cytosolic fatty acid synthesis uses citrate export to supply acetyl groups. ACC generates malonyl-CoA, which both supplies biosynthesis and inhibits CPT I. Hepatic mitochondria form ketones, while extrahepatic mitochondria utilise them through SCOT. Red blood cells cannot do either oxidative task because they lack mitochondria. Peroxisomes handle substrates that cannot efficiently begin ordinary mitochondrial beta oxidation, and plant glyoxysomes illustrate a different solution to converting lipid carbon into carbohydrate.

The central exam skill is to declare the starting molecule, compartment, reaction endpoint and cofactor convention before calculating an ATP yield. For a free saturated C16 chain under the usual modern convention, seven cycles and eight acetyl units yield 106 ATP equivalents; an activated chain gives 108. A cis double bond may bypass a flavin-linked step, an odd terminal fragment may replenish the TCA pool, and a peroxisomal first oxidation does not have the usual mitochondrial ATP value. These are reasons to derive a ledger rather than rely on a memorised number.

The present chapter has established the fundamental routes for producing, shortening, modifying and distributing fatty acid carbon; the next question is how these building blocks are assembled into the complex lipids that cells use for storage, membranes and signalling.
