1. From phenylpropanoids to a family of pigments

A developing fruit can change from green to purple while its seed coat becomes brown and its unripe flesh loses much of its astringency. These changes are not simply different concentrations of one pigment. They reflect the allocation of carbon into related but chemically distinct specialized products. A flower may use coloured anthocyanins to attract pollinators; a transparent-looking epidermis may contain ultraviolet-absorbing flavonols; a seed coat may accumulate tannins that alter its interaction with herbivores and microbes. To explain these outcomes, begin with the carbon skeleton and follow the enzymes that transform it.

The preceding chapters established that the shikimate pathway supplies aromatic amino acids and that phenylalanine can enter phenylpropanoid metabolism. Here the phenylpropanoid branch meets acetate-derived metabolism. This meeting creates the fifteen-carbon framework of flavonoids. The same framework can be converted into many pigments and nonpigmented products. By contrast, a substantial class of plant tannins arises from gallic acid and does not require that fifteen-carbon framework.

What makes a flavonoid?

The conventional flavonoid skeleton has two six-carbon aromatic rings, A and B, joined through a three-carbon segment (C6–C3–C6). In most subclasses the connecting segment is incorporated into an oxygen-containing heterocycle, ring C. Chalcones retain an open central chain. Isoflavonoids shift the attachment position of the B ring from C2 to C3 of the central ring. The skeleton therefore defines a family, not a single molecule. Hydroxylation, methylation, glycosylation and acylation generate many additional derivatives.

A phenolic hydroxyl is attached directly to an aromatic carbon. Several such groups can enable hydrogen donation, electron transfer, metal binding and noncovalent interactions, but actual biological effects depend on concentration, localization and chemical environment. A flavonoid present in a vacuole cannot be assumed to directly neutralize every reactive oxygen species formed in a chloroplast. Distinguish chemical capacity measured in a test tube from the physiological role of a compound in a living cell.

Central distinction

Flavonoid describes a structural and biosynthetic family. Anthocyanins are glycosylated flavonoid pigments. Tannins are operationally defined polyphenols with substantial macromolecule-binding capacity; only condensed tannins are flavonoid-derived.

2. Chalcone formation and the first committed reactions

Supply of the aromatic starter

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) eliminates ammonia from L-phenylalanine to form trans-cinnamate. Cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), an endoplasmic-reticulum-associated cytochrome P450, converts cinnamate into p-coumarate using oxygen and reducing power supplied through a P450 reductase. 4-Coumarate:CoA ligase (4CL) then activates the acid with ATP and CoA, producing p-coumaroyl-CoA through an acyl-adenylate intermediate. The ATP is converted to AMP and pyrophosphate; pyrophosphate hydrolysis helps drive activation.

This activated C9 starter is shared by several routes, including structural phenolics. Increasing PAL expression can increase the supply entering phenylpropanoid metabolism, but it does not guarantee more flavonoids: a competing branch might consume the additional starter. Flux depends on downstream enzyme capacities, availability of malonyl-CoA and the demands of the tissue.

Supply of the extension units

Acetyl-CoA carboxylase forms malonyl-CoA from acetyl-CoA, bicarbonate and ATP. The added carboxyl group is removed during each decarboxylative condensation catalysed by chalcone synthase (CHS), a type III polyketide synthase. The cytosolic malonyl-CoA pool used by specialized metabolism must be distinguished from plastidial malonyl units used in fatty-acid synthesis. Compartments are not interchangeable reservoirs.

CHS uses one molecule of p-coumaroyl-CoA and three molecules of malonyl-CoA. It binds the starter to a catalytic cysteine, extends the chain three times and directs the resulting polyketide into a cyclic product, naringenin chalcone. The enzyme is often termed the first committed enzyme of the mainstream flavonoid pathway because earlier phenylpropanoid steps also feed other products.

Carbon and cofactor balance

p-Coumaroyl-CoA + 3 malonyl-CoA → naringenin chalcone + 4 CoA-SH + 3 CO₂ (schematic net reaction). Nine starter carbons plus six retained extension carbons give fifteen product carbons. The reaction does not directly consume ATP; ATP is used upstream to activate the precursors.

Chalcone isomerase (CHI) catalyses intramolecular cyclization, yielding predominantly (2S)-naringenin, a flavanone. The reaction can occur slowly without enzyme, but CHI makes it efficient and stereoselective. Thus a CHI defect may leave residual flavonoid synthesis in some contexts, unlike a simple textbook diagram that predicts a perfectly absolute block. In certain species, chalcones themselves accumulate as yellow pigments.

A note on historical evidence

Classical tracer experiments provided a way to test mixed biosynthetic origin before genes encoding the enzymes were cloned. Label introduced into a phenylpropanoid precursor appeared in one part of the flavonoid skeleton, whereas labelled acetate appeared in the other. Today purified enzymes, gene knockouts and mass spectrometry offer complementary evidence. The strongest inference combines chemical identity, enzyme activity and genetic rescue rather than inferring a pathway from colour alone.

3. Flavanones and the major flavonoid branches

Naringenin is a decision point. Enzymes alter the oxidation state of ring C, the position of the B ring and the pattern of substituents on aromatic rings. The first useful question in an exam is whether a transformation introduces a hydroxyl group, a double bond, a carbonyl or a new stereochemical configuration. This is more reliable than memorizing names that sound similar.

Flavones, flavonols and isoflavonoids

Flavone synthase (FNS) forms flavones from flavanones. Two major enzymatic solutions have evolved: soluble Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate-dependent FNS I and membrane-associated P450 FNS II. Flavanone 3-hydroxylase (F3H) instead hydroxylates the central ring to make a dihydroflavonol, such as dihydrokaempferol. Flavonol synthase (FLS) then introduces a double bond, producing kaempferol. The distinction between F3H and F3′H is essential: the unprimed 3 refers to ring C, whereas 3′ refers to ring B.

Isoflavone synthase (IFS), a P450, performs an oxidative aryl migration that changes where ring B attaches. The immediate hydroxylated intermediate undergoes dehydration to form an isoflavone. Legume isoflavonoids may participate in chemical communication with compatible rhizobia or be converted into antimicrobial phytoalexins. These functions are specific to compound, species and context; not every flavonoid triggers nodulation.

Competition for a shared intermediate

Dihydroflavonols can be oxidized by FLS to flavonols or reduced by dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR) toward anthocyanins and condensed tannins. This is a branch competition, not a linear sequence in which all flavonols eventually become anthocyanins. Suppression of FLS may make more substrate available for DFR, provided DFR is present, accepts the substrate and downstream reactions can accommodate the extra flux.

A useful way to classify enzymes is by reaction chemistry. CHS is a polyketide synthase; CHI is an isomerase; F3H, FLS and ANS are typically Fe(II)/2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenases; DFR and ANR are reductases; F3′H and F3′5′H are usually P450 hydroxylases; glycosyltransferases transfer sugars from activated nucleotide-sugar donors. Cofactor deficiency can therefore affect several steps simultaneously, whereas disruption of one specific enzyme usually has a narrower primary effect.

4. Anthocyanidin biosynthesis and anthocyanin modification

The first reduction after the dihydroflavonol branch is catalysed by DFR using NADPH. Its products, leucoanthocyanidins, are largely colourless flavan-3,4-diols. Anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), also called leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase (LDOX), catalyses oxidative conversion toward anthocyanidin aglycones. In living cells these unstable intermediates are rapidly modified, rather than accumulating as a large pool of free pigment.

An anthocyanidin is the aglycone; an anthocyanin is its glycosylated derivative. UDP-glucose:flavonoid 3-O-glucosyltransferase (UFGT or related UGTs) can transfer glucose to the hydroxyl at C3. Other transferases add different sugars, modify other positions, attach aromatic or aliphatic acyl groups, or methylate hydroxyl groups. Glycosylation generally improves solubility and stability in the aqueous vacuolar environment. Aromatic acylation can promote intramolecular co-pigmentation, shielding the chromophore from hydration.

F3′H introduces a hydroxyl at position 3′; F3′5′H can produce the more highly hydroxylated pattern. Additional methylation produces derivatives such as peonidin from cyanidin-type compounds and petunidin or malvidin from delphinidin-type compounds. Colour tendencies are not fixed rules. The same aglycone can appear differently when sugar, acylation, co-pigments, metal ions and vacuolar pH change.

A classic application is flower colour engineering. If a plant lacks functional F3′5′H, delphinidin-based pigmentation may be absent. Introducing the hydroxylase can supply the necessary intermediate, but success also requires DFR to accept the resulting dihydromyricetin and the tissue to provide suitable downstream modification and vacuolar conditions. A new enzyme does not automatically produce a desired blue flower. In the reverse direction, some DFR enzymes poorly accept dihydrokaempferol, so pelargonidin accumulation may remain low even when that substrate is present.

Exam trap

Anthocyanidins are not synonymous with anthocyanins. ANS is not a glycosyltransferase; UGT is not the enzyme that creates the anthocyanidin chromophore.

5. Why anthocyanin colour depends on the cellular environment

The colour of an anthocyanin arises from its conjugated electron system, which absorbs some wavelengths of visible light. The light that is transmitted or reflected determines the apparent hue. Under strongly acidic conditions, the flavylium cation is favoured and often appears red. As pH changes, proton transfer, hydration and ring-opening equilibria generate other chemical forms. Quinoidal bases can shift the absorption toward purple or blue; hydrated hemiketal and chalcone forms may be weakly coloured or effectively colourless.

This is not a universal red-to-blue pH meter. At a particular pH the relative concentrations depend on the pigment structure, temperature, time and stabilizing interactions. In simple extracts a pigment may lose colour rather than become blue as the pH rises. In flowers, organized pigment complexes and co-pigmentation can stabilize blue forms even when the vacuole remains mildly acidic. Strongly alkaline exposure may cause degradation.

Co-pigmentation and metal coordination

A colourless or pale flavone or flavonol can associate with an anthocyanin through aromatic stacking and other noncovalent interactions. Co-pigmentation can increase colour intensity and change the absorption maximum, in part by making the chromophore less accessible to water. Some pigments also form metal-containing complexes involving suitable B-ring hydroxyl groups. Hydrangea sepal colour is a familiar example in which aluminium availability and chemical context influence a delphinidin-containing pigment system. Soil pH can influence aluminium availability; it does not act as a direct indicator of vacuolar pH.

Where the pigments are stored

Many flavonoid biosynthetic reactions take place on or near the cytosolic face of the endoplasmic reticulum. Glycosylated anthocyanins are commonly accumulated in vacuoles. Glutathione S-transferase family proteins such as Arabidopsis TT19 can support pigment sequestration without requiring every stored pigment to be covalently conjugated to glutathione. Tonoplast transport mechanisms include MATE family and ABC transporters in different systems, and vesicular routes can also contribute. Proton gradients may power secondary active transport.

Vacuolar sequestration separates the pigment from incompatible metabolic processes and creates the chemical environment required for stable colour. A colourless mutant might therefore possess functioning biosynthetic enzymes but fail to transport pigment, acidify the vacuole or stabilize the chromophore. To distinguish these possibilities, compare precursor and product concentrations, measure vacuolar pH and localize the pigment at cellular resolution.

6. Tannins: condensed and hydrolysable families

An unripe persimmon or a strongly brewed tea can produce an astringent sensation. Astringency is distinct from bitterness: interactions of polyphenols with salivary proteins can reduce lubrication and create a drying or puckering feeling. Tannins can associate with proteins through multiple hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions and, under some conditions, oxidation-dependent covalent reactions. The strength of binding depends on polymer size, hydroxylation pattern, protein sequence, pH and ionic environment.

The historical use of tannin-rich extracts to stabilize hides gave rise to the term tannin. It does not designate one chemical skeleton. In higher plants two major groups are condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins) and hydrolysable tannins (gallotannins and ellagitannins). Some complex tannins combine structural features of both groups. Brown algal phlorotannins are a separate class outside the main higher-plant pathways covered here.

A tannin can be water-soluble under particular extraction conditions without being an anthocyanin. Both families may interact with proteins, and both may be found in a single fruit. A chemical assay reporting total phenolics cannot identify which family is present. Likewise, a brown seed coat can indicate oxidation of condensed tannins but colour alone cannot establish the structure.

7. Condensed tannins: flavan-3-ol units and polymer formation

Condensed tannin biosynthesis shares the upstream pathway with anthocyanin synthesis. After DFR produces a leucoanthocyanidin, leucoanthocyanidin reductase (LAR) can generate a catechin-type flavan-3-ol. Alternatively ANS produces an anthocyanidin-related intermediate and anthocyanidin reductase (ANR, encoded by BANYULS in Arabidopsis) contributes to epicatechin-type products and their precursors. These reactions help establish distinct stereochemical configurations of the monomeric units.

The conventional introductory model associates LAR with 2,3-trans catechin and ANR with 2,3-cis epicatechin. This model is useful for examination reasoning, but the detailed pathway is species-dependent. Some LAR and ANR enzymes also contribute to formation or modification of extension-unit precursors; polymer accumulation cannot be described completely as simple random condensation of free catechin molecules. A plant that lacks LAR may still synthesize abundant proanthocyanidins through other routes.

What the polymer name means

Proanthocyanidins are named for their ability to yield coloured anthocyanidins under suitable strong-acid oxidative cleavage conditions. Procyanidins contain units corresponding mainly to catechin or epicatechin and can yield cyanidin; prodelphinidins contain more highly hydroxylated units and can yield delphinidin. The intact polymers are not identical to the anthocyanins stored in flowers. Many are colourless or pale initially but darken as they oxidize during tissue maturation.

In a B-type proanthocyanidin, adjacent units are typically joined by a C4–C8 or C4–C6 carbon–carbon bond. A-type compounds contain an additional ether linkage. A dimer contains two flavan-3-ol-derived units; oligomers contain a few and polymers can contain many. Degree of polymerization influences binding and solubility, but larger is not invariably stronger in every protein-binding assay because molecular accessibility and precipitation also matter. Galloylation of flavan-3-ols adds ester-linked galloyl groups without changing the basic classification of the interflavan polymer.

The transparent testa model

Arabidopsis wild-type mature seeds are brown in part because proanthocyanidins accumulate in the seed coat and undergo oxidation. Transparent testa mutants have altered seed-coat pigmentation. TT4 encodes CHS, TT5 encodes CHI, TT6 encodes F3H, TT3 encodes DFR, and BAN encodes ANR. TT2, TT8 and TTG1 are regulatory proteins that promote expression of late pathway genes in relevant seed-coat cells. TT12 is a MATE transporter involved in vacuolar accumulation of proanthocyanidin precursors.

These mutants reveal why a phenotype is not a complete diagnosis. A tt4 mutant lacks an early committed reaction and should have a broad flavonoid defect. A ban mutant can retain upstream flavonoid pathways but is impaired in the normal seed-coat proanthocyanidin branch and may accumulate abnormal anthocyanin pigmentation. A tt12 defect can alter intracellular accumulation despite upstream enzyme activity. Genetic complementation, tissue-specific expression and metabolite profiles distinguish the causes.

8. Hydrolysable tannins: the gallic acid route

The alternative tannin family illustrates the value of returning to the shikimate pathway. 3-Dehydroshikimate can be converted into gallic acid, a seven-carbon aromatic acid with three adjacent phenolic hydroxyls. This branch can operate before formation of chorismate or phenylalanine; it is therefore not dependent on CHS or the canonical PAL-to-flavonoid route. Shikimate dehydrogenase-related activities participate in gallic acid formation in relevant species.

Building gallotannins

UDP-glucose-dependent glycosyltransferases can form β-glucogallin (1-O-galloyl-β-D-glucose). Although the enzyme belongs to a glycosyltransferase family, the bond in this product is an ester between the gallic acid carboxyl group and the glucose hydroxyl. β-Glucogallin then acts as an activated galloyl donor for further position-specific transfer reactions. Stepwise addition of galloyl groups to the glucose core produces di-, tri- and tetragalloylglucose, eventually reaching pentagalloylglucose (PGG). Some gallotannins carry additional galloyl groups linked to existing galloyl substituents.

Gallotannin hydrolysis cleaves ester bonds, yielding glucose or another core polyol and gallic acid. The ease and completeness of release depend on conditions. A single free gallic acid molecule is a phenolic acid, not automatically a tannin: the multiple aromatic binding surfaces provided by the larger molecule are important to typical tannin properties.

Building ellagitannins

Adjacent galloyl groups attached to glucose can undergo oxidative coupling to form a hexahydroxydiphenoyl (HHDP) group. Oxidative enzymes, including laccase activities in characterized systems, can participate. Hydrolysis of the ester linkages liberates HHDP-derived material that can spontaneously lactonize to ellagic acid. Thus ellagic acid is a characteristic hydrolysis-derived product of many ellagitannins, not a synonym for the intact polymer. Further oxidation, coupling and modification generate a wide diversity of molecules.

Compare the diagnostic consequences of blocking CHS and blocking galloyltransferase. The former should directly depress flavonoid-derived products including proanthocyanidins, but gallic acid-based hydrolysable tannins need not fall. The latter should affect hydrolysable tannin assembly without directly preventing naringenin formation. Broad effects on carbon supply or shared regulation remain possible, so an enzyme-specific prediction is not a guarantee of the entire organism's response.

9. Regulation, localization and developmental control

Specialized pathways are expensive. Carbon consumed in flavonoids cannot simultaneously be used for cellulose, starch or respiration; reducing equivalents and energy must also be supplied. Plants respond by regulating genes and enzymes in the tissues and stages where the products provide benefits. An epidermal UV screen is useful at the surface; the same compound need not be accumulated uniformly in every root or vascular cell.

Transcriptional coordination

In Arabidopsis, MYB11, MYB12 and MYB111 regulate overlapping sets of early flavonol-associated genes. Many late anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin genes are regulated by combinations of R2R3-MYB, basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) and WD40-repeat proteins, collectively called MBW complexes. Specific components differ by tissue and product: PAP1/MYB75 is associated with anthocyanin regulation, whereas TT2/MYB123 acts in seed-coat proanthocyanidin regulation with TT8 and TTG1. A regulatory mutation may therefore alter several pathway transcripts simultaneously.

High light, UV-B, sugar status, temperature and nutrient conditions can influence flavonoid production. The UV-B receptor UVR8 participates in signalling through COP1 and HY5, which can promote expression of protective flavonoid pathway genes. Jasmonate and other hormone networks can influence transcriptional regulators. Phosphate limitation and high sugar can promote anthocyanin accumulation in some plants, but a purple leaf is not a uniquely diagnostic sign of phosphate deficiency.

Developmental timing and compartmentation

In grape berries, flavonoid classes accumulate with different developmental patterns. Proanthocyanidins often accumulate early in skins and seeds, whereas anthocyanin accumulation rises during ripening in coloured varieties. Tissue-specific sampling is essential: whole-fruit measurements may dilute a strong skin response. During fruit maturation, changes in tannin solubility, polymerization and association with cell walls can decrease astringency even if total phenolic carbon has not disappeared.

A useful flux model is to separate precursor delivery, branch allocation, downstream chemical modification, transport and turnover. A change in any one of these can alter final pigment abundance. High transcript abundance is not equivalent to high enzyme activity, and high activity is not equivalent to accumulation if degradation or export is rapid. This distinction prepares the student for metabolic engineering in node 6.G.10.

10. Physiological and ecological functions

Light protection and signalling

Flavones and flavonols in epidermal tissues absorb UV wavelengths and can reduce penetration into sensitive internal tissues. Some flavonoids also influence redox signalling or modulate auxin transport; these effects depend on the specific compound and localization. Anthocyanins can act as optical screens in particular tissues and may contribute to photoprotection under high light or low temperature. A leaf's purple appearance alone does not prove higher photosynthetic efficiency; measure chlorophyll fluorescence, growth or photodamage against suitable controls.

Attracting mutualists

Flowers use visible pigment patterns and sometimes ultraviolet patterns invisible to humans. Pollinators differ in spectral sensitivity, so a pigment pattern that appears uniform to humans may provide a contrasting signal to an insect. Anthocyanins can participate in ripe fruit signals that promote dispersal by animals. But flower colour evolved in the context of many traits, including scent, shape and nectar. A pigment mutation may influence pollinator visitation without inevitably reducing reproductive fitness in every environment.

Defense and interactions with consumers

Tannins may reduce digestibility by associating with dietary proteins or digestive enzymes; they may also alter microbial growth and feeding preference. Their ecological effects are context-dependent. Some herbivores possess tannin-binding salivary proteins, behavioural adaptations or gut conditions that reduce the impact. Small amounts may have different effects from high concentrations, and different tannin structures have different protein affinities. A simple statement that every tannin is toxic to every herbivore is incorrect.

Flavonoids also contribute to interactions with microbes. Certain legume root exudates stimulate rhizobial nod-gene expression, while some isoflavonoid derivatives act in defense against pathogens. Whether a compound is preformed (constitutive defense) or synthesized after challenge (induced defense) depends on species and conditions. No class is restricted to a single ecological role.

Connect structure to function

UV absorption depends on conjugation; aqueous storage depends in part on glycosylation and transport; astringency and protein binding depend on multiple interaction sites and molecular architecture. These relationships are stronger explanations than a list of disconnected examples.

11. Experimental evidence and quantitative interpretation

Choosing appropriate measurements

Thin-layer chromatography can separate crude plant pigments, but spot colour and retention factor are only preliminary identifiers. HPLC with diode-array detection distinguishes compounds by retention and absorption spectra; liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry adds molecular mass and fragmentation evidence. Authentic standards and appropriate calibration improve identification and quantification. An absorption peak at about 520 nm alone cannot establish a specific anthocyanin structure.

The pH differential method estimates monomeric anthocyanins using the large reversible absorbance difference between pH 1.0 and pH 4.5. A wavelength near the pigment's visible absorption maximum is paired with a 700 nm measurement to correct turbidity. Beer–Lambert law (A = εlc) converts corrected absorbance into concentration under the assumptions of linear absorbance, suitable path length and a known reference extinction coefficient. Reporting as cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents is not proof that every pigment in the sample is cyanidin-3-glucoside.

Worked example: anthocyanin concentration

At pH 1.0, A520 = 0.84 and A700 = 0.04; at pH 4.5, A520 = 0.24 and A700 = 0.03. Corrected A = (0.84 − 0.04) − (0.24 − 0.03) = 0.59. For dilution factor 5, MW = 449.2 g/mol, ε = 26,900 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and path 1 cm: concentration = (0.59 × 449.2 × 5 × 1000)/(26,900 × 1) ≈ 49.3 mg/L as cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents.

The acid-butanol assay produces coloured anthocyanidin products from condensed tannins; DMACA reacts preferentially with flavan-3-ol-related compounds. Calibration depends on standard, polymer composition and extraction. Folin–Ciocalteu reagent measures overall reducing capacity and may also respond to nonphenolic reductants. Protein precipitation assays address binding behaviour rather than the exact abundance of each molecule. Orthogonal methods are necessary to make a structural claim.

Carbon tracing and flux accounting

In an idealized tracer experiment, a fully carbon-labelled p-coumaroyl starter and unlabelled malonyl units would produce a flavonoid with nine labelled carbons. Conversely, unlabelled starter plus three fully labelled malonyl units yields six labelled retained carbons, because three labelled carboxyl carbons are released as CO₂. In an intact plant, metabolic interconversion, pool dilution and isotope scrambling complicate this idealized interpretation. A short pulse, appropriate controls and positional fragmentation can help resolve the origin.

Testing UV protection

Grow genetically matched wild type and a CHS-deficient line under UV-B and UV-B-filtered light. Keep photosynthetically active radiation, temperature, water and developmental stage comparable. Measure epidermal UV absorbance, pigment profile, growth and PSII maximum quantum yield (Fv/Fm). A genotype-by-treatment interaction, in which the mutant suffers specifically under UV-B, supports a protective role. Complementation of CHS strengthens causal inference. Adding a sunscreen externally would test optical screening but would not by itself restore all flavonoid signalling functions.

In a pathway question, mark the common precursor, then determine which downstream products require the inhibited enzyme. In a colour question, separate the abundance of a pigment from the chemical form it adopts in the vacuole. In a tannin question, identify the linkage and the biosynthetic origin before predicting the hydrolysis product.

13. CSIR-NET and SET practice with explained answers

These original practice items are intended to test pathway order, biochemical reasoning and interpretation. They are not claimed to be previous-year questions.

1. MCQ — Which combination is directly used by CHS?

A. Three acetyl-CoA and one cinnamate; B. One p-coumaroyl-CoA and three malonyl-CoA; C. One phenylalanine and three glucose; D. One chorismate and three IPP.

Answer and explanation

B. The C9 starter and three decarboxylated extension units form the C15 chalcone skeleton.

2. MCQ — Which enzyme forms (2S)-naringenin from naringenin chalcone?

A. CHI; B. FLS; C. DFR; D. PAL.

Answer and explanation

A. CHI catalyses stereoselective cyclization.

3. MCQ — Which enzyme competes most directly with DFR for dihydroflavonols?

A. PAL; B. CHS; C. FLS; D. UFGT.

Answer and explanation

C. FLS diverts the shared substrate to flavonols.

4. MCQ — Which enzyme introduces a hydroxyl at position 3′ of ring B?

A. F3H; B. F3′H; C. ANR; D. 4CL.

Answer and explanation

B. The prime distinguishes B-ring numbering.

5. MCQ — A colourless leucoanthocyanidin is oxidatively converted toward an anthocyanidin by which enzyme?

A. ANS; B. CHI; C. LAR; D. IFS.

Answer and explanation

A. ANS/LDOX catalyses the oxidative step, prior to glycosylation.

6. MCQ — Which of the following is an anthocyanin rather than an anthocyanidin?

A. Cyanidin; B. Pelargonidin; C. Delphinidin; D. Cyanidin-3-O-glucoside.

Answer and explanation

D. It has a covalently attached sugar.

7. MCQ — What is the common central precursor of gallotannins and ellagitannins?

A. Naringenin; B. Pentagalloylglucose; C. Dihydroquercetin; D. Farnesyl diphosphate.

Answer and explanation

B. Galloylation of glucose precedes oxidative coupling in ellagitannin formation.

8. MCQ — Which product can arise after hydrolysis and lactonization of HHDP groups?

A. Ellagic acid; B. Catechin; C. Lignin; D. Genistein.

Answer and explanation

A. HHDP-derived material can lactonize to ellagic acid.

9. MCQ — Which Arabidopsis gene encodes an early committed flavonoid pathway enzyme?

A. TT4; B. TT12; C. BAN; D. TTG1.

Answer and explanation

A. TT4 encodes CHS; the others concern transport, reduction or regulation.

10. MCQ — Which protein combination is commonly associated with late flavonoid pathway regulation?

A. MYB, bHLH, WD40; B. RuBisCO, PEP carboxylase, ATPase; C. Histone, tubulin, actin; D. CHS, CHI, FLS.

Answer and explanation

A. These three regulatory protein families form an MBW complex.

11. Assertion–reason — Assertion: CHS deficiency may reduce flavonols and anthocyanins. Reason: CHS acts before their shared branch intermediates.

Choose: A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true but not explanatory; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Answer and explanation

A. The early committed step supplies carbon to both pathways.

12. Assertion–reason — Assertion: All hydrolysable tannins require DFR. Reason: DFR reduces dihydroflavonols.

Use the same A–D scheme, with D meaning assertion false.

Answer and explanation

D. The assertion is false; the reason is true. Gallic acid-based tannins bypass DFR.

13. Assertion–reason — Assertion: A purple flower must contain delphinidin. Reason: Vacuolar pH and co-pigments can change hue.

Use the A–D scheme.

Answer and explanation

D. Purple colour can be produced by more than one pigment system; the reason is true.

14. Analytical — A CHS mutant produces neither flavonols nor anthocyanins but retains gallic acid-rich tannins. Explain.

Identify which biosynthetic route has been blocked.

Answer and explanation

CHS blocks the mixed-origin flavonoid skeleton. Gallic acid formation and glucose galloylation belong to another branch.

15. Analytical — DFR expression doubles but anthocyanin accumulation stays constant. Give three explanations.

Consider substrate, downstream reactions and turnover.

Answer and explanation

Dihydroflavonol supply may be limiting; ANS or UGT capacity may be limiting; vacuolar transport or increased degradation can offset production. DFR activity might also not increase despite mRNA.

16. Analytical — A mutant retains normal extracted anthocyanin concentration but its flower changes from purple to red. What would you measure?

Distinguish amount from chemical state.

Answer and explanation

Measure vacuolar pH, pigment molecular profile, metal content and co-pigments. Compare absorption spectra at matched pH; changes in colour need not reflect lower synthesis.

17. Analytical — A flavonoid extract has high Folin–Ciocalteu signal. Can one conclude it contains abundant proanthocyanidins?

Explain the assay limitation.

Answer and explanation

No. Other phenolic and nonphenolic reductants react. Use a PA-targeted assay with suitable standards and chromatography or mass spectrometry for confirmation.

18. Numerical — Starting with 20 μmol p-coumaroyl-CoA and 48 μmol malonyl-CoA, find the maximum chalcone production and CO₂ release, assuming quantitative CHS conversion.

Use 1:3 stoichiometry.

Answer and explanation

48/3 = 16 μmol chalcone; malonyl-CoA limits. CO₂ release is 3 × 16 = 48 μmol; 4 μmol starter remains.

19. Numerical — A labelled starter has nine 13C atoms; each malonyl-CoA is unlabelled. How many labelled atoms are retained per flavonoid in the idealized reaction?

Ignore dilution and rearrangement in other metabolic pools.

Answer and explanation

Nine labelled atoms. Total carbon skeleton contains fifteen atoms, so 9/15 = 60% labelled carbon positions.

20. Numerical — A corrected pH differential absorbance is 0.40, dilution factor 4, path length 1 cm, MW 449.2 and ε 26,900. Calculate mg/L.

Use the reference pigment convention.

Answer and explanation

0.40 × 4 × 449.2 × 1000 / 26,900 ≈ 26.7 mg/L cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents.

21. Data interpretation — A line has 25 nmol/g flavonols and 60 nmol/g anthocyanins; wild type has 50 and 30 respectively. Does the data prove FLS inhibition?

Discuss alternative explanations.

Answer and explanation

No. The shift is compatible with altered branch allocation, but enhanced DFR, altered transcription, differential turnover or tissue composition could explain it. Direct FLS activity or genetic evidence is needed.

22. Conceptual — Why do tannins of similar mass differ in protein precipitation capacity?

Consider physical chemistry.

Answer and explanation

Hydroxyl pattern, flexibility, stereochemistry, linkage, galloylation, accessibility and target protein properties affect multivalent binding.

23. Conceptual — Why is vacuolar transport important for pigment formation even if all biosynthetic enzymes work?

Separate synthesis from storage.

Answer and explanation

Sequestration enables stable accumulation and an appropriate chemical microenvironment. Transport failure can promote degradation or mislocalization.

24. Conceptual — Why does the presence of catechin not establish that an extract contains a large condensed tannin polymer?

Distinguish monomer and polymer.

Answer and explanation

Catechin is a flavan-3-ol monomer. Polymer identity and degree of polymerization require evidence of interflavan linkages and molecular size.

14. Rapid revision and connections

Begin with phenylalanine → PAL → cinnamate → C4H → p-coumarate → 4CL → p-coumaroyl-CoA. CHS adds three malonyl-CoA units, retaining six new carbons. CHI forms naringenin. FNS yields flavones, IFS yields isoflavone-related products, and F3H yields dihydroflavonols. FLS and DFR then compete. DFR → ANS → UGT leads to anthocyanins; LAR and ANR-related routes supply condensed tannin units.

For pigment diversity, remember the progressive B-ring hydroxylation patterns of pelargonidin, cyanidin and delphinidin. Glycosylation distinguishes an anthocyanin from an anthocyanidin. Vacuolar pH, co-pigmentation, metals, acylation and transport influence hue and stability. For tannins, compare interflavan carbon–carbon bonds with galloyl ester bonds. Hydrolysable tannins branch from 3-dehydroshikimate through gallic acid, β-glucogallin and PGG; oxidative coupling leads to ellagitannins.

The earlier shikimate pathway chapter (6.G.04) explains aromatic carbon supply, and the metabolic engineering chapter (6.G.10) applies the principles of branch competition and pathway modification. The next curricular node, 6.G.07, extends the study to nitrogen-containing specialized metabolites, especially alkaloids.

One-minute self-test

Can you draw the CHS reaction, identify the FLS–DFR branch, distinguish an anthocyanidin from its glycoside, and explain why gallic acid-based tannins survive an idealized CHS knockout? If so, you have the central pathway logic.
