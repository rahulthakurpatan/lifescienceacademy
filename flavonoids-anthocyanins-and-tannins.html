1. From photosynthate to plant phenolics

A purple petal, a brown seed coat and a bitter unripe fruit may seem to present three unrelated biological phenomena. Their chemistry reveals a connection: each can contain specialized products of plant carbon metabolism with aromatic rings and hydroxyl groups. These compounds are not simply waste left after photosynthesis. The plant commits carbon, energy and enzyme capacity to making molecules whose properties influence survival and reproduction. A petal can advertise a flower to a pollinator, an epidermal cell can intercept damaging ultraviolet radiation, and a seed coat can protect the developing embryo. The same family of metabolic reactions can therefore solve problems at several different scales, from the stability of an individual molecule to an interaction between organisms.

A phenolic compound contains at least one hydroxyl group attached directly to an aromatic ring. An aromatic ring has a delocalized electron system, which affects both absorption of light and chemical reactivity. A phenolic hydroxyl group is not equivalent to an ordinary alcohol group attached to a saturated carbon. The electrons of the aromatic ring can stabilize the products of certain oxidation and deprotonation reactions. Many plant phenolics contain several such groups, but the name polyphenol alone does not identify a biosynthetic route. Flavonoids are a family defined substantially by their carbon framework and biosynthetic history, whereas tannins are a partly functional grouping of compounds capable of interacting strongly with macromolecules. Anthocyanins belong within the flavonoids; hydrolysable tannins usually do not.

The distinction between structural identity and biological function is especially important. The fact that two metabolites absorb ultraviolet light does not mean that one is synthesized from the other. Nor does the presence of a sugar on an aromatic compound automatically make it an anthocyanin. Begin classification by identifying the underlying carbon skeleton and the type of chemical bonds present. Then ask where its precursors come from and which enzymes make it. Finally, relate those features to physiological function. This ordering makes classification an explanation rather than a collection of unfamiliar names.

The preceding phenylpropanoid pathway supplies activated aromatic compounds for several alternative destinations. Phenylalanine, itself derived from the shikimate pathway, is deaminated by phenylalanine ammonia lyase to produce cinnamate. Hydroxylation and activation lead to p-coumaroyl-CoA. Its thioester bond gives the molecule a chemically activated acyl group. Instead of proceeding toward wall-associated phenolics such as lignin, this intermediate can enter a condensation reaction with malonyl-CoA. Thus one part of the flavonoid skeleton is supplied through aromatic amino acid metabolism and another through the acetate–malonate route. Carbon from photosynthesis ultimately supports both routes, but the immediate biochemical precursors are distinct.

It is useful to distinguish carbon allocation from nitrogen allocation here. Phenylalanine contains an amino group, but the common flavonoid skeleton does not contain nitrogen. The nitrogen is removed during the PAL reaction; it need not accompany the aromatic skeleton into the product. This contrasts with many alkaloids, whose nitrogen remains an integral feature of the specialized metabolite. Such differences explain why the identity of a starting amino acid cannot by itself determine whether the final product is a nitrogenous compound.

To understand why specialized metabolism uses familiar primary-metabolic intermediates, follow the fate of a carbon atom fixed by a leaf. The Calvin–Benson cycle produces triose phosphates that may enter carbohydrate storage, transport or other biosynthetic pathways. Conversion into phosphorylated intermediates supplies phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose 4-phosphate for aromatic amino acid synthesis. Other transformations can supply acetyl-CoA, from which malonyl-CoA is formed. The crucial point is that the plant does not have a separate source of carbon for pigments. Production of a pigment is linked to the same biochemical network that supplies membrane lipids, cell wall components and energy metabolism. Nevertheless, a plant receiving plentiful light does not necessarily synthesize abundant pigment. Precursor supply is only one condition; developmental programs and environmental responses decide where and when pathway enzymes are expressed.

The distinction between a precursor and an intermediate is relative to the reaction being considered. Phenylalanine is a precursor for the phenylpropanoid network, but for CHS it is an upstream metabolite rather than an immediate substrate. Naringenin is a product of chalcone isomerization but a precursor for several later routes. Naming the exact transformation is therefore indispensable when interpreting a diagram. In an enzyme assay containing phenylalanine and purified CHS, the expected chalcone is not formed simply because phenylalanine is upstream. The substrate must already have undergone deamination, hydroxylation and CoA activation. A whole-cell assay may support those preceding reactions, which is one reason that the interpretation of a cellular experiment differs from interpretation of a purified-enzyme experiment.

Flavonoid nomenclature is easiest to master by separating the scaffold from decoration. A scaffold is the set of connected atoms that identifies the main structural family; the addition of sugars, methyl groups or acyl groups produces derivatives. Quercetin and quercetin glucoside retain the flavonol scaffold even though their solubility, molecular mass and localization may differ. Likewise a common anthocyanidin may generate several anthocyanins with distinct patterns of sugars. A measurement that sums derivatives after removing sugars may answer a question about the underlying scaffold but lose information about the molecular species actually present in vivo. The distinction is useful in comparative studies in which two plants have similar aglycone composition but different metabolic modifications.

2. Chalcone synthase and the fifteen-carbon framework

The typical flavonoid skeleton has fifteen carbon atoms, arranged as two six-carbon aromatic rings connected through a three-carbon unit. This is commonly written C6–C3–C6. In many subclasses the connector forms part of a heterocyclic ring containing oxygen, so the structure is often drawn as three rings called A, C and B. The C ring is not necessarily aromatic and the oxidation state and arrangement of atoms in this ring help distinguish flavanones, flavones, flavonols and other classes. Chalcones are open-chain precursors with a three-carbon bridge rather than the closed heterocyclic C ring.

Chalcone synthase, abbreviated CHS, is a type III polyketide synthase. Its common substrate combination in this pathway is one p-coumaroyl-CoA and three malonyl-CoA molecules. The coumaroyl group contributes nine carbons. Each malonyl group starts with three carbons, but a carbon is released as carbon dioxide during each condensation, leaving a two-carbon contribution. The resulting skeleton therefore contains nine plus three times two, or fifteen carbons. This accounting is a practical way to test whether a proposed precursor combination is chemically plausible. Coenzyme A is released rather than becoming part of the final aromatic product.

The reaction proceeds through an enzyme-bound growing chain. Decarboxylation of the malonyl extender makes carbon–carbon bond formation favorable, and repeated extension produces a linear polyketide intermediate that can cyclize. CHS controls the arrangement in which the extended chain closes to yield naringenin chalcone. The aromatic ring originally present in the coumaroyl group becomes the B ring of the common flavonoid scaffold; the three malonyl-derived two-carbon units form the A ring. The three-carbon connection comes from the remaining coumaroyl skeleton. A carbon label in the original phenyl ring will therefore have a different destination from one in the retained positions of malonyl-CoA.

The malonyl-CoA pool is generated from acetyl-CoA by carboxylation, an ATP-dependent reaction. The carbon added in forming malonyl-CoA is subsequently released in the chain-extension step. Although these two processes may appear to cancel, the temporary carboxyl group allows a controlled carbon–carbon condensation. This is a familiar theme in biosynthesis: activation and transient groups can determine the feasibility of a reaction without being retained in the final product. ATP expenditure in precursor formation should not be confused with direct ATP use by CHS during each extension.

Chalcone isomerase, or CHI, catalyzes intramolecular cyclization of naringenin chalcone to predominantly the biologically relevant (2S)-naringenin. The same gross formula can be rearranged into molecules with different connectivity and stereochemistry. An uncatalyzed ring closure can occur, but the enzyme accelerates and directs the reaction stereoselectively. Therefore a CHI mutation may reduce pathway throughput even when some flavanone remains detectable. A reduction in products is not necessarily an absolute absence, especially when alternate enzymes or spontaneous reactions are available.

Compare CHS with stilbene synthase. Both can draw on the same starter and extender substrates, but they direct the chain toward different cyclization and product outcomes. Stilbene formation includes an additional carbon loss and commonly gives a fourteen-carbon framework, rather than the fifteen-carbon flavonoid scaffold. Increasing activity at one branch can reduce precursor availability to the other, although the extent depends on substrate supply and spatial organization. A branch diagram consequently predicts possibilities, not a fixed quantitative partition independent of cellular context.

The carbon map becomes clearer if the chain is considered before ring closure. The starter carries an aromatic ring attached to a three-carbon unsaturated side chain. Malonyl extension adds a series of two-carbon segments to the activated chain. Those segments contain oxygenated carbonyl-derived positions that can participate in ring closure and subsequent aromatization. In the canonical chalcone product, the newly constructed A ring retains hydroxyl substitution characteristic of this chemistry. The oxygen-containing heterocyclic C ring is formed afterward in the isomerase reaction; CHS has already assembled the carbon framework. This ordering distinguishes the formation of a new aromatic ring by polyketide chemistry from formation of an oxygen-containing ring by a subsequent cyclization.

A thioester is an ester-like bond in which sulfur occupies the position normally occupied by an oxygen linking the acyl group to the leaving group. Acyl-CoA molecules are thioesters. They can transfer acyl groups in enzymatic reactions more readily than would an equivalent unactivated free acid under many cellular conditions. In the CHS reaction the enzyme temporarily carries the growing chain. The ability to bind and position starter and extender groups limits uncontrolled side reactions and helps determine the final cyclization. Thus enzyme selectivity operates on both substrate recognition and the reaction trajectory after substrate binding. It is not enough for an enzyme to encounter two appropriate carbon-containing molecules; the active site must bring the correct atoms into an arrangement compatible with bond formation.

A tracer problem can distinguish whether the extra carbon in malonyl-CoA came from bicarbonate during its formation or was retained from acetyl-CoA. If bicarbonate alone is labeled during the acetyl-CoA carboxylase reaction, the added carboxyl carbon is expected to leave as carbon dioxide in the subsequent extension. In an idealized single-reaction system the label does not persist in the corresponding two-carbon contribution. In a metabolically active leaf the carbon dioxide may be reassimilated, however, creating indirect labeling. This caveat illustrates why short experiments and controlled precursor pools are easier to interpret than prolonged feeding of an intact organism.

Another useful distinction is between the number of molecules consumed and the number of carbon atoms contributed. One starter and three extender molecules form one chalcone scaffold. If one hundred nanomoles of starter and two hundred nanomoles of extender are the only available amounts, the maximum product is limited by the extender to sixty-six and two-thirds nanomoles, not one hundred nanomoles. This assumes quantitative conversion with no side reactions, accessible substrates and no replenishment. Because these amounts are molar quantities, fractional nanomoles are meaningful; only an argument about individual molecule counts requires an integer number of products. The remaining starter in this simple model is thirty-three and one-third nanomoles.

3. Flavanones, flavones, flavonols and isoflavonoids

Naringenin occupies a central branch point because it carries the characteristic flavonoid framework while still being suitable for several transformations. A flavanone typically has a carbonyl at carbon 4 and a saturated carbon 2–carbon 3 connection. Formation of a double bond between carbons 2 and 3 gives a flavone framework. Introduction of a hydroxyl group at carbon 3 leads toward dihydroflavonols and ultimately flavonols. A student who draws only the two aromatic rings will miss these distinctions; the chemical state of the connecting region is essential.

Flavone synthases provide a route from flavanones to flavones such as apigenin. Different plant lineages can use distinct enzyme types for this overall transformation, including soluble oxygenases and membrane-associated cytochrome P450 enzymes. The broad outcome is the introduction of unsaturation in the central ring. Flavones commonly accumulate as glycosides, and their optical and defensive roles vary with tissue and species. The sugar substitutions of a flavone do not change its membership in the flavone subclass because the underlying aglycone remains a flavone.

Flavanone 3-hydroxylase, F3H, produces a dihydroflavonol from a flavanone; the enzyme is a member of the iron and 2-oxoglutarate-dependent oxygenase family. Naringenin can give dihydrokaempferol. Flavonol synthase, FLS, converts appropriate dihydroflavonols into flavonols by creating the double bond in the central ring while retaining the 3-hydroxyl group. Kaempferol and quercetin are familiar flavonols. They differ in the substitution of the B ring. Their structures help account for differences in ultraviolet absorption and oxidation chemistry. Importantly, flavonols need not be strongly colored in the visible range to function as effective light-screening molecules.

Hydroxylation of the B ring changes the number and placement of hydroxyl groups. Flavonoid 3′-hydroxylase and flavonoid 3′,5′-hydroxylase are commonly membrane-associated P450 enzymes. Their substrate preferences and points of action differ among species, so a simplified map showing hydroxylation of dihydroflavonols is not intended to imply a universal strict sequence. A prime mark identifies positions on the B ring and distinguishes them from carbon 3 on the central ring. Confusing F3H with F3′H would make a prediction about a pigment mutant fundamentally incorrect.

Isoflavonoids illustrate a different kind of diversification. In an ordinary flavonoid scaffold the B ring is attached at carbon 2 of the central ring; in an isoflavonoid it is attached at carbon 3. Isoflavone synthase, a P450 enzyme found prominently in legumes, catalyzes oxidative rearrangement of a flavanone to a hydroxylated isoflavanone intermediate. Subsequent dehydration gives an isoflavone. Genistein and daidzein are examples, with differences in their substitution patterns. These compounds can participate in interactions with symbiotic bacteria or be precursors to defensive metabolites. The isoflavonoid branch should be understood as a rearrangement of the skeleton rather than an unrelated aromatic pathway.

In a metabolic network, competition occurs only where two activities have access to a common substrate. FLS and dihydroflavonol 4-reductase can compete for certain dihydroflavonols, whereas flavone synthase draws at an earlier flavanone branch. A high abundance of FLS transcript is not enough to quantify that competition, because protein abundance, catalytic properties, substrate preference and local substrate levels can all differ. Examination questions often simplify these details by assuming identical precursor input and no feedback. Such assumptions are legitimate in a model problem but must be identified before making a prediction.

Oxidation and reduction should be interpreted at particular atoms rather than equated with addition or loss of oxygen in every case. A change in a carbonyl to an alcohol corresponds to reduction of that carbon, while formation of a double bond from a saturated carbon pair can involve removal of hydrogen. The central ring of a flavanone provides a framework on which both sorts of transformations can occur. Flavone formation introduces unsaturation; flavonol synthesis requires a hydroxyl group at carbon 3 as well as the unsaturated central ring. The DFR route instead reduces the carbonyl at carbon 4. These alternative chemical outcomes are why the same upstream substrate may participate in pathways yielding compounds with dissimilar visible spectra or polymerization properties.

Oxygenases that depend on 2-oxoglutarate couple substrate oxidation to oxidative decarboxylation of 2-oxoglutarate, generating succinate and carbon dioxide. Molecular oxygen and iron in the active site are important to their chemistry. F3H and FLS belong to this broad catalytic family, but they are not interchangeable simply because they share cofactors. Their active sites recognize different substrates and direct different transformations. Cytochrome P450 monooxygenases likewise use molecular oxygen, with electrons commonly supplied through a reductase system. When a question describes a requirement for oxygen, that alone cannot identify the enzyme. The substrate, changed position and product are stronger identifying features.

A flavonoid branch can have a narrow spatial distribution. Legume roots, for example, can release flavonoid-related compounds into their surroundings, where suitable compounds influence expression of nodulation genes in compatible rhizobia. This is a case in which a plant metabolite functions not only as a component of the plant but also as information received by another organism. The precise response depends on the bacterial regulatory proteins and the mixture of signals. The connection to biological nitrogen fixation is ecological and regulatory; the released flavonoid is neither a nitrogenase substrate nor a direct substitute for combined nitrogen.

Isoflavonoids can also contribute to chemical defense. Some legumes form isoflavonoid-derived phytoalexins when challenged. A phytoalexin is generally synthesized or accumulated in response to infection or stress, whereas a phytoanticipin is present before attack or is generated from a pre-existing precursor. The classification refers to the timing and context of defense, not solely the structure of the molecule. The same chemical family can therefore contain metabolites assigned different defensive roles in different plants. It is important to reserve detailed immune signaling for the stress-physiology unit rather than confuse a downstream chemical product with the entire immune system.

4. Anthocyanidin synthesis and anthocyanin modification

The pigment branch begins when dihydroflavonol 4-reductase (DFR) reduces the carbonyl group of a dihydroflavonol, commonly using NADPH as a reducing equivalent. The resulting leucoanthocyanidin is generally not a strongly colored visible pigment. Anthocyanidin synthase (ANS), also called leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase, catalyzes oxidation leading toward the anthocyanidin framework. In the simplified teaching pathway, leucopelargonidin, leucocyanidin and leucodelphinidin are precursors of pelargonidin, cyanidin and delphinidin, respectively. Substrate specificity means that not every DFR in every species accepts every dihydroflavonol equally.

Anthocyanidins are the aglycone forms. Anthocyanins are their glycosylated derivatives. The flavylium structure of the colored forms contains a positively charged oxygen in the central ring under sufficiently acidic conditions, with electronic delocalization across the conjugated framework. This delocalization allows absorption at wavelengths within the visible spectrum. Different substitution patterns influence the electronic energy levels and the wavelengths most strongly absorbed. However, the color of an intact petal cannot be deduced from the aglycone name alone, because glycosylation, acylation, environmental pH and other solutes modify the optical behavior.

Common anthocyanidin classes illustrate B-ring chemistry. Pelargonidin has a single B-ring hydroxyl at 4′. Cyanidin has hydroxyls at 3′ and 4′; delphinidin has hydroxyls at 3′, 4′ and 5′. Other widely encountered anthocyanidins arise through methylation, including peonidin from a cyanidin-type pattern and petunidin or malvidin from delphinidin-type patterns. In many comparable chemical environments increasing hydroxylation shifts the absorption toward longer wavelengths, but there are exceptions when molecules are modified or interact with the surrounding medium.

Glycosyltransferases transfer a sugar residue from an activated donor such as UDP-glucose to an acceptor hydroxyl group. In many anthocyanin pathways glycosylation occurs at position 3 of the aglycone. Subsequent glycosylation at other positions, acylation of sugar hydroxyls and methylation can greatly diversify the products. Sugar attachment generally enhances compatibility with the aqueous environment and can improve chemical stability; it also provides a recognition feature for particular transport and storage processes. Adding glucose does not mean that the core flavonoid skeleton has changed from fifteen carbons: the total molecule has gained carbons in a substituent.

If an experimental sample is reported as cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, its name communicates three facts. Cyanidin identifies the aglycone, 3 identifies a position of attachment, and O-glucoside indicates that glucose is connected through an oxygen-containing glycosidic bond. This is different from a C-glycoside, where the sugar is directly linked by a carbon–carbon bond. A hydrolytic treatment can cleave many O-glycosides and release an aglycone; the susceptibility of C-glycosides is different. Therefore analysis of intact metabolites and analysis after hydrolysis answer different questions.

A red leaf does not necessarily contain more anthocyanin than a purple leaf. A spectrophotometer measures absorption at selected wavelengths, whereas the eye sees light that is reflected or transmitted from the tissue. A change in the spectrum can occur at constant pigment concentration. Tissue structure, chlorophyll content, surface properties and the presence of other pigments influence perceived color. Chemical analysis and optical observations are complementary measurements rather than interchangeable ones.

A useful way to reason about B-ring hydroxylation is to think about the set of substrates available to a reductase. With only the singly hydroxylated substrate dihydrokaempferol, a compatible DFR and downstream enzymes can yield a pelargonidin-type pigment. A doubly hydroxylated substrate such as dihydroquercetin supports a cyanidin-type route, whereas dihydromyricetin can support a delphinidin-type route. Yet the final ratio of pigments is not simply proportional to the ratios of precursor concentrations. Different DFR proteins may show preferences that make one precursor an inefficient substrate. The later enzymes and storage system must also handle the product. This explains why introduction of a hydroxylase into a plant does not guarantee the expected flower color.

Methylation replaces the hydrogen of a hydroxyl group with a methyl group in many flavonoid modifications. A common methyl donor is S-adenosylmethionine. The change can alter polarity, hydrogen bonding and electron distribution without removing the aromatic ring. Acylation instead adds an acyl group to a suitable hydroxyl, frequently on a sugar attached to an anthocyanin. Aromatic acyl groups can interact with the pigment framework within the same molecule, in a manner related to co-pigmentation. These changes are not equivalent to creating a new anthocyanidin by changing the number of carbon atoms in its basic skeleton; they are modifications of a pigment that can profoundly affect its stability.

Pigment formation also depends on developmental regulation. A leaf primordium, a mature epidermal cell and a petal cell contain the same genome in ordinary circumstances, but their expressed sets of enzymes differ. Transcriptional regulators can activate coordinated groups of flavonoid genes in particular tissues. The MYB–bHLH–WD40 regulatory complex is an important example in numerous anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin systems. MYB and bHLH proteins participate in transcriptional control, while the WD40 component can help organize the regulatory complex. Different members of these protein families can specify distinct expression patterns. Thus one should not assume that every MYB gene activates anthocyanin synthesis or that a mutation in a shared regulatory component affects only one enzyme.

In a gene expression experiment, increased transcript abundance of DFR and ANS indicates altered regulation at the RNA level. It does not directly give pigment synthesis rate. Translation, protein stability, cofactors, substrate availability, and export all intervene between a transcript and accumulation of product. A good experiment measures transcript, enzyme activity or protein abundance where relevant, and the final chemical phenotype. The distinction becomes especially useful in a time course: transcription may increase rapidly, whereas pigment requires time to be synthesized and stored. A delay between transcript and pigment is not necessarily evidence that the transcript is unrelated to the pathway.

5. Compartmentation, proton gradients and color

Much of the core biosynthetic activity takes place on the cytosolic side of the endoplasmic reticulum or in the cytosol, with membrane-associated P450 enzymes organized in that environment. Anthocyanins generally accumulate in the vacuole, which is separated from the cytosol by the tonoplast. Production and storage are consequently distinct processes. A mutation that disrupts transport can cause low vacuolar pigment without abolishing the catalytic pathway that synthesizes it. Measuring an enzyme in a tissue extract cannot establish that the product was delivered to the correct compartment in a living cell.

Transport strategies differ among plants and metabolites. Some ATP-binding cassette transporters couple transport to ATP hydrolysis; certain MATE proteins exploit a proton gradient. Glutathione S-transferase-like proteins can assist pigment accumulation by binding or facilitating transfer without necessarily conjugating glutathione covalently to each anthocyanin. Vesicular delivery also contributes in some settings. Rather than assume that all anthocyanins use one transporter, identify whether the evidence concerns a particular species, sugar modification or cell type.

The vacuolar environment affects anthocyanin chemistry after accumulation. At low pH the flavylium cation is often favored. Deprotonation gives quinoidal species, and hydration can generate less strongly colored hemiketal forms; ring opening may produce chalcone forms. These are interconnected equilibria, not a universal three-color switch. A simple statement that acidic anthocyanins are red and alkaline anthocyanins are blue is incomplete because changing pH can also favor colorless or unstable forms. Co-pigmentation and molecular modifications can stabilize particular colored species in environments in which the isolated pigment would behave differently.

A co-pigment can associate noncovalently with an anthocyanin and modify its absorption or reduce access of water to parts of the pigment. Flavones and flavonols can participate in such interactions. Some blue flowers also contain metal–pigment complexes, depending on the ligand and cellular environment. The hue therefore reflects chemical composition and local environment together. A comparison of two cultivars that differ in hue must examine more than the identity of one biosynthetic gene.

Petunia provides particularly useful experimental evidence. Verweij and colleagues identified PH5 as a tonoplast P-type proton ATPase linked to petal vacuole acidification and flower color. When the acidification system is defective, the lumen becomes less acidic and the petal hue can shift. Later work showed a contribution from PH1 in association with PH5. These results establish that a gene affecting an ion transport process can modify the appearance of a pigment without being a structural enzyme in its biosynthetic pathway. This is an important bridge between membrane physiology and plant specialized metabolism.

The pH scale is logarithmic: pH equals minus the base-ten logarithm of hydrogen-ion activity. Under dilute-solution approximations, a change from pH 4 to pH 5 corresponds to a tenfold decrease in hydrogen-ion concentration. In a vacuole, buffering and counter-ion movements complicate the relationship between net proton transport and pH. A measurement of pH alone cannot tell us how many protons a pump transported without knowing volume and buffer capacity. Yet the logarithmic relationship provides a sound first comparison of relative proton activity.

A proton gradient has both chemical and electrical components. Protons are charged, so pumping them across a membrane changes not only their relative activities but potentially the membrane potential. Ion conductances and the movement of counterions can influence how far acidification proceeds. Vacuoles also contain organic acids and other buffers that bind or release protons. Consequently, two cells with identical proton pump abundance may establish different steady-state pH values if their membrane permeability or buffering differs. In a simple exam problem a decrease in active proton transport is often assumed to raise lumen pH, but a real cell can compensate through several transport processes.

The perception of a flower as blue can involve selective absorption rather than emission of blue light. If the pigment absorbs strongly in parts of the yellow or orange region and the remaining light is scattered back to the observer, the flower may look bluish. Describing the color as the wavelength of maximum absorption is therefore potentially misleading. A spectral plot of absorbance and a spectral plot of reflectance represent different measurements. A petal is also not a transparent cuvette: light scatters from cell walls and air spaces, and the optical path length is not simply the physical thickness of the petal. For this reason comparisons of pigment concentration should preferably be made in appropriately prepared extracts rather than inferred solely from the darkness of photographs.

The protonation state of an anthocyanin can change after extraction. A petal pigment placed into a strongly acidic analytical medium may become redder than it was in the living vacuole, because the new solvent favors a different distribution of molecular species. This is not a contradiction. The chemistry depends on the current environment. To decide whether two petals differ in pigment identity or environment, analyze their extracts under matched conditions and compare intact tissue pH and spectral properties separately. If color differences disappear in standardized solution while chromatographic profiles match, the environment hypothesis becomes stronger. The experiment does not on its own prove which ion transporter is responsible.

6. Condensed tannins and seed-coat pigmentation

Condensed tannins are also called proanthocyanidins. They are oligomers or polymers built mainly from flavan-3-ol-related units, such as catechin and epicatechin. These are not the same compounds as anthocyanins despite the related names. A flavan-3-ol has a hydroxyl group at carbon 3 and differs in the oxidation state of its central ring from an anthocyanidin. Catechin and epicatechin are stereoisomers, which means their atoms are connected similarly but arranged differently in three-dimensional space. Both can participate in tannin structures.

The DFR product leucoanthocyanidin can contribute to flavan-3-ol formation through leucoanthocyanidin reductase (LAR). Anthocyanidin reductase (ANR) provides another route from anthocyanidin-related intermediates. These routes are often represented with catechin and epicatechin as representative products, but actual stereochemistry and downstream assembly vary by species and enzyme. The pathways leading to the starter and extension units of a polymer are more complicated than a single enzyme simply joining free catechin molecules. At examination level, retain the shared origin and the distinction between reduced monomeric units and visible anthocyanin glycosides; do not assume that every polymerization mechanism is fully settled.

In common B-type proanthocyanidins the flavan units are connected by carbon–carbon bonds, often involving carbon 4 of one unit and carbon 8 or carbon 6 of another. Some A-type structures have an additional ether linkage. Chain length, stereochemistry, hydroxylation and galloylation contribute to chemical diversity. An extract can therefore contain a population of oligomers and polymers rather than a single molecule of precisely specified mass. A reported total condensed tannin concentration depends partly on the assay and reference standard.

Proanthocyanidins may be nearly colorless before oxidation. In the Arabidopsis seed coat, accumulation and subsequent oxidation are associated with the familiar mature brown testa. Transparent testa mutants provide a powerful entry point for studying the pathway. A pale seed can arise through defects in precursor formation, ANR-dependent reactions, transport, oxidation or differentiation of the cells that normally accumulate tannin. The visual phenotype is the starting observation, not an automatic identification of the defective gene.

The BANYULS gene encodes anthocyanidin reductase in Arabidopsis. Its role helps explain why the anthocyanin and proanthocyanidin branches can compete for related intermediates. When the route to proanthocyanidins is impaired, precursors may be redirected into other products, but the actual outcome depends on the presence of other enzymes in the same cells. Studies of BAN expression and transparent testa regulatory mutants show that seed-coat cell identity and transcriptional control can be as important as the presence of a biosynthetic enzyme. This provides a connection between specialized metabolism and developmental biology.

A seed coat is a maternal tissue derived from the integuments of the ovule. When interpreting crosses involving seed pigmentation, it is therefore important not to automatically treat the seed-coat genotype as the genotype of the embryo. A seed produced on a mutant maternal plant may have an altered coat even if its embryo inherits a functional allele from the pollen parent. Reciprocal crosses can distinguish a maternal tissue effect from an ordinary embryo-genotype effect. Because the developing seed contains multiple genetically distinct tissues, the experimenter must specify which part of the seed was measured. The transparent testa phenotype is a valuable case for linking developmental origin to metabolic function.

The degree of polymerization of a proanthocyanidin is the number of monomer-derived units it contains. A dimer contains two units, a trimer three, and larger molecules have progressively more potential interaction sites. This simple count does not determine the full geometry or activity of a tannin. Different stereochemistry and linkage positions can change the three-dimensional shape and accessibility of hydroxyl groups. A preparation with the same mean degree of polymerization may have a different distribution of individual chain lengths. Therefore an average chain length can describe one aspect of the material without uniquely describing its molecular composition.

The name proanthocyanidin arises in part from the observation that appropriate chemical cleavage of these polymers can generate colored anthocyanidin-related products. An analytical treatment is not the same thing as the biosynthetic route in a living plant. Detecting red color after a treatment does not show that a red anthocyanin was initially stored in the seed coat. It indicates that the sample contains structures capable of producing colored derivatives under those assay conditions. This distinction is particularly useful when a question compares a colorless precursor with a colored product formed during extraction. The identity of the starting structure must be inferred from the chemistry of the transformation, not from color alone.

7. Gallotannins and ellagitannins

Hydrolysable tannins have a different biosynthetic origin from the condensed tannins. They commonly contain a glucose core esterified with gallic acid-derived groups. Gallic acid is a trihydroxybenzoic acid with seven carbons, and it can arise from the shikimate pathway intermediate 3-dehydroshikimate. This route diverges before the phenylalanine-to-p-coumaroyl-CoA sequence that supplies CHS. Consequently, a mutation that removes CHS activity need not abolish hydrolysable tannin biosynthesis.

Formation of beta-glucogallin, an ester of gallic acid and glucose, is an important early step. A UDP-dependent glycosyltransferase can catalyze formation of this activated galloyl-glucose derivative. Further galloyl-transfer reactions populate additional hydroxyl groups on the glucose core. Pentagalloylglucose has five esterified galloyl residues and is an important intermediate or product in many hydrolysable tannin pathways. The glucose core is a chemical scaffold, not a chain of flavonoid monomers.

Gallotannins contain multiple galloyl groups, sometimes with further galloylation. Ellagitannins are distinguished by oxidative coupling between suitably positioned galloyl residues, generating groups such as hexahydroxydiphenoyl (HHDP). Hydrolysis of the ester bonds releases related acids; the liberated HHDP-derived group can lactonize to ellagic acid. Ellagic acid detected after hydrolysis therefore does not imply that the intact plant compound was simply free ellagic acid. The analytical treatment can create a product characteristic of the original structure.

The term hydrolysable describes a prominent chemical susceptibility of the ester-linked architecture. Condensed tannins are not similarly cleaved into their constituent flavan units by ordinary ester hydrolysis because their principal interflavan links are carbon–carbon bonds. They may nevertheless undergo degradation or cleavage in more severe chemical conditions. Thus the contrast is not between a compound that can be broken down and one that is indestructible; it is between distinct types of bonding and reaction behavior.

Both tannin groups can coexist in a plant or tissue. Galloylated flavan-3-ols also exist, so the detection of gallic acid following an extraction or cleavage treatment is not by itself proof of a pure gallotannin sample. Classification of an unknown sample should combine information on the core scaffold, cleavage products, chromatographic separation and the chemical bonds inferred from suitable structural analysis. This is a recurring examination lesson: a single analytical signal may be consistent with more than one explanation.

The stoichiometry of ester formation can be used to derive a simple relationship for hydrolysable tannin composition. Glucose has five hydroxyl groups available for ordinary galloylation in the cyclic glucopyranose framework, including the anomeric hydroxyl. A fully pentagalloylated core contains five gallic acid-derived residues. If one writes a hypothetical overall condensation between one glucose and five free gallic acid molecules, each ester linkage corresponds formally to loss of one water molecule. With glucose C6H12O6 and gallic acid C7H6O5, subtracting five H2O from their combined formula gives C41H32O26. This is formula accounting, not a claim that free gallic acid directly esterifies glucose without enzymatic activation in a plant. The biological reactions use activated donors and sequential transfer.

Galloyl transfer and oxidative coupling solve different chemical problems. Galloylation attaches an aromatic acyl group to a hydroxyl oxygen. Oxidative coupling creates a new covalent relationship between appropriately placed aromatic groups already present on the scaffold. An ellagitannin may therefore have fewer independently releasable galloyl groups than an apparently similar gallotannin, depending on its coupling pattern. Under hydrolysis the coupled unit can generate ellagic acid after lactonization. A chemical analysis that measures gallic acid alone may underestimate the extent of the original galloylation if coupled groups are present. Interpreting both classes of products gives a more informative picture.

Compare the precursor requirements of hydrolysable tannins and flavonoids using a selective lesion. A block in PAL reduces the conversion of phenylalanine to cinnamate and can affect several phenylpropanoid products. The gallic acid branch arises earlier from shikimate-related intermediates, so it does not require the same deamination reaction. A lesion in the shikimate pathway at a point upstream of both routes may affect both, but the precise outcome depends on the position of the lesion and availability of metabolites. This is why one must place the blocked enzyme on a pathway diagram before predicting which products disappear. The broad classification phenolics is not sufficient for predicting metabolic dependencies.

8. Light screening, reproduction and ecological defense

The location of a metabolite is often as important as its identity. A flavonol in the outer cell layers of a leaf is positioned to absorb ultraviolet radiation before it reaches sensitive structures deeper in the tissue. This optical function differs from direct chemical scavenging of reactive oxygen species. Both processes can contribute to protection, but a pigment need not enter a chloroplast or react with radicals to reduce the radiation reaching it. UV exposure may induce flavonoid accumulation through light-responsive regulatory networks. Comparing sensitive and tolerant genotypes requires controlling for unrelated differences in leaf anatomy, chlorophyll and stress signaling.

Anthocyanins absorb visible light, which can influence pollinator and seed-disperser behavior. The relevant signal is the spectrum perceived by the organism interacting with the plant, not simply the color category used by humans. Some pollinating insects detect ultraviolet patterns that human vision cannot see. Flavonols and other UV-absorbing compounds can contribute to these patterns even where the tissue appears nearly colorless to us. A change in flower color can affect visitation, but seed set also depends on pollinator effectiveness, floral reward, flower number and reproductive compatibility.

In vegetative tissues, anthocyanin accumulation can accompany high irradiance, nutrient limitation, low temperature or other changes. The pigment may filter excess radiation or participate in stress-associated chemistry, but a correlation between redness and stress tolerance does not demonstrate causation. One must compare genotypes or manipulations that differ in the pigment while accounting for other traits. The same environmental condition can activate a broad response involving many proteins and metabolites.

Tannins are commonly associated with astringency. Their multiple phenolic groups can interact with proteins through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, sometimes leading to aggregation. Astringency in the mouth involves interactions with salivary proteins and changes in lubrication. In herbivore feeding, tannins may affect palatability, nutrient availability or digestive processes. Their impact depends on tannin structure, diet, gut conditions and the animal's adaptations. It is incorrect to infer from one feeding experiment that all tannins are toxic to all herbivores.

Phenolics can participate in redox reactions because oxidation products may be stabilized by electron delocalization. An antioxidant assay performed in solution can establish activity against a particular reagent under defined conditions. It does not establish that the same concentration reaches a biological target or that a compound has a particular health benefit. Within a plant, compartmentation, enzymes, reaction partners and local concentrations determine physiological effects. Some phenolics can also participate in oxidative reactions under certain conditions. Use the term antioxidant with a specified context instead of treating it as an unconditional property.

Production and storage carry opportunity costs. Carbon and reducing power allocated to specialized metabolism are unavailable for other reactions at that instant, although photosynthesis and regulation can adjust supply. More tannin is not automatically advantageous: defense can involve tradeoffs with growth, reproduction and interactions with beneficial organisms. The optimal metabolic allocation is therefore shaped by the environment and by the organism's developmental stage. The broader regulation of these allocations is addressed later in the subunit.

Protein binding by tannins can be considered as a multivalent interaction. A single phenolic hydroxyl can participate in a weak hydrogen bond, but a molecule bearing many suitably positioned groups can contact several regions of a protein. Binding may be reinforced by hydrophobic contacts between aromatic surfaces and nonpolar protein regions. At suitable concentrations a tannin can bridge proteins or alter their solubility, resulting in aggregation. However, increasing the tannin concentration does not always yield a proportional increase in precipitated protein; there may be saturation, soluble complexes or changes in the ratio of binding partners. Thus the observed degree of precipitation depends on the assay conditions as well as the identity of the compound.

A plant defense trait may change the feeding choice of an herbivore without directly changing its growth rate on a forced diet. Conversely, an herbivore may choose a food with favorable cues but grow poorly on it if digestion is impaired. Choice experiments and performance experiments therefore answer different questions. In a two-choice experiment compare consumption of otherwise matched tissues with different tannin content. In a no-choice experiment measure growth or survival while controlling nutrient and water content. A defense interpretation is strongest when chemical measurements, behavioral outcomes and physiological performance are consistent, and when differences in unrelated tissue traits have been considered.

Microbial inhibition by phenolic extracts is likewise context dependent. An extract may contain mixtures of metabolites with different solubility and reactivity. If a crude extract reduces growth, one cannot assign all activity to its most abundant tannin without separating or otherwise testing components. A purified compound that inhibits growth in vitro may not achieve a similar concentration at the site of infection. Conversely, a metabolite may act in combination with a physical barrier or other immune response. The physiological conclusion must be proportional to the experimental evidence. This reasoning avoids treating every secondary metabolite as a universally effective antibiotic.

9. Experimental logic and quantitative interpretation

A pathway mutant can be analyzed by comparing its precursor and product pools with a wild-type control grown under the same conditions. Consider an illustrative CHS-deficient genotype in which the flavanone pool is low and both flavonols and anthocyanins are low. That result fits a block near the shared entry point. If instead flavonols remain abundant while anthocyanins decline, an alteration after the divergence toward dihydroflavonol reduction is more plausible. Such inferences depend on the assumption that the same tissue, developmental stage and relevant environmental conditions are sampled.

To distinguish a DFR defect from a glycosylation or vacuolar storage defect, one should examine more than final color. Dihydroflavonol accumulation may suggest a block in its conversion; enzyme assays or complementation can provide additional evidence. Detection of an altered balance between aglycones and glycosides suggests a modification defect. Localization of pigment or intermediates in the wrong cellular compartment suggests a transport or storage defect. No single pattern necessarily proves a particular causal gene because feedback, alternative substrates and turnover can modify the observed pools.

Genetic complementation offers a stronger test of causality. Introduction of a functioning allele into an otherwise comparable mutant may restore the affected phenotype. A rescue experiment requires an appropriate negative control, and restoration of pigment should be accompanied by a biochemical measurement if the proposed mechanism concerns metabolism. A mutation can affect a regulator that controls several enzymes simultaneously, so gene rescue alone does not identify the exact molecular step unless other evidence supports it.

In isotope-label reasoning, track atoms rather than entire molecules. Suppose only the six aromatic carbons of supplied phenylalanine carry a label, and the label is not redistributed by unrelated metabolism during the observation period. A flavonoid made from that precursor can contain six labeled atoms in its B ring. By contrast, labeling the retained two-carbon portion of each of three malonyl extenders produces up to six labeled carbons in its A ring. The assumptions are essential: long labeling periods may allow carbon redistribution through central metabolism, and the presence of an isotopic label in a pool is not a direct measurement of net flux without kinetic information.

Consider an idealized branch with a common dihydroflavonol input of 100 nmol per hour. Suppose 40 nmol per hour enters the FLS branch, 50 enters DFR and 10 exits through other routes. If supply and the other loss remain constant while FLS flux rises to 65, mass balance leaves 25 for DFR at steady state. The reduction is 25 nmol per hour, or 50 percent of the original DFR flux. This is a calculation from deliberately simplified assumptions, not measured plant data. In a real tissue the precursor may accumulate transiently, total input can change and pathways can be regulated in response.

Absorbance measurements can help compare pigment amounts when the conditions are appropriate. The Beer–Lambert relation is A = εlc, where A is dimensionless absorbance, ε is molar absorptivity, l is optical path length and c is molar concentration. For a hypothetical purified pigment with ε = 25,000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, a one-centimeter cuvette and absorbance 0.50 give c = 0.50 divided by 25,000, or 20 micromolar. If the sample was diluted fivefold, the original concentration was 100 micromolar. This example assumes the same absorbing form and pH used to define ε, negligible scattering and a linear absorbance response. A mixture of different pigments cannot generally be quantified using one extinction coefficient without additional assumptions.

A pH-differential approach to anthocyanin measurement exploits differences in absorbance between controlled pH conditions. Its interpretation depends on pigment chemistry, equilibration, turbidity correction and the reference compound used for reporting equivalent concentration. Chromatography can resolve compounds that overlap in a bulk absorption measurement. Mass spectrometry can assist identification of molecular masses and fragments, but isomeric compounds may require additional evidence. For NET reasoning, the key distinction is between an operational total-pigment measurement and an identification of individual compounds.

An experiment testing UV protection could compare a flavonoid-deficient line, its complemented line and the corresponding wild type under both normal light and added UV. A meaningful response might include growth, photosystem performance or DNA damage, together with measured epidermal UV absorption. If the mutant is impaired only under UV and complementation restores both absorption and performance, the evidence is stronger than an association between color and survival. It still matters whether the complemented gene changes other metabolic features. Appropriate replication and controls allow the experimenter to separate genotype, environment and their interaction.

A simple model of metabolite accumulation can separate production from destruction. For an amount P of a pigment in a cell, write dP/dt = vproduction − vloss. At steady state dP/dt is zero, so production and loss rates are equal, even if the amount of pigment is large. Suppose loss is approximately first order, vloss = kP, with k in inverse hours. The steady-state amount is then vproduction/k. A doubling of stored pigment could result from doubled synthesis, halved loss, or a combination of changes. This is why concentration is not equivalent to flux. A labeling experiment or a time series following controlled changes can provide information unavailable from a single static measurement.

Consider a hypothetical pigment pool with a production rate of 12 nanomoles per hour and a first-order loss constant of 0.3 per hour. The predicted steady-state amount is 40 nanomoles. If a mutation reduces the loss constant to 0.15 per hour while production remains fixed, the new steady-state amount is 80 nanomoles. The enzyme producing pigment did not become more active in this model. A color increase could instead result from slower turnover. These numbers are illustrative and refer to an amount in a defined compartment; concentration would require knowledge of its volume. The model assumes constant production and first-order loss over the relevant interval.

A second analysis concerns normalization. An extract from a 200-milligram petal sample contains 10 nanomoles of a particular anthocyanin. A sample from a different cultivar contains 12 nanomoles but weighs 400 milligrams. The first sample contains 50 nanomoles per gram fresh mass, while the second contains 30 nanomoles per gram. The sample with greater total amount has lower mass-normalized concentration. Fresh mass can itself change with water content, so dry-mass normalization may be useful in some experiments. Neither normalization is universally correct: the appropriate denominator depends on whether the question concerns pigment per tissue, per dry material, per cell or per whole organ.

A comparison between a wild type and a mutant should include biological replication. Several measurements from the same extract may estimate analytical precision but do not independently sample biological variation. Multiple independently grown plants allow inference about reproducibility across organisms. If plants differ in developmental stage, light exposure or nutrition, those conditions can confound a genotype comparison. A controlled design uses comparable material and distributes environmental variation as consistently as possible. This principle applies equally to the analysis of flower pigmentation, leaf UV screening and seed coat chemistry. It is more important to understand the reason for the control than to memorize a prescribed number of replicates.

10. Common misconceptions

All phenolics are flavonoids

Gallic acid and many phenylpropanoids are phenolics without a flavonoid skeleton.

CHS uses only phenylalanine

The immediate substrates are p-coumaroyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA; PAL acts upstream.

Three malonyl groups add nine retained carbons

Three carbon dioxide molecules are lost, leaving six retained carbons.

CHI adds oxygen to chalcone

It catalyzes intramolecular isomerization, not net addition of oxygen.

F3H and F3′H are interchangeable

F3H acts at the central ring; F3′H changes B-ring substitution.

Flavones and flavonols differ only in color

Flavonols have a characteristic 3-hydroxyl group.

Isoflavones have a different carbon count

Their key distinction is B-ring attachment at carbon 3 rather than carbon 2.

Anthocyanidin and anthocyanin are synonyms

Anthocyanins are glycosylated anthocyanidin derivatives.

A blue petal proves delphinidin is present

pH, co-pigments, metals and modifications can alter hue.

Higher vacuolar pH always gives a stable blue pigment

Hydration and other equilibria can reduce color or stability.

All anthocyanin synthesis occurs inside vacuoles

Core biosynthetic reactions occur outside the vacuole; storage is often vacuolar.

Condensed tannins are anthocyanin polymers

Their principal building units are flavan-3-ol-related structures.

All tannins require CHS

Hydrolysable tannins arise from gallic acid and glucose-derived scaffolds.

Ellagic acid must have been free in the original sample

Hydrolysis of HHDP-containing tannins can produce it.

Antioxidant assay activity proves in-vivo protection

The physiological outcome also depends on concentration, localization and reaction partners.

11. Original examination practice

Question 1 · Part B

Which extender is used by CHS?

Answer and reasoning

Malonyl-CoA Decarboxylative extension adds two retained carbons per extender.

Question 2 · Part B

How many retained carbons does p-coumaroyl-CoA contribute to the common flavonoid core?

Answer and reasoning

Nine Its aromatic ring and three-carbon side chain supply the nine-carbon starter.

Question 3 · Part B

Which enzyme converts naringenin chalcone into predominantly (2S)-naringenin?

Answer and reasoning

CHI Chalcone isomerase directs stereoselective intramolecular cyclization.

Question 4 · Part B

Which class carries the B ring at position 3 of the central ring?

Answer and reasoning

Isoflavonoids The rearrangement distinguishes the isoflavonoid skeleton.

Question 5 · Part B

Which enzyme produces dihydroflavonols from flavanones?

Answer and reasoning

F3H Hydroxylation is at central-ring carbon 3, not B-ring carbon 3′.

Question 6 · Part B

What distinguishes anthocyanins from anthocyanidins?

Answer and reasoning

Glycosylation An anthocyanin contains sugar substituents on the anthocyanidin-derived framework.

Question 7 · Part B

Which enzyme reduces dihydroflavonols toward leucoanthocyanidins?

Answer and reasoning

DFR This is a reduction step using a reducing equivalent such as NADPH.

Question 8 · Part B

Which anthocyanidin has three B-ring hydroxyl groups at 3′, 4′ and 5′?

Answer and reasoning

Delphinidin Cyanidin has two, while pelargonidin has one.

Question 9 · Part B

What are the main structural units of condensed tannins?

Answer and reasoning

Flavan-3-ol-related units They are not polymers of anthocyanin glycosides.

Question 10 · Part B

Which early shikimate-pathway intermediate supplies gallic acid?

Answer and reasoning

3-Dehydroshikimate This is a route to hydrolysable tannins distinct from CHS.

Question 11 · Part B

What is the membrane surrounding a vacuole called?

Answer and reasoning

Tonoplast It separates the stored pigment environment from the cytosol.

Question 12 · Part B

What is characteristic of ellagitannins?

Answer and reasoning

Oxidatively coupled galloyl groups such as HHDP Hydrolysis may release groups that yield ellagic acid.

Question 13 · Part C

A line loses anthocyanins and flavonols simultaneously. Which common upstream enzyme is a candidate, and why is the result insufficient to prove it?

Answer and reasoning

CHS is a candidate; a shared regulator or precursor defect is also possible. The products share the entry route, but multiple genetic or metabolic disruptions could cause the same phenotype.

Question 14 · Part C

A line accumulates flavonols but has very little anthocyanin. Why is complete CHS loss unlikely?

Answer and reasoning

Flavonol synthesis requires the shared CHS entry pathway. A downstream branch or modification defect is more consistent, assuming the flavonols are synthesized in the sampled tissue.

Question 15 · Part C

Two petals have identical anthocyanin profiles but different vacuolar pH. Propose a mechanistic cause of their different hue.

Answer and reasoning

A shift in chemical equilibria of the pigment. Vacuolar proton transport can alter the distribution of colored and less-colored forms without changing the original pigment composition.

Question 16 · Part C

A precursor flux of 80 nmol h⁻¹ is split between FLS (30), DFR (40) and other loss (10). If FLS rises to 50 and all other stated inputs and losses remain fixed, find DFR flux.

Answer and reasoning

20 nmol h⁻¹ At steady state 80 − 50 − 10 = 20; the DFR flux has halved.

Question 17 · Part C

A vacuole changes from pH 5.5 to 4.5. What happens to hydrogen-ion activity?

Answer and reasoning

It rises tenfold. One pH unit is a factor of ten on the logarithmic scale.

Question 18 · Part C

A purified pigment with ε = 20,000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ gives absorbance 0.4 in a 1 cm cuvette. Find concentration.

Answer and reasoning

20 μM c = A/(εl) = 0.4/20,000 = 0.00002 M, assuming a linear response and the specified pigment form.

Question 19 · Part C

Why could a CHS-null genotype still contain gallotannins?

Answer and reasoning

The gallic acid route is independent of the CHS entry reaction. The tannins are assembled around an esterified glucose scaffold, not the flavonoid skeleton.

Question 20 · Part C

A pale seed coat mutant has normal CHS activity. Suggest two distinct hypotheses.

Answer and reasoning

ANR-related pathway impairment or altered transport/oxidation. CHS activity does not demonstrate that downstream synthesis, localization or seed-coat oxidation is normal.

Question 21 · Part C

If the aromatic ring of phenylalanine alone is labeled, which ring of a newly synthesized ordinary flavonoid should contain the directly retained label?

Answer and reasoning

B ring The coumaroyl starter contributes the B ring, assuming no metabolic redistribution.

Question 22 · Part C

How could you distinguish an effect of UV screening from a generic genotype effect?

Answer and reasoning

Compare genotypes under both UV and control light and include complementation. An interaction with UV exposure plus restored optical screening and physiological protection strengthens the causal inference.

Question 23 · Part B

Which compound is the typical C9 starter for chalcone synthase?

Answer and reasoning

p-Coumaroyl-CoA It supplies the B ring and three-carbon connector; malonyl-CoA builds the A ring.

Question 24 · Part B

Which reaction removes the amino group before formation of common flavonoids?

Answer and reasoning

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase reaction PAL converts phenylalanine into cinnamate with release of ammonia.

Question 25 · Part B

Which cofactor is generally consumed by DFR?

Answer and reasoning

NADPH DFR performs a reduction, requiring reducing equivalents.

Question 26 · Part B

Name a representative flavonol.

Answer and reasoning

Quercetin Quercetin has the flavonol framework including the 3-hydroxyl and C2–C3 double bond.

Question 27 · Part B

What activated donor commonly supplies glucose to an anthocyanidin?

Answer and reasoning

UDP-glucose A glycosyltransferase transfers the sugar from an activated nucleotide-sugar donor.

Question 28 · Part B

What structural feature differentiates a flavone from a flavanone?

Answer and reasoning

A double bond between C2 and C3 Flavanones retain a saturated C2–C3 bond, whereas flavones have the unsaturated connection.

Question 29 · Part B

Which Arabidopsis gene encodes anthocyanidin reductase?

Answer and reasoning

BANYULS (BAN) The enzyme participates in seed-coat proanthocyanidin formation.

Question 30 · Part B

What is the typical storage compartment of anthocyanins?

Answer and reasoning

Vacuole Core biosynthesis occurs outside the vacuole, followed by delivery and storage.

Question 31 · Part B

Which linkage is characteristic of many B-type condensed tannins?

Answer and reasoning

C4–C8 or C4–C6 carbon–carbon linkage This differs from the ester bonds characteristic of galloylated glucose.

Question 32 · Part B

Which compound is an early galloyl-glucose intermediate?

Answer and reasoning

β-Glucogallin It can serve in further galloylation reactions.

Question 33 · Part B

What type of enzyme is Petunia PH5?

Answer and reasoning

Tonoplast P-type proton ATPase It helps acidify the vacuolar lumen.

Question 34 · Part B

Which chemical family commonly participates in legume–rhizobium signaling?

Answer and reasoning

Flavonoids Specific compounds can influence nod gene expression in suitable rhizobia.

Question 35 · Part C

A plant has high FLS transcript but unchanged flavonol accumulation. Why is this not contradictory?

Answer and reasoning

Transcript abundance need not track flux or steady-state abundance. Substrate availability, enzyme abundance, catalysis, turnover and transport intervene.

Question 36 · Part C

A mutant has normal total anthocyanin after acid extraction but an altered petal hue. Suggest an additional measurement.

Answer and reasoning

Vacuolar pH or intact-tissue spectral properties. Standardized acidic extraction can mask environmental differences. A pH measurement can test one candidate mechanism.

Question 37 · Part C

A system receives 120 nmol starter and 270 nmol malonyl-CoA without replenishment. What is the theoretical maximum chalcone output?

Answer and reasoning

90 nmol Each product requires three extenders; 270/3 = 90, smaller than 120.

Question 38 · Part C

A pigment has constant production of 15 nmol/h and first-order loss constant 0.5/h. Calculate steady-state amount.

Answer and reasoning

30 nmol At steady state production = kP, so P = 15/0.5.

Question 39 · Part C

A 0.25 g sample contains 15 nmol pigment and a 0.50 g sample contains 20 nmol. Which has higher amount per gram?

Answer and reasoning

First sample The normalized values are 60 and 40 nmol/g respectively.

Question 40 · Part C

Why can prolonged feeding with labeled bicarbonate obscure direct carbon tracing through malonyl-CoA?

Answer and reasoning

Released labeled CO₂ can be reassimilated. Label redistribution through central metabolism violates the assumption that the tracer follows only one direct reaction.

Question 41 · Part C

A PAL mutant retains gallotannins but has reduced flavonols. Explain.

Answer and reasoning

The gallic acid branch does not require PAL, whereas the common flavonoid starter is produced through PAL-dependent metabolism. The two phenolic routes share earlier metabolism but diverge before PAL.

Question 42 · Part C

A petal line expresses F3′5′H but lacks delphinidin-type pigment. Name two possible explanations.

Answer and reasoning

DFR substrate incompatibility or a downstream conversion/storage limitation. Hydroxylase expression alone is not evidence that subsequent enzymes accept or accumulate its products.

Question 43 · Part C

A mutation in a tonoplast transporter decreases visible pigment despite normal CHS activity. Which step is implicated?

Answer and reasoning

Intracellular delivery or storage is a candidate. The entry enzyme can remain functional while pigment delivery or retention fails; additional localization data are needed.

Question 44 · Part C

Why can a reciprocal cross be useful for seed coat pigmentation?

Answer and reasoning

Seed coat is maternal tissue. The embryo and maternal integuments differ in genetic origin, so reciprocal crosses can separate maternal effects from embryo genotype effects.

Question 45 · Part C

A tannin extract strongly precipitates one protein but not another at equal concentration. Why?

Answer and reasoning

Protein structure and binding chemistry differ. Multivalent tannin interactions depend on accessible binding sites and reaction conditions, not tannin concentration alone.

Question 46 · Part C

A UV-deficient mutant grows poorly with or without UV. Does this alone establish a UV-screening defect?

Answer and reasoning

No. A genotype effect independent of UV is compatible with unrelated growth problems; an interaction with UV and rescue would be stronger evidence.

Question 47 · Part C

Two treatments yield equal final pigment abundance but one has twice the production flux. How is that possible at steady state?

Answer and reasoning

Its loss flux must also be twice as high. Steady-state abundance reflects balance between production and loss rather than either rate alone.

Question 48 · Part C

A sample yields ellagic acid only following hydrolysis. What does this suggest?

Answer and reasoning

An HHDP-containing precursor is a candidate. Released HHDP-related groups can lactonize to ellagic acid; the untreated sample need not contain free ellagic acid.

Question 49 · Part C

An experiment compares three absorbance measurements of one extract with one measurement from each of three independent plants. Which better samples biological variation?

Answer and reasoning

Measurements from independent plants. Technical replicates estimate measurement precision but are not independent biological samples.

Question 50 · Part C

A candidate flavonoid-deficient mutant shows reduced UV absorbance, but adding back the functional gene restores both absorbance and growth under UV. What is the strongest supported interpretation?

Answer and reasoning

The gene-dependent pathway contributes to UV protection under the tested conditions. Complementation strengthens causality; it does not by itself exclude all pleiotropic mechanisms.

Question 51 · Part C

At constant total precursor input, DFR branch flux increases by 15 units while other losses are fixed. What happens to FLS flux in a two-branch steady-state model?

Answer and reasoning

It decreases by 15 units. Mass balance requires the sum of branch fluxes to remain constant under the assumptions.

Question 52 · Part C

An extract shows antioxidant activity in a reagent assay. What additional information is needed before claiming an in-plant protective role?

Answer and reasoning

Localization, relevant concentrations and physiological evidence. The assay establishes a chemical property under selected conditions, not effectiveness at a biological target.

Question 53 · Part C

If two petal extracts have equal pigment concentration but different extinction coefficients, will they give equal absorbance at the same wavelength and path length?

Answer and reasoning

Not necessarily. A = εlc, so absorbance depends on molar absorptivity as well as concentration.
