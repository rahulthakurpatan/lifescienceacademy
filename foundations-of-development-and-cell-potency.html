1. Development as an explanatory problem

A fertilized animal egg looks nothing like the adult organism that it can produce. It has no functional brain, beating heart, circulating blood, articulated skeleton or differentiated skin. Nevertheless, it contains or receives the material and regulatory information from which these structures can arise. Developmental biology asks how this transition occurs, how cells become different from one another, and how the resulting diversity is assembled into a coherent individual. The problem cannot be solved simply by listing the genes present in the egg. Genes are necessary, but their activity, the organization of the cytoplasm, the positions of cells, their interactions and the history of previous events all contribute to the outcome. The same genome may support several different cellular states, and the cells occupying those states do different jobs in the organism.

Development is not identical to growth. Growth refers to an increase in size or mass. A cell can grow without changing its identity, as when a differentiated cell increases its cytoplasmic volume. A tissue can grow by increasing the number of cells or the amount of extracellular material without creating a new tissue type. Conversely, an early embryo can undergo profound developmental changes without appreciably increasing its overall volume. During cleavage, a large egg is partitioned into progressively smaller blastomeres. The increasing number of cellular compartments allows regulatory information to be distributed and interpreted differently, even when the total amount of material inherited from the egg changes little. Thus, counting cells or measuring body length tells us only part of the developmental story.

Differentiation is the acquisition of characteristic cellular properties. An immature precursor becomes a neuron when it develops neuronal molecular machinery, appropriate morphology and physiological activity. These characteristics need not emerge simultaneously. A cell may start expressing a transcription factor associated with a neural lineage long before it extends an axon or generates an action potential. Developmental investigators distinguish early regulatory changes from the eventual specialized function. Morphogenesis is another dimension of development: it concerns the production and organization of shape. Epithelial cells may bend a sheet into a tube, migrate to a different region, change their adhesion or reorganize their cytoskeleton. A tissue with correct cell types can still be abnormal if those cells occupy inappropriate positions. Differentiation and morphogenesis therefore address different questions, even though they influence one another.

Consider a developing vertebrate spinal cord. Neural precursors must produce appropriate kinds of neurons and glial cells. The resulting cells must also be positioned correctly, connect to the appropriate targets and survive. A perturbation that changes neuronal identity differs from a perturbation that merely disrupts axon guidance. A third perturbation may allow neuronal identity to appear but prevent cells from surviving. A microscopic image showing fewer neurons at the end of development cannot by itself distinguish these possibilities. To explain the phenotype, one needs evidence about earlier states, movements, divisions and cell death. This distinction between describing an endpoint and identifying the mechanism is central to developmental reasoning.

Development is a process in time as well as space. An embryonic signal delivered early can produce a different response from the same signal delivered later because cells change their capacities to respond. A signal may initially act on a broad field of cells and subsequently act on only a restricted population. The history of a tissue matters because previous exposures may alter transcription factors, receptor abundance or chromatin accessibility. A developmental stage is consequently more informative than chronological age alone when comparing embryos that develop at different rates. Experimental comparisons should use compatible morphological stages, appropriate reference samples and, where relevant, precise exposure durations.

In an animal, development normally connects gamete formation, fertilization, embryogenesis and later processes such as growth, maturation, tissue maintenance and regeneration. In a flowering plant, embryogenesis establishes the primary body plan, but substantial organ production continues throughout the life of the plant. Meristems retain cells that generate new tissues and organs. A plant may initiate a new branch or produce a root at a site where an animal would not normally generate a comparable new body part. This contrast does not imply that animal development stops at birth or that plant cells are universally unrestricted. It shows that organisms organize developmental potential differently over their life histories.

A useful unifying question is what a particular cell can become. The question may sound straightforward, but it has several possible meanings. We might ask what the cell normally becomes in an intact embryo, what it can form if removed from that embryo, what happens if we place it beside different neighbors, or what it can produce after an intervention that fundamentally changes its regulatory state. Each question requires a different experiment. The term potency will allow us to describe the range of developmental outcomes a cell is capable of producing, whereas fate describes its actual or usual developmental outcome. Keeping these ideas distinct prevents confusion throughout the rest of developmental biology.

2. Cell fate, potential, identity and the conditions of an assay

Imagine labeling one cell in an embryo with a stable marker that is inherited by its descendants. When the animal develops normally, the marker is found only in a particular population of skeletal muscle cells. The observation establishes that the labeled lineage contributed to muscle in the observed embryos. It does not establish that the original cell was incapable of making a neuron or a blood cell. Those alternative outcomes might be inaccessible under the normal position and signaling conditions experienced by that cell, even if the cell could produce them in a different environment. A lineage-tracing experiment provides a record of ancestry and realized outcomes. It cannot automatically establish the complete developmental repertoire of the starting cell.

Potency concerns the range of cell types, tissues or developmental outcomes that a cell can generate under specified conditions. It is a functional property, not simply a description of how a cell looks. Two cells can appear almost identical under a microscope yet differ substantially in their potential because they express different regulatory proteins or have different chromatin states. Conversely, cells that differ in their shape or position may retain overlapping developmental capacities. To classify potency, investigators must ask what a cell actually does when tested in an appropriate context. An experiment may reveal only part of a cell's repertoire because the available culture conditions do not support every possible outcome.

This operational definition introduces an important distinction between a cell's existing potency and its capacity to acquire a different potency. A skin fibroblast may be converted experimentally into a pluripotent cell. The resulting cell can be tested for a broad developmental repertoire. The original fibroblast was not thereby demonstrated to be naturally pluripotent before conversion. The intervention changed the cell state. Describing the starting fibroblast as pluripotent would erase the very biological transition that the experiment was designed to investigate. Similar reasoning applies when a somatic nucleus is placed into an egg: the reprogramming environment is a decisive part of the evidence.

For clarity, think of a developmental state as comprising a genome, its current regulatory configuration, intracellular molecules, cellular organization and a physiological condition. A cell is not merely a DNA sequence. Its repertoire depends on which genes can be activated, which pathways can respond and whether it can survive and participate in the relevant developmental setting. The experimentally observed potential is a property of the state in the environment used for testing. When comparing potency classifications, ask whether the investigators supplied ordinary permissive developmental cues, changed positional information, or introduced a strong state-altering intervention. These situations should not be treated as equivalent.

Fate also requires a defined context. A cell that would normally become part of the epidermis in one embryo region may develop differently if transplanted to a region that induces neural identity. If a label follows the transplanted cell and identifies its descendants in neural tissue, the original fate prediction has changed in the new location. This is evidence of conditional developmental behavior if the transplantation preserves viability and the identity change is genuine. However, the result cannot establish the ability to generate every other lineage. A single unexpected outcome demonstrates one additional possibility, not an unlimited repertoire.

Specification and determination are experimental concepts that help connect fate and potency. A specified cell is biased or committed sufficiently to follow a particular developmental path in an appropriately neutral environment. Determination is inferred when a cell retains its original developmental program even after transplantation to a different location that would ordinarily favor another fate. The neutrality of an isolation environment and the instructive capacity of a transplant environment must be justified. Cells may appear committed simply because they are stressed or unable to respond to the test conditions. Genuine determination is inferred through controls demonstrating that the assay can distinguish responsive from resistant cells. The detailed types of specification are developed in a later chapter; here they clarify why a predicted fate and a restricted potential are not interchangeable concepts.

Differentiation refers instead to expressed properties. A cell may be determined to become muscle but not yet produce mature contractile structures. A differentiated cell may retain some plasticity, especially when injured tissue provides new regulatory cues. Commitment, differentiation, potency and proliferation are therefore separate axes of cellular description. The words cannot safely be substituted for each other in examination statements. In particular, a lineage-restricted precursor might divide rapidly, whereas an adult stem cell with broader potential might remain quiescent for a long period.

At the level of a population, an additional problem arises. Suppose ten cultured cells collectively generate neural, muscle and intestinal derivatives. This may reflect a population of broadly potent cells or a mixture of separate lineage-restricted cells. Without evidence that one starting cell generated the distinct lineages, the experiment cannot establish individual-cell potency. Investigators use clonal methods, single-cell sorting, genetic barcoding and other approaches to identify the descendants of individual cells. These methods require controls for doublets, overlapping labels, contamination and cell fusion. A broad population-level outcome is important but does not have the same evidential meaning as a broad clonal outcome.

The meaning of a negative result also depends on the assay. If an isolated cell fails to generate cardiac tissue, the simplest interpretation might be that its cardiac potential is absent. But the cell may have died, the medium may have lacked a necessary signal, or the observation may have ended before cardiac differentiation could occur. A properly designed experiment measures viability, includes known responsive control cells, and assesses both early lineage specification and mature function when feasible. Negative evidence is strongest when the assay is demonstrably capable of revealing the potential being tested.

3. The classical potency hierarchy and its biological meaning

The familiar developmental hierarchy begins with totipotency, followed by pluripotency, multipotency, more restricted intermediate states and unipotency. This hierarchy summarizes the breadth of developmental output rather than ranking cells by importance or health. A specialized hepatocyte may be indispensable for survival even though it has a much narrower developmental repertoire than a cell in an early embryo. Broad potential is valuable during the generation of cell diversity, whereas restricted identity supports stable organization and specialized physiological function. Normal development depends on both properties at different times and places.

Totipotency is the broadest developmental capacity. In the mammalian context, a strictly totipotent cell can generate the embryonic tissues of the individual and the fetal extraembryonic lineages needed for development of an integrated conceptus, given appropriate maternal and environmental support. The fertilized egg is the standard example. Some early cleavage-stage blastomeres can demonstrate extensive developmental autonomy in particular species and experimental conditions. The strength of the classification depends on the developmental criterion actually met. Production of cells with trophoblast-like and embryonic markers is weaker evidence than production of properly organized embryonic and extraembryonic tissues that sustain complete development. Maternal tissues of the uterus do not have to arise from the embryo; they are part of the physiological environment.

Pluripotency denotes a capacity to generate a broad spectrum of body cell types representing the embryonic germ layers. A pluripotent cell can normally give rise to ectodermal, mesodermal and endodermal derivatives when appropriate differentiation cues are provided. It is not classified as totipotent merely because it can generate many tissues. Conventional mammalian embryonic stem cells are the standard example. Their ability to contribute to an embryo proper can be impressive, but the ability to build all necessary supporting extraembryonic tissues and organize complete development independently has not been demonstrated simply by showing three-germ-layer differentiation. Particular culture states can display broader or different extraembryonic contributions; these findings require careful assay-specific interpretation rather than a change in terminology based on a single marker.

Multipotency refers to the ability to generate several cell types, commonly within a defined tissue system or related group of lineages. Hematopoietic stem cells provide a useful example because they can generate multiple classes of blood cells over time. A hematopoietic stem cell that produces erythroid, myeloid and lymphoid descendants demonstrates a repertoire extending across blood lineages. It does not ordinarily produce neurons, skeletal muscle fibers or intestinal epithelial cells. Multipotency is therefore broader than a single restricted fate but narrower than the broad capacity of a pluripotent cell. The precise breadth of a multipotent population may depend on its developmental stage, physiological setting and assay.

Oligopotency is sometimes used for a cell with only a few related developmental alternatives, such as a progenitor with a limited subset of hematopoietic outcomes. Bipotency is a particularly useful description when two distinguishable lineage outputs can be demonstrated. These words emphasize that restriction need not happen in one dramatic step from multipotency to unipotency. Intermediate states can differ in the probability of adopting each outcome, in how readily they respond to particular cues and in how stably their choices are maintained. A textbook diagram of discrete branches is helpful, but actual biological differentiation may involve gradual changes in regulatory state.

Unipotency describes the capacity to produce a single differentiated lineage or cell type under the relevant conditions. A unipotent stem cell must also retain self-renewal, whereas a unipotent progenitor may only divide a limited number of times. The term should not be confused with a terminally differentiated cell that cannot generate descendants. The capacity of a precursor to replenish one lineage is a developmental potential even though it has only one usual output. This distinction is particularly important in tissues that are maintained for many years by specialized stem cells. A cell can be developmentally restricted and still be exceptionally important for tissue maintenance.

The familiar ordering is best understood as a conceptual comparison of repertoires. It is not a universal timetable through which every cell necessarily passes. Some lineages may segregate before a conventional pluripotent state can be defined. The extraembryonic lineages of a mammalian conceptus diverge early and follow their own developmental trajectories. Tissue-specific precursors can be restricted at different times. Plants organize developmental plasticity in distinct ways from mammals, and their terminology cannot always be transferred without qualification. The hierarchy provides a language for comparing experimental outcomes, not an assertion that all organisms share identical cellular intermediates.

There is also no requirement that potency loss be perfectly irreversible. Some normally restricted states can change when regulatory barriers are altered by injury, culture conditions or artificial reprogramming. A mature lineage may be redirected toward another lineage, or a somatic cell may be returned to a broader stem-cell-like state. These transitions are exceptional relative to the ordinary developmental path and should be described using evidence about the starting and resulting cell states. A schematic arrow pointing from broader to narrower potential represents the typical direction during differentiation. It does not prove that every reverse or lateral transition is physically impossible.

4. Classical embryology and the discovery of developmental regulation

Before molecular biology made gene expression experimentally accessible, embryologists investigated how the parts of an embryo contribute to the whole. They observed development, physically separated or damaged regions, and followed the resulting structures. Their most consequential discovery was that the fate of a part in the intact embryo does not always define the capacity of that part in isolation. Classical experiments are best understood by reconstructing the question, the intervention and the alternative explanations rather than by memorizing a name and a result.

In the late nineteenth century Wilhelm Roux investigated early frog embryos by damaging one of the two blastomeres after the first cleavage. The experimental question was whether the surviving blastomere would produce a complete embryo or only the structures normally derived from its region. Roux observed incomplete, half-embryo-like development in some of these experiments. This supported the idea that developmental responsibilities could be allocated to early blastomeres. Yet a critical feature of the manipulation was that the damaged blastomere remained attached. A damaged neighbor is not equivalent to an absent neighbor. It can occupy space, alter physical relations and prevent normal reorganization. The experiment demonstrated a response to a particular defect, but it did not by itself establish that an undamaged isolated frog blastomere lacked broader potential.

Hans Driesch approached a related question with early sea urchin embryos. Rather than leaving damaged cells attached, he separated early blastomeres and observed what each could produce. Isolated blastomeres could form smaller but proportionately organized larvae. In an intact early embryo, each blastomere would normally contribute only part of the larval tissues. The ability of a separated blastomere to produce an organized larva revealed a developmental repertoire broader than its normal contribution. The size of the larva mattered: the result was not restoration of the original quantity of egg cytoplasm but reorganization of available material into a smaller integrated structure.

The contrast between these experiments is a classic lesson in experimental design. It is tempting to say that Roux proved mosaic development and Driesch disproved it. The more useful interpretation is that they used different species and different interventions. A defect experiment tests how remaining cells behave in a damaged configuration. An isolation experiment tests what separated cells can do when their immediate surroundings are changed. Different species can also differ in their capacity to regulate. To isolate the influence of the experimental manipulation, one would ideally compare physical separation and selective ablation in the same species at comparable stages, using viable untreated embryos as controls. The classical studies generated hypotheses whose generality required further testing.

Regulative development is the capacity of an embryo or a developmental field to adjust its pattern after perturbations. If an embryo loses part of its starting cell population and nevertheless forms the expected arrangement of structures, its development has compensated for the changed conditions. The compensatory process can involve rearrangement, changes in signaling relationships or altered proportions of cell types. Regulative behavior does not mean that all cells retain unrestricted potency throughout development. An embryo can compensate for a particular perturbation using cells that have only a limited additional repertoire. The extent of compensation depends on stage, species, position and the nature of the damage.

Mosaic development, in a classical sense, refers to a situation in which particular early cells contribute to defined developmental structures and their removal causes predictable deficiencies that other cells do not readily replace. This behavior is often associated with unequal distribution of developmental determinants. A cell may inherit particular maternal regulatory molecules that make one program more likely to be activated. However, mosaic behavior is not synonymous with a literal loss of the genes needed for other tissues. Even a strongly lineage-restricted cell may retain the genome needed for many developmental outcomes. Its restriction may arise from the regulatory state in which those genes are embedded.

A lineage can also have both mosaic and regulative aspects at different steps. An initial determinant may establish a population with a broad tissue identity, whereas interactions between members of that population subsequently decide finer cell types. The ability of the embryo to compensate may therefore vary depending on which level of identity is tested. The removal of an entire population carrying a necessary determinant may cause a severe defect, while removal of some cells within a later responsive field may be compensated. Labeling a whole species as exclusively mosaic or exclusively regulative hides this complexity. The useful unit of analysis is the developmental event being investigated.

Hans Spemann's constriction experiments in amphibian eggs provided another line of evidence that developmental outcomes depend on how nuclear material and cytoplasmic regions are distributed. By manipulating early embryos with a fine ligature, he explored whether separated regions could undergo organized development. Such studies linked early embryonic organization to the capacity of cells and nuclei to support development. They helped prepare the conceptual ground for later work on nuclear transplantation and embryonic induction. The detailed interpretation of nuclear transfer and organizer experiments belongs to subsequent nodes, but the general principle is already visible: developmental potential is revealed by asking what changes when the normal relationship among nucleus, cytoplasm and surrounding tissue is modified.

An important modern lesson from these historical experiments is that a developmental structure must be evaluated at multiple levels. An isolated blastomere might form a hollow sphere that superficially resembles a blastula, yet fail to undergo later morphogenesis. Another might generate cells with markers of several tissues without arranging them into a normal larva. Thus the criterion used to describe successful development affects the conclusion about potency. The more demanding the endpoint, the more specific the inference. Classical morphological evidence is valuable, but modern molecular and lineage tools help establish precisely which cell types have formed and whether their organization and function are appropriate.

5. Totipotency: from fertilization to organized developmental competence

The zygote is a distinctive cellular state formed by fertilization. It receives a genome from each gamete in a typical sexually reproducing diploid animal and a cytoplasmic environment that is largely supplied by the egg. The cytoplasm contains stored RNAs, proteins, organelles and regulatory machinery that support the first stages of development. A zygote does not begin with a fully assembled adult transcriptional program. Instead, it progressively reorganizes its regulatory systems, proceeds through early divisions and establishes conditions for the emergence of distinct cell identities. Its broad developmental capacity depends on this entire organization, not merely on the possession of a diploid chromosome set.

The parental genomes arrive with different histories of chromatin packaging and gene regulation. Their regulatory states are remodeled during early development. Maternal molecules support development during periods when the embryonic genome has not yet assumed full control of gene expression. The timing of the maternal-to-zygotic transition varies among organisms. In some species, early divisions occur under strong maternal control for an extended period, whereas in others embryonic transcription begins earlier. The general principle is that early developmental competence depends on coordination between inherited cytoplasmic factors and the activation of new transcriptional programs. The exact timing should not be generalized from a single animal model.

Cleavage partitions the egg into blastomeres. Division alone does not necessarily narrow potential: a daughter cell can retain extensive developmental capacity even though it contains less cytoplasm than the original egg. But cleavage changes the physical and regulatory conditions experienced by cells. It can alter contact with neighboring cells, orientation relative to the embryo surface, the allocation of maternal factors and the amount of cytoplasm inherited. These differences can eventually stabilize distinct developmental programs. Consequently, the relationship between cell number and potency is not a simple arithmetic rule. A four-cell embryo does not necessarily contain four equally totipotent cells merely because the zygote was totipotent.

In mammals, early embryonic development must produce both the embryo proper and supporting tissues. In the mouse blastocyst, an outer trophectoderm surrounds an inner cell mass and a fluid-filled cavity. The trophectoderm contributes to trophoblast lineages involved in the formation of the fetal component of the placenta. The inner cell mass later resolves into epiblast and primitive endoderm populations. The epiblast produces the embryo proper and contributes to certain extraembryonic structures; the primitive endoderm contributes to supporting endodermal lineages. This organization illustrates why the phrase all embryonic germ layers does not capture the full scope of mammalian totipotency. A cell that can form ectoderm, mesoderm and definitive endoderm may still lack the capacity to produce other necessary lineages.

The distinction between fetal and maternal contributions is essential. A mammalian conceptus depends on an appropriate uterine environment and maternal physiological support. Totipotency is not a claim that the zygote can build maternal organs or develop without external resources. A seed likewise depends on suitable conditions for germination, although the organization of plant reproductive development differs from that of mammals. A biological capacity can be genuine even when its expression requires a permissive environment. What matters is whether the starting cell can organize the appropriate developmental lineages under conditions that support normal development for the organism.

Experimental claims about totipotency require a careful distinction between lineage contribution and developmental autonomy. If a cell is incorporated into an embryo and contributes to both an embryonic and an extraembryonic lineage, this is evidence that it has a broad repertoire under that assay. However, the host embryo may provide organizing functions that the introduced cell could not supply on its own. An assay in which a single starting cell produces a complete, organized, viable organism provides more stringent evidence, especially when the descendants of that starting cell can be verified. The two assays answer related but distinct questions. A claim about one should not be extended automatically to the other.

Embryo splitting provides a conceptual illustration of this issue. In some mammals, separated early embryonic cells or cell groups can support the formation of separate individuals under suitable conditions. Natural monozygotic twinning also shows that an early conceptus can sometimes generate more than one individual. But twinning by itself does not identify the exact potency of each blastomere at every stage. The separation event may involve a group of cells, and the developmental outcome may depend on whether supporting tissues are shared or independently formed. To demonstrate the repertoire of one particular cell, the starting material and its descendants must be identified with greater precision.

Culture systems have made it possible to generate cells with features associated with very early embryos. Such states may express transcripts characteristic of cleavage stages or produce a wider range of lineage markers than conventional embryonic stem cells. These are valuable experimental models because they permit investigation of early regulatory programs. Yet a similarity in gene expression is not equivalent to functional totipotency. The cells may fail to organize development, may lack appropriate epigenetic states or may make structures that resemble early embryonic tissues without recapitulating their full function. Terms such as totipotent-like or expanded potential are therefore appropriate when they accurately describe the evidence and limitations of a specific study.

This distinction is particularly important in interpreting studies of human development. Ethical and legal restrictions appropriately limit the kinds of experiments that may be performed. One cannot simply transfer the most stringent mouse developmental assay to humans. Human cell states must instead be assessed using scientifically and ethically permitted evidence, such as molecular characterization and differentiation assays. The interpretation should state exactly what those experiments establish, rather than asserting complete organism-forming capacity that has not been tested. Examination questions may use idealized definitions, but understanding the limits of experimental proof is part of scientific mastery.

A final subtlety is the difference between totipotency of a whole cell and potential of its nucleus. An isolated nucleus cannot perform all the metabolic and organizational activities of a zygote. When a somatic nucleus supports development after transfer into an enucleated egg, it reveals retained genetic information and the power of the recipient cytoplasm to reorganize nuclear regulation. The success does not establish that the original differentiated cell would have formed an organism without that intervention. This difference explains why nuclear reprogramming is a separate concept from the normal potency of the donor cell. It also prepares the ground for studying genomic equivalence in the next relevant chapter.

6. Pluripotency and the developmental origin of diverse body tissues

After the earliest developmental decisions, cells belonging to the embryonic lineage can retain the capacity to generate a great diversity of body cell types. This broad but more limited repertoire is pluripotency. The word refers to developmental output and does not mean that a cell already performs the functions of all those mature types. A pluripotent cell is not simultaneously a neuron, cardiomyocyte and intestinal epithelial cell. It occupies a regulatory state from which different developmental pathways can be initiated under appropriate conditions.

The three embryonic germ layers provide a convenient framework for describing pluripotent output in animals. Ectoderm gives rise to the epidermis and major parts of the nervous system, among other derivatives. Mesoderm contributes to skeletal and cardiac muscle, blood, connective tissues and many internal structures. Definitive endoderm contributes to epithelial linings and associated organs such as liver and pancreas. These examples are not a complete inventory of every derivative. They show why producing several related cell types is insufficient evidence of pluripotency. A cell that produces several classes of neurons and glia may possess a broad neural repertoire, but those outputs alone do not demonstrate mesodermal or endodermal potential.

Germ layers should not be understood as three permanently isolated boxes that appear in every animal in precisely the same form. The germ-layer framework applies particularly well to triploblastic animal development, and the detailed allocation of tissues has evolutionary variation. The mammalian pluripotency classification is based on an experimentally meaningful range of embryonic derivatives. Plant regeneration does not proceed through animal ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm, so these criteria cannot simply be imposed on a plant cell. A strong answer to an examination question states the organism and the relevant criterion rather than assuming all potency categories are defined by identical tissue lists across life forms.

Embryonic stem cells made pluripotency experimentally tractable because they can be propagated in culture while retaining the ability to differentiate when conditions are changed. In 1981, Martin Evans and Matthew Kaufman reported the establishment of pluripotential cell lines from mouse embryos. Their work demonstrated that embryo-derived cells could be isolated and maintained outside the embryo in a state useful for studying developmental decisions. The significance was not merely that cells survived in a dish. It was that a broadly competent population could be expanded and used to investigate how environmental and genetic changes affect differentiation.

To understand the evidence required, consider a cell culture that proliferates for many passages and expresses a set of transcription factors associated with embryonic stem cells. These observations support the possibility that it is an undifferentiated stem-cell culture, but they do not establish its full differentiation capacity. An appropriate test challenges the cells with conditions that favor different germ-layer lineages. The resulting cells should express relevant combinations of markers, show characteristic properties and, where possible, exhibit lineage-specific function. Cells should also be assessed for genomic integrity, because culture-associated mutations can change growth and differentiation behavior. The functional outcome, not the attractive appearance of the culture, supports the potency classification.

Pluripotency can coexist with self-renewal, but the two properties remain distinct. A cell can possess broad developmental potential without being capable of indefinite expansion in culture. Conversely, a culture can proliferate indefinitely while retaining a narrow or abnormal differentiation repertoire. Conventional embryonic stem-cell systems are valuable because they combine both broad differentiation capacity and sustained propagation under defined conditions. Maintenance of this state requires an environment that supports survival and an appropriate regulatory network. Removing the maintenance signals or adding differentiation cues can shift the balance toward lineage-specific programs.

Different pluripotent states also exist. In mouse embryonic development, the epiblast before implantation and the epiblast after implantation have different molecular properties and responses to culture signals. The terms naive and primed pluripotency are commonly used to describe related states with different developmental correspondences. A naive mouse embryonic stem-cell state is more similar in important respects to preimplantation epiblast, whereas a primed state resembles aspects of later epiblast. Both can have broad embryonic differentiation potential, even though their signaling requirements and behavior in particular assays differ. The distinction explains why one culture condition or marker set cannot serve as a universal definition of pluripotency across species and stages.

In an examination, the appearance of a germ layer marker should prompt a question about what exactly has been demonstrated. A cell expressing a transcription factor associated with endoderm may be entering an endoderm-like regulatory program. That is not the same as demonstrating a mature, functional hepatocyte or pancreatic cell. A single marker may be expressed transiently or in more than one developmental context. A convincing differentiation assay combines appropriate marker panels, temporal progression and functional evidence. The more specific the biological claim, the more specific the evidence must be.

7. Multipotency, oligopotency and lineage-restricted development

Adult multicellular organisms must replace cells that are lost through normal turnover or injury. Different tissues solve this problem through different arrangements of stem cells, progenitors and differentiated cells. The hematopoietic system provides one of the clearest examples because mature blood cells can be identified by morphology, molecular markers and physiological function. Red blood cells transport respiratory gases, neutrophils participate in innate defense, lymphocytes carry out immune functions, and megakaryocytes produce platelets. Although these cell types differ considerably, they can arise from hematopoietic stem cells in the appropriate tissue environment. Their common ancestry illustrates multipotency within a broad but defined physiological system.

A useful way to understand hematopoiesis is to separate the problem of maintaining a stem-cell pool from the problem of producing differentiated cells. A hematopoietic stem cell can generate descendants that retain stem-cell properties while also producing cells whose developmental repertoire is restricted. These progenitors expand and differentiate into mature blood-cell populations. The stem cell need not directly produce a mature erythrocyte in one division. Its potential is expressed through a series of intermediate states. The existence of intermediates does not diminish the potency of the starting cell; it describes the biological pathway through which its potential is realized.

Classical teaching diagrams often divide hematopoiesis into myeloid and lymphoid branches followed by increasingly restricted progenitors. These diagrams are useful for learning mature lineage relationships, but modern experimental evidence indicates that the actual organization is more heterogeneous than a single rigid binary tree. Some stem and progenitor cells have lineage biases, and developmental trajectories may overlap or diverge gradually. A cell can have a higher probability of producing one type without losing all capacity for another type. Consequently, a lineage hierarchy represents an abstraction from observations across many cells and conditions. It should not be interpreted as a requirement that every individual cell traverses the same sequence of precisely defined intermediates.

The work of James Till and Ernest McCulloch in the early 1960s laid a foundation for functional investigation of blood-forming precursors. After transplantation of mouse bone marrow cells into irradiated recipients, colonies could be observed in the spleen. The investigators related colony numbers to the number and treatment of transplanted cells. Subsequent research on the cellular origin and composition of these colonies helped develop the concept of clonogenic hematopoietic progenitors and stem cells. The critical point for potency is that colony formation alone does not prove long-term self-renewal or the full blood-cell repertoire. These properties require additional evidence, such as multilineage production and sustained repopulation after transplantation.

A long-term repopulation assay asks whether the donor population can regenerate blood production over an extended period in a suitable recipient. Donor-specific markers distinguish newly produced donor-derived cells from surviving host cells. When donor contribution is found in distinct blood lineages, the experiment supports multilineage repopulation. If donor-derived cells can be transplanted into a secondary recipient and again sustain blood production, the evidence for self-renewal becomes stronger. The assay requires controls for conditioning of recipients, donor-cell viability, homing efficiency and the possibility that several donor cells rather than one cell produced the observed lineages. Failure to repopulate may reflect defective homing rather than loss of developmental capacity.

A hematopoietic stem-cell sample may contain cells that differ in their output patterns. For example, one clone may contribute extensively to myeloid populations and less to lymphocytes, while another clone shows a different balance. This variation can arise from differences in regulatory state, cell age, environmental conditions or measurement. Multipotency does not require identical output frequencies for each permissible lineage. Instead, the classification depends on the range of outcomes demonstrable for the relevant cell and assay. The distinction between breadth and bias is crucial: a strongly biased cell may still be multipotent if alternative lineages can be generated under appropriate conditions.

Neural development offers another example of tissue-specific potential. Neural stem and progenitor cells can produce neurons and glial cell types in particular regions and developmental stages. A precursor that can generate neurons, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes has a broader neural repertoire than a progenitor restricted to one neuronal class. Yet it is not thereby pluripotent. All three outputs belong to the neural developmental system. Some neural stem-cell populations in adult organisms have more limited output than embryonic neural precursors, and their behavior depends strongly on their tissue location. Age and anatomical region therefore matter when assigning a potency classification.

Other tissue systems illustrate that multipotency need not take exactly the same form everywhere. Intestinal stem cells replenish several cell types of the intestinal epithelium, including absorptive and secretory derivatives. Epidermal and hair-follicle stem-cell populations maintain distinct regions of the skin under ordinary conditions, and injury can change the extent of their contributions. Skeletal muscle satellite cells ordinarily support myogenic repair. These examples show that tissue-specific potential is organized around the requirements of the tissue. The word multipotent should describe the experimentally demonstrated output of a particular population, not automatically every stem cell associated with a named organ.

Oligopotent and bipotent progenitors are valuable intermediates for studying how lineage restriction is established. Imagine a clonal progenitor that can produce two distinguishable mature populations, but not a third population that is readily produced by an earlier precursor. If appropriate conditions support all three outputs in a positive control and the restricted progenitor remains viable, the evidence supports a narrowed developmental repertoire. To establish bipotency, it is not enough for one group of cells to produce population A and a different group to produce population B. The assay must connect both outputs to a single starting clone or otherwise justify an individual-cell claim. This is a recurring principle in every potency classification.

Lineage restriction has physiological value. An intestinal progenitor that accidentally activates an unrelated developmental program would disrupt the organization of the epithelium. Specialized tissue maintenance requires mechanisms that favor the correct local cell types and prevent inappropriate differentiation. Restriction also enables the production of large numbers of particular descendants when demand increases. During infection, hematopoietic activity may change to favor production of needed immune populations, but such shifts generally occur within the hematopoietic developmental system. The balance between plasticity and restriction allows tissues to respond to demand while preserving their structural and functional identity.

8. Unipotency, self-renewal and terminal differentiation

Unipotency is sometimes taught as the final category of a potency hierarchy, but its meaning is most useful when considered in relation to tissue maintenance. A unipotent cell can generate descendants of one defined differentiated lineage in the tested conditions. If it also maintains its own population through repeated production of equivalent precursor cells, it can be described as a unipotent stem cell. If its capacity for division is transient, it is more appropriately described as a lineage-restricted progenitor. Thus, unipotency is a statement about breadth, not about how long a cell can continue producing descendants.

Skeletal muscle repair provides an instructive example. Mature skeletal muscle fibers are large multinucleated cells specialized for contraction. Repair of damaged fibers depends substantially on resident satellite cells positioned in association with muscle fibers. When activated by injury and local regulatory changes, these cells can proliferate, generate myogenic descendants and contribute to repair. Some descendants return to a stem-cell-like state, helping preserve the precursor pool. The ordinary physiological role of these cells is strongly myogenic. This is why satellite cells are commonly used to illustrate lineage-restricted stem-cell behavior, although experimental and biological context must be considered when evaluating claims about alternative potential.

A mature muscle fiber and a muscle stem cell both belong to the myogenic system, but their capabilities differ. The mature fiber contains the machinery needed for contraction and is not simply a proliferating stem cell with narrow output. The satellite cell can generate differentiated myogenic descendants and replenish the precursor population. If a question asks which of these cells is unipotent, the intended distinction usually concerns a precursor capable of generating one lineage, not the mere fact that a mature cell is specialized. A terminally differentiated cell may have little or no ordinary capacity to generate new cell types through division, so it is not automatically equivalent to a unipotent stem cell.

Similar distinctions apply to germline stem cells. A spermatogonial stem-cell population contributes to the male germ-cell lineage while maintaining precursor cells. Its descendants pass through several developmental stages, including proliferative and meiotic stages, before producing sperm. Although the mature descendants differ morphologically and physiologically from the stem cells, the ordinary lineage output remains within the male germline. Developmental transitions along one lineage should not be counted as independent lineages simply to inflate the potency category. The classification depends on whether the starting cell can generate alternative differentiated identities, not the number of recognizable intermediate stages on its normal pathway.

Self-renewal does not require that every division produce exactly one stem cell and one differentiated daughter. A population can remain stable through a balance of symmetric self-renewing divisions, divisions producing two differentiating daughters and asymmetric divisions. If some stem cells produce two stem-like daughters while others leave the pool, the total population can remain approximately constant. The outcome depends on regulatory feedback and tissue requirements. This population-level perspective helps avoid confusing one particular division pattern with a universal defining property of stem cells.

A precursor pool may transiently expand during injury. If the pool grows because more cells remain in a stem or progenitor state, the number of cells available for future differentiation increases. Once sufficient repair has occurred, regulatory signals may restore the previous balance of self-renewal and differentiation. Such changes affect the population size and the rate of cell production without necessarily changing potency. A unipotent precursor can increase its proliferative activity dramatically while remaining restricted to its usual lineage. The measurement of proliferation and the measurement of lineage output must therefore be interpreted separately.

The term terminal differentiation generally describes a specialized state that is relatively stable and often associated with withdrawal from the cell cycle. However, the relationship between specialization and proliferation differs among cell types. Some mature cells can re-enter the cell cycle or divide while retaining their identity, as occurs in certain regenerative responses. Other specialized cells, such as mature mammalian erythrocytes, lack a nucleus and cannot divide. Still others retain nuclei but normally show very limited proliferative activity. These cases demonstrate why differentiation cannot be defined solely by loss of proliferation. A functional identity, a stable regulatory program and a particular cell-cycle behavior are related but distinguishable properties.

9. Molecular foundations of potency restriction

A developing cell carries a genome containing many genes that are not being expressed at a given moment. The genes that are actively transcribed, the regulatory proteins available to bind DNA and the accessibility of regulatory sequences help determine the cellular state. During differentiation, particular groups of genes become activated, while other programs become less accessible or less responsive. This change makes specialized cellular activities possible and can also reduce the range of developmental alternatives. The genome is therefore analogous to a collection of possible instructions whose use is controlled by regulatory mechanisms, rather than a list of instructions that are all executed simultaneously.

Transcription factors contribute to potency by activating and repressing particular sets of genes. A factor may bind regulatory DNA near genes involved in a lineage program, alter their transcription and cooperate with other regulatory proteins. The resulting gene products may reinforce the state by activating additional factors or inhibiting competing programs. Such interactions can generate a regulatory network that persists after the original developmental cue has disappeared. Stabilization of one network can make an alternative identity more difficult to initiate because key components of the alternative program are absent or actively repressed. This is a molecular explanation for why commitment can precede visible differentiation.

Chromatin provides another regulatory dimension. DNA is associated with histones and other proteins, and its organization influences access to regulatory sequences. Some regulatory regions are relatively accessible to transcription factors, while others are less accessible. Histone modifications, DNA methylation and chromatin-remodeling complexes can help establish and maintain these patterns. During differentiation, enhancers associated with one developmental program may become active, while enhancers associated with other programs become less active. The change is not a simple global switch from open to closed chromatin. Specialized cells require accessible chromatin at the genes and regulatory regions that support their own functions.

Pluripotent mammalian cells are often associated with a regulatory network involving OCT4, SOX2 and NANOG. These transcription factors help support the undifferentiated pluripotent state in appropriate cellular contexts. Their effects depend on their levels, binding partners and other regulatory conditions. A factor associated with pluripotency can also participate in other developmental programs; SOX2, for example, is expressed in some neural progenitors. The presence of any one factor cannot by itself establish pluripotency. The network must be considered in relation to the broader molecular state and functional differentiation evidence. An examination statement claiming that expression of OCT4 alone proves totipotency or pluripotency should therefore be rejected.

Changes in signaling pathways can shift the balance between maintenance of a broadly competent state and entry into differentiation. In conventional mouse embryonic stem-cell culture, leukemia inhibitory factor can support self-renewal through signaling involving STAT3 in a suitable culture context. Other approaches can stabilize naive mouse pluripotency by modulating pathways that otherwise favor differentiation. Primed pluripotent states and conventional human embryonic stem-cell cultures have different requirements, commonly involving FGF and TGF-beta-related signaling. These differences are not contradictions. They reflect the fact that different pluripotent states respond to distinct environments, and that experimental culture conditions preserve particular states rather than reproducing every feature of the intact embryo.

One of the most informative features of developmental networks is feedback. If transcription factor A promotes its own expression directly or indirectly, a temporary signal that raises A can lead to a persistent state. If A also suppresses a competing factor B, and B suppresses A, the system may favor one of two alternative states. This simplified model helps explain how a graded or transient signal can lead to relatively stable lineage choices. Real networks contain many more interactions, and not every developmental decision is bistable. Nevertheless, feedback provides a useful conceptual bridge between an external developmental cue and a cell-intrinsic change in future responsiveness.

The molecular organization of a cell is also influenced by metabolism, protein turnover and cell-cycle status. Cells require energy and biosynthetic substrates to survive, divide and differentiate. Regulatory enzymes use metabolic intermediates as substrates or cofactors, linking the metabolic state to chromatin and gene regulation. However, metabolic changes associated with a cell state may be consequences, contributors or both. A study showing that pluripotent and differentiated cells have different metabolic profiles does not automatically establish that altering one metabolic pathway is sufficient to change potency. Causal evidence requires a perturbation, appropriate controls and a functional developmental outcome.

The concept of genomic equivalence helps separate genetic information from the way it is used. Most differentiated nucleated cells retain broadly similar genomic information to the zygote from which they arose. Nevertheless, exceptions exist, including mutations accumulated during life, programmed DNA rearrangements in immune cells and specialized cells that lose their nuclei. These exceptions do not invalidate the general principle that differentiation usually does not require systematic deletion of every gene associated with alternative tissues. Instead, regulation of gene activity is a major mechanism of cellular specialization. The nuclear-transfer evidence for this principle will receive detailed treatment in node 5.A.03.

10. Cell position, polarity and the first restrictions of embryonic potential

An early embryo offers an opportunity to observe how cells that share a recent ancestry begin to occupy different states. In the mouse, cleavage and subsequent compaction establish distinct spatial relationships. Cells at the embryo surface have a free outer surface, whereas cells located internally are surrounded by other cells. Surface and internal cells experience different patterns of contact, polarity and mechanical organization. These differences can influence the activity of signaling pathways and transcriptional networks. The emergence of distinct populations is thus not adequately explained by cell number alone. The spatial arrangement of cells contributes to the regulatory decisions made during development.

The Hippo signaling pathway provides a useful mechanistic example in the early mouse embryo. In broad outline, signaling activity differs between inner and outer cells and influences the localization and activity of the transcriptional coactivator YAP. In outer cells, lower activity of the relevant Hippo kinase pathway is associated with nuclear YAP and transcriptional activity involving TEAD factors, supporting trophectoderm-associated gene expression including CDX2. In inner cells, active Hippo signaling promotes phosphorylation and cytoplasmic retention of YAP, favoring a different regulatory program. This description is a simplified model, not a complete account of every molecular interaction. Its educational value is that cell geometry and polarity can be connected to differential gene expression through an identifiable signaling mechanism.

To reason about this mechanism, consider a hypothetical perturbation that promotes nuclear YAP in cells that would otherwise occupy an inner position. If nuclear YAP is sufficient in the tested context to favor part of the outer-cell program, one would predict an increase in expression of some trophectoderm-associated genes in the affected inner cells. Yet a change in marker expression alone would not prove that those cells became functional trophoblast or that their complete developmental repertoire changed. One should examine the cell's position, survival, additional lineage markers and eventual contribution to tissues. A suitable control would preserve the normal relationship between position and pathway activity, allowing the effect of the intervention to be distinguished from manipulation-induced abnormalities.

Conversely, reducing a factor associated with outer-cell identity may prevent the correct differentiation of outer cells. It does not follow that every affected outer cell has regained the full potential of a zygote. Loss of a lineage program can leave a cell abnormal, arrested or capable of only some alternative outcomes. A developmental defect is not automatically an increase in potency. This point applies widely when interpreting loss-of-function mutations. An experiment showing that a factor is necessary for normal differentiation answers a different question from an experiment demonstrating that loss of the factor broadens the cell's developmental repertoire.

The subsequent division of the inner cell mass into epiblast and primitive endoderm illustrates another way in which cell populations acquire distinct identities. In the mouse, FGF signaling contributes to the establishment and resolution of these lineages. Cells with initially overlapping patterns of gene expression can become more distinct as signaling interactions and regulatory feedback stabilize different programs. The epiblast and primitive endoderm do not merely differ in location; they carry out different developmental roles. The relationship between gene expression, cell position and future contribution must be followed over time to understand how the distinction emerges. Detailed mechanisms of lineage specification belong to later nodes, but this example shows how restriction can arise through interacting cell-intrinsic and extracellular processes.

The question of when a cell loses a particular developmental alternative cannot always be answered with a single universal stage. Cells can display biases before they become stably restricted. A cell expressing a higher level of one regulator may have an increased likelihood of entering a particular lineage but still respond to a changed environment. The effect of a bias depends on the magnitude and duration of the signal, the cellular state and the organization of the embryo. Researchers can test these properties by following cells over time, changing their positions or altering the signals they receive at defined stages. A developmental timeline should therefore distinguish the first appearance of a bias, the stabilization of a fate and the acquisition of mature function.

This logic also explains why a potency assay performed on a dissociated embryo can yield results that differ from normal development. Dissociation removes cell contacts and changes access to signals. Cells may reorganize their polarity, experience stress or establish new interactions after being placed in culture. Their behavior in this setting provides information about what they can do under the new conditions. It does not necessarily reproduce the behavior they would have shown in the intact embryo. To connect the assay to normal development, the experimental perturbation and the state of the starting cells must be considered together.

11. Experimental standards for proving cellular potency

The most reliable way to classify potency is to connect a clearly identified starting cell or population to carefully characterized developmental outcomes. The ideal experiment is not identical for every potency class. A test for hematopoietic multipotency uses different lineages, environments and endpoints from a test for embryonic pluripotency. Nevertheless, the underlying principles are shared. Investigators must establish what was introduced into the experiment, what conditions were provided, which descendants appeared and how confidently those descendants can be attributed to the starting material. The strength of the claim depends on the weakest link in this chain of evidence.

A clonal differentiation assay begins with a single viable cell or a population for which clonal origin can be reliably established. The starting cell is allowed to divide, and its descendants are examined under conditions that reveal potential lineage outcomes. If descendants of one clone are shown to produce multiple distinct lineages, the evidence supports individual-cell multipotency or another broad category, depending on the identities of those lineages. Clonal analysis is especially important when the original population is heterogeneous. The observation that a mixture can generate many cell types does not show that every individual member has that capacity. A well-designed assay therefore documents the starting cell number and rules out accidental addition of another cell.

Several practical issues affect the interpretation of clonal assays. A cell-sorting system may deposit two cells into one well. Cells can migrate between wells if physical separation fails, and a contaminating cell can expand more rapidly than the intended founder. Fluorescent markers may be transferred between cells or may not remain stable during differentiation. These possibilities can be addressed by imaging the initial wells, using heritable labels, verifying genotype and including appropriate negative controls. The purpose of such precautions is not merely technical perfection. Without reliable ancestry information, the central inference about the repertoire of a single cell is unsupported.

Lineage markers are indispensable because many immature cell types cannot be reliably distinguished by shape alone. A marker may be a transcription factor, a surface protein, an enzyme or a reporter driven by a lineage-associated regulatory sequence. However, no marker should be interpreted independently of its biological context. A protein expressed in one cell type may also be present in an earlier precursor or a different lineage. The timing, subcellular localization and combination of markers matter. Functional validation strengthens the result: electrically excitable neurons, contractile cardiomyocytes and metabolically active hepatocyte-like cells provide different kinds of evidence beyond a single transcriptional marker.

Directed differentiation of pluripotent stem cells commonly uses signaling conditions that favor particular germ-layer programs. An experiment may expose separate aliquots of an initially validated culture to conditions promoting ectodermal, mesodermal and endodermal differentiation. The resulting cultures can be analyzed for characteristic markers and functional properties. A positive outcome across the three germ layers supports broad differentiation capacity at the level tested. If the claim concerns a particular clone, the investigators must also establish that the aliquots descend from that clone. The ability to generate all three germ layers in culture does not establish the ability to produce an integrated organism or all fetal supporting tissues.

Historically, the teratoma assay has been used to assess broad differentiation of pluripotent cells in an in vivo setting. Introduced pluripotent cells may generate a mass containing tissue derivatives of different germ layers. Histological analysis is used to identify these derivatives. The assay can support pluripotency but does not require normal spatial arrangement of an embryo or complete organism formation. Nor does it show that every cell in the introduced population was equally potent. Ethical standards, animal welfare considerations and alternative differentiation assays matter when selecting appropriate experimental methods. For examination purposes, the major point is the difference between formation of diverse tissues and formation of a coherent developing individual.

Chimera assays offer another functional approach in appropriate animal models. A chimera contains cells derived from genetically distinct starting sources. When introduced cells contribute to multiple tissues of a developing host embryo, their ability to participate in normal development is demonstrated more directly than in a culture differentiation assay. Germline contribution is especially informative about developmental integration, because descendants of the introduced cells can contribute to the reproductive lineage. However, the host embryo still provides a developmental context and may supply tissues that the introduced population cannot produce. Chimera formation is therefore not equivalent to independent generation of a complete conceptus from a single introduced cell.

Tetraploid complementation provides a more stringent assay for developmental capacity of mouse pluripotent cells. In the usual conceptual description, tetraploid host cells preferentially contribute to extraembryonic supporting tissues, while introduced diploid pluripotent cells can generate much of the embryo proper. Development of a viable animal in this setting is powerful evidence of the ability of the diploid cells to generate embryonic tissues and participate in coordinated development. Yet the tetraploid host contributes essential support. The assay does not establish that the diploid cells alone could generate all supporting lineages and independently organize a complete conceptus. Its strength lies in the specific claim it tests rather than in an unrestricted claim of totipotency.

Transplantation assays also require attention to the distinction between potential and engraftment. A donor cell may be capable of producing a particular lineage but fail to survive or reach an appropriate location in the recipient. In hematopoietic transplantation, for instance, donor cells must survive the procedure, home to suitable sites and respond to the recipient environment. The absence of donor-derived mature cells may result from failure at any one of these steps. Investigators can measure early survival and localization separately from later differentiation. By identifying where the process failed, they avoid assigning a developmental restriction to a problem of delivery or survival.

A rigorous interpretation uses converging evidence rather than a single dramatic result. Molecular profiles characterize starting and ending states, lineage tracing establishes ancestry, differentiation assays test output, and transplantation or other functional tests establish integration in a relevant environment. Each method has limitations that can be addressed by another method. A cell that satisfies several independent criteria is more confidently classified than a cell that merely expresses one attractive marker. This framework is applicable to both classical embryology and modern stem-cell biology and will be useful when evaluating the experimental passages encountered in Part C questions.

12. Reprogramming and developmental plasticity without confusing initial and acquired potency

Differentiation often stabilizes cell identity, but it does not generally eliminate the genomic information needed for other identities. The possibility of changing a stable identity was investigated long before the discovery of induced pluripotent stem cells. Nuclear transplantation experiments, including John Gurdon's studies using nuclei from differentiated tadpole intestinal epithelial cells, provided influential evidence that differentiated nuclei can retain the information necessary to support substantial development in an appropriate egg cytoplasm. The crucial distinction is between the donor nucleus in its original cellular environment and the same nucleus after exposure to a powerful reprogramming environment. The experimental outcome concerns the capacity of the transferred nucleus under new conditions.

Nuclear transfer can be understood conceptually as replacing one regulatory environment with another. The recipient egg supplies cytoplasmic machinery capable of remodeling chromatin and initiating developmental processes. A successful outcome indicates that the donor genome was not irreversibly stripped of all instructions required for the resulting tissues. Low success rates and abnormal development in such experiments also provide important information. Retaining genetic information is not identical to being readily reprogrammable. Stable regulatory states can be difficult to erase, and the coordination of nuclear and cytoplasmic events can fail. The details of nuclear-transfer evidence will be revisited in node 5.A.03; here the lesson is that acquired potential must not be attributed to the original unmodified cell.

In 2006, Kazutoshi Takahashi and Shinya Yamanaka reported that introduction of four defined transcription factors, OCT3/4, SOX2, KLF4 and c-MYC, could induce pluripotent stem-cell-like states from mouse fibroblast cultures. This work demonstrated that changing a limited set of regulatory inputs could reorganize somatic cell identity in a suitable culture setting. The resulting induced pluripotent stem cells were not simply fibroblasts that had been labeled differently. They exhibited a changed regulatory state and could be examined for broad differentiation capacity. Subsequent work improved the functional characterization and developmental competence of induced pluripotent cells and extended the approach to human fibroblasts.

The original 2006 mouse study used selection and characterization strategies appropriate to its time, and its early induced cell lines were not identical in every functional respect to fully characterized embryonic stem cells. Later studies established more stringent pluripotency properties. This historical sequence illustrates the difference between introducing a transformative experimental approach and proving every possible functional property of the resulting cells. It is scientifically preferable to state the evidence from each experiment rather than retrospectively attributing all later achievements to the initial study. The broad lesson is that a stable differentiated state can be reorganized without replacing the genome with a newly invented set of developmental genes.

Reprogramming can be inefficient because the introduced factors must operate within an existing regulatory network. Some cells may not express the factors at suitable levels. Others may initiate partial changes without reaching a stable pluripotent state. Some cells may die or continue proliferating without acquiring the desired differentiation repertoire. Consequently, a population exposed to reprogramming factors can contain several different cellular outcomes. Selection of cells with a particular marker enriches the desired state but does not substitute for functional validation. The efficiency of colony formation and the frequency of genuinely pluripotent clones are related but different measurements.

Direct lineage conversion, sometimes called transdifferentiation when a differentiated identity is converted into another, provides a conceptual contrast with induced pluripotency. If a starting somatic cell is redirected into another differentiated lineage without traversing a validated pluripotent state, the experiment demonstrates a change of identity rather than reacquisition of broad pluripotency. To support such a conclusion, investigators need evidence about the identity of the original cell, intermediate states and the properties of the final cell. Observing an endpoint with a new marker does not by itself establish the route taken. Time-resolved evidence helps distinguish direct conversion from conversion through a transient progenitor or pluripotent-like state.

Dedifferentiation refers to a transition toward a less specialized state. It need not restore the entire developmental capacity of the earliest embryo. A mature cell may become more proliferative, reduce expression of specialized genes and acquire features of a tissue progenitor while remaining restricted to its original tissue system. Similarly, a regenerating tissue can recruit resident stem cells without requiring any mature cells to dedifferentiate. The terms dedifferentiation, transdifferentiation and reprogramming describe different types or aspects of state change. Their use should follow the evidence rather than serving as interchangeable labels for any regenerative process.

The relationship between potency and plasticity can therefore be understood by comparing three situations. During ordinary development, a cell realizes one of the outcomes accessible to its current state. During a change in environment, a cell may realize a previously unobserved alternative without necessarily undergoing a fundamental reset. During strong reprogramming, a cell can acquire a new state with a different repertoire. The boundaries between these situations can require careful empirical investigation, but distinguishing them conceptually prevents the common error of treating any possible experimentally induced outcome as proof that the starting cell was already broadly potent.

13. Potency in plants, regeneration and comparative development

Flowering plants provide a complementary perspective on developmental potential because their body architecture continues to be elaborated after embryogenesis. A shoot apical meristem produces cells that contribute to stems, leaves and, after a developmental transition, reproductive structures. Root apical meristems supply cells for continued root growth. Other populations contribute to secondary growth or participate in repair and formation of new organs. A plant does not need to establish every adult organ during embryogenesis. Instead, it retains organized populations with developmental capacity and uses them repeatedly as conditions change.

Meristematic cells are not equivalent to a collection of unconstrained zygotes. Their behavior is regulated by their position within the meristem, signals from neighboring cells and intrinsic gene-regulatory networks. In a shoot meristem, the maintenance of a stem-cell domain must be coordinated with the production of cells that leave the domain and enter developing organs. If all cells remained indefinitely in a proliferative undifferentiated state, the meristem would fail to produce the organs needed by the plant. If every cell differentiated immediately, the source of future growth would be depleted. Developmental potential must be maintained in some cells and restricted in others, just as in animal stem-cell systems, even though the structures and regulatory details differ.

The remarkable regenerative ability of many plants has motivated research on tissue culture. A piece of plant tissue can sometimes produce a mass of proliferating cells, roots, shoots or somatic embryos when exposed to suitable conditions. These outcomes demonstrate that cells outside the ordinary zygotic developmental pathway can participate in new developmental programs. However, the conclusion depends on which cells in the starting tissue generate the new structures. An explant usually contains many cell types, including cells with distinct developmental histories. The formation of a whole plant from an explant is not by itself proof that every mature cell in the explant possessed identical whole-plant-forming capacity.

Callus is a proliferating tissue mass that can arise in response to injury or particular culture conditions. Its appearance is not sufficient evidence of totipotency. Some callus tissues can generate shoots or roots under appropriate conditions, whereas others remain proliferative without regenerating an organized plant. Research has shown that callus formation in certain experimental systems can involve developmental programs related to root formation and can originate from particular competent cell populations. The older description of every callus as an entirely undifferentiated homogeneous mass is therefore misleading. Understanding the developmental origin of the callus helps explain why one explant may regenerate efficiently while another does not.

The relative activities of auxin and cytokinin are classical examples of environmental control over plant tissue development in culture. Depending on species, tissue and culture conditions, auxin and cytokinin treatments can favor proliferative growth, root formation or shoot formation. A familiar rule of thumb associates relatively high auxin activity with root formation and relatively high cytokinin activity with shoot formation in certain systems. This is a useful starting model, not an invariant numerical law. Endogenous hormone production, receptor activity, tissue age, genotype and culture history influence the response. Identical external hormone concentrations need not produce identical outcomes in different explants.

A somatic embryo develops from non-zygotic starting material and can establish an embryonic organization with shoot and root developmental poles. In favorable circumstances, it can give rise to a complete plant. Such an outcome differs from regeneration through separate organ formation, in which a shoot or root develops first and additional organs are subsequently produced. Both pathways can yield a regenerated plant, but their developmental routes and implications differ. If the claim is that a single somatic cell acquired whole-plant-forming capacity, clonal origin must be demonstrated and the developmental outcome must be followed. A group of cooperating cells forming a plant is not automatically evidence that each member was independently totipotent.

A rigorous study of plant regeneration begins by identifying the starting cells. The experiment might label a particular cell population before injury or culture and follow the label as new tissues arise. If the new shoot develops only from labeled descendants of a known precursor population, that provides evidence about the cellular source of regeneration. If unlabeled cells also contribute, the interpretation depends on labeling efficiency and the possibility of additional cellular sources. Investigators should compare treated and untreated tissue, verify that the marker remains associated with the lineage and establish whether regenerated organs are physiologically functional. These principles parallel the ancestry and functional-evidence requirements used in animal potency studies.

Regenerative capacity also varies widely among animals. Some organisms can reconstruct large parts of the body after injury, while others possess more limited tissue-specific repair. Planarians are a particularly instructive example because populations of proliferating neoblasts contribute to regeneration, and experiments have identified individual cells with broad regenerative capacity. The fact that an adult animal can regenerate does not establish that all of its differentiated cells are equally potent. The organism may rely on particular resident precursor populations, changes in positional information or coordinated interactions among surviving tissues. An experiment must identify which cells generate the new tissue and which cells instead provide regulatory support.

In vertebrates, tissue regeneration often involves a mixture of proliferation, cell-state changes and interactions among several cell populations. For example, the production of new cells during repair may involve resident stem cells, division of surviving differentiated cells or recruitment of progenitor populations. The relative contribution of these mechanisms depends on the tissue and injury. The same term regeneration can therefore describe very different developmental processes. A limb that regrows, a liver that restores functional mass and a blood system that recovers after injury have different structural requirements and sources of replacement cells. Potency classification should be based on the demonstrated repertoire of individual populations, not simply on the impressive appearance of the recovered organ.

Evolutionary comparisons reveal the importance of developmental organization. Broad developmental potential may be preserved in adult cells of some organisms and more strongly restricted in others. The distribution of potential reflects differences in life history, growth pattern and tissue organization. However, no simple evolutionary ladder ranks all organisms according to how easily they regenerate. A species may have extensive regenerative ability in one tissue and limited ability in another. Furthermore, the capacity to generate a cell type is only one component of successful regeneration. The correct arrangement of tissues, establishment of positional information, physiological integration and regulation of growth are equally necessary.

14. Quantitative interpretation: measuring breadth, frequency and confidence

Potency is a qualitative classification supported by quantitative observations. Consider a hypothetical experiment in which one hundred independently derived clonal cultures are challenged under validated conditions for two alternative lineages. Forty clones generate both lineages, thirty generate only the first, twenty generate only the second, and ten fail to generate either. At the level of the initial culture, both lineages are clearly available. At the clonal level, however, the outcomes differ. The observed fraction of clones with demonstrated dual output is forty out of one hundred, or forty percent. The remaining sixty percent should not all be classified as definitively unipotent, because ten produced neither output and some of the single-output clones might have failed to express an alternative capacity in the tested conditions.

The distinction between an observed fraction and a true biological fraction is important whenever assays are imperfect. Suppose a known bipotent control clone produces both outcomes only eighty percent of the time because one of the differentiation procedures is technically unreliable. An experimental population with a true bipotent fraction of fifty percent could then yield a lower observed dual-output fraction even if the starting cells were correctly classified. Under a deliberately simplified model in which all bipotent cells have the same detection probability and there are no false-positive dual outcomes, the observed fraction equals the true fraction multiplied by detection probability. Dividing the observed forty percent by a detection probability of eighty percent gives an estimated fifty percent. This correction is valid only under those assumptions; real experiments may require more complex analysis.

Cell survival introduces another denominator problem. Imagine that eighty cells were initially isolated, but only sixty survived the first day. Of the sixty survivors, thirty generated a lineage of interest. One may report a differentiation fraction of thirty out of sixty among surviving cells, which is fifty percent. Alternatively, the overall yield from the initial eighty cells is thirty out of eighty, or thirty-seven and a half percent. Both numbers are correct but answer different questions. The first describes observed differentiation among viable cells; the second describes the total efficiency of the procedure. Neither is automatically a direct measure of intrinsic potency because the lineage output depends on the conditions provided.

A developmental perturbation can change the apparent fraction of cells entering a lineage by changing survival rather than identity. In a hypothetical control embryo, forty of one hundred descendants have an early neural marker. In a treated embryo, forty of eighty surviving descendants have the same marker. The neural fraction rises from forty to fifty percent, but the absolute number of marker-positive descendants has not increased. The result could arise if twenty non-neural cells were lost rather than if additional cells were converted toward neural identity. Measurements of total cell number, lineage-specific proliferation and cell death are needed to distinguish these possibilities. Percentages alone can conceal the underlying cellular process.

An absence of observed alternative outcomes is another quantitative problem. If ten viable clones are tested for a particular alternative lineage and none produces it, the result provides some evidence of restriction. But it does not prove that the probability of the outcome is exactly zero. In an idealized model with independent trials and perfect detection, the probability of observing no successful outcomes is the probability of failure in one trial raised to the number of trials. If the underlying success probability were ten percent, the chance of observing no successes in ten trials would be approximately thirty-five percent. Thus, a zero count in a small experiment is compatible with a low but nonzero probability of the outcome.

Increasing the number of independent biological replicates improves the ability to detect rare outcomes, provided the assay is appropriate. With one hundred independent trials and a true success probability of ten percent, the probability of seeing no successes becomes very small. But the mathematical calculation assumes independence and constant success probability. One hundred cells isolated from the same unhealthy embryo may not provide the same information as cells obtained from many independent embryos. Shared conditions can make the observations correlated. Experimental design must therefore identify the unit of replication. Technical replicates measure aspects of assay variability, whereas independent biological replicates support inference about variability among biological samples.

Time-course analysis provides a way to distinguish developmental bias from stable restriction. Suppose cells isolated at three embryonic stages are placed into an environment favoring an alternative lineage. At the earliest stage, most cells respond; at the middle stage, fewer respond; at the latest stage, none respond under the same assay. If the cells remain viable and positive controls confirm the effectiveness of the environment, the pattern supports progressive loss of responsiveness to that alternative. The graph does not establish the precise molecular mechanism of restriction. To identify the mechanism, one might compare expression of relevant receptors, downstream signaling activity and lineage-specific regulatory factors across the three stages, then perturb candidate mechanisms to test causality.

A particularly useful quantitative distinction is between the probability of an outcome and the number of outcomes available. Consider two multipotent clones that can each generate three cell types. The first clone produces the three types in nearly equal proportions, while the second produces one type much more frequently than the others. The clones have the same demonstrated repertoire, but different lineage biases. It would be incorrect to classify the second clone as unipotent if the alternative outputs have been convincingly demonstrated. Measures of diversity or distribution can summarize lineage output, but they do not replace the biological definition of the potency category. 15. Integrating potency with developmental reasoning and applications

A useful way to organize developmental reasoning is to ask three separate questions about any cell population: what is its present identity, what is its normal contribution, and what additional outcomes can it produce under defined conditions? The answers can be obtained through different experiments and may not coincide. A molecular profile describes aspects of present identity. A fate map describes normal contribution. A transplantation or differentiation experiment can reveal additional outcomes. If the experiment changes the regulatory state profoundly, a fourth question becomes necessary: what new developmental capacity was acquired during the intervention? Keeping these questions separate allows the same vocabulary to be used consistently from classical embryology to stem-cell biotechnology.

Consider an embryo in which a mutation prevents a particular cell population from forming its usual tissue. The first task is to determine whether the cells were generated, whether they survived and whether they initiated the expected lineage program. If the cells are absent early, the mutation may disrupt proliferation or survival. If cells are present but lack the expected molecular program, the mutation may affect specification. If the cells initiate the program but fail to acquire mature function, differentiation may be impaired. A claim about altered potency requires evidence that the range of potential outcomes has changed, rather than merely showing that one usual outcome is defective. This logical sequence helps avoid attributing every developmental phenotype to a change of potency.

The relationship between competence and potency deserves particular attention. Competence describes the ability of a cell or tissue to respond to a particular developmental signal. Potency describes the range of developmental outcomes accessible to a cell. A broadly potent cell need not be immediately competent to respond to every signal at every moment. It may first need to enter an intermediate state in which the relevant receptors or downstream regulatory machinery are present. Conversely, a restricted progenitor may respond strongly to a signal that directs its only usual lineage. Competence is therefore often defined relative to a particular signal and developmental stage, whereas potency is a broader description of developmental repertoire.

Stem-cell technology draws on the distinction between maintenance and differentiation. For example, a pluripotent culture may be expanded under maintenance conditions and later exposed to cues that favor a medically or experimentally useful lineage. Researchers can investigate how genetic changes alter the resulting differentiation process, compare cells derived from different individuals or study responses to drugs in a defined cellular system. These applications depend on reliable differentiation and characterization, not merely on starting with a culture that carries a pluripotency label. The maturity, purity and physiological performance of the resulting cells determine how useful the model will be for a particular question.

Regenerative medicine also highlights why broad potential must be controlled. A preparation intended to replace a specific tissue should produce the correct functional cells in the appropriate place. Residual undifferentiated pluripotent cells can pose risks of inappropriate growth and tissue formation. Cells may be immature, heterogeneous or unable to integrate with the recipient tissue. Immune interactions, genetic integrity and the stability of the differentiated state can influence outcomes. These are biological problems that follow directly from the relationship between potency and identity. Increasing developmental breadth is not always desirable; for a specific therapeutic objective, stable and appropriately restricted function may be more valuable.

In plant biotechnology, regeneration makes it possible to recover plants after certain tissue-culture and genetic-manipulation procedures. The efficiency with which an explant produces viable plants can limit the success of a broader biotechnology workflow. An intervention may increase cell proliferation without improving organized regeneration. Another may increase shoot production but reduce rooting or later plant vigor. Evaluating the outcome requires attention to the complete developmental route, not just the number of proliferating cells or the appearance of one organ. Understanding the cellular source and regulation of regeneration can therefore inform practical decisions about which tissue and developmental stage to use.

Finally, potency must be understood as part of an organized developmental system. A zygote has broad capacity, yet its descendants do not develop successfully by randomly choosing cell identities. Spatial relationships, timing, signaling, feedback, mechanical organization and gene regulation direct the realization of particular outcomes. Normal differentiation restricts potential while building specialized functions. Some cells retain a broader repertoire to support future growth and maintenance, and some restricted states can be altered under exceptional circumstances. These principles explain why developmental biology cannot be reduced either to a static lineage tree or to an assertion that every genome-containing cell can become anything. The important task is to connect the repertoire of each cellular state to the evidence, mechanisms and biological context that define it.

Mechanism recap
