1. Heredity as an experimental problem

A seed can be small enough to hold between two fingers, yet it contains the embryo of a plant that may eventually produce hundreds of additional seeds. In some respects the mature plant resembles the plant that supplied the ovule and the plant that supplied the pollen. In other respects it differs from both. The central question of genetics is how such resemblance and difference arise from the transmission, expression and variation of hereditary information. An explanation needs to account for the continuity of biological characteristics across generations without assuming that every individual is an exact copy of its parents. It must also explain why a characteristic may be absent in one generation yet appear again in another, even when the environment is similar. These problems become approachable when reproduction is treated as an experimental process with identifiable parents, measurable offspring and predictions that can be tested against observations.

The word heredity describes the transmission of biological information between generations. Variation describes differences among individuals, including individuals belonging to the same species or family. Inheritance is not the same as the expression of an inherited characteristic. A plant may receive a particular version of a gene, but the eventual size, colour or biochemical composition of the plant may depend on other genes, the age of the tissue and environmental conditions. The inherited information is therefore best distinguished from the biological outcome produced by that information. This distinction will be essential when we encounter individuals that carry a recessive allele but do not display the corresponding recessive phenotype. An organism need not look like the allele it transmits.

Imagine two experimentally maintained lines of peas, one producing round mature seeds and the other producing wrinkled mature seeds. Suppose fertilisation between these lines produces only round seeds, yet allowing the resulting plants to reproduce produces both round and wrinkled seeds. A model in which the hereditary contributions of the parents become permanently mixed has difficulty explaining why the wrinkled form returns without an additional source of wrinkled hereditary material. A model with persistent hereditary units offers a different explanation: the information responsible for the wrinkled form may have been present throughout the apparently round generation but its effect was concealed by the relationship between the two inherited versions. The decisive observation is not simply that the first generation looks round. It is that a reproducible second generation contains a characteristic which appeared to be missing.

An experimental geneticist must distinguish a qualitative observation from a quantitative prediction. A statement that offspring sometimes resemble their grandparents is qualitative. A statement that, for a particular cross under a specified model, approximately one quarter of offspring should show a particular characteristic is quantitative. The latter can be challenged by counting offspring. The power of Mendelian genetics comes from connecting these two kinds of reasoning. A qualitative observation suggests a possible hereditary mechanism; the mechanism yields probabilities; probabilities predict patterns in the descendants; and actual observations then constrain the mechanism. A ratio is therefore not an isolated fact to memorise but the numerical consequence of a model that can be evaluated.

This approach is especially useful because the hereditary units of interest were initially invisible. Gregor Mendel did not have a molecular model of DNA or modern chromosome imaging. Rather than infer molecular structure directly, he inferred rules of transmission from controlled breeding experiments. A simple mathematical representation of hidden hereditary factors allowed him to make predictions about future descendants. Later cytological work provided a physical framework for the same transmission rules, and molecular genetics eventually identified many underlying genes. These are distinct levels of explanation. The observable pattern, the inferred transmission model, the cellular mechanism and the molecular identity of a gene reinforce one another, but evidence for one level does not automatically establish every detail at the others.

The problem of transmission can be narrowed by selecting a single character. In everyday language a character and a trait are often interchangeable; in classical teaching, a character is a property that can vary, such as seed shape, while a trait is a particular observed form, such as round or wrinkled. For a useful experiment the alternative forms must be reliably distinguishable in the relevant material and at the relevant developmental stage. Measuring the mass of every seed may provide more information than classifying its shape, but the two measurements answer different questions. Mass can vary continuously, whereas a shape classification may reveal discrete hereditary classes. Neither type of measurement is universally superior. The investigator must choose a measurement appropriate to the proposed mechanism.

A single-locus cross isolates an especially tractable question: what happens when the parents differ at one nuclear locus and the investigator follows its inheritance? Other genes and environmental influences do not cease to exist. Instead, the analysis begins with a controlled approximation in which the focal locus explains the distinction being studied. This is comparable to holding temperature constant in an enzyme experiment while varying substrate concentration. The simplification is useful because it permits a clear prediction; the simplification becomes misleading if it is treated as a complete account of all biological variation. A careful genetic analysis therefore specifies its assumptions before explaining a result.

The experimental unit and generation are important. A mature pea seed contains an embryo produced by fertilisation, surrounded by tissues that may have different developmental origins. The embryo has its own nuclear genotype. The seed coat originates from maternal ovule tissue. Consequently, not every visible property of a seed necessarily reports the genotype of its embryo. This matters whenever an investigator chooses a seed character as the measured phenotype. Seed shape and cotyledon colour can be followed using suitable genetic crosses, whereas a property restricted to maternal seed coat tissue must be interpreted with the maternal genotype in mind. The broader lesson is that the visible tissue has to be connected to the individual whose genotype is being inferred. Later chapters examine maternal effects and extrachromosomal inheritance in detail.

Before any ratio is calculated, a geneticist asks what was crossed, how fertilisation took place, what was measured, and which generation the observations represent. These four questions prevent many mistakes. For example, a seed harvested directly after pollination between parental plants may contain the first filial embryo. A plant grown from that embryo belongs to the first filial generation. Seeds produced by self-fertilisation of that plant contain second filial embryos. Depending on the character, the experimenter may score the embryos in seeds or may grow them into mature plants before scoring. Assigning a seed to a generation by the age of its maternal plant would mix these distinct genetic entities.

The aims of this chapter follow from this logic. We shall first understand why pea breeding could reveal the existence of persistent hereditary units. We shall then represent those units using modern genetic language and follow their cellular transmission through meiosis and fertilisation. Finally, we shall use that model to predict and interpret the outcomes of monohybrid crosses. Throughout, a key distinction will be maintained between a rule governing the passage of alleles and a rule governing the phenotypic appearance of individuals that possess them. The two rules are related through the genotype, but they are not the same rule.

2. Mendel's experimental design

Mendel communicated the results of his hybridisation experiments in 1865 and the paper appeared in print in 1866. The research concerned the behaviour of distinct plant characters through several generations, rather than a direct search for DNA or chromosomes. His investigation used cultivated peas of the genus Pisum. The choice was important: a workable model organism must allow the experimental question to be answered with sufficient control and precision. In peas it was possible to obtain contrasting varieties, maintain lines by self-fertilisation, arrange controlled pollination and obtain enough descendants to compare recurrent numerical patterns. Experimental suitability is not simply a consequence of an organism being easy to cultivate; it depends on whether the organism's reproductive biology can be brought into alignment with the question.

A pea flower contains both male and female reproductive structures. Pollen originates in the anthers and carries the male gametophytic contribution; the ovules contain the female gametophytes. Natural self-pollination can occur because pollen from a flower can reach its own stigma. This property makes it convenient to maintain a line that repeatedly yields the same focal trait. Controlled cross-pollination requires excluding unintended self-pollen before the relevant pollination event and transferring pollen from the selected donor. Mendel's work depended on careful manipulation of flowers and on limiting unwanted fertilisations. The details matter because an unexpected seed could otherwise originate from an accidental self-fertilisation instead of from a genuine hybrid cross. The experiment is meaningful only if parentage is sufficiently well controlled.

Pollination is the transfer of pollen to the stigma; fertilisation is the eventual union of gametes. These are connected events, but not synonyms. The pollen grain must function in the reproductive environment before sperm cells can reach the female gametophyte. In flowering plants double fertilisation produces the embryo and the endosperm through distinct fusion events. Our simple nuclear inheritance calculations concern the diploid embryo produced by fusion of an egg and a sperm, rather than the ploidy or genotype of every surrounding seed tissue. When following a plant-height character, one normally grows the resulting embryos into plants, whereas some seed characters can be classified earlier. This connection between the developmental origin of tissue and the phenotypic observation is part of a sound experimental design.

Mendel first examined the stability of the selected varieties across generations. A true-breeding line repeatedly produces the same form of the focal character when self-fertilised under the tested conditions. In the simplest single-locus diploid model, such a line is homozygous at the relevant locus. True breeding for seed shape does not mean that every gene throughout the genome is necessarily homozygous or that the plant will never vary in any other property. The term is operational: it concerns the reproducible inheritance of the specified character. Testing the line before crossing helps ensure that variation discovered after hybridisation arose in the experimental pedigree rather than being an unresolved mixture of parental genotypes.

The contrast between two parental lines should be sufficiently distinct to score without ambiguous thresholds. Classical summaries describe seven paired characters: mature seed shape, cotyledon colour, flower colour, pod shape, unripe pod colour, flower position and stem length. Their alternative forms are round or wrinkled, yellow or green, violet or white, inflated or constricted, green or yellow, axial or terminal, and tall or dwarf. The flower-colour contrast was historically associated with seed-coat pigmentation and other pigmentation features. These seven contrasts were selected from a broader range of possible variation; the study does not imply that all characters of peas come in only two classes. It is important not to confuse a carefully selected experimental character with the universal organisation of phenotypic variation.

Mendel's comparison of alternative forms also reduced the problem of measurement noise. If the distinction under investigation is strongly expressed and the observer knows when to score it, a large fraction of offspring can be classified with little ambiguity. This permits a clear comparison with a probability model. In contrast, a quantitative property such as height in a genetically mixed population exposed to unequal soil conditions might show overlapping distributions. Height can still be studied genetically, but scoring rules and environmental controls become more important. The success of the classical crosses therefore depended both on the biology of the chosen loci and on the selection of phenotypes that could be recognised reliably.

The parental generation is denoted P. Offspring of the specified parental cross are designated F1, the first filial generation. Descendants produced from an appropriate cross of F1 individuals, or from self-fertilisation of an F1 plant, constitute F2. The letter F describes the filial relationship, not a particular genotype. For example, an F2 individual can be homozygous for either allele or heterozygous. The labels are tied to the breeding history of the experiment. F1 and F2 are not inherent biological properties of individual plants outside that history. If an investigator initiates a new cross using descendants of an earlier experiment, the generation notation must be defined relative to the new cross.

A particularly informative observation is that crosses between true-breeding lines with contrasting traits often gave a uniform first filial phenotype. If one parental line had a round-seed form and the other a wrinkled-seed form, the hybrid embryos showed the round form under the classical conditions. This uniformity does not show that the hybrids contain only one hereditary contribution. They result from fertilisation involving both parents. The crucial hypothesis is that the hereditary factor associated with the wrinkled form persists, even though the resulting combination expresses a round phenotype. Testing that hypothesis requires observing what the hybrid transmits to its descendants.

Mendel allowed hybrids to produce the next generation and counted the different phenotypes. His published seed-shape experiment reported 5,474 round or roundish seeds and 1,850 wrinkled seeds. These figures are historical observations, not constructed classroom data. The total is 7,324; the ratio of round to wrinkled is about 2.96 to 1. The wrinkled fraction is about 25.26 percent. A prediction of one wrinkled seed in four is very close to that aggregate observation, but it is not identical to it. The slight difference illustrates an important feature of probabilistic prediction: a model specifies expected frequencies, not a requirement that every observed finite sample match the expectation perfectly.

Self-fertilisation of the second generation allows a more discriminating test than merely observing its phenotypes. Under a two-allele model, individuals exhibiting the recessive phenotype must contain two recessive alleles, provided the phenotype is fully penetrant and no alternative cause produces the same phenotype. Dominant-looking individuals, however, can include both homozygotes and heterozygotes. When their descendants are scored separately, these two kinds of parents should be distinguishable: homozygotes should breed true at the focal locus, while heterozygotes should again produce segregating descendants. The progeny test thus turns a hidden genotype difference into an observable difference among families.

The use of individual families is important. Imagine collecting seeds from many dominant-looking F2 plants into a single container and recording only the total number of recessive descendants. This could reveal an overall pattern but would destroy information about whether the recessive descendants came from all parents or only a subset. Keeping the progeny of each parent separate permits the investigator to distinguish genetically uniform lines from segregating hybrid families. The Mendelian model predicts a pattern within families as well as a pattern across the original population of descendants. Consistency at both levels is stronger evidence than an aggregate ratio alone.

A reciprocal cross changes the sex or reproductive role of the parental genotypes while keeping their focal alleles the same. In pea breeding, the line providing the ovules in the first cross provides the pollen in the reciprocal cross, and the other line assumes the opposite role. If the two lines differ at a single autosomal nuclear locus and transmission and expression do not depend on the parent's role, the hybrids have the same focal nuclear genotype in both crosses. Similar results therefore support that simplified model. A difference is informative because it directs attention to mechanisms involving parental role, such as cytoplasmic inheritance, maternal genotype, sex linkage in appropriate organisms, imprinting or differences in transmission and viability. It is not enough to know that two plants were crossed; the direction of the cross can be a deliberate experimental variable.

The most defensible reconstruction of Mendel's contribution is therefore a sequence of tests rather than a list of isolated laws. Establish stable parental phenotypes; make controlled crosses; observe the first generation; follow segregation in the next generation; distinguish segregating and true-breeding families; and use the results to evaluate a hereditary model. The sequence remains useful in modern genetics even when the organism, molecular tools and statistical methods are different.

3. Genetic language: loci, alleles and genotypes

A chromosome is an organised DNA-containing structure. A gene is a hereditary genomic unit whose sequence and regulatory context contribute to a functional product or biological activity. In classical genetics, a gene may initially be recognised through the inheritance of a character even before its molecular sequence is known. A locus is a position or defined genomic region at which a gene or marker is situated. Different sequence versions at that locus are called alleles. These terms answer different questions: a gene concerns a functional hereditary unit, a locus concerns its genomic position, and an allele concerns a particular version of the unit at that position. Two alleles of one locus are not ordinarily two independent genes at unrelated chromosome positions.

In a typical diploid organism, a nuclear chromosome pair consists of two homologous chromosomes. They carry corresponding sets of loci, although their DNA sequences are not necessarily identical. One chromosome of the pair is inherited through each parental gamete. At a given single-copy autosomal locus, the diploid embryo therefore normally has two allele copies. If both copies are the same allele, the individual is homozygous at that locus. If the copies are different, the individual is heterozygous. Homozygosity is always relative to the locus or set of loci under discussion. It does not imply that the two homologous chromosomes are identical along their entire length.

For a simple biallelic example we can denote the round-seed allele by R and the wrinkled-seed allele by r. The possible diploid genotypes are RR, Rr and rr. In this notation Rr and rR describe the same unordered genotype unless the origin of each allele has been explicitly assigned a special meaning. The order of two letters in a simple autosomal genotype does not, by itself, indicate the maternal or paternal source. A question that specifically distinguishes parental origin must label the contributions separately. This becomes important in later study of imprinting, but it is unnecessary for the basic monohybrid calculation.

An individual with genotype Rr possesses one copy of each allele at the same locus. It is misleading to say that it contains one gene for roundness and a second gene for wrinkling if that wording suggests two independently inherited loci. The experimentally relevant variation here concerns allelic versions of the same gene. During ordinary gamete formation the two homologous chromosome copies separate, and a normally formed haploid gamete receives one allele at the focal locus. The gamete may contain thousands of additional genes elsewhere in its genome, but only its allele at the focal locus is needed in our current prediction.

The genotype describes the genetic constitution of an individual. The term may refer to a single locus, several loci or a much larger part of the genome depending on the question. The phenotype is the observed or measured biological state. It can concern a visible property, a biochemical activity, a physiological measurement or a molecular marker. A person's or plant's entire phenotype is not exhausted by a single observable trait. Thus a statement that RR and Rr have the same phenotype needs an explicit qualification: they have the same scored phenotype for the focal character under the defined conditions. They may differ at other measurable levels, including the quantity of functional gene product.

Genotype and phenotype are related through biological processes rather than through the typography of allele symbols. A gene product may catalyse a metabolic reaction, contribute to a structural component or regulate other genes. Alternative alleles can change the amount, timing, localisation or activity of that product. Environmental conditions and other loci can affect the resulting biological process. The final phenotype is therefore the outcome of the genotype operating in a particular cellular and developmental context. In elementary crosses we simplify this relationship by assuming a consistent mapping from each genotype to one of two scored phenotypes. The simplification must be remembered whenever an observed pattern deviates from prediction.

A diploid organism generally has two alleles at one autosomal locus, but a population can contain many alternative alleles of the same gene. The number of alleles present across a population must not be confused with the number in one diploid individual. For example, three distinct alleles can exist in a population while an ordinary diploid individual at a single-copy autosomal locus still possesses only two copies. Multiple-allele inheritance and allelic series will be developed in Node 8.A.03. Here the two-allele representation is sufficient to derive the basic inheritance rules. It is an experimental restriction, not a claim that every gene has only two possible sequence versions.

The symbols used in genetics are conventions. Uppercase and lowercase letters are convenient for displaying complete dominance in introductory examples. They do not identify whether a variant is common, healthy, evolutionarily advantageous, ancestral or molecularly active. In actual genetic nomenclature the symbol may reflect a species-specific convention, the phenotype in which a mutation was discovered or the history of the laboratory that named it. Consequently, a capital letter is not evidence of the molecular mechanism. A question should define the allele symbols and their phenotypic relationships before using them as shorthand.

One can distinguish allele transmission from gene expression by imagining a genotype assay and a phenotype assay performed on the same organisms. A genotype assay might identify two allele-specific DNA sequences, whereas the phenotype assay might classify seed shape. In a complete-dominance model the DNA assay separates three genotypes but the shape assay separates only two classes. There is no contradiction: the two assays measure different properties. Molecular assays may reveal distinctions that are invisible in gross morphology. Conversely, an environmental effect may alter the scored morphology without changing the DNA sequence at the locus.

A carrier is an individual that possesses an allele whose associated recessive phenotype is not expressed in the heterozygous state under the specified model. In this sense the Rr plant carries the r allele even though its seeds are round. Carrier status concerns genotype and transmission potential, not the presence of a hidden physical wrinkled structure inside an otherwise round seed. When the plant forms gametes it can transmit r. If a compatible recessive allele arrives through the other gamete, the resulting embryo may express the recessive form. This is why the recurrence of recessive phenotypes can be understood without invoking the spontaneous reappearance of information that had been lost.

The simplest inheritance model can be represented as two consecutive maps. First, parental genotypes determine the possible gametic allele distributions and, through fertilisation, the probability distribution of offspring genotypes. Second, the offspring genotypes are translated into observed phenotypes by a rule describing allelic expression in the defined context. Keeping these two maps separate is the most useful habit for solving genetics problems. It avoids the error of assigning different gamete frequencies to dominant and recessive alleles just because their effects on the phenotype differ.

4. Dominance and the expression of hereditary variation

Complete dominance describes a specific phenotypic relationship among three genotypes. If the heterozygote Rr is classified in the same phenotypic category as RR, and rr belongs to a distinct category, then R is dominant to r with respect to that scored character under the conditions used. The r allele is described as recessive relative to R. Dominance is not a property of one allele considered entirely in isolation: it is a relationship between alleles in a heterozygous genotype and a specified phenotype. A different allele at the same locus, another measured feature, or a changed environmental setting can yield a different relationship. The comparison must therefore be stated carefully.

At the level of visible seed shape, RR and Rr may be grouped together because their mature seeds are round. But it does not follow that the cellular chemistry of both genotypes is identical. A biochemical assay might distinguish the amount of a particular enzyme in the two genotypes while the visible outcome remains in the same class. This is a threshold relationship between molecular activity and phenotype. If a sufficient level of functional activity is reached by a single functional copy, the gross phenotype can be similar in the heterozygote and homozygote. The amount of activity need not be exactly half or double; expression and metabolic control can modify that simple dosage expectation.

A useful molecular example is the classical round-versus-wrinkled seed character of peas. Bhattacharyya and colleagues reported in 1990 that the recessive rugosus allele was associated with a transposon-like insertion in a gene encoding an isoform of starch-branching enzyme. Starch-branching activity contributes to the structure of storage carbohydrate. The altered enzyme function changes the composition of the developing seed, including starch and soluble sugar metabolism. The physical properties of the developing seed and changes during maturation and drying then contribute to the wrinkled appearance. Thus the difference in mature shape can be connected to a molecular alteration through biochemical and developmental consequences rather than attributed to a literal gene that directly draws wrinkles on a seed.

Consider the chain of causation carefully. The DNA variant affects a gene product. The product influences carbohydrate metabolism in the developing seed. Altered carbohydrate composition affects the physical state of storage tissue and water relations. During maturation, differences in tissue contraction become visible as differences in seed shape. Each step concerns a different level of organisation. A structural difference in DNA is not itself a seed wrinkle. The mature trait emerges from the consequences of that difference during development. This mechanistic interpretation complements the classical inheritance model, which correctly predicts which allele combinations are inherited without needing to specify every intermediate biochemical step.

A loss-of-function allele may be recessive when one normal copy provides sufficient activity for the measured function. This is a common explanation but not a universal rule. If half the usual functional dosage is insufficient, a heterozygote can have an altered phenotype. Some variants produce altered products that interfere with the function of the normal product, and some produce activity in an inappropriate context or increase activity. These situations can produce dominant phenotypes. Mutation classes and dominant-negative mechanisms will be treated in the genes and mutations subunit. For now the important conclusion is that dominance cannot be equated with the presence of a functional allele or recessiveness with the absence of function in every case.

Nor is dominance a statement about the evolutionary success of an allele. Fitness concerns reproductive contribution in a particular environment. An allele associated with a dominant visible phenotype could be rare, neutral, beneficial or deleterious. A recessive allele could be common or rare and might be favoured in particular conditions. The phenotype assigned to the heterozygote does not tell us how many descendants each genotype will leave. A prediction about future allele frequencies would require additional information about reproduction, survival, transmission and the population's initial composition. Those questions belong to population genetics; the immediate Mendelian question is the probability distribution of genotypes within a defined cross.

The same distinction applies to allele frequency. A rare allele can be dominant to a common allele, and a common allele can be recessive. If a heterozygous plant makes gametes according to equal Mendelian segregation, its two alleles have equal transmission probabilities regardless of the proportions of those alleles in the surrounding population. Population-level frequency changes the chance that a randomly chosen mate possesses a given genotype, but it does not by itself change the segregation probabilities within this heterozygote. A problem about a defined parental cross and a problem about a random population sample thus use different information.

Dominance also does not imply stronger transmission. In an Rr parent the R and r copies occupy corresponding genetic positions on homologous chromosomes. In the ordinary segregation model each is represented in half of the functional gametes. Their different phenotypic effects in the diploid parent provide no reason to assign one of them a larger gametic probability. A cross showing an excess of one allelic class requires investigation of the transmission process, viability, sampling or another relevant mechanism. Calling the more frequent class dominant does not explain the excess. Dominance should first be established from the heterozygote's phenotype, not from counting alleles in the gametes.

The word recessive likewise does not mean silent at every biological level. An allele may be transcribed in the heterozygote, may produce a measurable product and may affect a biochemical quantity without changing the class used to score the phenotype. The expression of the allele and its dominance relationship must not be collapsed into one claim. In incomplete dominance, an intermediate heterozygous phenotype separates all three genotype classes; in codominance, distinct contributions of both alleles can be detected in the heterozygote. These relationships modify phenotype predictions while ordinary allele segregation can remain unchanged. They will be the focus of a later node.

A conceptual test is to imagine replacing the shape assay with a perfectly discriminating genotype assay. In a heterozygote cross, the genotype probabilities derived from gamete union remain one quarter RR, one half Rr and one quarter rr. With the shape assay those probabilities are grouped into three quarters round and one quarter wrinkled. With the genotype assay there are three detectable classes, in a one-to-two-to-one ratio. Changing the assay does not change the events of meiosis or fertilisation that produced the embryos. It changes which differences are detectable. This is why the expression rule should be applied only after the transmission calculation has been performed.

5. From hereditary factors to chromosomes

The idea that a hybrid possesses two hereditary factors at a locus is an abstract description of transmission. To explain why these factors occur in pairs and why a gamete receives only one, the geneticist must examine the relationship between nuclear chromosomes and sexual reproduction. A typical diploid organism begins as a zygote with two sets of chromosomes, one set from the egg and one set from the sperm. During ordinary mitotic growth the chromosome complement is maintained, whereas the cell divisions associated with sexual reproduction reduce the number of chromosome sets. The alternating processes of reduction and fertilisation provide a physical framework for the repeated pairing and separation of hereditary information across generations.

Early studies of chromosomes demonstrated that these structures do not simply disappear at each division and reappear in an arbitrary arrangement. They behave as discrete units, undergo duplication before division and are allocated to daughter nuclei in an ordered process. Theodor Boveri and Walter Sutton contributed independently to the formulation of the chromosome theory of inheritance at the beginning of the twentieth century. Boveri's investigations of sea urchin development supported the significance of obtaining a complete and appropriate chromosome complement. Sutton investigated chromosome behaviour in grasshopper spermatogenesis and connected the pairing and separation of homologues to Mendelian transmission. His 1903 paper explicitly developed the relationship between chromosome behaviour and heredity. The resulting theory identified chromosomes as physical carriers of the hereditary units inferred from breeding experiments.

The reasoning was compelling because several independent observations corresponded. Diploid cells possess two chromosome sets just as the simple Mendelian model assigns two alleles to an individual. The two members of a homologous pair become associated during meiosis, just as the genetic model treats the two alleles as corresponding versions of a unit. Chromosome reduction distributes homologues to separate nuclei, corresponding to the separation of alleles. Fertilisation restores the paired state, corresponding to the combination of one hereditary contribution from each parent. This parallelism does not mean that Sutton or Boveri identified the DNA sequence of a Mendelian gene. It means that observable cellular events could supply a coherent physical interpretation of the rules inferred from breeding results.

A chromosome carries many loci. This fact is necessary to avoid an overly literal reading of the correspondence between chromosome pairs and allele pairs. Suppose chromosome 1 carries loci A, B and C. Each has a corresponding position on the homologous chromosome. The separation of homologues affects all these loci; it is not a separate cellular division for every gene. Alleles at different loci on the same chromosome may be transmitted together, subject to the effects of recombination. In contrast, loci on different independently orienting homologous pairs can have independent transmission probabilities under ordinary conditions. These multi-locus consequences are the subject of subsequent chapters. Here it is enough to recognise that the one-locus Mendelian model describes the behaviour of a defined genomic position embedded within a larger chromosome.

The distinction between homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids is indispensable. Before DNA replication, each chromosome consists of a single chromatid. The homologous partner has a corresponding arrangement of loci but may carry different alleles. After replication, each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined through the machinery that maintains sister association. In the absence of mutation or exchange, the sisters of a chromosome carry the same allele at the focal locus. The two homologues are still distinguishable chromosomes even though each now consists of two chromatids. A diploid Rr cell after replication contains four DNA copies at the locus, two R and two r, but it is not a tetraploid RRRR or RRrr organism. Ploidy refers to chromosome sets, not merely the amount of DNA present after replication.

This difference can be understood by drawing one homologous pair at three stages. Initially the cell contains a chromosome carrying R and a homologue carrying r. After DNA replication, each chromosome has two sister chromatids. If there has been no exchange between the centromere and the focal locus, the two chromatids of one chromosome carry R and the two of the other chromosome carry r. At the first meiotic division the homologues move to different daughter nuclei; at the second division the sister chromatids are separated. The four products collectively contain two copies of R and two of r. The starting genotype has not changed from Rr into a new inherited genotype during replication; it has undergone a change in DNA content preparatory to distributing the two parental alleles.

The physical picture also explains how both true-breeding and segregating parents can undergo the same meiotic mechanism. An RR parent still has two homologous chromosomes at the focal locus. The homologues may differ at other loci, but their copies of R are identical for the purpose of the focal analysis. When they separate, the gametes all contain R. An rr parent likewise produces only r at that locus. The absence of phenotypic segregation in the offspring of a true-breeding line does not mean the chromosomes failed to segregate. The two homologues separated normally, but the molecular identity of the allele at the measured locus was the same on both sides.

In a heterozygote, the difference between the two allele copies becomes experimentally visible through their alternative distributions to gametes. An investigator does not ordinarily observe every meiotic product of a flowering plant directly. Instead, the effects of their distributions can be inferred from progeny genotypes or appropriately chosen phenotypes. Where organisms permit analysis of all four products of a single meiosis, the distribution can be examined much more directly. The key point is that cytological observation, gamete analysis and breeding experiments provide related but nonidentical kinds of evidence. The strength of the chromosome theory lies in their agreement when the hypotheses and measurements are correctly matched.

The theory does not require that every hereditary trait be determined by a single nuclear locus. Organelles possess genomes, chromosome number can vary, and genes may influence one another. Nor does every trait have a simple relation to one allele pair. The chromosome theory provides the foundation for ordinary nuclear transmission, while later discoveries extend the model. A model can be foundational without being exhaustive. The exam-relevant skill is to identify the level at which an observation departs from a simple Mendelian prediction before proposing a more complex mechanism. A departure in phenotype ratios is not automatically a departure from the physical segregation of chromosomes.

6. The cellular mechanism of segregation

Meiosis is a specialised cell division in which one round of DNA replication is followed by two divisions. It generates haploid products from a diploid starting nucleus. In animals this process is associated with the formation of gametes; in flowering plants meiosis produces spores that subsequently develop into gametophytes through mitotic divisions. The haploid gametophytes produce gametes. Thus the immediate product of meiosis in a plant is not always the gamete itself, although the reduction in chromosome number established during meiosis is preserved through the ordinary haploid developmental stages that lead to gametes. The terminology should respect this difference between life cycles while recognising the shared genetic principle.

During prophase of meiosis I, homologous chromosomes pair and recombination may take place between non-sister chromatids. At metaphase I the homologous pairs align on the spindle. Their centromere-associated attachments permit the two homologues of each pair to move towards opposite poles at anaphase I. Sister chromatids remain associated through the first division in the usual sequence. After the first division, each daughter nucleus has received one member of each homologous pair, although the chromosomes still possess two chromatids. The second meiotic division separates the sister chromatids, producing nuclei with a reduced chromosome complement and the ordinary haploid DNA content at the end of the sequence.

For a focal heterozygous locus, the standard model predicts that half of the meiotic products carry one allele and half carry the other, assuming normal chromosome behaviour and no exceptional conversion of one allele into another. It is common to depict segregation of the two alleles as separation of the homologues at anaphase I. This drawing is accurate for a locus that has not recombined with its centromere in that meiosis. If crossing over occurs between the centromere and the locus, the chromatids associated with one centromere can carry different alleles at the locus after exchange. In that case the alleles may separate from one another at the second meiotic division when individual products are considered. The overall two-to-two allele representation among four ordinary products remains the same. This nuance prevents the inaccurate assertion that every locus invariably displays first-division segregation in a tetrad.

To see why the overall distribution is robust, count the copies rather than the appearance of the chromosomes. A heterozygous Rr cell begins with one copy of R and one copy of r. DNA replication produces two copies of each. Ordinary reciprocal exchange between chromatids can change which chromosome segments are associated with which centromere, but it does not in itself change the total number of R and r copies at the locus. If all four products receive a single copy at that position, the products collectively contain two R and two r. Recombination is therefore not synonymous with segregation distortion. The arrangement of alleles at multiple loci can change through exchange, while the marginal distribution of alleles at one ordinary heterozygous locus remains equal.

The distinction between products of meiosis and functional gametes also matters. In many animals, oogenesis produces one large functional egg and smaller polar bodies. Equal representation among all meiotic products does not imply that all those products have an equal role in reproduction. The Mendelian expectation for eggs requires that either of the two alleles have an equal chance of entering the functional egg. In the absence of a bias, the alternative outcomes are equally probable across many independent meioses. In plants, subsequent gametophyte growth and competition may influence which products participate in fertilisation. The standard model assumes that these stages do not systematically favour one allele at the locus under study.

A statement such as a heterozygote produces fifty percent R gametes and fifty percent r gametes is thus a probabilistic description of functional gamete output under specified conditions. It does not require every physical sample of gametes to contain exactly equal counts. Nor does it mean that each gamete contains half an R allele and half an r allele. Each ordinary haploid gamete receives one complete allele copy at the focal position. The percentage refers to the relative number of alternative gametes, not to a fractional genetic constitution of an individual gamete. The language of fractions is meaningful only when the denominator is specified: the fraction of gametes carrying the named allele.

The law of segregation expresses the separation of the two alleles of a gene during the formation of gametes so that the alleles can be transmitted separately. Its ordinary equal-transmission form predicts a probability of one half for either allele of a heterozygote. It is useful to distinguish physical segregation from equality of transmission. Chromosome segregation can occur while the eventual functional gamete pool shows bias, for instance when certain products are preferentially retained or survive better. In that circumstance allele copies may still separate, but the simple fifty-fifty model fails to describe the reproductive contribution accurately. The standard crosses below explicitly use the unbiased form of the model.

Nondisjunction is a different kind of event: chromosomes or chromatids fail to separate in the usual manner. Some products may then contain an extra chromosome or lack a chromosome. These abnormal gametes need not carry the ordinary one-copy complement at the focal autosomal locus. A calculation assuming all gametes contain exactly one allele would not apply unchanged to such products. Chromosome alterations and abnormal chromosome numbers are developed in Subunit 8.F. Here the example demonstrates why specifying normal gamete formation is not merely an unnecessary mathematical qualification. It is a biological condition that justifies the simple allele-distribution model.

Fertilisation reverses the reduction in chromosome-set number by bringing together the haploid nuclear contributions. In an ordinary cross between diploid parents, each gamete contributes one allele at a single-copy autosomal locus. The zygote's two alleles may match or differ. The zygote later undergoes DNA replication and mitosis as it develops, preserving that focal genotype in its ordinary nuclear cell lineages. Thus the cycle of reduction and restoration maintains diploid chromosome-set number from one sexual generation to the next. If ordinary gametes retained both homologous sets and fused without a reduction process, the chromosome-set number would increase in each generation instead of being restored.

One common reasoning error is to suppose that dominance acts during chromosome movement so that the dominant allele is preferentially retained in the functional gamete. Dominance was defined by the relationship of diploid genotypes to a phenotype. It gives no information about spindle attachments or the eventual competitive ability of individual gametes. Another error is to infer that a recessive allele must be destroyed when a heterozygote divides because the heterozygote shows the dominant phenotype. The evidence from subsequent crosses demonstrates that the recessive allele remains transmissible. Chromosomal segregation explains how the two copies separate despite the fact that their phenotypic contributions can differ.

These principles can be represented by a compact bookkeeping table. For an RR parent, the allele probabilities are P(R) = 1 and P(r) = 0. For an Rr parent, P(R) = 1/2 and P(r) = 1/2. For an rr parent, P(R) = 0 and P(r) = 1. In each case the probabilities sum to one because the alternatives are exhaustive and mutually exclusive for an ordinary gamete at the focal locus. The table is not derived from the dominance relationship; it is derived from the genotype and the model of gamete formation. It can be used regardless of whether the heterozygote's phenotype is dominant, intermediate or codominant.

7. Constructing and interpreting a monohybrid cross

A monohybrid cross follows one segregating locus. The word monohybrid is sometimes used broadly for a cross between parents differing in a single character, but the calculation that interests us is a one-locus calculation with two specified alternative alleles. Consider a parental line RR and another line rr. The first produces only R gametes at the locus and the second only r. When one gamete from each parent fuses, the resulting diploid embryos all have genotype Rr. Under the complete-dominance model they all show the round phenotype. The prediction is not that the dominant parent contributes more hereditary material; the zygote contains one copy from each parent, and the phenotype follows from the relationship between those two copies.

The next experimental step is to allow an F1 plant to self-fertilise. This is equivalent at the focal locus to a cross Rr × Rr, provided the ovule-side and pollen-side contributions follow ordinary equal transmission. Each parental gamete source has two possible alleles. When their contributions combine independently in the fertilisation model, there are four ordered combinations: R from the egg and R from the sperm, R from the egg and r from the sperm, r from the egg and R from the sperm, and r from both gametes. Each combination has probability 1/2 multiplied by 1/2, or 1/4. The middle two combinations have the same unordered nuclear genotype, Rr, and their probabilities can be added.

A Punnett square is a visual representation of these possible unions. It is a small table in which the gamete alternatives of one parent are written along the rows and those of the other parent along the columns. Each cell records the genotype produced by the corresponding pair of gametes. The square is a bookkeeping device, not a picture of an actual seed or a statement that every group of four offspring will occupy the four cells in order. Its equal-sized cells represent equal probabilities only when the row and column alternatives are themselves equally probable. If unequal gamete frequencies are specified, each cell must be weighted by the product of the appropriate row and column probabilities.

In this cross, the expected genotype ratio is 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr. The expected phenotype ratio under complete dominance is 3 round : 1 wrinkled because RR and Rr share the round phenotype, whereas rr is wrinkled. It is important to label the ratios. A ratio of three to one is not an allele ratio, and a ratio of one to two to one is not a phenotype ratio in a complete-dominance system. The genotype ratio describes the expected classes of individuals. The phenotype ratio is obtained by grouping these classes according to the phenotype map. The allele ratio counts individual allele copies across genotypes and answers yet another question.

The allele frequencies among a large set of F2 embryos from this cross are expected to be equal when the cross follows the model. To derive the expected R frequency, note that each RR embryo carries two R copies, each Rr embryo carries one and each rr embryo carries none. The expected number of R copies per embryo is 2(1/4) + 1(1/2) + 0(1/4) = 1. There are two focal allele copies per embryo. Dividing gives an expected R frequency of one half, and the same reasoning yields one half for r. The dominant phenotype nevertheless occurs in three quarters of embryos. A high frequency of the dominant phenotype does not imply that three quarters of all focal allele copies are dominant.

This calculation also illustrates the difference between a genotype distribution and a population equilibrium statement. The genotype distribution of a single Rr × Rr cross happens to have the same form as the binomial distribution of two equally probable alleles. That does not demonstrate Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in a real population. Population equilibrium requires a population-level mating and transmission model and appropriate assumptions. A deliberately chosen pair of parents can produce these genotype proportions without satisfying the conditions of a natural randomly mating population. The present analysis concerns the descendants of a defined cross, not the dynamics of allele frequencies over many generations.

The result can be generalised to all six unordered combinations of the three genotypes. RR × RR yields only RR offspring. RR × Rr yields equal expected numbers of RR and Rr offspring, all round under complete dominance. RR × rr yields only Rr offspring, all round. Rr × Rr yields RR, Rr and rr in the expected one-to-two-to-one proportions. Rr × rr yields Rr and rr in equal expected proportions. Finally, rr × rr yields only rr. Listing these crosses is useful because an examiner may give an observed phenotype pattern and ask which of several parental crosses is compatible with it. The genotype predictions provide the starting point for the reverse inference.

Consider the cross RR × Rr in more detail. One parent produces only R gametes; the other produces R and r in equal expected proportions. Every embryo receives at least one R allele. Therefore all embryos are round under complete dominance, but they are not genetically identical: one half are expected to be RR and one half Rr. This is a useful contrast with RR × rr, which also gives all round embryos but makes all of them heterozygous. Two different crosses can therefore give the same immediate phenotypic result while producing different future transmission patterns. An all-dominant phenotype is not sufficient evidence that all offspring have the same genotype.

Suppose 160 embryos are generated from a classical Rr × Rr cross. The expected numbers are 40 RR, 80 Rr and 40 rr, with 120 round and 40 wrinkled. These numbers are obtained by multiplying the total number of embryos by each predicted probability. They are expectations, not guaranteed integer allocations. If the total is 163 rather than 160, the expected RR count is 40.75, the Rr count is 81.5 and the rr count is 40.75. A fractional expectation is legitimate because it describes an average over repeated samples; an actual embryo count cannot be fractional. One must not round expected classes prematurely when doing quantitative comparisons.

Inverting this calculation requires attention to the assumptions. If a set of 160 offspring includes 39 wrinkled individuals, a simple model predicts 40, so the observation is close to the expectation. But the numerical resemblance alone does not uniquely prove Rr × Rr. A different model could produce a similar fraction, and the experimental pedigree or direct genotype information may provide additional support. Conversely, if 48 wrinkled offspring are observed, the fact that 48 is not exactly 40 does not by itself establish a departure from Mendelian inheritance. Whether the difference is larger than expected from sampling variation requires a statistical analysis with the relevant sampling design. The next node introduces formal goodness-of-fit testing.

The expression rule can be changed while leaving the cross calculation untouched. If the heterozygote had an intermediate phenotype instead of resembling RR, the three genotype classes would correspond to three different phenotype classes. Their expected phenotypic proportions would then be one quarter, one half and one quarter. The fact that the three-to-one phenotype ratio is conditional on complete dominance is therefore not a minor exception to memorise; it is a direct consequence of the two-stage method of genetic reasoning. Segregation produces a genotype distribution. Dominance and related expression relationships determine how that distribution becomes visible.

A further conditional assumption is that genotype classes are equally likely to be represented among the individuals actually scored. Suppose rr embryos arise at their expected probability of one quarter but fail to survive to the developmental stage at which the investigator counts the plants. The original zygotic genotype distribution is still one-to-two-to-one, but among the surviving RR and Rr plants the expected proportions become one third and two thirds, respectively, if the two surviving classes are otherwise equally represented. All survivors would show the round phenotype. The missing recessive phenotype in this sample would reflect a difference between conception and observation, rather than the absence of r gametes. This example illustrates the need to specify the developmental stage at which a ratio is predicted.

A useful way to avoid confusion is to write the events in chronological order. First define the parental genotypes. Then specify the gamete probabilities, including any unusual transmission described in the question. Next calculate the zygotic genotype probabilities from the relevant fertilisation model. Then, if necessary, apply survival or selection factors appropriate to the developmental stage. Finally, group the surviving genotypes into phenotype categories using the stated expression rule. If a question supplies a sample of observed individuals, identify whether the observation corresponds to zygotes, seedlings, adults or another stage before choosing which prediction to compare against it.

8. Testcrosses, backcrosses and hidden genotypes

Complete dominance creates an inference problem. A plant whose mature seeds are round might have genotype RR or Rr. The phenotype alone cannot distinguish those two possibilities. A testcross is designed to reveal the genotype of such an individual by crossing it with a known homozygous recessive tester. The tester supplies only r gametes at the focal locus. Consequently, the genotype and, under the simple complete-dominance model, the phenotype of each offspring directly reveal which allele the unknown parent contributed. An offspring with genotype Rr is round; an offspring with genotype rr is wrinkled. The tester does not conceal the difference between the unknown parent's alternative gametes because it never supplies R.

If the unknown plant is RR, all gametes it contributes carry R. Crossing it with rr yields only Rr offspring, all round. If the unknown plant is Rr, it contributes R and r with equal expected probabilities. Crossing it with rr yields equal expected proportions of Rr and rr, corresponding to round and wrinkled phenotypes. The comparison between these two predictions is the basis of the testcross. The experiment can be particularly informative because the production of a recessive offspring is incompatible with an RR unknown parent under the assumed model of correct parentage, normal transmission and reliable scoring. The recessive offspring must have acquired an r allele from each contributing parent.

A testcross must be interpreted as an experiment with finite evidence. Suppose the unknown is actually Rr and the tester is rr. Each independently produced offspring has probability one half of being round. The probability that the first offspring is round is therefore one half. If the second is an independent fertilisation with the same probability, the probability that both are round is one half multiplied by one half, or one quarter. Extending the argument, the probability of obtaining n round offspring in succession from this heterozygote testcross is (1/2) raised to the power n. This expression calculates a conditional probability of the data given a particular parental genotype; it is not automatically the probability that the unknown parent is heterozygous.

For example, four round offspring from the testcross have probability 1/16 if the unknown parent is Rr. Eight have probability 1/256 under the same assumptions. Ten have probability 1/1024, approximately 0.098 percent. In each case the observed result is possible for a heterozygote, although increasingly unlikely as the number of all-round offspring rises. A larger sample therefore supplies stronger evidence against the heterozygote model. The observation does not prove the unknown is RR with mathematical certainty because the heterozygote model still assigns a nonzero probability to that finite outcome. Moreover, practical experimental errors or unusual biological mechanisms could invalidate the simple model.

The distinction between P(data given genotype) and P(genotype given data) is important in analytical questions. If the prior probabilities of RR and Rr among potential unknown parents are known, they can be combined with the relative likelihood of the observed outcome to update the probabilities. Suppose the unknown was chosen at random among the dominant-looking F2 plants from a classical Rr × Rr cross. Before the testcross, the probability that it is RR is one third, while the probability that it is Rr is two thirds. Four round offspring have probability one under the RR model and 1/16 under the Rr model. The unnormalised weights for the two explanations are therefore 1/3 and (2/3)(1/16) = 1/24. Their sum is 3/8. Dividing the heterozygote weight by this sum gives (1/24)/(3/8) = 1/9. The updated heterozygote probability is one ninth under these specified prior and independence assumptions.

The result differs from 1/16 because the latter is the likelihood of observing the data if the plant is heterozygous, whereas one ninth is the probability of heterozygosity given the observation and the stated initial distribution. If the unknown instead came from a collection of lines with a different proportion of RR and Rr parents, the updated probability would differ even though the likelihood of four round progeny under the Rr model remained the same. This is why a question asking for genotype probability after observing offspring must either give a prior distribution or specify a pedigree from which one can derive it. Without such information one can compare likelihoods, but cannot justify a unique posterior probability.

A backcross is a cross between a hybrid and one of its parental genotypes or a representative of a parental line. In the classical RR × rr experiment, the F1 is Rr. Crossing that hybrid with the rr parental line is both a backcross and a testcross because the recurrent parent is the known homozygous recessive tester. Crossing the hybrid with the RR parental line is also a backcross, but it is not the usual phenotype-revealing recessive testcross. That cross yields one half RR and one half Rr and, under complete dominance, all offspring are round. The distinction is based on the purpose and parental relationship of the cross, not on whether the hybrid happens to be the pollen or ovule donor.

These definitions imply that a testcross need not always be a backcross. An unknown dominant-looking plant may be crossed with a known recessive tester from a separate line that was not one of the parents of the original plant. The experiment still tests the unknown's allele contribution. Conversely, a backcross to a dominant parent can fail to reveal the hidden genotype through the immediate phenotype classes, even though it has a well-defined expected genotype distribution. In plant breeding, backcrosses can be used to recover features of a recurrent parental genetic background while retaining an allele of interest. More detailed breeding and genetic mapping applications belong to later chapters; here the single-locus result establishes the logic.

Self-fertilisation or an appropriate progeny test is another way to distinguish a homozygous dominant plant from a heterozygote. If the plant is RR, selfing yields only RR offspring. If it is Rr, selfing yields one quarter rr offspring under ordinary conditions. The appearance of a recessive descendant reveals that the parent carried r, given the model assumptions. Compared with a recessive testcross, selfing a heterozygote produces a lower probability of a recessive offspring: one quarter rather than one half. Thus the testcross is usually more informative per offspring when recessive phenotypes are scored and the tester is available. Selfing remains useful when experimental circumstances favour it or when one wishes to establish whether a line is true breeding.

For instance, the probability of failing to observe a recessive offspring in six independent descendants from a heterozygote is (3/4)^6, approximately 0.178, in a selfing experiment. In a recessive testcross, the corresponding probability is (1/2)^6, approximately 0.0156. Both procedures can yield a finite sample containing only dominant-looking offspring even when the parent is heterozygous, but the testcross produces such an outcome less often under the ordinary model. The calculation explains the difference in experimental discriminating power rather than merely declaring the testcross to be a useful method.

A molecular genotype assay can, in principle, distinguish RR and Rr directly if a validated marker identifies the relevant alleles. The testcross instead infers genotype from the ability to transmit an allele and express a characteristic in descendants. Each approach has distinct requirements. A molecular assay needs a reliable association between the detected sequence and the focal genetic variant. A phenotypic testcross requires appropriate mating, reliable tester genotype, sufficient progeny and an adequately penetrant phenotype. A testcross is especially revealing when the molecular identity of the locus is not yet known. The logic is not obsolete simply because molecular genotyping is available; the two approaches answer related questions at different levels.

Reciprocal testcrosses provide another diagnostic comparison. If an Rr plant is crossed with an rr tester in each reproductive direction, the standard nuclear model predicts the same offspring genotype proportions. If recessive offspring are frequent in one direction but rare in the other, one should examine whether allele transmission or survival differs between pollen-side and ovule-side contributions, whether there is a parent-of-origin effect, or whether the scoring procedure measures a maternally derived tissue. These possibilities cannot be distinguished from the ratio alone. The informative experimental response is to collect additional observations, check genotype distributions and test plausible competing mechanisms.

9. F2 progeny tests and the structure of segregating families

The three classes of F2 genotype predicted by the monohybrid model are not equally visible. RR and Rr individuals share the same dominant-looking phenotype, so counting the original F2 individuals reveals only the combined probability of those genotypes. If a plant is selected specifically from the round F2 class, the relevant sample space excludes the wrinkled rr class. In the original cross the RR probability is one quarter and the Rr probability is one half. Within the dominant class these values must be divided by the total dominant probability, three quarters. The resulting conditional probabilities are one third for RR and two thirds for Rr. These fractions describe the composition expected among the selected plants, not among all F2 plants.

The change of denominator can be demonstrated by following four equally probable conception pathways. One pathway yields RR, two yield Rr and one yields rr. Selection for the dominant phenotype removes the rr pathway. Three equally probable pathways remain, of which two lead to a heterozygote. A student who simply assigns a one-half chance of heterozygosity to the chosen plant is using the original sample space rather than the new one. The problem is not that the original one-half value was incorrect. It was correct for an individual selected without regard to phenotype; the selection criterion has changed which individuals are eligible to be chosen.

Now consider growing 120 round F2 plants separately and collecting seeds from each by self-fertilisation. If the parental cross was Rr × Rr and selection does not distinguish RR from Rr within the round class, we expect approximately 40 true-breeding families from RR parents and 80 segregating families from Rr parents. These are family counts. Within a segregating family, the expected offspring phenotype distribution is three quarters round and one quarter wrinkled. It would be incorrect to state that the 80 families must each contain precisely three round seeds for every wrinkled seed, because each family is a finite sample of fertilisation outcomes. The family-level classification may itself be uncertain if only a few descendants are produced.

The historical experiment illustrates the power of looking beyond the F2 ratio. In the seed-shape analysis Mendel followed round descendants and distinguished lines that remained round from lines that produced both forms. He reported 193 constant round lines and 372 lines that yielded both forms among 565 plants raised from round seeds in this experiment. The observed fractions are approximately 34.2 percent and 65.8 percent, respectively. The predicted fractions under the simple model are 33.3 and 66.7 percent. The counts are from his historical paper, not a simulated example. The close correspondence between these family outcomes and the two-to-one expectation provides a different and complementary line of evidence to the aggregate three-to-one phenotype ratio.

It is helpful to distinguish the levels of organisation of these numbers. The original F2 sample contains individual embryos. A subset of round embryos is grown into plants; each plant becomes the parent of a progeny family. A family belongs to the constant or segregating category according to its pattern of descendants, subject to sufficient sampling. The predicted family proportions are one third and two thirds. Within a segregating family, a new set of embryos is formed with a one-to-two-to-one genotype distribution. Mixing the family proportions with the offspring proportions would confuse an inference about parental genotypes with a prediction about descendants. In an examination question, the unit being counted is often the most important clue.

One can also derive a prediction for a randomly chosen descendant of a randomly chosen dominant F2 plant. Suppose every selected F2 plant contributes equally to the sample, and a single selfed descendant is chosen from each without selecting its phenotype. The probability that the chosen parent is RR is one third; an RR parent produces only RR descendants. The probability of an Rr parent is two thirds; its descendants include rr with probability one quarter. The overall probability of an rr descendant is therefore (1/3)(0) + (2/3)(1/4) = 1/6. The corresponding genotype probabilities are P(RR) = 1/3 + (2/3)(1/4) = 1/2 and P(Rr) = (2/3)(1/2) = 1/3. The phenotypic expectation is five sixths round and one sixth wrinkled. This ratio differs from both the original F2 ratio and the ratio within an individual heterozygous family.

The equal-contribution condition in the previous calculation is substantive. If RR plants are more productive than Rr plants, a sample of seeds pooled in proportion to each plant's actual output will overrepresent descendants from RR parents. If one seed is sampled independently from each parent, the family contributions are intentionally equal. The same original F2 genotype proportions can therefore give different pooled F3 phenotype proportions under different sampling schemes. Genetic reasoning requires attention to both inheritance and the process by which experimental material enters the sample. A model of parental genotype probabilities is not enough to specify the properties of a sample whose families have been weighted differently.

An analogous calculation can be made without selecting F2 plants by phenotype. Assume the F2 plants have the expected RR, Rr and rr proportions of one quarter, one half and one quarter. Self each plant and sample descendants with equal contribution per parent. RR parents contribute only RR descendants, rr parents contribute only rr descendants, and Rr parents produce the usual three genotype classes. The pooled probabilities are P(RR) = 1/4 + (1/2)(1/4) = 3/8, P(Rr) = (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4 and P(rr) = 1/4 + (1/2)(1/4) = 3/8. Although the expected allele frequencies remain one half each in this idealised example, the heterozygote fraction falls from one half to one quarter. Repeated selfing changes genotype composition without necessarily changing allele frequencies.

The decrease of heterozygosity can be understood directly at one locus. A homozygote remains homozygous in every descendant when selfed under the model, whereas a heterozygote produces heterozygous descendants with probability one half. Therefore if the fraction of heterozygous parents in a generation is H and each parent contributes equally to the next selfed generation, the expected heterozygous fraction in the next generation is H/2. Starting from an entirely heterozygous F1, after k generations of selfing the expected heterozygous fraction is (1/2)^k. The calculation assumes no new mutation, no outcrossing, no selection, and equal sampling. It is a one-locus breeding prediction rather than a general claim that every part of a genome becomes homozygous at an identical realised rate.

This result has a practical implication for the construction of stable experimental lines. Selfing can increase the proportion of homozygous loci in the descendants of hybrids, but a single individual selected for a dominant-looking phenotype may still be heterozygous at the focal locus. To establish a line that reliably transmits a particular allele, one must either follow its descendants with a sufficiently informative breeding design or genotype the relevant locus directly. Repeatedly selecting the dominant phenotype is not equivalent to selecting only RR individuals because Rr individuals are eligible for selection too. The observed phenotype can guide the breeding decision, but a suitable inference procedure is needed to establish the genetic state.

The mathematical expressions in this section follow from one-locus conditional probabilities rather than from independent assortment between loci. Their purpose is to clarify why Mendel examined the descendants of selected F2 individuals. A more general treatment of the rules of probability, multiple independent events and multilocus segregation follows in Node 8.A.02. The key skill here is to connect an experimental selection criterion with its correct denominator and to distinguish the genotype of a parent from the distribution among that parent's offspring.

10. Interpreting segregation observations and experimental evidence

When a Mendelian cross is scored, the observed count of a class depends on two ingredients: the probability of that class under the genetic model and the number of individuals examined. The expected count for a category is N times p, where N is the sample size and p is the model probability. N is a count of the relevant individuals or other experimental units; p is dimensionless. The product is an expected count. If the same experiment were repeated many times under stable conditions, the mean class count would tend towards that expected value. Individual experiments can give different realised counts. It is therefore essential to keep the terms observed and expected separate when constructing a results table.

The seed-shape counts from Mendel's publication provide a concrete example. For 7,324 seeds and a model with probabilities three quarters round and one quarter wrinkled, the expected counts are 5,493 round and 1,831 wrinkled. The observed counts are 5,474 and 1,850. The differences observed minus expected are −19 and +19, respectively. The differences must sum to zero because the two categories exhaust the same total number of seeds. The proportion of observed wrinkled seeds is 1,850/7,324, approximately 0.2526, compared with the model's 0.25. This is a difference of about 0.26 percentage points, not a difference of 26 percent. Careful specification of the denominator gives a meaningful description of the departure.

The magnitude of a deviation must be interpreted in relation to sample size. A group of eight F2 seeds with six round and two wrinkled exactly matches the predicted fractions, but that sample contains little information about alternative models with slightly different probabilities. A sample of thousands can distinguish smaller changes in class frequency, provided the observations are suitable for the statistical analysis. Conversely, a large absolute difference from the expected count may correspond to a small fractional difference when the total sample is large. Comparing the absolute number of unexpected observations without considering N would be misleading. The next chapter develops formal quantitative tests; here we concentrate on the reasoning that makes a comparison scientifically interpretable.

Consider two hypothetical F2 families produced by heterozygous parents. The first contains 11 round and 1 wrinkled seed. The second contains 8 round and 4 wrinkled. Each sample has N = 12, so the expected counts are 9 and 3. The first is two above expectation for round and two below for wrinkled; the second is one below for round and one above for wrinkled. Both samples are possible under the model. Pooling them gives 19 round and 5 wrinkled among 24, compared with 18 and 6 expected. Pooling reduces the apparent aggregate departure in this example because deviations in the two families partly offset each other. But if the families were exposed to different conditions or arose from different parental genotypes, the pooled ratio could conceal biologically relevant differences. An investigator should retain information about the families and compare their experimental contexts before pooling.

A family with no observed wrinkled offspring is another instructive example. If eight descendants are independently sampled from an Rr × Rr cross, the probability that each is round is 3/4. The probability all eight are round is (3/4)^8, approximately 0.1001. Thus even a genuinely segregating heterozygous family has about a one-in-ten chance of appearing uniform in an eight-offspring sample under this model. The probability calculation does not imply that ten percent of every group of families must be misclassified, but it quantifies a potential sampling limitation. Repeating the progeny test with more offspring can reduce the chance of overlooking segregation.

Experimental evidence is stronger when the investigator distinguishes between observations that test different predictions. A uniform F1 supports the phenotype relationship between the alleles. The reappearance of the recessive class in F2 supports persistence and separation of the hereditary factors. The F2 class proportions are consistent with equal segregation and ordinary fertilisation under the assumptions of the model. The progeny behaviour of dominant-looking F2 individuals tests the predicted mixture of hidden genotypes. A testcross provides a more direct readout of the unknown parent's alternative gametes. A molecular assay can then test allele identity independently of the visible trait. The observations reinforce each other because they probe different implications of the same model, not because they merely repeat one number.

Suppose an investigator has obtained a new pair of pea lines that differ in seed appearance. One line produces smooth seeds and the other produces irregular seeds. The investigator proposes that the distinction reflects a single nuclear locus with complete dominance. A suitable experimental design begins by establishing that the lines reproducibly express their respective phenotypes under comparable conditions and that controlled crosses can be made. Reciprocal parental crosses test whether the first-generation phenotype depends on which line supplies the ovule. F1 selfing tests segregation in the next generation. Testcrosses of F1 plants with the putatively recessive line examine the expected one-to-one distribution. Genotyping or separate progeny tests can help distinguish the hidden dominant-class genotypes. These steps follow a causal sequence rather than assuming the conclusion from a visually striking F1 phenotype.

A control is an observation or experimental condition that makes an alternative interpretation less plausible. In a controlled breeding design, selfed parental lines grown under the same conditions can establish whether the original phenotypes remain stable. Carefully documented pollination and checks of parentage reduce the possibility of accidental pollen contamination. Recording the number of ovules, developing seeds and surviving seedlings can reveal losses between conception and phenotypic scoring. If phenotype scoring depends on maturity, plants should be compared at an equivalent developmental stage. Blinded scoring, where practicable, reduces the chance that knowledge of the expected category influences classification. These are general principles of experimental inference rather than specialised laboratory instructions.

Distinguish a biological replicate from repeated measurements of the same individual. Scoring the same seed twice may assess measurement consistency, but it does not create two independent fertilisation outcomes. Seeds from different flowers or plants can provide additional observations, although shared parental environment or biological dependence can affect how those observations should be analysed. The simple probability examples assume independent fertilisation outcomes with constant probabilities. A real experiment must examine whether that assumption is reasonable for its design. Increasing the number of measurements of one individual cannot compensate for a lack of independently informative breeding outcomes.

An investigator may encounter an unexpected class that does not fit either parental phenotype. Before proposing a new inheritance mechanism, examine whether the scoring categories were adequate. An intermediate heterozygote phenotype, an age-dependent character, a tissue-specific trait or a variable environmental effect may require a more detailed phenotype map. A new or unusual class can be scientifically informative, but it must first be demonstrated to be reproducible rather than a product of inconsistent scoring. The appropriate analysis may involve comparing genotypes with measured biochemical or morphological values, rather than forcing every observation into two categories. The principle is to preserve observations while allowing the model to be revised.

Historical evidence and illustrative datasets must be distinguished. The figures of 5,474 and 1,850 are recorded in Mendel's published study. The example with two twelve-seed families is a constructed teaching example intended to clarify sampling and pooling. It is not an experimental report. Modern studies of the molecular basis of seed wrinkling supply another kind of evidence, connecting the r locus to starch-branching enzyme function. Those findings were not available to Mendel when he inferred the transmission rules. Understanding the chronology helps a student appreciate how a predictive model can precede direct identification of its molecular mechanism.

When interpreting data in a Part C question, begin by identifying the competing hypotheses and their distinct expected results. If the unknown parent could be RR or Rr, a testcross provides sharply different expectations. If ordinary transmission and viability differences are both possible, a gamete genotype assay and a comparison of early embryos with mature descendants may separate them. If reciprocal crosses differ, a change of parental role may have affected inheritance or expression. A simple numerical ratio is most useful when placed in this broader evidence framework. The aim is not to find a memorised ratio that resembles the numbers most closely, but to infer which mechanism can account for the data and which observations would discriminate among remaining alternatives.

11. The assumptions and limits of a Mendelian prediction

The simple monohybrid prediction describes a particular model: two alleles at one ordinary diploid nuclear locus, unbiased allele transmission, one allele supplied by each gamete, independent union of the sampled gametes, comparable representation of genotype classes at the stage scored and a consistent phenotype map. The 1:2:1 genotype ratio requires the relevant transmission and fertilisation assumptions. The 3:1 phenotype ratio additionally requires complete dominance and the specified relationship between genotype and survival or scoring. Separating these conditions helps diagnose deviations. The observation that an F2 does not display a three-to-one ratio does not automatically show that Mendel's law of segregation has failed.

A difference in phenotype expression may change observed categories without changing the genotypes. Suppose all three genotypes are present in a one-to-two-to-one ratio, but Rr individuals exhibit an intermediate phenotype. Three distinct phenotypic classes may now be observed instead of two. The underlying genotype ratio is still compatible with ordinary segregation. A direct genotype assay would confirm that the difference lies in the phenotype mapping rather than gamete transmission. Similarly, if some individuals with the same genotype fail to show an expected phenotype, the observed classes need not match the prediction from a fully penetrant model. Incomplete dominance and penetrance are treated in detail in later nodes; their introduction here establishes the logical distinction.

A difference in viability acts at another stage. Consider a hypothetical genotype rr whose early embryos are produced at the expected frequency but whose relative probability of surviving to adult scoring is one half that of RR and Rr. The initial conception probabilities are 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. Weight these by the relative survival values 1, 1 and 1/2. The unnormalised frequencies are 1/4, 1/2 and 1/8. Their sum is 7/8. Dividing by this sum gives survivor genotype probabilities 2/7, 4/7 and 1/7. Under complete dominance the expected adult phenotype ratio becomes 6 round to 1 wrinkled. The calculation does not change the assumed segregation of alleles at conception. It changes the distribution among survivors.

This example illustrates a general principle of conditioning: one divides by the probability of meeting the inclusion criterion. The early genotype distribution describes all conceptions. The adult distribution describes only conceptions that survive. The survival factors change the relative contribution of the categories to that restricted sample. In the special case where a genotype has no survival to the scoring stage, its contribution is zero and the remaining categories are normalised to sum to one. If every genotype has the same survival probability, the common factor cancels, and the relative genotype proportions among survivors remain unchanged. In an examination, a stated reduction in survival should therefore be applied as a weight, not arbitrarily subtracted from the Mendelian ratio.

Transmission bias changes the probabilities at an earlier stage. Suppose a heterozygous pollen donor transmits R with probability 0.6 and r with probability 0.4, while an Rr ovule donor transmits each allele with probability 0.5. In a hypothetical experiment with independent union of these contributions, P(RR) = 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.30, P(rr) = 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.20 and P(Rr) = 0.5 × 0.4 + 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.50. The round fraction under complete dominance is 0.80 and the wrinkled fraction is 0.20. The altered prediction follows from the supplied gamete frequencies. It should not be attributed to dominance merely because R is the more frequently transmitted allele in this example.

If a distorted F2 ratio were observed without direct information about the gamete probabilities, more than one explanation could fit it. For example, a reduced frequency of wrinkled adults could arise because r-bearing gametes contribute less frequently to fertilisation, because rr embryos survive less well, or because some rr individuals are classified as round under the scoring conditions. These alternatives make different predictions for genotypes measured in functional gametes, early embryos and mature plants. An experiment that samples only mature morphology may not distinguish them. A well-designed follow-up collects information at a stage at which the alternatives differ, rather than applying a more complicated label to the same limited observations.

Reciprocal experiments can be informative in such situations. A transmission bias confined to the pollen-side contribution would produce a different testcross result when the heterozygote serves as the pollen donor than when it supplies the ovules, assuming the other processes are comparable. A difference could also result from the developmental origin of the scored tissue or a parent-of-origin expression effect. Therefore reciprocal asymmetry is a clue to direction-dependent biology, not a diagnosis of one unique mechanism. Its interpretation depends on genetic and developmental information, including the nature of the phenotype being measured. The study of sex-linked and extrachromosomal inheritance later in this subunit builds on this distinction.

A further complication is that a locus may not fit the assumptions of a simple two-allele diploid model. An individual could carry a different number of chromosome sets or an altered chromosome complement, or a population could contain more than two allele variants. In many such circumstances the gametic distributions and phenotype categories require additional specification. The foundational cross calculations cannot simply be transferred to every organism without checking its life cycle and chromosome complement. A haploid organism, for example, ordinarily possesses one allele at a single-copy nuclear locus, so a diploid dominance relationship cannot be observed within its haploid state in the same way. The terminology of homozygosity and heterozygosity also has to be used in relation to the organism's genetic organisation.

Environmental effects need not imply that the genotype has changed. Consider a hypothetical plant-height locus for which a particular allele combination is associated with greater elongation under favourable conditions. Severe nutrient limitation may reduce elongation in all genotypes so that the original phenotypic distinction becomes difficult to score. The genotype distributions produced by the cross may remain Mendelian while the height distributions overlap. This is why an experiment should define the environmental conditions and the scoring criterion. Environmental sensitivity is a property of the biological phenotype and may affect interpretation, but it is not by itself evidence for a change in the chromosomal transmission of alleles.

The term Mendelian should consequently be used with care. At one level it denotes the regular separation and inheritance of nuclear alleles; at another, a textbook Mendelian phenotype ratio may refer to a specific expression relationship plus ordinary survival and sampling. A student should specify which aspect is under discussion. A codominant locus can segregate in a perfectly Mendelian fashion despite not yielding a three-to-one phenotype ratio. A transmission-distorting locus may deviate from equal gametic representation even if heterozygotes exhibit complete dominance. Recognising these distinct possibilities prepares the learner to interpret later chapters on extensions of inheritance without discarding the useful foundations established here.

The analytical habit to carry forward is to separate genotype, transmission, fertilisation, development, phenotype and sampling. They form a causal sequence, and each has its own assumptions and possible sources of deviation. Once that sequence has been established for one locus, the next step is to ask how the behaviour of one locus relates to that of another. Meiotic orientation, independent assortment and linkage will make that question quantitative. The current chapter ends with a deliberately single-locus model because the meaning of multi-locus ratios is clearest when the student already knows exactly how one-locus ratios arise.

  2. The observed dominant fraction is 0.60; sampling and alternative mechanisms must be evaluated.
  3. The unknown is definitely RR.
  4. The recessive allele is never transmitted.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. The observed dominant fraction is 0.60; sampling and alternative mechanisms must be evaluated.

The sample proportions are 0.60 and 0.40. A finite deviation alone does not prove distortion or exclude sampling variation; wrinkled offspring demonstrate r transmission.

45. CSIR-NET Part C

In a hypothetical fully penetrant cross, 40 RR, 80 Rr and 40 rr zygotes form. Half of rr and all other embryos survive. What is the adult round:wrinkled ratio?

  1. 3:1
  2. 6:1
  3. 2:1
  4. 4:1
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. 6:1

Round survivors total 120; wrinkled survivors total 20. The ratio is 6:1, illustrating how viability can modify phenotype ratios without modifying conception ratios.

46. CSIR-NET Part C

  2. Normal allele and genotype transmission measured directly, but genotype-associated phenotype differences

Correct answer: B. Normal allele and genotype transmission measured directly, but genotype-associated phenotype differences

Genotyping separates transmission from expression. A phenotype difference despite ordinary transmission directs the analysis toward phenotype mapping rather than gametic bias.

Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. Every heterozygote must show complete dominance.

Segregation is a transmission principle. Complete dominance is a separate expression relationship, and not every locus exhibits it.

Revision and mastery

Conceptual summary

Mendel used stable contrasting lines, controlled crosses and multigeneration progeny observations to infer persistent hereditary factors. Modern chromosome theory explains ordinary allele segregation through homologous chromosome and chromatid behaviour. A heterozygote can transmit either allele even when its visible phenotype is dominant. Separate parental genotypes, gamete probabilities, zygotic genotype classes, viability and phenotype mapping before comparing observations with predictions.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Dominance means stronger transmission.

Correct concept: Dominance is defined by heterozygote expression, not gamete probability.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Students apply the visible 3:1 ratio to gametes; derive gametic probabilities before grouping phenotypes.

2. A recessive allele disappears in the F1.

Correct concept: It persists and can enter gametes.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Visible absence is mistaken for genetic absence; trace the allele copies through F2.

3. A dominant phenotype identifies RR.

Correct concept: RR and Rr can look identical.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Expression is mistaken for genotype; use the tester or a genotype assay.

4. A 3:1 ratio is the genotype ratio.

Correct concept: The underlying ordinary ratio is 1:2:1.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Heterozygotes are grouped with RR in phenotype scoring; label ratios explicitly.

5. One in four offspring must be recessive in every set of four.

Correct concept: One quarter is an expected probability.

Reasoning and exam strategy: A Punnett square is interpreted as an allocation schedule; distinguish finite data from expectations.

6. A testcross with four dominant offspring proves RR.

Correct concept: Rr can yield that sample with probability 1/16.

Reasoning and exam strategy: An absent class is treated as impossible; assess sample size and conditional likelihood.

7. Every testcross is a backcross.

Correct concept: A tester need not belong to either original parental line.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Both cross types are taught together; check experimental purpose and pedigree.

8. Every backcross uses a recessive tester.

Correct concept: A hybrid can be crossed to either parental line.

Reasoning and exam strategy: The recessive-parent example is overgeneralised; examine the recurrent parent's genotype.

9. A replicated diploid cell becomes tetraploid.

Correct concept: Replication doubles DNA content without doubling chromosome sets.

Reasoning and exam strategy: DNA copy number and ploidy are confused; count homologous sets.

10. Homologues and sisters are the same.

Correct concept: Homologues carry corresponding loci; sisters arise by replication.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Chromosome diagrams look similar; identify replication and homologous pairing.

11. A heterozygote must segregate its focal alleles at meiosis I in every tetrad.

Correct concept: Locus–centromere recombination can cause second-division segregation while preserving 2:2 overall.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Simplified drawings omit exchange; trace the allelic chromatids.

12. Dominant means common, ancestral or beneficial.

Correct concept: Dominance does not establish population frequency, ancestry or fitness.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Everyday language suggests superiority; separate expression from population history.

13. Recessive alleles are always unexpressed at the molecular level.

Correct concept: They may produce RNA or protein without determining the gross phenotype.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Phenotype masking is confused with complete molecular silence; specify the assay.

14. All dominant F2 plants have a 1/2 probability of heterozygosity.

Correct concept: Conditioning on dominant phenotype yields 2/3.

Reasoning and exam strategy: The original F2 sample space is retained incorrectly; divide by 3/4.

15. A distorted phenotype ratio proves distorted allele segregation.

Correct concept: Expression, survival, sampling and transmission can all affect the observed ratio.

Reasoning and exam strategy: The final phenotype is treated as a direct gamete assay; compare stages.

16. A seed coat necessarily has the embryo genotype.

Correct concept: Seed coat tissue is maternally derived.

Reasoning and exam strategy: A seed is treated as one genetic tissue; identify developmental origin.

17. A true-breeding line is homozygous across every locus.

Correct concept: True breeding is defined for the character under study.

Reasoning and exam strategy: Focal homozygosity is extrapolated to the whole genome; restrict the inference.
