1. Encounters between hosts and microorganisms

Every multicellular organism occupies a world filled with other living organisms and biological particles. The outside of an animal is in contact with its surroundings, but so are the surfaces lining its digestive and respiratory systems. A leaf is similarly exposed to air, water, dust and microorganisms. In all of these situations, contact between an organism and a microorganism is ordinary, whereas infection is a particular biological outcome. An encounter might end when a particle is carried away, when a microorganism dies in an unsuitable environment, or when two organisms establish an association that does not injure either participant. The central question of host–pathogen interaction is therefore not simply which microorganisms are present. It is which relationships become established, where they occur, and how those relationships change the function of living tissues.

A host is an organism, or in a narrower cellular discussion a cell, that provides an environment in which another biological entity may reside or perform part of its life cycle. The word host does not itself imply disease. A bacterium normally residing on an animal's skin and a virus reproducing within an epithelial cell both use a host environment, although the nature of that dependence is very different. A pathogen is an infectious biological agent capable of producing disease in a susceptible host under appropriate circumstances. The qualification is important because the same microbial species may be harmless in one association but damaging in another. Susceptibility refers to the capacity of a host or cell to acquire an infection; it should not automatically be equated with the severity of a disease that might follow.

Microbial associations form a continuum rather than a simple division into good and bad organisms. In mutualism, both partners derive a benefit; in commensalism, one partner benefits and the other experiences no appreciable benefit or harm in the context being discussed. Parasitism involves a benefit to one partner at the expense of another. These terms describe relationships, not immutable properties of species. A microorganism that ordinarily resides without producing disease may become harmful when transferred into an inappropriate anatomical compartment or when a host loses an important protective function. An opportunistic pathogen is an agent that takes advantage of such changes in circumstances. Recognizing an opportunity as a change in the interaction helps explain why illness may develop without a newly introduced microbial species.

A useful way to begin this chapter is to distinguish presence from activity. Suppose a biological sample contains a molecular marker associated with a microorganism. That observation demonstrates that the marker is present in the sample, assuming the assay is specific and valid. It does not necessarily demonstrate that an intact, viable microorganism is present, that it has multiplied in the host, or that it has caused the symptoms that motivated sampling. Each of those is a separate proposition requiring appropriate evidence. A fragment of microbial nucleic acid can remain after an organism has been eliminated, and an intact organism may be present at a body surface without causing disease. This distinction between detection and causal explanation will recur throughout the chapter.

The simplest mental model of infection is a sequence of transitions. An agent first encounters a host, reaches an accessible site, remains associated with that site, establishes a population or replicative process, and may eventually alter tissue function. These transitions are not always separate in time. For a virus, attachment and penetration may occur in rapid succession at the cellular scale, whereas the interval between cellular infection and noticeable tissue damage may be much longer. For an extracellular bacterial association, there may be no penetration into the host-cell cytoplasm at all. A sequence is useful because it separates distinct biological questions; it becomes misleading only if it is treated as an obligatory itinerary followed by every infectious agent.

The size of the entity matters for the way we describe its relationship with the host. A bacterium is a cell, with its own plasma membrane, ribosomes and metabolic capacities, although its degree of dependence on host-derived nutrients varies. A virus is not a cell and lacks an independent translation apparatus; production of progeny virus depends on a suitable living cell. Consequently, a bacterial population can exist outside host cells and still be associated with an infection, while viral replication requires intracellular activity. Both agents may be detected outside cells during particular stages of their life cycles. The presence of an extracellular virion does not conflict with the intracellular requirement for viral reproduction.

Different scales of observation also yield different descriptions of infection. At the molecular scale one may study a host surface molecule and its interacting partner. At the cellular scale one may ask whether the agent is bound outside a cell or has crossed a membrane. At the tissue scale the concern may be the distribution of affected cells, and at the organismal scale one may ask whether the host retains ordinary physiological function. An accurate explanation connects these levels instead of treating them as interchangeable. A change affecting a surface molecule can have consequences at several levels, but the consequences depend on the anatomical location and physiological role of the cells carrying that molecule.

The host is not a passive container. It has physical boundaries, organized cellular surfaces, resident microbial populations and regulatory systems that maintain physiological conditions. A microorganism is likewise not simply a name on a list; it has structural and functional properties that determine which surroundings are compatible with its activities. Even when an agent possesses a property necessary for one stage of an interaction, that property need not be sufficient for the entire sequence. The same reasoning is familiar from cell signaling: binding of a ligand may be necessary for a response, but the downstream signaling machinery must also be available. The present chapter applies this necessary-versus-sufficient distinction to recognition, entry and subsequent infection.

An important consequence is that disease should not be defined by the mere identity of a microorganism. Disease is a disturbance of normal biological structure or function. Signs and symptoms are observations associated with that disturbance, but a detectable change may occur before symptoms become obvious. Some infected hosts remain asymptomatic; some illnesses involve damage in tissues where the infectious agent itself is scarce or absent. Those possibilities require careful separation of the site of the agent, the site of its activity, and the site of physiological injury. This provides the conceptual foundation for comparing animal and plant infections without reducing either to a catalogue of diseases.

The distinction between a resident population and a damaging association is illustrated by bacteria of the genus Bacteroides, a familiar component of the large-intestinal microbiota. Their presence in that compartment is compatible with normal digestive physiology. Bacteria associated with the intestine can nevertheless contribute to serious injury if the integrity of the intestinal wall is lost and internal tissue compartments are affected. The conceptual explanation is not that a resident bacterium suddenly ceases to be bacterial, nor that the intestine is normally sterile. The explanation concerns a different anatomical and physiological relationship to the host. In an examination question, the distinction between the initial site of residence and the site of subsequent damage is more informative than a label applied to the microbial genus in isolation.

2. From association to causal evidence

The modern explanation of infectious disease developed gradually as investigators learned to distinguish observations from causal relationships. Before microorganisms could be studied reliably, a recurring association between a particular environment and a disease could suggest a connection without identifying a causal biological agent. Improved microscopy made previously invisible organisms observable. However, visibility created another difficulty: microorganisms might be observed both in diseased material and in apparently healthy material. Their presence alone did not show whether they were responsible for the disease or had accumulated as a consequence of it. Establishing causation required observations that distinguished those explanations.

The work associated with Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch during the nineteenth century helped establish the microbial basis of many infectious diseases. The significance of Koch's classical postulates was their attempt to connect a specific biological agent with a reproducible disease rather than relying on a single microscopic observation. In their traditional formulation, the proposed agent should be associated with disease, be isolated and studied independently, reproduce the disease in a suitable susceptible host, and be recovered from the newly affected host. These are historical principles of causal reasoning, not a universal description of how every infectious disease is investigated today. Their strength lies in asking whether the proposed cause is consistently associated with the outcome and whether evidence can separate that cause from accompanying circumstances.

Several limitations became apparent as microbiology developed. Some infectious agents cannot reproduce independently of living cells and therefore do not satisfy a requirement for growth in a cell-free medium. An organism may be recovered from a healthy carrier, invalidating a literal interpretation that the agent must be absent from all healthy hosts. Experimental reproduction of illness in people would raise fundamental ethical problems, and a convenient animal model may not reproduce the physiology of the natural host. Infections involving more than one microorganism also resist a simple one-agent explanation. These limitations do not negate the importance of causal evidence; instead, they require evidence appropriate to the biology of the system.

Plant virology offers an instructive example of the need to reconsider assumptions about infectious agents. In the late nineteenth century, Dmitri Ivanovsky and Martinus Beijerinck studied the transmissible disease of tobacco that came to be associated with tobacco mosaic virus. Material capable of transmitting disease could pass through filters that retained ordinary bacteria. The observation challenged the assumption that every infectious agent was an independently growing bacterium. Later studies established the particulate and molecular nature of viruses. For our purposes, the historical lesson is that a biological phenomenon must be interpreted in the light of observations, not forced into a pre-existing classification simply because that classification had explained earlier diseases.

The development of cell biology added an important level to causal explanation. Instead of asking only why one organism became diseased, researchers could ask why only some cells within an organism were affected. The isolation of cells and the investigation of cellular membranes, receptors and intracellular processes revealed that the host actively determines the biological setting of infection. A tissue-specific pattern that was previously descriptive could be analyzed in terms of accessible cellular surfaces and differences in cellular function. That development provided a direct intellectual link between host–pathogen interaction and the preceding chapters on communication between cells.

A further conceptual development concerned the origin of damage. Classical descriptions often emphasized properties of the microorganism. Yet an interaction involves at least two biological participants. The damage-response framework developed by Arturo Casadevall and Liise-anne Pirofski, published in 2003, explicitly treated host damage as an outcome of host–microorganism interactions. It accommodates damage associated with insufficient protection, damage associated with an excessively harmful host response and damage due to microbial activity itself. The framework is a conceptual model rather than an assertion that all infections follow the same damage-versus-response curve.

For a student, these developments suggest three distinct questions to ask whenever presented with evidence about infection. First, what agent or microbial component has been identified? Second, what biological process connects that agent to a host compartment or cellular response? Third, what observation establishes the disturbance of function that is attributed to the interaction? A data set answering one of these questions need not answer the other two. For instance, a molecular marker can identify an agent without showing the site of damage, and a tissue lesion can establish injury without identifying a particular causal agent. The strongest mechanistic interpretations link complementary observations across these levels.

Evidence is not strengthened merely by using more technical vocabulary. A classical observation may be decisive if it discriminates between rival explanations, whereas a technologically sophisticated measurement may be uninformative if it does not distinguish attachment from entry or association from causation. An examination question may present an apparently persuasive correlation between two variables and then ask what additional conclusion is justified. The appropriate response is to identify exactly what was measured, identify plausible alternative interpretations, and avoid claiming evidence for stages that were never observed.

The classical history of tobacco mosaic disease also illustrates a distinction between the observation of transmissibility and an explanation of the infectious entity. A filterable agent was a surprising finding in the context of contemporary bacteriology, but filterability alone could not reveal the organization of a virus particle or explain how the agent depended on plant cells. Those later questions required different kinds of evidence. This progression is characteristic of science: a landmark result can constrain a model without supplying every part of its replacement. It is therefore important to state the observation that supported a historical advance and not attribute later structural knowledge to the earliest discovery. The plant-virus example supplies a direct bridge between causal microbiology and the molecular recognition and entry questions that follow in this subunit.

3. Exposure, attachment, colonization, infection and disease

Exposure is the opportunity for contact between a potential infectious agent and a host. It is the broadest stage in the conceptual sequence because it does not require a stable association. When an organism is exposed, the agent may be carried past a relevant body surface, remain in an external compartment, or be removed before meaningful cellular contact occurs. The outcome is not determined by exposure alone. One must ask whether a suitable anatomical site is accessible and whether the agent is still biologically competent at the time of contact. Exposure and infection therefore belong to different categories of evidence.

Attachment, or adhesion, means association with a surface that persists beyond the initial physical encounter. For a cellular microorganism this may involve several components of its external structure interacting with substances on a host tissue. For a virus the comparable first step is often called adsorption or attachment to an accessible host-cell surface. The word adsorption in this context denotes association at an interface; it does not mean absorption into a tissue or passage into the cell. Attachment can be reversible, and an attached entity can subsequently detach or be removed. A photograph showing objects at a cellular surface cannot on its own establish internalization.

Colonization refers to establishment of an association with a host site, commonly involving persistence or multiplication of cellular microorganisms on a normally exposed surface. The exact operational definition depends on the biological system and clinical context, but it is particularly useful when an organism resides at a site without producing overt disease. Colonization is more informative than a single detection event because it implies an established local relationship. It does not imply that the organism has invaded the cytoplasm of neighboring cells. A host-associated bacterial population on a mucosal surface illustrates the spatial distinction: the bacteria can be in close contact with epithelial cells while remaining extracellular.

Infection generally refers to establishment of an infectious agent within a host, involving its biological activity or replication in the relevant context. Definitions vary somewhat between microbiology and clinical usage; the crucial distinction here is that infection need not entail symptomatic disease. Viral infection is commonly discussed at the cell level, where entry and subsequent biological activity are considered. Bacterial infection is often described at the tissue or organismal level, including extracellular infections. The term cannot be equated in all cases with penetration of a host-cell membrane. Indeed, the choice between cell-level and tissue-level language changes how a statement about infection should be interpreted.

Invasion is passage beyond a defined boundary. A microorganism may invade a tissue by crossing a surface epithelium, or it may invade an individual cell by crossing that cell's plasma membrane. These are distinct spatial transitions. The term should always prompt the question: which boundary has been crossed? A microorganism located between cells in a tissue can be invasive at the tissue level yet remain extracellular at the cellular level. Conversely, an intracellular agent in one superficial cell may not yet have disseminated through the surrounding tissue. Correct classification therefore requires explicit identification of the spatial compartment.

Establishment after entry is a separate issue. A host cell or extracellular niche may not provide the conditions needed for sustained biological activity. A cell can bind an agent and internalize it without subsequently supporting reproduction. An initial microbial association may disappear when the host environment changes or the agent is cleared. Survival, persistence and production of new infectious entities are therefore not automatic consequences of reaching a host compartment. In the case of viruses, productive infection refers to an infection resulting in the formation of progeny virus, while an abortive infection fails to complete that outcome. Entry alone is not proof of productive infection.

Dissemination refers to spread beyond the initial site. Spread may be restricted to neighboring cells, extend through nearby tissue, or involve transport to distant sites. The adjective local describes confinement to a limited anatomical region; systemic describes a much broader distribution, often involving distant parts of an organism. A systemic physiological response does not by itself establish systemic dissemination of the agent. For example, a change in whole-organism physiology could result from a soluble product or a host signaling molecule arising at a localized site. To classify the spread of infection, evidence must concern the location of the agent or its relevant biological activity, rather than the location of every observed consequence.

Disease is the outcome in which normal function or structure is disturbed. Injury can result from the agent, its products, the host response, or a combination of these. Some infection states produce little detectable injury. Other states may produce progressive functional disturbance. Therefore, a diagram showing exposure, adhesion, colonization, invasion and disease should contain branches, not just a single uninterrupted arrow. Clearance and non-damaging persistence are biologically meaningful alternatives. Some routes also bypass steps that are characteristic of different agents; for example, an intracellular viral replication cycle should not be forced into a bacterial surface-colonization model.

Time adds another independent dimension. The incubation period is the interval between infection or exposure, as operationally defined in a given setting, and the appearance of clinical disease manifestations. It is not a synonym for the duration of attachment, the duration of intracellular replication, or the period during which an agent can be transmitted. A symptom-free interval may contain substantial biological activity. Likewise, persistent detection may outlast a particular functional disturbance. Distinguishing the time of an event from the time at which it becomes observable is central to interpreting an infection time course.

The most useful examination approach is to ask which transition is directly supported by a stated observation. Detecting an agent at an exposed site supports presence at that site. Evidence that its association persists can support colonization in the appropriate context. Intracellular localization provides evidence about entry but not necessarily replication. A change in normal tissue function demonstrates injury but does not automatically establish the distribution of the causative agent. Combining these observations creates a more complete explanation than treating all positive findings as equivalent indications of infection.

4. Animal surfaces and the anatomy of access

An animal's plasma membranes are not all equally accessible to its surroundings. Many cells are enclosed within tissues, while others form the boundaries across which exchange with the environment takes place. An epithelial tissue is a closely organized layer of cells that separates compartments. It can be a single layer, as in many absorptive surfaces, or consist of multiple layers, as in the protective epidermis. Epithelia combine physical separation with specialized transport and secretion. An agent encountered at an animal's surface must therefore be considered in relation to the architecture of that particular surface. A cell deep in an organ may carry a molecule compatible with microbial binding but remain inaccessible during an ordinary surface encounter.

The skin illustrates the value of structural protection. Its outer epidermal layer provides a mechanically resistant interface that restricts entry into underlying living tissues. The cells forming the surface have differentiated to serve this protective role, and the organization of layers contributes to the barrier as a whole. Descriptions that emphasize a single cell membrane overlook the fact that an external agent initially encounters the complete structure, not simply a naked living cell. Normal surface-associated microorganisms are compatible with that arrangement. Their presence on the exterior does not imply that the barrier has ceased to protect the interior.

A mucosal surface faces a different problem. A digestive surface must exchange nutrients and water with material in the lumen, and a respiratory surface must participate in gas exchange. Such tissues cannot simply be constructed as uniformly thick, impermeable walls. Instead, their barrier properties are coupled to cellular organization and secreted material. Mucus forms a hydrated layer that can separate a microorganism from the plasma membrane beneath it. It contains macromolecules whose interactions with particles affect how readily those particles reach epithelial cells. The presence of mucus should not be interpreted as evidence that the underlying epithelium itself has been penetrated.

In the respiratory system, coordinated ciliary movement helps transport mucus and entrapped material. This is a form of mechanical clearance: the surface can remove material without requiring each particle to be individually identified and destroyed. The concept is important because attachment at a living interface is not a simple equilibrium between two stationary molecules. The surface and surrounding fluid are moving. An association observed on an isolated surface may not persist in a tissue that continually removes material. Ciliary activity, mucus turnover and epithelial renewal are examples of host processes that change the opportunity for an encounter to develop into a longer-lasting interaction.

The digestive tract provides a second example of regional specialization. Its different regions have distinct physical and chemical conditions, and its luminal environment differs from the internal milieu beyond the epithelium. The composition of secretions, the chemical environment and the presence of resident microorganisms all contribute to the relationship between the host and microbes at the surface. A microorganism compatible with a particular digestive compartment does not thereby have unrestricted access to underlying tissues. It is misleading to refer to all parts of an animal as though they represent one homogeneous habitat. Even neighboring regions may differ in the cellular and extracellular features that determine whether an association persists.

Cell polarity further refines the concept of accessibility. An epithelial cell has an apical surface directed toward a lumen or external compartment and a basolateral surface oriented toward adjacent cells and underlying tissue. These membrane domains contain different complements of proteins and lipids. A receptor that participates in an association at one membrane domain may be absent or inaccessible at another. A measurement of total receptor abundance in a tissue therefore does not show that the receptor is exposed to an agent arriving at the luminal surface. The distinction follows directly from cellular organization studied in Unit 2 and cell communication studied in 4.B.

Epithelial junctions help maintain both the barrier and the distinction between membrane domains. Tight junctions restrict passage through the space between adjacent cells and contribute to the maintenance of polarity. Adherens junctions and other adhesive structures help maintain tissue organization. A molecule in the lateral membrane may be structurally appropriate for interaction with a microbial ligand but separated from the exposed environment by the arrangement of neighboring cells. Tissue architecture is thus an independent explanation for differences between observations in an intact tissue and observations made with individual cells removed from that tissue.

A protective surface is not simply a physical obstacle. It also supports chemical and ecological mechanisms. Secreted substances can interact with microorganisms, and resident communities can occupy sites and consume available resources. Some constituents of normal microbiota contribute to colonization resistance, meaning the capacity of the resident community and associated host environment to oppose establishment of another organism. This is not a statement that every member of a resident community is protective or that a particular organism is always harmless. It describes an emergent property of the local biological setting.

A change in anatomical containment can alter the significance of an existing microbial population. Bacteria that are compatible with the conditions in one compartment may cause injury when located in a different compartment. The difference does not require a new definition of the bacterium's chemical composition. It follows from a changed relationship to tissues that have different functions and protective mechanisms. This is a valuable model for opportunistic infection because it separates the properties of the organism from the context in which those properties are expressed.

A common interpretive mistake is to infer that any disruption of a barrier must produce infection. Barrier impairment changes the set of possible interactions, but other requirements still apply. An encountered agent may be incompatible with the local cells, may fail to remain at the site or may be eliminated. Barrier integrity is consequently one variable within a larger causal system. Likewise, the observation of infection at a particular site does not establish which barrier function was responsible for its initial establishment. Different biological mechanisms can produce superficially similar outcomes.

The animal surface also has functional constraints imposed by physiology. A respiratory epithelium must remain capable of exchanging gases, and an intestinal epithelium must support absorption while maintaining separation of luminal contents from internal tissues. A protective change that compromises the function of the surface can itself be harmful. The benefit of a barrier is not measured by impermeability alone but by its ability to maintain useful exchange while limiting unwanted interactions. This explains why a living host regulates surface conditions rather than constructing a uniform passive shell.

To reason about a tissue-level observation, first determine the direction of the encounter. Is the agent on the exterior, in a lumen, between cells, in the extracellular matrix or within the cytoplasm of an individual cell? Then identify which evidence distinguishes these possibilities. A stain showing a signal next to a membrane has different implications from a signal enclosed within a membrane-bounded cellular region. A tissue section showing a microorganism beneath the surface demonstrates a different spatial relationship from a surface sample. The interpretation must respect what the measurement actually resolves.

The respiratory epithelium is a useful example of a protective process whose physiological role can be studied independently of any particular pathogen. In healthy conducting airways the movement of cilia contributes to the transport of mucus. Disorders of motile cilia can impair that clearance and are associated with recurrent respiratory problems. This relationship shows why an anatomical barrier includes dynamic physiological functions, not merely the existence of a layer of cells. It does not imply that every person with impaired clearance experiences the same infection or that ciliary impairment determines which molecular receptor is present. An examination question may ask which property of the barrier is affected in this situation. The answer concerns transport of surface material, rather than a universal loss of all epithelial or immune functions.

5. Plant surfaces, cell walls and extracellular compartments

Plants confront the same general problem of maintaining exchange with their surroundings while preserving cellular integrity, but their anatomy creates different relationships between an infectious agent and a living cell. A leaf surface has an outer cuticle over much of its epidermis, and plant cells have walls outside their plasma membranes. The wall provides mechanical support while remaining part of a hydrated extracellular matrix. Thus, reaching the surface of a plant cell wall is not equivalent to reaching the plasma membrane, and reaching the plasma membrane is not equivalent to entering the cytoplasm. These distinctions are particularly important when comparing plant and animal pathogens.

The cuticle contributes to the organization of the aerial plant surface and limits uncontrolled exchange with the environment. It is not the same structure as the cellulose-rich cell wall. The cell wall lies outside the plasma membrane and supports cell shape, whereas the cuticle forms an external covering in relevant epidermal regions. A student should distinguish both structures from the plasma membrane, which regulates exchange between cytoplasm and extracellular surroundings. A drawing that collapses cuticle, wall and membrane into one line cannot accurately represent the possible positions of a microorganism.

A plant also needs openings for physiological functions. Stomata are regulated pores in the epidermis associated with guard cells. Their importance to photosynthesis and transpiration explains why they cannot be considered merely defects in a defensive wall. In the context of an interaction with microorganisms, they represent interfaces whose anatomical and physiological properties matter. The detailed regulation of stomata belongs to plant physiology and the mechanisms of initial plant colonization are developed in 4.D.10. Here the relevant principle is that reaching the interior of a leaf through an opening differs from crossing the plasma membrane of an epidermal or mesophyll cell.

The apoplast is the extracellular continuum of plant cell walls and intercellular spaces, including the associated fluids and gases; in a broader physiological sense it includes nonliving conductive spaces. It lies outside the plasma membranes of living cells. A bacterium in a leaf apoplastic space is therefore inside the plant tissue but outside the cytoplasm of the plant cells surrounding it. This is an important example of why the word inside must be qualified. A location can be internal at the organ scale and extracellular at the cellular scale.

The symplast is a contrasting concept. It consists of the connected cytoplasmic domains of living plant cells, with connections through plasmodesmata. These channels are anatomically distinct from the intercellular extracellular spaces. A plant virus reproduces within a suitable living cell, and subsequent cell-to-cell movement is conceptually different from bacterial multiplication in extracellular spaces. In later nodes, the mechanisms supporting that movement will be studied explicitly. At present it is enough to understand that the two lifestyles occupy different spatial compartments and consequently impose different requirements on the infectious agent.

A comparison with an animal epithelium makes the point clearer. Both systems have cellular plasma membranes and both maintain organized extracellular environments. The plant, however, adds a rigid cell wall around each cell and often a cuticular layer at the tissue surface. Many animal viruses attach to accessible surface molecules on a host-cell membrane before cellular entry. For many plant viruses the initial introduction of viral material into a living cell involves a physical discontinuity in protective structures, often associated with natural transmission processes. It would be scientifically incorrect to assume that the first step of every plant-virus infection is an ordinary receptor-mediated entry process identical to that of animal viruses.

Other plant-associated microorganisms have different relationships with the wall. Some grow in extracellular tissue compartments, and others develop structures closely associated with living cells. A structure may extend into a position surrounded by the host cell without being freely exposed to its cytoplasm, because the host membrane can remain at the interface. This principle is useful for understanding the later discussion of fungal associations and specialized interfaces. The detailed anatomical and molecular mechanisms are not needed to grasp the foundational distinction between proximity, tissue penetration and cytoplasmic access.

The physiological conditions of a plant vary between organs, developmental stages and cell types. A root and a mature leaf have different structures, exposure histories and resource exchanges. Even a microorganism that can associate with one organ may not establish the same relationship with another. Therefore, the host specificity of a plant-associated agent cannot be inferred solely from the species name of the plant. Tissue organization and cell state matter in addition to the identity of the organism.

A plant disease can be recognized as a disturbance in the function of a particular tissue or in the coordinated performance of the whole plant. A visible alteration in pigmentation, growth or water relations establishes a physiological phenotype, not necessarily the exact location of the causative organism. If two leaves display a similar change in appearance, it does not follow that the same class of agent is responsible. A mechanistic explanation must link the biological activity of the agent to the affected tissue process. This distinction is common to animal and plant systems even though their cellular structures and disease manifestations differ.

At this level, the essential comparison is not a list of named diseases. It is the recognition that both plants and animals possess nested anatomical boundaries and that biological interpretations depend on which boundary is under discussion. A diagram labeling a plant bacterium as intracellular simply because it is located beneath the leaf surface is incorrect. Equally, a diagram placing a reproducing virus permanently outside a living cell omits its fundamental dependence on intracellular machinery. Students should be able to use this spatial framework before learning any pathogen-specific molecular mechanism.

The tobacco mosaic virus example can also be used to clarify the difference between the initial encounter and activity within the plant. As a virus, it requires living cellular machinery to express its genetic information and produce new viral material. Its life cycle cannot be understood as multiplication in the intercellular air spaces of a leaf. By contrast, textbook descriptions of many foliar bacterial diseases include bacterial populations occupying the extracellular apoplast. Neither arrangement should be generalized to every member of its class without further evidence, but the contrast establishes two different biological settings for studying plant infection. The membrane surrounding living cytoplasm is central to the distinction, even when both populations occur in the same broad anatomical region of a leaf.

6. Molecular recognition: ligands, receptors and specificity

Cell surfaces are chemically complex. A plasma membrane contains lipids and proteins, and its external face may carry carbohydrates associated with lipids or proteins. Extracellular matrices and secreted macromolecules supply additional potential binding surfaces. A receptor, in the context of pathogen recognition, is a host molecule that participates in a specific interaction with a component of the infectious agent. The interacting component on the agent can be called a ligand. These terms describe roles in an interaction rather than a requirement that either molecule evolved for that purpose.

A host molecule used as a binding partner may normally function in transport, adhesion, communication or another physiological process unrelated to infection. An agent's interaction with that molecule does not imply that the host synthesized the molecule in order to receive the agent. This is the same conceptual distinction encountered when studying endogenous signals and receptor-binding experiments in cell signaling. A molecule can have its normal biological role and, independently, be involved in a host–microorganism interaction. Its role in infection depends on the particular molecular partner and the context of the encounter.

Binding specificity arises from chemical and structural complementarity. Molecular surfaces differ in shape, charge distribution, hydrogen-bonding opportunities and the arrangement of hydrophobic groups. Interactions may involve several of these features simultaneously. A simple lock-and-key drawing conveys the importance of compatibility but can obscure conformational flexibility and the role of surrounding molecules. Two surfaces need not be completely rigid for a selective association to occur. Recognition is therefore better understood as the formation of a favorable molecular interaction within a particular chemical environment.

The forces involved are ordinarily noncovalent. Hydrogen bonds, electrostatic interactions, van der Waals interactions and the hydrophobic effect can contribute to association. Individual interactions may be modest, but an arrangement of multiple contacts can make the overall association favorable. It is important not to conclude that a noncovalent interaction is necessarily short-lived or biologically unimportant. The biological significance of an association depends on the combined interaction, its kinetics and the other processes operating at the interface.

The terms affinity and avidity refer to related but distinguishable concepts. Affinity describes the strength of a particular binding interaction, often expressed through an equilibrium constant for a defined molecular pair. Avidity refers to the combined functional strength of multiple simultaneous or repeated contacts in a multivalent interaction. A surface carrying many binding components may make several contacts with another surface. A single-contact affinity measured in solution therefore cannot automatically predict the stability of the complete surface association. The geometry and availability of the components also matter.

Receptor abundance is not the same as receptor accessibility. A host cell may produce a protein that is retained inside a cellular compartment, expressed at a membrane surface facing away from the encounter, or masked by other extracellular material. A measurement of messenger RNA does not directly establish the amount of accessible surface protein, and total protein measurements do not necessarily establish its localization. A question that supplies only a tissue expression value leaves open the possibility that the relevant molecule cannot participate in a surface encounter.

A receptor's presence is not automatically sufficient to establish a productive infection. Binding may be followed by a cellular transition that requires additional components, or the agent may encounter an intracellular environment that does not support the next stage. A surface molecule participating in the first step is therefore one part of a causal sequence. It is especially important to distinguish a molecule shown to bind an agent from one independently established to be functionally involved in entry. Evidence for physical association cannot by itself specify which subsequent event the molecule controls.

The word recognition is used in more than one direction within host–pathogen biology. From the viewpoint of a microbial particle, recognition can mean molecular compatibility with a host surface. From the viewpoint of the host, recognition can mean detection of microbial features by a protective system. The second meaning was developed in 4.C and will not be repeated as a complete immune-signaling discussion here. Confusing these meanings leads to incorrect conclusions: a host pattern-recognition receptor and an attachment receptor are functional descriptions with different implications, although individual molecules can have multiple roles.

A physical encounter can occur without specific molecular recognition. Nonspecific interactions, diffusion and contact with extracellular material can bring two biological surfaces together. Specific binding then describes a more selective interaction between defined components. The distinction is useful because experimental measurements of total material associated with a cell can include both specific and nonspecific contributions. A measurement showing association must be interpreted in relation to its background and to evidence that identifies the interacting molecular pair.

A host molecule may be shared by several cell types, but the surrounding extracellular environment and later cellular events can differ. The consequences of recognition therefore vary even when an individual binding interaction is similar. This observation connects molecular chemistry to tissue tropism. Selectivity measured at the molecular level is one source of selectivity at the cellular level, but it does not exhaust the explanations for cellular or tissue distribution. The full interaction involves access, binding, intracellular or extracellular compatibility, and the response of the host environment.

The distinction can be illustrated with a hypothetical cell-surface binding assay. If a labeled noninfectious ligand associates with a cell preparation, one may conclude that the preparation contains a compatible association site under the conditions of the measurement. One cannot conclude that an infectious agent carrying that ligand would complete its life cycle in the intact organism. The assay concerns a limited event and does not reproduce tissue architecture or subsequent biological requirements. This is not a weakness of the assay when the research question concerns binding; it becomes a weakness only when conclusions are extended beyond the event being measured.

The most useful way to connect these ideas to examination reasoning is to separate the molecular claim from the physiological claim. A statement that a host protein interacts with a microbial component concerns molecular recognition. A statement that the interaction is required for cellular entry concerns a causal role at a later stage. A statement that the receptor determines the distribution of disease concerns the organism and requires additional evidence. The three statements may all be correct in a particular system, but they do not follow logically from each other without evidence connecting the intervening stages.

7. Host range, tissue tropism and cellular permissiveness

The term host range describes the kinds of hosts in which an agent can establish a biologically relevant association or infection. In virology it commonly refers to the species in which a virus can infect and reproduce, although a statement about host range should specify the particular outcome measured. A report of viral material in an organism may not establish that the organism is a competent host for productive infection. A host that supports infection also need not display disease of the same severity as another host. The distinction between host range and disease range follows from the difference between establishment and damage introduced earlier.

Tissue tropism describes a preference for particular tissues or cell types within a host. The term is especially familiar in virology but is useful more broadly when organisms preferentially associate with particular cellular or anatomical environments. A tissue preference is a pattern of biological distribution. It requires explanation, and the explanation may be different for different agents. Preferential interaction with a tissue can result from its accessibility, its molecular composition, its physiological state or the compatibility of its cells with subsequent stages of the infectious process. The word tropism is not itself an explanation for the pattern.

Host range and tissue tropism operate at different scales. Two animal species may differ in their susceptibility to an agent, while two tissues within the susceptible species may differ in the extent of infection. Conversely, a comparable cell type in two organisms may have similar cellular properties but differ in how it is organized within the intact host. Species-level and tissue-level comparisons are therefore complementary rather than interchangeable. It is possible for a microorganism to have a broad host range yet show a relatively restricted tissue association in each host, or to have a narrow host range but affect several different tissues in the compatible species.

A useful distinction in cell-based virology is that between susceptibility and permissiveness. A susceptible cell is often defined operationally as a cell capable of admitting a particular virus, while a permissive cell supports the intracellular stages needed for viral replication or productive infection. Authors sometimes use susceptibility more broadly, so an examination answer should follow the stated definitions in the question. When the two terms are used in the narrower sense, they separate two different requirements: the opportunity to establish entry and the availability of an intracellular environment supporting the subsequent biological process.

Consider four logically possible cellular categories. A cell may support both entry and productive activity. It may admit an agent but fail to support its subsequent activity. It may lack an ordinary route of entry while otherwise having cellular components compatible with subsequent stages. Finally, it may lack both types of compatibility. The third category is particularly useful conceptually because it separates an intracellular property from a surface property; however, evidence about the intracellular property must come from a measurement that actually addresses it. The absence of productive infection by itself cannot distinguish these categories.

The first category illustrates why access and compatibility must coincide in ordinary infection. The second illustrates why an intracellular signal alone does not establish a complete infectious cycle. The fourth demonstrates that multiple restrictions can exist simultaneously. An investigator who finds one limiting condition has not necessarily identified every limitation. A student should avoid interpreting a single negative observation as proof that a particular molecular structure is absent, especially when the endpoint integrates several biological transitions.

Differentiation is another contributor to variation among cells. As cells acquire specialized functions, they express different sets of genes, organize their membranes differently and maintain distinct intracellular states. These differences are central to normal tissue function and can also affect the distribution of infection. A marker of cell identity can correlate with an infectious outcome without itself participating in the interaction. This is an important general principle of causal reasoning: a correlate of tissue preference is not automatically the cause of tissue preference. Evidence about molecular interaction and evidence about differentiated cellular function must be evaluated separately.

The anatomical distribution of injury also requires careful interpretation. In an intact organism, the tissue in which a disturbance is most visible may not be identical to the tissue in which an agent is concentrated. A local injury can affect an organ's output and produce consequences at a distant site. Similarly, a host response can extend beyond the immediately affected cells. The tissue tropism of an agent should be described from evidence of its association or biological activity in particular cells, not inferred solely from symptoms or whole-organism physiological changes.

Comparative biology introduces variation within host populations. Different individuals of the same species may have different patterns of gene expression, developmental stages or histories of exposure. They do not all experience identical outcomes. A designation such as susceptible species is consequently a statement about the possibility of infection in that species, not a claim that every individual encounters the agent or becomes infected. Group-level patterns are meaningful only when the biological endpoint and the population being compared are clear.

The idea of specificity should not be confused with absolute exclusivity. Biological binding and cellular responses can differ in degree. A molecule may interact with more than one partner, and an agent may associate with more than one cell type while showing a measurable preference for one. In this situation, a statement about relative tropism requires a comparison with the appropriate denominator. If one tissue contains far more cells of the relevant type than another tissue, the total number of associated cells alone may not reflect a difference in their individual probabilities of association.

A student interpreting a graph of signal in two tissues should therefore ask what the signal represents. Does it indicate total agent-associated material, the fraction of cells affected, the amount of a microbial product, or a physiological disturbance? Is the quantity normalized to tissue mass, cell number or another relevant denominator? These questions change the interpretation of the same numerical values. A graph with a larger absolute signal in one tissue may show a smaller signal per cell if that tissue contains many more cells. The underlying biological question determines which comparison is appropriate.

A useful final distinction is between a restriction and an observation of failure. Failure at an endpoint is a result: for example, an assay may show no evidence of productive activity. A restriction is a mechanistic explanation of that result. Several different restrictions may produce the same failure. The disciplined examination response does not infer a particular restriction from an endpoint alone. It identifies the evidence that would support a specific interpretation, acknowledges uncertainty where appropriate, and connects the observed pattern to the correct spatial scale.

8. Extracellular, intracellular and mixed biological lifestyles

A host-associated microorganism may occupy several different positions during its life cycle. To describe its lifestyle, it is necessary to identify the compartment in which its characteristic biological activities occur. A microorganism that remains outside host cells while growing in a host-associated environment is extracellular with respect to those cells. A microorganism that performs part of its biological activity within host cells has an intracellular phase. These categories describe spatial and functional relationships. They do not, by themselves, describe how serious the resulting disease is.

An extracellular microorganism can affect living cells without entering them. A cell can respond to a soluble molecule that binds its surface, alter transport in response to a change in its environment, or lose normal function when its supporting tissue is disturbed. Such phenomena follow from basic physiology. Intracellular location of the microorganism is not a universal requirement for changes in host-cell behavior. Conversely, evidence of microbial material within cells does not establish that those cells are suffering irreversible damage. Location and functional consequence must be examined independently.

An extracellular environment within an animal includes tissue fluid and spaces associated with extracellular matrices. It is distinct from the cytoplasm of neighboring cells, and it is also distinct from the exterior of the organism. An extracellular population may be separated from the external environment by several anatomical barriers. The statement that a bacterium is extracellular consequently does not mean that it lies outside the host. It means that the bacterium is outside the host cells being considered. In plants, the apoplast supplies a particularly clear example of this difference between organismal location and cellular location.

Intracellular microorganisms or infectious material may be located in different cellular compartments. The interior of a membrane-bounded vesicle is distinct from the cytosol, even though the vesicle itself lies within the cell boundary. An observation that identifies material within a cell does not determine which intracellular compartment contains it unless the measurement has sufficient spatial resolution. The compartment matters because it determines which cellular components are accessible and which cellular processes can act on the material. Later nodes examine these relationships for particular biological examples; here the fundamental point is to avoid treating the cell interior as one chemically and physically uniform space.

The terms obligate and facultative intracellular describe dependence on the intracellular environment rather than a momentary location. An obligate intracellular agent requires a suitable cellular environment for reproduction. Viruses are obligate intracellular replicating entities because they depend on host-cell machinery for essential aspects of their multiplication. Certain bacteria also have an obligate intracellular lifestyle. By contrast, bacteria with a facultative intracellular lifestyle have the capacity for intracellular activity but are not categorically dependent on living host cells in all circumstances. A bacterium observed within a cell is not automatically an obligate intracellular bacterium.

A further distinction concerns phagocytic uptake. Some host cells normally internalize extracellular material as part of their physiological function. Microbial material within such cells may reflect protective clearance rather than the establishment of a replicative niche. The same spatial observation can have different implications depending on the subsequent fate of the material. Internalization is thus not a reliable synonym for microbial invasion in the functional sense of successful infectious activity. The professional phagocyte is an example in which the host, rather than the agent, may initiate uptake.

An agent that exists outside cells at one point in a life cycle and inside cells at another is not a contradiction in classification. The free extracellular state of a virus is a transport or encounter phase, whereas replication depends on a living cell. Other agents can have more varied distributions. A classification scheme based on where an agent is detected at one time must not be confused with a scheme based on its requirements for reproduction. Examination questions sometimes use this distinction to test whether a student understands the meaning of obligate intracellular rather than merely recognizing the term.

Persistence refers to continued presence or activity over time. It is not identical to continuous rapid multiplication. In a persistent association, the balance between continued biological activity and removal can maintain an observable population without a steadily rising count. Viral latency is a particular form of persistence in which a viral genome remains associated with cells in a state that differs from ongoing productive infection; the details vary among viruses. Persistent, latent and productive are therefore not interchangeable descriptions. They concern different temporal and functional properties of an interaction.

The language of survival also requires care. A population count at two time points represents a net outcome. A constant total can occur because very little production and removal take place, or because substantial production and removal balance each other. A falling total may coexist with some ongoing production. A rising total indicates net accumulation under the assumptions of a suitable measurement, not the individual rate of every contributing process. This is a general population principle that applies to biological interpretation without requiring a detailed pathogen-specific growth model.

Mixed lifestyles illustrate the usefulness of the compartment approach. One portion of an agent's life cycle may involve the exterior environment, another an extracellular host compartment and another a living host cell. Each transition involves a different biological question. The physical presence of the agent in one compartment should not be extrapolated into a claim that its entire life cycle occurs there. A careful scientific diagram makes compartment boundaries explicit and uses directional arrows to distinguish movement from local activity.

A student's reasoning becomes more reliable when it starts with the observed location and then adds the observed function. An extracellular signal is compatible with an extracellular association, released material or a transient transport stage. An intracellular signal is compatible with uptake, ongoing activity or remnants of degraded material. Evidence of production of new biological material adds a distinct kind of information. A physiological phenotype adds another. The location, activity, time course and physiological effect should be considered as separate dimensions rather than reduced to one label.

9. From local interaction to altered tissue function

The transition from an established association to a disease is a transition in the biological state of the host. A tissue is a coordinated collection of cells and extracellular components performing particular functions. Its organization permits processes that individual cells could not accomplish in isolation. An alteration in the activities of some cells can disturb the function of the tissue even if many other cells remain unaffected. For this reason, the relationship between the number of infected cells and the magnitude of tissue dysfunction need not be proportional.

Different cells contribute differently to the function of an organ. The loss of a rare specialized cell type may have a disproportionate effect on a particular physiological process. Conversely, a tissue with substantial functional reserve may maintain its performance despite measurable injury. Functional reserve refers to the capacity to continue performing a biological task even when some of the contributing components are impaired. A measured change in cellular integrity therefore does not translate directly into a fixed amount of organismal disease. The tissue's normal organization and ability to compensate determine the consequences.

One route to damage involves direct alteration of a host cell by an infectious agent or its products. Changes in cell structure, membrane integrity or normal metabolic activities may interfere with the cell's physiological role. Another route involves extracellular disturbances that alter the conditions required for ordinary cellular function. A third route involves the host response itself. These categories can overlap in a single interaction. The important reasoning task is to distinguish the immediate cause of a particular cellular phenotype from the biological event that initiated the sequence.

A cell need not die for a tissue to become dysfunctional. A cell that remains alive but no longer carries out its specialized transport, secretory or signaling activity can contribute to disease. This follows from the distinction between survival and function. In many physiological systems, the proper magnitude and direction of transport matter as much as the viability of the transporting cells. A measurement of cell death alone is therefore an incomplete indicator of tissue performance. Conversely, a measurable loss of some cells need not immediately produce a detectable whole-organism phenotype if the tissue compensates.

Host responses can both limit infection and affect tissue integrity. The preceding immunology subunit established that protective systems can recognize microbial features, recruit effector mechanisms and alter local signaling. At this point, those details are prerequisites rather than new chapter content. Their significance is that a response beneficial in one respect can carry a physiological cost in another. A protective response that removes damaged cells, for example, may reduce the biological activity of an agent while temporarily decreasing the number of functional cells in the tissue. There is no contradiction in a simultaneous reduction in microbial burden and a transient increase in tissue injury.

The damage-response framework introduced earlier expresses this relationship at a conceptual level. It places host damage at the center of the outcome rather than assuming that the presence of a particular microorganism has a fixed meaning in every host. It also allows for variation in the contribution of host responses. The framework is not a numerical equation that predicts severity from a single measurement of immunity. Different types of biological damage have different causes and time courses, and a one-dimensional representation can only illustrate an aspect of a more complex system.

Resistance and disease tolerance describe two different ways in which hosts may limit the consequences of infection. Resistance concerns the ability to prevent or reduce the burden of an infectious agent. Disease tolerance concerns the ability to limit the loss of host health or function at a given burden. A host can possess both properties. Distinguishing them is important because two hosts with comparable amounts of infectious material may differ in their physiological condition, while two hosts with similar physiological condition may differ in their burdens. The term disease tolerance in this context should not be confused with the antigen-specific immunological tolerance studied in 4.C.

The difference is apparent in a hypothetical comparison of two host groups. If the groups differ in measured agent burden but have similar relationships between burden and tissue function, the difference is consistent with a change in resistance under the specified conditions. If groups have comparable burden but consistently differ in the loss of function attributable to infection, the result is consistent with a difference in disease tolerance. Neither observation alone establishes the precise cellular mechanism involved. Baseline health, timing and the validity of burden measurements also need consideration. The analytical framework is useful precisely because it specifies what a measurement can and cannot support.

Recovery is a separate biological outcome. Removal of the agent can reduce the initiating disturbance, but restoration of tissue function may depend on repair, regeneration or physiological compensation. Some tissues can replace damaged cells more readily than others. A host can therefore have declining microbial burden while function remains impaired for a period. Conversely, restoration of function does not in every case establish complete removal of the agent. The kinetics of burden and of tissue recovery are related but not necessarily identical.

The location of a disease manifestation may reflect physiological connectivity rather than microbial dissemination. A change in one tissue can alter the composition of circulating substances, the activity of signaling networks or the delivery of resources to another tissue. A plant with disturbed water transport can display effects in leaves even when the initial disturbance occurs elsewhere. An animal with a local cellular disturbance may experience a change in a systemic physiological variable. These examples show why the spatial distribution of symptoms and the spatial distribution of microbial activity must be assessed separately.

The term pathogenicity refers to the capacity of an agent to cause disease in a relevant host context. Virulence is commonly used for the relative degree of pathogenicity or damage in a specified context, although definitions and endpoints vary across disciplines. Comparisons of virulence require the host, conditions and outcome measure to be defined. A numerical difference in the prevalence of a surface-associated organism is not by itself evidence for a difference in virulence. A difference in disease outcome is not automatically evidence for a difference in entry. Each claim must be matched to the relevant biological measurement.

The central relationship can be summarized in terms of causal levels. Molecular recognition can influence where association occurs. Cellular events can influence whether biological activity continues. Tissue organization determines how local changes affect physiological performance. Host responses influence both agent burden and injury. Finally, repair and compensation modify the organismal outcome. A full explanation of disease connects the relevant levels without assuming that all must contribute equally in every case.

Cholera illustrates how an infectious disease can arise from a functional disturbance of epithelial cells without requiring widespread intracellular bacterial invasion. The classical disease mechanism involves a disturbance of normal intestinal ion and water transport associated with bacterial toxin activity. The resulting physiological loss is an example of disease arising through altered cellular function rather than the requirement that every affected epithelial cell contain a bacterium. The specific signaling mechanism is assigned to 4.D.04 and the comparative disease cases to 4.D.09. For this foundation chapter, the case is valuable because it separates the site of bacterial association, the action of a microbial product, and the physiological endpoint. It also connects directly with earlier studies of membrane transport and cellular signaling.

10. Local distribution, dissemination and temporal outcomes

The word spread describes a change in distribution. A microbial marker may be detected at one location initially and at additional locations later. Before assigning a mechanism to this observation, it is necessary to decide whether the later marker represents living organisms, infectious particles, microbial products or remnants of biological material. An agent can move as an intact entity, while soluble products and fragments may be transported independently. A map of marker distribution is consequently not always a map of sites supporting infection. The distinction is particularly important when the measurement concerns nucleic acid, antigen or another molecular feature rather than a direct indication of biological activity.

Local spread takes place in or around the region of initial establishment. The affected area may include neighboring cells, extracellular compartments and cells that enter the tissue from elsewhere. However, detection in adjacent cells does not by itself establish the direction or sequence of movement. The neighboring sites might have been exposed independently. To infer a progression from one location to another, spatial observations must be considered together with temporal evidence and anatomical relationships. A single image gives useful information about distribution at the time of observation but cannot reveal the full history of the interaction.

In multicellular organisms, systems for transport connect anatomical regions. Animals have circulatory systems that distribute substances and cells, while vascular plants have conducting tissues that support movement of water, minerals and assimilated products. These systems normally serve essential physiological functions. Their existence also explains why material associated with a local disturbance may appear far from its point of origin. Yet a substance detected in a conducting compartment is not necessarily being produced there. Transport, local production and persistence are distinct explanations for a measured signal, and the interpretation depends on what the measurement distinguishes.

A local disease process may produce remote physiological effects without dissemination of living infectious agents. Consider a simplified animal tissue that secretes a signaling substance when disturbed. That substance can change the behavior of cells elsewhere even if the disturbance remains anatomically localized. A plant provides a parallel example: impaired function in one part of its transport system can affect water relations in another part. In each case, the physiological connectivity of the organism contributes to the spatial distribution of consequences. The classification of infection as localized or disseminated should rely on evidence about the agent's distribution and activity, not merely the extent of symptoms.

For plant biology, the spatial distinction between apoplastic and symplastic movement introduced earlier becomes useful. The extracellular continuum and the cytoplasmic connections between cells are different compartments. An observation showing a bacterial population associated with intercellular spaces and an observation showing viral material associated with living cells should not be drawn with identical arrows across cell membranes. At the scale of the whole plant, distant leaves can show related disturbances; at the cellular scale, the biological relationships within those leaves can differ. The later chapters of this subunit will explain representative mechanisms without requiring the present chapter to duplicate them.

A common source of confusion is the assumption that movement and replication must be simultaneous. Distribution may change because biological material has been transported, because it is produced at new sites, or because it is removed from some sites more rapidly than others. These processes have different implications. An increase in the fraction of detected material at a distant site may reflect a decrease in the original site rather than a new increase in the distant site. Therefore both absolute and relative quantities are useful, and a comparison must specify which has been measured.

Temporal descriptions are similarly dependent on the chosen endpoint. An acute infection is generally characterized by a relatively limited course compared with a chronic or persistent infection, but there is no single duration appropriate to every biological system. A brief episode of symptoms does not by itself prove that an infection has ended. Persistence refers to continued association or biological presence over time, whereas latency describes a more specific state of some agents, especially viruses, in which continued genomic presence is distinguished from ongoing production of progeny. Reappearance of symptoms does not on its own distinguish a reactivation of a previous infection from a new infection.

The word carrier can refer to an individual harboring an agent without obvious disease manifestations, with its precise meaning depending on the organism and clinical context. It is not synonymous with vector. A vector participates in transmission between hosts; its relationship with the agent varies among biological systems. A vector may itself support some stage of the agent's life cycle, but the word describes its role in transmission rather than the severity of its own disease. A carrier's lack of symptoms does not establish whether transmission occurs. These terms are best understood by specifying the biological role rather than by assuming that symptom status determines every aspect of the interaction.

It is useful to separate three time courses when reading an experimental graph: the amount of agent-associated material, the magnitude of a host response and the extent of physiological disturbance. These quantities may peak at different times. A host response may continue after an initiating stimulus has declined; repair may continue after the response itself decreases. A delayed peak in dysfunction therefore need not indicate that the agent burden is simultaneously rising. This is a general consequence of causal systems in which downstream processes require time and have their own rates of resolution.

Sampling time can also affect the apparent spatial distribution. A tissue with an early detectable signal may later show little signal, while another tissue may exhibit a prolonged marker. A late observation alone may underestimate the importance of the first tissue in the history of the interaction. Conversely, an early measurement may not reveal the full distribution of later consequences. The interpretation of a temporal series should distinguish absence at the measured time from absence throughout the process. That distinction is especially relevant in Part C questions based on data collected at a limited number of time points.

11. Quantitative reasoning about molecular association and observations

Quantitative biology gives precision to distinctions that have already been developed qualitatively. It is most useful when the variables correspond to clearly defined observations. In the present chapter, a simple reversible association between a noninfectious soluble ligand and a purified receptor illustrates the mathematical meaning of molecular affinity. The model is deliberately restricted to a single molecular interaction. It does not predict whether an infectious agent enters a cell, establishes an infection or causes disease. Those endpoints involve additional biological processes not represented by the equation.

Let R denote the free receptor, L the free ligand and RL the complex formed when one molecule of each associates. The reversible reaction is R + L ⇌ RL. At equilibrium the association and dissociation fluxes balance. If the forward reaction obeys the elementary mass-action expression k_on[R][L] and the reverse reaction obeys k_off[RL], their equality gives k_on[R][L] = k_off[RL]. Rearrangement gives K_d = k_off/k_on = [R][L]/[RL]. The bracketed quantities are concentrations, the association rate constant has dimensions of inverse concentration per unit time, and the dissociation rate constant has dimensions of inverse time. Their ratio therefore has dimensions of concentration.

The total concentration of receptor in this simple model is the sum of free and ligand-bound receptor: [R]_total = [R] + [RL]. The fraction of receptor bound, denoted by θ, equals [RL]/[R]_total. From the equilibrium definition, [R] = K_d[RL]/[L] for nonzero free ligand concentration. Substitution gives θ = [L]/(K_d + [L]). The equation expresses a relationship between a dimensionless fraction and two concentrations expressed in the same units. It shows why K_d has a useful graphical meaning: when free ligand concentration equals K_d, the predicted fraction of occupied receptors is one half.

This derivation assumes independent equivalent sites, a single reversible binding state and equilibrium. It also assumes that the quantity inserted for ligand is the free concentration rather than simply the total amount added to a system in which appreciable ligand may be bound. The distinction matters because conservation of ligand and conservation of receptor are separate relationships. A measurement taken while the association is still changing cannot automatically be interpreted with the equilibrium formula. Similarly, an interacting surface with multiple binding contacts may display behavior that a one-site equation does not capture. The model is a conceptual bridge from Unit 1 chemical equilibrium to the interpretation of molecular recognition.

The shape of the theoretical curve is more informative than the equation alone. At zero free ligand the fractional occupancy is zero in the model. At low concentrations relative to K_d the fraction increases approximately in proportion to free ligand concentration. At high concentrations relative to K_d the fraction approaches one rather than continuing to increase indefinitely. This plateau represents saturation of the finite receptor population. It is not a prediction that all cells in a tissue become infected. Receptor occupancy is a molecular variable; infected-cell fraction is a cellular outcome with different requirements and a different denominator.

Two quantities often confused in experimental questions are the fraction occupied and the number of occupied sites. Imagine two preparations with the same fractional occupancy but different total receptor amounts. The preparation containing more total receptor has a greater amount of bound complex, even though the fraction occupied is equal. A measurement of total bound signal may therefore differ even when the equilibrium fraction is unchanged. The inference that a larger binding signal demonstrates a different intrinsic affinity is not valid without consideration of the amount and availability of receptor.

Concentration units must also be handled carefully. A nanomolar concentration is one billionth of a mole per litre, while a micromolar concentration is one millionth of a mole per litre. Before comparing K_d values or calculating occupancy, the concentrations must be expressed in compatible units. The dimensionless result can then be stated as a fraction or a percentage. A mathematical answer outside the interval from zero to one is inconsistent with this model. This simple plausibility check often identifies arithmetic mistakes before more complicated calculations are attempted.

A second form of quantitative reasoning concerns the interpretation of measured fractions. Suppose a microscopic survey examines cells and classifies each according to whether a defined marker is present. The fraction of marker-positive cells equals the number of positive cells divided by the number of examined cells. That fraction must not be confused with the total number of positive cells. If two tissues differ in their number of sampled cells, the tissue with the larger absolute count may have a smaller positive fraction. The appropriate denominator depends on the question being asked.

A positive fraction is also different from signal intensity. A preparation may contain many cells with weak signal or a smaller number of cells with strong signal. An average across the preparation can conceal these different distributions. A student examining a graph must determine whether the ordinate represents a count, a fraction, an average intensity per cell, or a total amount of material. Conversion between these quantities requires additional information. For instance, a fraction by itself cannot determine a total number without a known denominator.

Background correction supplies another example of careful interpretation. In an idealized assay with a background contribution measured independently under equivalent conditions, the net signal can be calculated by subtracting the background value from the observed value. A higher background can create the appearance of a larger uncorrected signal. The corrected result, however, remains an assay-specific quantity. Subtraction does not transform a molecular measurement into evidence for cellular entry or physiological dysfunction. It only clarifies the quantity the assay was designed to measure.

Finally, proportional change and percentage-point change are not interchangeable. If a fraction increases from twenty percent to thirty percent, the absolute difference is ten percentage points, whereas the relative increase compared with the initial fraction is fifty percent. These are two correct but distinct descriptions. A claim about comparative frequency must identify which calculation is intended. Neither calculation establishes a cause merely because the difference is large. Quantitative reasoning improves causal interpretation only when the mathematical variable is connected to the biological event under investigation.

12. Reading experimental evidence: what a measurement demonstrates

Experiments addressing host–pathogen interactions can be understood as attempts to discriminate among plausible explanations. A well-formed research question identifies a particular relationship rather than simply asking whether an infection occurred. One question concerns whether a molecule associates with a receptor. Another concerns whether a marker is located within a cell. A third concerns whether a biological process continues after entry. A fourth concerns whether tissue function has changed. Each has a different appropriate observation, and no single measurement automatically answers all four. The purpose of experimental reasoning in this chapter is to recognize those distinctions.

Consider an observation based on microscopy. A fluorescent signal overlapping the outline of a host cell may be interpreted as association with that cell. In a two-dimensional image, however, a signal at the far surface, near surface or within the cell can appear to overlap the same outline. Spatial resolution and the geometry of the measurement affect what can be inferred. A claim about localization must identify the relevant membrane boundary and provide evidence that separates an interior signal from a signal superimposed on the cell image. The observed overlap is real, but the stronger interpretation requires additional spatial information.

An antibody-based or nucleic-acid-based signal similarly identifies a molecular target under the assumptions of specificity and valid controls. The molecular target may remain after the biological entity carrying it has been damaged or removed. Consequently, molecular detection is not automatically equivalent to viable biological activity. This is why interpretation often depends on complementary observations. Two methods that detect the same molecular feature may confirm that feature with greater confidence without independently demonstrating replication or physiological function.

An assay for a biological product addresses a different question. A change in the amount of a product over time can support a claim about biological activity when the identity and origin of that product are established. Nevertheless, a product may be produced by the host rather than the infectious agent, or it may be released from previously existing material. A time-dependent signal is not self-interpreting. One must know the biological origin of the measured molecule and understand whether the observed change could result from processes other than new production. The logic is the same as distinguishing synthesis, transport and degradation of an endogenous cellular molecule.

Controls are observations that help identify the source of a signal or the meaning of a comparison. A negative control can reveal the signal present when the proposed specific association is absent. A reference or positive control can establish that a measurement is capable of detecting a known target or phenomenon. An unperturbed comparison establishes the baseline for a proposed change. A viability measure addresses whether an apparent difference in biological activity is confounded by the general loss of cellular function. These controls answer different questions; one cannot assume that the presence of any control resolves every ambiguity in an interpretation.

Imagine a purely hypothetical set of cell observations in which a noninfectious molecular probe is associated with two groups of cells. Both groups show comparable surface signal, but only one shows a distinct internal signal. The most direct conclusion is that the two groups differ in the observed intracellular localization of the probe despite similar surface association. It would not be justified to claim that the internal signal represents productive infection, since the probe is noninfectious. The example illustrates how separating the measurement of two stages gives a more specific interpretation than measuring an aggregate endpoint.

In another hypothetical data set, two groups of host tissue display equal values for a validated measure of burden but differ in physiological performance. The observation is compatible with differences in the response of the tissue to that burden. Baseline physiological performance must also be considered, because a difference present before infection would complicate the interpretation of loss of function attributable to infection. A student's conclusion should distinguish an association consistent with disease tolerance from proof of a particular molecular mechanism. An endpoint can support a broad biological category without establishing the precise pathway responsible.

A useful concept is normalization, which relates an observed quantity to a relevant reference. A tissue with twice the mass may contain twice the total signal without a greater signal per unit mass. A cell sample containing fewer viable cells may show less total activity because fewer cells contribute to the measurement, not because each cell is intrinsically less active. Normalization is not a universal mathematical remedy, since the choice of reference must make biological sense. A cell-count denominator answers a different question from tissue mass, and neither is appropriate if the main question concerns the proportion of a particular cell type.

Sampling variation is a further limitation. Measurements from several independent biological samples provide information about variation among those samples. Repeated readings of the same sample mainly characterize aspects of measurement precision and are not equivalent to independent biological replication. If two group means differ, the interpretation depends on the distribution, the measurement uncertainty and the sampling design. For foundational examination questions, it is often sufficient to distinguish a consistent difference from an isolated observation and to avoid assuming that numerical separation automatically demonstrates a reproducible biological effect.

Causal interpretation also requires attention to confounding variables. A comparison between two cell types may simultaneously involve differences in cell-surface composition, intracellular function and measurement conditions. If the observed signal differs, the comparison identifies an association with cell type but does not isolate the individual contributing variable. A controlled comparison changes or examines a defined factor while accounting for plausible alternatives. This principle can be understood without knowing specialized microbiological procedures. Its examination value is that it helps identify conclusions that are stronger than the data justify.

Some questions use a conceptual loss-of-function scenario to test whether a student understands causal order. For example, if an ordinary noninfectious ligand no longer associates with a surface when its binding site is absent, the evidence concerns the association step. A downstream response may also disappear because it depends on that step. The disappearance of both endpoints does not independently demonstrate that the same molecular site directly carries out the downstream process. It may merely be required earlier in the sequence. This is the distinction between a direct role and an indirect consequence that students have already encountered in signaling pathways.

Temporal evidence can strengthen an interpretation but does not alone establish causality. An event must generally precede a consequence attributed to it, yet many events precede a later change without causing it. If a marker appears before tissue dysfunction, this chronology is consistent with the marker being involved in the disturbance, but an alternative process may produce both observations. A mechanistic claim becomes stronger when chronology, spatial localization and an independently supported causal relationship point toward the same explanation. None of these forms of evidence should be silently replaced by another.

The most reliable method for solving an unfamiliar data question is to read the observation in its most literal scientifically meaningful form, identify its scale and endpoint, and then compare the available interpretations. An answer claiming only an observed difference may be less dramatic than one asserting a complete causal mechanism, but it is often more correct. This restraint is not merely an examination technique. It is a fundamental principle of experimental biology that keeps scientific explanations tied to the evidence that supports them.

A diagnostic observation and a mechanistic observation may have different purposes. A reliable molecular marker can be useful for identifying the presence of a biological agent, while a study of tissue organization may be designed to explain the consequences of an association. The absence of a complete mechanistic account does not make an appropriate diagnostic observation useless. Conversely, the usefulness of a marker for one purpose does not give it unlimited interpretive meaning. In biotechnology, this distinction is important when evaluating what an assay measures and what biological variable is represented by its signal. It is equally important in interpreting plant-disease observations, where an external change in appearance may help identify a disturbance but need not localize the agent. The correct application depends on defining the question before choosing the interpretation.

13. Comparative and evolutionary context of host–microorganism relationships

The relationships between hosts and microorganisms are shaped by the characteristics of both participants. The fact that a particular association persists does not imply that its features evolved to serve a predetermined purpose. Natural selection acts on heritable variation through differences in survival and reproduction. In host-associated systems, the outcome of such variation can depend on the biological environment provided by the other participant. This is a context-dependent relationship rather than a directed process in which either organism consciously develops a particular property.

The molecular features that support ordinary cellular functions can also influence interactions with microorganisms. Host membrane proteins and cell wall components are constrained by their roles in transport, organization and signaling. Differences in these structures across organisms may change their participation in an association. However, a change in a host molecule can carry consequences for its normal physiological function as well as for microbial recognition. This explains why the evolution of host features cannot be understood by considering infectious interactions alone.

Host variation is relevant at several scales. Differences between species can reflect long histories of divergence in anatomy and physiology. Differences within species may reflect genetic variation, developmental stage or environmental history. These sources of variation should not be conflated. A correlation between a host trait and an infection-related observation may motivate a biological hypothesis, but it does not necessarily establish the trait as the cause. Comparative evidence is strongest when the observed differences are related to a clearly defined stage of the interaction.

The contrast between animal and plant hosts illustrates both conservation and divergence. Both possess plasma membranes, proteins that participate in molecular recognition, compartmentalized intracellular environments and regulatory systems. Plants additionally organize their living cells within walls and connect many of their cytoplasmic domains through plasmodesmata. Animals organize many cells into epithelial sheets and tissues with extracellular matrices. Shared principles of molecular association therefore coexist with important differences in the anatomical setting. It would be incorrect either to treat plant and animal infection as identical or to assume that no general principles are shared.

Microorganisms likewise have diverse biological organizations. Bacteria possess cellular machinery, viruses depend on living cells for reproduction, and fungi are eukaryotic organisms with their own cellular organization. These differences affect the meanings of entry, survival and multiplication. A bacterium growing in an extracellular host compartment and a virus producing new particles within a cell may both be associated with disease, but they do not do so through identical cellular relationships. Understanding those differences at the level of general biology is more useful at this stage than memorizing a long list of organisms.

The distinction between disease and host association also has an evolutionary implication. A severe physiological disturbance is not an inevitable property of every stable relationship between organisms. Different interactions can persist with little measurable harm, produce intermittent injury or cause substantial disturbance. There is no universal evolutionary rule that all host-associated agents must become harmless over time, nor a rule that they must become progressively more damaging. The biological and ecological circumstances of each relationship matter, and the existence of a particular outcome cannot establish a general direction for evolution.

The host's ability to maintain function is an important part of the comparative picture. One organism may experience a greater physiological consequence than another despite a similar measurement of burden because their tissues differ in reserve, compensatory capacity or responses to disturbance. This principle applies to plants as well as animals. Comparison of visible symptoms alone may therefore misrepresent the relative burden of the agent, while comparison of burden alone may misrepresent the relative loss of function. The two outcomes answer distinct biological questions.

In ecological terms, a tissue is not an isolated collection of host molecules. It includes surrounding substances, other cells and sometimes resident organisms. Its properties change over time as the host develops or responds to ordinary physiological demands. The context in which a microbial interaction occurs is therefore dynamic. This does not make mechanistic explanations impossible; it means that an explanation must state the conditions under which an observation is valid. General biological principles organize those conditions without pretending that every system has the same quantitative behavior.

The foundations developed in this chapter now connect directly to the remaining subunit. The next chapter will examine bacterial association with animal surfaces in greater molecular detail. Later nodes will distinguish particular bacterial cellular entry mechanisms, viral recognition and entry, changes in host-cell behavior, plant infection and cell fusion. Those topics are applications of the framework rather than exceptions to it. The most important preparation for them is the ability to identify the relevant biological compartment, distinguish a stage from its outcome, recognize the limitations of each measurement and follow the causal sequence from molecular association to physiological function.

These distinctions also explain why protective measures can act at different levels of a biological interaction. Maintaining physiological barriers concerns the initial relationship between an organism and its surroundings. Immune protection studied in 4.C concerns recognition and control of biological disturbances, while processes of tissue repair concern restoration of normal function. The categories are analytically useful even though they can interact in a living organism. An examination scenario in which a host maintains better physiological function without a corresponding reduction in burden should not be interpreted in exactly the same way as a scenario in which burden is reduced while the relationship between burden and injury remains unchanged. The foundation is therefore not a single descriptive sequence, but a set of linked biological variables that can be examined separately.

Worked numerical examples

Example 1. Fractional occupancy of an inert soluble ligand

Free ligand = 30 nM; Kd = 10 nM. The one-site equilibrium assumptions are stipulated. θ = 30/(10 + 30) = 0.75, or 75%. This is a fraction of receptor sites occupied, not a fraction of cells infected.

Example 2. Concentration unit conversion

Free ligand = 0.05 µM = 50 nM; Kd = 50 nM. θ = 50/(50 + 50) = 0.5. Both concentrations must be in the same unit. The equality implies half occupancy only in the one-site equilibrium model.

Example 3. Number versus fraction of occupied sites

Two hypothetical preparations each have θ = 0.4. Preparation A has 250 available sites; B has 500. A has 0.4 × 250 = 100 occupied sites and B has 0.4 × 500 = 200. B gives twice the number of occupied sites without a difference in fractional occupancy.

Example 4. Choosing a cell-count denominator

A marker survey reports 90 of 300 cells positive in tissue A and 120 of 600 in tissue B. Fractions are 90/300 = 0.30 and 120/600 = 0.20. B has more positive cells in the sample, while A has the higher positive fraction. Neither observation alone identifies a mechanism.

Example 5. Correcting an idealized background signal

Observed signal for A = 84 arbitrary units, background = 24; for B = 73, background = 8. A net = 60 and B net = 65 arbitrary units. Although A has the larger raw signal, B has the larger corrected signal under the stipulated additive-background assumption.

Example 6. Relative versus percentage-point differences

A measured fraction changes from 0.20 to 0.30. Absolute change = 0.10 = 10 percentage points. Relative increase = (0.30 − 0.20)/0.20 = 0.50 = 50%. These quantify different features of the same change.

Example 7. Interpreting loss of host function

Two hypothetical hosts have an equal burden reading. Both had baseline functional scores of 100 units. A now scores 80, B scores 55. Relative losses are (100 − 80)/100 = 20% and (100 − 55)/100 = 45%. A shows the smaller loss at this particular burden, consistent with better disease tolerance; a full relationship across burdens would require additional evidence.

Part B — Conceptual practice

Original practice questions. Select the best answer, then open the explanation.

1. Which observation establishes exposure without necessarily establishing attachment?

  1. A population remains associated with a surface over several observations.
  2. A marker has been spatially resolved in a cellular compartment.
  3. A microbial particle is encountered at an external surface.
  4. A sustained tissue-associated population is observed.
Answer and explanation

C. A microbial particle is encountered at an external surface. Exposure is an opportunity for contact. The other alternatives report later biological events.

2. The term apoplast refers most directly to which compartment?

  1. Extracellular continuum of plant walls and intercellular spaces.
  2. Cytoplasm connected through plasmodesmata.
  3. Interior of the plant nucleus.
  4. Interior of a living cell's vacuole.
Answer and explanation

A. Extracellular continuum of plant walls and intercellular spaces. The apoplast is outside living plasma membranes; the symplast is the connected cytoplasmic domain.

3. An obligate intracellular replicating agent is defined by which property?

  1. It can never be detected outside cells.
  2. It must always cause cell death.
  3. It is necessarily a bacterium.
  4. Its multiplication depends on a suitable host-cell environment.
Answer and explanation

D. Its multiplication depends on a suitable host-cell environment. Dependence for reproduction, rather than momentary physical location or injury, defines the category.

4. Which term best describes an established microbial association at a host site without overt disease?

  1. A transient detection during an isolated encounter.
  2. Colonization.
  3. A tissue disturbance attributed to an established agent.
  4. Passage of an agent beyond a defined cell boundary.
Answer and explanation

B. Colonization. Colonization implies establishment at a site; disease requires disturbance of function. The association may remain extracellular.

5. What is tissue tropism?

  1. The range of host species in which an agent establishes activity.
  2. Preference for particular tissues or cell types within a host.
  3. The magnitude of host dysfunction at a stated burden.
  4. The anatomical distribution of symptoms without localization evidence.
Answer and explanation

B. Preference for particular tissues or cell types within a host. Tropism concerns biological distribution within hosts. It is distinct from severity, host range and timing.

6. Which distinguishes disease tolerance from resistance?

  1. Tolerance describes elimination of the agent at a given tissue function.
  2. Tolerance is defined by lower exposure, irrespective of subsequent outcome.
  3. Tolerance is defined solely by the absence of an adaptive immune response.
  4. Tolerance limits functional loss at a given burden.
Answer and explanation

D. Tolerance limits functional loss at a given burden. Resistance primarily concerns burden control. Disease tolerance concerns loss of function; it is not synonymous with immunological tolerance.

7. Which is characteristic of the apical membrane of a polarized epithelial cell?

  1. It faces the relevant lumen or external compartment.
  2. It necessarily faces the basement membrane.
  3. It is identical in composition to every lateral membrane.
  4. It is a plant cell wall.
Answer and explanation

A. It faces the relevant lumen or external compartment. Apical and basolateral domains differ in location and composition. The apical domain faces the lumen or external surface.

8. A host receptor used for microbial attachment may normally function in what?

  1. Only a dedicated microbial-sensing function.
  2. Only a function associated with the process of intracellular replication.
  3. Ordinary cellular adhesion or signaling.
  4. A host function that is necessarily absent in uninfected tissues.
Answer and explanation

C. Ordinary cellular adhesion or signaling. Host molecules generally have physiological functions independent of their participation in microbial recognition.

9. Which term denotes the combined functional strength of multiple molecular contacts?

  1. Avidity.
  2. Virulence.
  3. Latency.
  4. Permissiveness.
Answer and explanation

A. Avidity. Avidity concerns combined contacts. Affinity refers to a defined binding interaction; the other terms concern different biological properties.

10. What does productive viral infection require?

  1. Retention of detectable viral genome in a nonproductive state.
  2. Successful cellular attachment and internal localization only.
  3. Formation of progeny virus.
  4. Expression of a subset of viral products without formation of progeny.
Answer and explanation

C. Formation of progeny virus. Productive infection is defined by progeny production; attachment and internalization may occur without that endpoint.

11. Which is an example of mechanical clearance?

  1. Viral genome translation.
  2. Ciliary transport of mucus.
  3. Antigen receptor rearrangement.
  4. Chromosome segregation.
Answer and explanation

B. Ciliary transport of mucus. Cilia move mucus and entrapped material. The remaining processes concern intracellular biology rather than removal from a surface.

12. What was the central aim of classical Koch postulates?

  1. Define a disease solely from the presence of a molecular marker.
  2. Establish receptor-mediated entry as the universal cause of disease.
  3. Equate microbial association in a healthy host with a causal disease relationship.
  4. Link a proposed microbial cause to a reproducible disease.
Answer and explanation

D. Link a proposed microbial cause to a reproducible disease. The postulates formalized causal evidence. Their original assumptions have limitations for carriers, obligate intracellular agents and some diseases.

13. Which statement about an extracellular pathogen is correct?

  1. It must remain beyond the external boundary of the organism.
  2. It cannot produce effects on cells without entering their cytoplasm.
  3. It can disturb a host tissue without entering the host-cell cytoplasm.
  4. Its extracellular detection necessarily establishes the site of multiplication.
Answer and explanation

C. It can disturb a host tissue without entering the host-cell cytoplasm. Extracellular describes location relative to cells. Extracellular agents or products can alter tissue function.

14. Which structure connects cytoplasmic domains of neighboring plant cells?

  1. Stomatal pores between guard cells.
  2. Plasmodesmata.
  3. Extracellular continuity of the apoplast.
  4. The cell wall of each neighboring cell without cytoplasmic continuity.
Answer and explanation

B. Plasmodesmata. Plasmodesmata provide symplastic continuity; stomata and intercellular spaces are not cytoplasmic connections.

  1. Time.
  2. Inverse time.
  3. Dimensionless fraction.
  4. Concentration.
Answer and explanation

D. Concentration. Kd is [R][L]/[RL], giving concentration units. Fractional occupancy is dimensionless.

16. Which result best defines a tissue-level disease phenotype?

  1. An impairment of normal tissue function.
  2. A validated marker indicating biological association but no measured dysfunction.
  3. An anatomical encounter at the outermost host surface.
  4. A molecular difference in a host receptor without a physiological endpoint.
Answer and explanation

A. An impairment of normal tissue function. Disease is a functional or structural disturbance. Presence and recognition may occur without that disturbance.

17. What is an opportunistic pathogen?

  1. A microbial species defined by a strictly extracellular lifestyle.
  2. An agent whose detection establishes overt disease regardless of context.
  3. A resident organism defined as incapable of causing injury under changed conditions.
  4. An agent capable of causing disease in a changed or susceptible host context.

D. An agent capable of causing disease in a changed or susceptible host context. Opportunity reflects context, such as changed host containment or defense, rather than universal disease.

A. Transmissible activity in material passing filters that retained ordinary bacteria. Filterability of the tobacco disease agent challenged the assumption that every infectious agent was an ordinary bacterium.

  2. Local infection cannot cause symptoms.
  3. Distant physiological effects can arise from a localized disturbance.
  4. All localized infections are asymptomatic.
Answer and explanation

C. Distant physiological effects can arise from a localized disturbance. Physiological connectivity allows remote effects without dissemination of the agent; the two must be measured separately.

B. Its stability can reflect the combined contribution of multiple contacts. Many noncovalent interactions contribute to molecular recognition. Stability depends on their combined properties and context.

21. A noninfectious fluorescent probe overlaps the perimeter of a cell in a single two-dimensional image. What conclusion is warranted?

  1. The probe is localized exclusively to the external cell membrane.
  2. The signal is associated spatially with the cell image; internalization is unconfirmed.
  3. The probe is internalized, but its subcellular compartment is unresolved.
  4. The probe is present in a membrane-bounded vesicle.

B. The signal is associated spatially with the cell image; internalization is unconfirmed. An image projection can superimpose surface and internal signal. Additional spatial evidence is needed for intracellular localization, and the probe is noninfectious.

  1. In cytosol because they are inside the leaf.
  2. In nuclei.
  3. Within tissue but extracellular to the living plant cells.
  4. In the symplast by definition.
Answer and explanation

C. Within tissue but extracellular to the living plant cells. The apoplast is internal to the tissue yet outside living plasma membranes. The observations do not show cellular invasion.

23. A validated marker is present in 35 of 100 cells in sample X and 60 of 300 cells in Y. Which inference follows?

  1. X has a higher positive fraction; Y has a larger absolute positive count.
  2. Y has a higher fraction because 60 exceeds 35.
  3. Both fractions are identical.
  4. X necessarily has more severe disease.
Answer and explanation

A. X has a higher positive fraction; Y has a larger absolute positive count. X = 35%; Y = 20%. Absolute counts and fractions have different denominators, and neither alone measures functional damage.

24. Two specimens show identical amounts of a defined surface marker. Only one shows an internal signal from an inert tracer. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. Comparable marker abundance establishes that the two groups bind the tracer with identical affinity.
  2. The difference demonstrates that the measured marker directly carries out internalization.
  3. The absence of internal tracer in one group establishes the absence of all uptake pathways.
  4. The observed intracellular tracer localization differs despite similar marker amount.
Answer and explanation

D. The observed intracellular tracer localization differs despite similar marker amount. The data distinguish two readouts, not productive infection or a causal role for the measured marker.

25. An epithelial protein is abundant in whole-cell extracts but is localized exclusively to a membrane domain away from the observed luminal contact. What is missing from the expression claim?

  2. Evidence that the protein has a second physiological role.
  3. Evidence that the entire cell population has different total protein abundance.
  4. Evidence that the protein is accessible at the interface being examined.
Answer and explanation

D. Evidence that the protein is accessible at the interface being examined. Whole-cell abundance is not the same as surface accessibility; polarity matters for interpreting an encounter.

  1. The latter necessarily has lower burden.
  2. The latter has a smaller functional loss at the measured burden.
  3. The former has no host response.
  4. Both have identical tolerance because burdens match.
Answer and explanation

B. The latter has a smaller functional loss at the measured burden. Losses are 30 and 10 units. A difference at matched burden is consistent with disease tolerance, not proof of an underlying mechanism.

27. A molecular assay remains positive after a valid independent observation shows that viable agents are absent. Which interpretation is consistent?

  1. The positive signal necessarily establishes continued productive activity.
  2. The assay shows that cellular injury has resolved.
  3. Residual molecular material remains detectable.
  4. The molecular marker establishes the anatomical distribution of living agents.
Answer and explanation

C. Residual molecular material remains detectable. A molecular target can persist after loss of viability. This does not determine tissue recovery or anatomical distribution.

28. A local lesion is followed by a distant physiological change, but an appropriate localization assay detects the agent only at the local site. Which conclusion fits?

  1. Distant effects do not independently demonstrate dissemination.
  2. Distant effects prove multiplication at the distant site.
  3. A localized lesion cannot affect distant physiology.
  4. The agent necessarily moved through every cell.
Answer and explanation

A. Distant effects do not independently demonstrate dissemination. Remote functional consequences can result from host or agent-associated soluble signals or altered organ function; dissemination requires its own evidence.

  1. 0.25.
  2. 3.0.
  3. 0.75.
  4. 0.33.

C. 0.75. θ = 12/(12 + 4) = 12/16 = 0.75. A fractional occupancy cannot exceed one; Kd/free ligand is not the formula.

  1. 60 and 60.
  2. 100 and 300.
  3. 0.6 and 1.8.
  4. 60 and 180.

D. 60 and 180. Multiply each site's total by 0.6. Equal fractions need not imply equal amounts of complex.

  4. Each covarying factor contributes equally, regardless of the experimental design.

B. A group-level association exists, but the individual causal contribution is not isolated. Multiple covarying features are confounders. Association does not isolate which feature caused the difference.

  2. The second: 64 versus 50.
  3. Both: 45.
  4. The first: 55 versus 88.
Answer and explanation

A. The first: 45 versus 40. Subtract matching additive backgrounds. The uncorrected ranking reverses after correction.

  2. Burden and functional recovery have different observed time courses.
  3. The observation establishes that repair is the only remaining physiological process.
  4. The change in function is a direct measure of the remaining burden.

B. Burden and functional recovery have different observed time courses. Resolution of an initiating process and recovery of function need not coincide; the observation alone does not identify a repair mechanism.

34. Two healthy host samples contain the same commensal species. A separate diseased compartment contains it as well. What inference is warranted?

  2. Presence of the species in a healthy sample excludes its causal contribution in the diseased compartment.
  3. Detection in the diseased compartment establishes it as the sole cause.
  4. The diseased association must reflect a change in microbial species identity.

35. One tissue contains 200 units of marker in 10 g; another contains 300 units in 30 g. Which has higher marker amount per gram?

  2. Both are 20 units/g.
  3. Neither can be normalized using the given data.
  4. The first: 20 units/g versus 10 units/g.

37. A hypothetical immune response coincides with declining burden and transiently worsening tissue injury. Which principle explains why this is not contradictory?

  1. The decline in burden proves the response contributes no injury.
  2. The injury establishes that the response cannot have a protective role.
  3. Both observed trends must reflect a single variable measured with different instruments.
  4. Protective responses and tissue damage can occur together.
Answer and explanation

D. Protective responses and tissue damage can occur together. A host response can limit an agent while causing collateral physiological disturbance. Burden and damage are separate variables.

38. An assay shows a positive fraction rising from 0.40 to 0.50. Which quantitative statement is correct?

  1. An increase of 25 percentage points and 10% relative.
  2. A 50% increase.
  3. An increase of 10 percentage points and 25% relative to baseline.
  4. A decrease of 10 percentage points.
Answer and explanation

C. An increase of 10 percentage points and 25% relative to baseline. The absolute fraction change is 0.10; relative change is 0.10/0.40 = 0.25.

  1. The particle is freely present in the host cytosol.
  2. The particle is within the cell boundary.
  3. The vesicle provides a distinct compartment.
  4. Internal localization alone does not establish reproduction.

  2. The former is extracellular to plant cells; the latter is intracellular but compartmentalized.
  3. Neither signal is inside a tissue.
  4. Both prove productive viral infection.

  2. Both designs provide identical evidence for between-specimen biological variation.
  3. The first design provides stronger evidence for biological differences because the same specimen is repeatedly measured.
  4. The second design removes all technical measurement uncertainty.
Answer and explanation

A. Only the second samples independent biological variation. Technical repeats characterize precision for one specimen; independent specimens address between-specimen biological variability.

42. A host-associated marker has a sharp peak before a functional decline. Which conclusion is supported by chronology alone?

  1. The marker is the direct cause because its peak precedes the decline.
  2. The marker precedes the decline; causation remains unresolved.
  3. A common upstream cause is excluded by temporal precedence.
  4. The peak establishes the anatomical source of the later functional decline.
Answer and explanation

B. The marker precedes the decline; causation remains unresolved. Temporal precedence is compatible with a causal relationship but does not exclude a common cause or another intermediate process.

43. A ligand-binding model predicts occupancy 0.5 at equal free ligand and Kd. A measured fraction exceeds one. Which response is scientifically appropriate?

  1. Accept it as evidence of super-occupancy.
  2. Conclude every cell is infected.
  3. Check units, normalization and assumptions because the fraction is physically invalid.
  4. Double Kd without examining the data.

C. Check units, normalization and assumptions because the fraction is physically invalid. A one-site fractional occupancy is bounded by zero and one. An out-of-range result indicates a calculation, measurement or modeling problem.

44. Two organisms of the same species show very different disease severity despite similar observed tissue association. What is the correct conceptual explanation?

  1. Comparable association requires equal severity when the organisms are of the same species.
  2. The more severe outcome establishes a greater agent burden despite the stated evidence.
  3. The difference proves a particular host molecular pathway is responsible.
  4. Host physiological state and responses may contribute; association is not sufficient to predict severity.
Answer and explanation

D. Host physiological state and responses may contribute; association is not sufficient to predict severity. The interaction outcome depends on multiple stages and host physiology. Severity cannot be deduced solely from an association measurement.

Common misconceptions and examination traps

Exposure means infection
Exposure gives an opportunity; infection requires establishment. The terms are conflated in everyday speech. Test: classify a contact-only observation.
Detection proves viability
An assay may detect residual material. Students confuse specificity with biological activity. Test: molecular marker after validated loss of viability.
Attachment equals entry
Binding at an interface does not show boundary crossing. Images can obscure depth. Test: surface signal versus spatially resolved internal signal.
Colonization means symptoms
Established association can be asymptomatic. Everyday use of infection-related words encourages confusion. Test: stable mucosal association without dysfunction.
Disease means systemic infection
Local damage can impair function; remote effects may be indirect. Test: local lesion with distant response.
Inside a leaf means inside a cell
Apoplast is extracellular. The word inside is used at different scales. Test: bacteria between cell walls.
Wall equals plasma membrane
These are anatomically distinct. Simplified drawings can obscure the separation. Test: identify the actual cytoplasmic boundary.
All viruses enter plants as animal viruses do
Plant walls and transmission processes create distinct entry contexts. Test: compare the anatomical barriers.
Receptor equals immune sensor
Attachment receptors and host protective recognition have different functional definitions. Test: classify the direction of recognition.
Receptor expression proves accessibility
Total protein or RNA abundance does not establish surface localization. Test: polarized epithelial membrane domains.
Binding is sufficient for productive infection
Later stages require suitable cellular conditions. Test: distinguish a surface readout from biological activity.
Host range equals tissue tropism
Host range compares host categories; tropism concerns preferred sites or cell types. Test: species-versus-tissue comparisons.
Intracellular equals cytosolic
A vesicular lumen is a distinct compartment. Test: identify the compartment bounded by an internal membrane.
Obligate intracellular means never outside
Reproductive dependence is the defining property. Test: extracellular virion versus its intracellular multiplication.
Burden equals severity
Physiological injury and burden are distinct. Test: equal burden but different functional loss.
Disease tolerance equals immunological tolerance
Disease tolerance concerns health at a given burden, not necessarily antigen-specific unresponsiveness. Test: identify the measured endpoint.
Raw signal equals normalized signal
The denominator or background matters. Test: compare total counts with fractions or background-corrected values.
Temporal precedence proves cause
Chronology is necessary for many causal accounts but not sufficient. Test: identify a plausible common cause.

Revision and student mastery

Integrated summary

Host–pathogen interactions are context-dependent relationships. Exposure creates the opportunity; association, establishment, survival and spread are distinct biological events. Cellular entry is not obligatory for all disease. The spatial scale, accessible molecular surfaces, cellular permissiveness, host response and tissue function determine what can be inferred. Molecular affinity, burden and health measure different dimensions.

Essential quantitative relationships

Kd = [R][L]/[RL]; θ = [L]/(Kd + [L]) for a one-site equilibrium; positive fraction = positive count / total examined; relative change = (final − initial)/initial; net signal = observed − matching additive background.
