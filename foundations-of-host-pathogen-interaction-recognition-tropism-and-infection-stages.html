1. Encounters between hosts and microorganisms

Every multicellular organism occupies a world filled with other living organisms and biological particles. The outside of an animal is in contact with its surroundings, but so are the surfaces lining its digestive and respiratory systems. A leaf is similarly exposed to air, water, dust and microorganisms. In all of these situations, contact between an organism and a microorganism is ordinary, whereas infection is a particular biological outcome. An encounter might end when a particle is carried away, when a microorganism dies in an unsuitable environment, or when two organisms establish an association that does not injure either participant. The central question of host–pathogen interaction is therefore not simply which microorganisms are present. It is which relationships become established, where they occur, and how those relationships change the function of living tissues.

A host is an organism, or in a narrower cellular discussion a cell, that provides an environment in which another biological entity may reside or perform part of its life cycle. The word host does not itself imply disease. A bacterium normally residing on an animal's skin and a virus reproducing within an epithelial cell both use a host environment, although the nature of that dependence is very different. A pathogen is an infectious biological agent capable of producing disease in a susceptible host under appropriate circumstances. The qualification is important because the same microbial species may be harmless in one association but damaging in another. Susceptibility refers to the capacity of a host or cell to acquire an infection; it should not automatically be equated with the severity of a disease that might follow.

Microbial associations form a continuum rather than a simple division into good and bad organisms. In mutualism, both partners derive a benefit; in commensalism, one partner benefits and the other experiences no appreciable benefit or harm in the context being discussed. Parasitism involves a benefit to one partner at the expense of another. These terms describe relationships, not immutable properties of species. A microorganism that ordinarily resides without producing disease may become harmful when transferred into an inappropriate anatomical compartment or when a host loses an important protective function. An opportunistic pathogen is an agent that takes advantage of such changes in circumstances. Recognizing an opportunity as a change in the interaction helps explain why illness may develop without a newly introduced microbial species.

A useful way to begin this chapter is to distinguish presence from activity. Suppose a biological sample contains a molecular marker associated with a microorganism. That observation demonstrates that the marker is present in the sample, assuming the assay is specific and valid. It does not necessarily demonstrate that an intact, viable microorganism is present, that it has multiplied in the host, or that it has caused the symptoms that motivated sampling. Each of those is a separate proposition requiring appropriate evidence. A fragment of microbial nucleic acid can remain after an organism has been eliminated, and an intact organism may be present at a body surface without causing disease. This distinction between detection and causal explanation will recur throughout the chapter.

The simplest mental model of infection is a sequence of transitions. An agent first encounters a host, reaches an accessible site, remains associated with that site, establishes a population or replicative process, and may eventually alter tissue function. These transitions are not always separate in time. For a virus, attachment and penetration may occur in rapid succession at the cellular scale, whereas the interval between cellular infection and noticeable tissue damage may be much longer. For an extracellular bacterial association, there may be no penetration into the host-cell cytoplasm at all. A sequence is useful because it separates distinct biological questions; it becomes misleading only if it is treated as an obligatory itinerary followed by every infectious agent.

The size of the entity matters for the way we describe its relationship with the host. A bacterium is a cell, with its own plasma membrane, ribosomes and metabolic capacities, although its degree of dependence on host-derived nutrients varies. A virus is not a cell and lacks an independent translation apparatus; production of progeny virus depends on a suitable living cell. Consequently, a bacterial population can exist outside host cells and still be associated with an infection, while viral replication requires intracellular activity. Both agents may be detected outside cells during particular stages of their life cycles. The presence of an extracellular virion does not conflict with the intracellular requirement for viral reproduction.

Different scales of observation also yield different descriptions of infection. At the molecular scale one may study a host surface molecule and its interacting partner. At the cellular scale one may ask whether the agent is bound outside a cell or has crossed a membrane. At the tissue scale the concern may be the distribution of affected cells, and at the organismal scale one may ask whether the host retains ordinary physiological function. An accurate explanation connects these levels instead of treating them as interchangeable. A change affecting a surface molecule can have consequences at several levels, but the consequences depend on the anatomical location and physiological role of the cells carrying that molecule.

The host is not a passive container. It has physical boundaries, organized cellular surfaces, resident microbial populations and regulatory systems that maintain physiological conditions. A microorganism is likewise not simply a name on a list; it has structural and functional properties that determine which surroundings are compatible with its activities. Even when an agent possesses a property necessary for one stage of an interaction, that property need not be sufficient for the entire sequence. The same reasoning is familiar from cell signaling: binding of a ligand may be necessary for a response, but the downstream signaling machinery must also be available. The present chapter applies this necessary-versus-sufficient distinction to recognition, entry and subsequent infection.

An important consequence is that disease should not be defined by the mere identity of a microorganism. Disease is a disturbance of normal biological structure or function. Signs and symptoms are observations associated with that disturbance, but a detectable change may occur before symptoms become obvious. Some infected hosts remain asymptomatic; some illnesses involve damage in tissues where the infectious agent itself is scarce or absent. Those possibilities require careful separation of the site of the agent, the site of its activity, and the site of physiological injury. This provides the conceptual foundation for comparing animal and plant infections without reducing either to a catalogue of diseases.

2. From association to causal evidence

The modern explanation of infectious disease developed gradually as investigators learned to distinguish observations from causal relationships. Before microorganisms could be studied reliably, a recurring association between a particular environment and a disease could suggest a connection without identifying a causal biological agent. Improved microscopy made previously invisible organisms observable. However, visibility created another difficulty: microorganisms might be observed both in diseased material and in apparently healthy material. Their presence alone did not show whether they were responsible for the disease or had accumulated as a consequence of it. Establishing causation required observations that distinguished those explanations.

The work associated with Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch during the nineteenth century helped establish the microbial basis of many infectious diseases. The significance of Koch's classical postulates was their attempt to connect a specific biological agent with a reproducible disease rather than relying on a single microscopic observation. In their traditional formulation, the proposed agent should be associated with disease, be isolated and studied independently, reproduce the disease in a suitable susceptible host, and be recovered from the newly affected host. These are historical principles of causal reasoning, not a universal description of how every infectious disease is investigated today. Their strength lies in asking whether the proposed cause is consistently associated with the outcome and whether evidence can separate that cause from accompanying circumstances.

Several limitations became apparent as microbiology developed. Some infectious agents cannot reproduce independently of living cells and therefore do not satisfy a requirement for growth in a cell-free medium. An organism may be recovered from a healthy carrier, invalidating a literal interpretation that the agent must be absent from all healthy hosts. Experimental reproduction of illness in people would raise fundamental ethical problems, and a convenient animal model may not reproduce the physiology of the natural host. Infections involving more than one microorganism also resist a simple one-agent explanation. These limitations do not negate the importance of causal evidence; instead, they require evidence appropriate to the biology of the system.

Plant virology offers an instructive example of the need to reconsider assumptions about infectious agents. In the late nineteenth century, Dmitri Ivanovsky and Martinus Beijerinck studied the transmissible disease of tobacco that came to be associated with tobacco mosaic virus. Material capable of transmitting disease could pass through filters that retained ordinary bacteria. The observation challenged the assumption that every infectious agent was an independently growing bacterium. Later studies established the particulate and molecular nature of viruses. For our purposes, the historical lesson is that a biological phenomenon must be interpreted in the light of observations, not forced into a pre-existing classification simply because that classification had explained earlier diseases.

The development of cell biology added an important level to causal explanation. Instead of asking only why one organism became diseased, researchers could ask why only some cells within an organism were affected. The isolation of cells and the investigation of cellular membranes, receptors and intracellular processes revealed that the host actively determines the biological setting of infection. A tissue-specific pattern that was previously descriptive could be analyzed in terms of accessible cellular surfaces and differences in cellular function. That development provided a direct intellectual link between host–pathogen interaction and the preceding chapters on communication between cells.

A further conceptual development concerned the origin of damage. Classical descriptions often emphasized properties of the microorganism. Yet an interaction involves at least two biological participants. The damage-response framework developed by Arturo Casadevall and Liise-anne Pirofski, published in 2003, explicitly treated host damage as an outcome of host–microorganism interactions. It accommodates damage associated with insufficient protection, damage associated with an excessively harmful host response and damage due to microbial activity itself. The framework is a conceptual model rather than an assertion that all infections follow the same damage-versus-response curve.

For a student, these developments suggest three distinct questions to ask whenever presented with evidence about infection. First, what agent or microbial component has been identified? Second, what biological process connects that agent to a host compartment or cellular response? Third, what observation establishes the disturbance of function that is attributed to the interaction? A data set answering one of these questions need not answer the other two. For instance, a molecular marker can identify an agent without showing the site of damage, and a tissue lesion can establish injury without identifying a particular causal agent. The strongest mechanistic interpretations link complementary observations across these levels.

Evidence is not strengthened merely by using more technical vocabulary. A classical observation may be decisive if it discriminates between rival explanations, whereas a technologically sophisticated measurement may be uninformative if it does not distinguish attachment from entry or association from causation. An examination question may present an apparently persuasive correlation between two variables and then ask what additional conclusion is justified. The appropriate response is to identify exactly what was measured, identify plausible alternative interpretations, and avoid claiming evidence for stages that were never observed.

3. Exposure, attachment, colonization, infection and disease

Exposure is the opportunity for contact between a potential infectious agent and a host. It is the broadest stage in the conceptual sequence because it does not require a stable association. When an organism is exposed, the agent may be carried past a relevant body surface, remain in an external compartment, or be removed before meaningful cellular contact occurs. The outcome is not determined by exposure alone. One must ask whether a suitable anatomical site is accessible and whether the agent is still biologically competent at the time of contact. Exposure and infection therefore belong to different categories of evidence.

Attachment, or adhesion, means association with a surface that persists beyond the initial physical encounter. For a cellular microorganism this may involve several components of its external structure interacting with substances on a host tissue. For a virus the comparable first step is often called adsorption or attachment to an accessible host-cell surface. The word adsorption in this context denotes association at an interface; it does not mean absorption into a tissue or passage into the cell. Attachment can be reversible, and an attached entity can subsequently detach or be removed. A photograph showing objects at a cellular surface cannot on its own establish internalization.

Colonization refers to establishment of an association with a host site, commonly involving persistence or multiplication of cellular microorganisms on a normally exposed surface. The exact operational definition depends on the biological system and clinical context, but it is particularly useful when an organism resides at a site without producing overt disease. Colonization is more informative than a single detection event because it implies an established local relationship. It does not imply that the organism has invaded the cytoplasm of neighboring cells. A host-associated bacterial population on a mucosal surface illustrates the spatial distinction: the bacteria can be in close contact with epithelial cells while remaining extracellular.

Infection generally refers to establishment of an infectious agent within a host, involving its biological activity or replication in the relevant context. Definitions vary somewhat between microbiology and clinical usage; the crucial distinction here is that infection need not entail symptomatic disease. Viral infection is commonly discussed at the cell level, where entry and subsequent biological activity are considered. Bacterial infection is often described at the tissue or organismal level, including extracellular infections. The term cannot be equated in all cases with penetration of a host-cell membrane. Indeed, the choice between cell-level and tissue-level language changes how a statement about infection should be interpreted.

Invasion is passage beyond a defined boundary. A microorganism may invade a tissue by crossing a surface epithelium, or it may invade an individual cell by crossing that cell's plasma membrane. These are distinct spatial transitions. The term should always prompt the question: which boundary has been crossed? A microorganism located between cells in a tissue can be invasive at the tissue level yet remain extracellular at the cellular level. Conversely, an intracellular agent in one superficial cell may not yet have disseminated through the surrounding tissue. Correct classification therefore requires explicit identification of the spatial compartment.

Establishment after entry is a separate issue. A host cell or extracellular niche may not provide the conditions needed for sustained biological activity. A cell can bind an agent and internalize it without subsequently supporting reproduction. An initial microbial association may disappear when the host environment changes or the agent is cleared. Survival, persistence and production of new infectious entities are therefore not automatic consequences of reaching a host compartment. In the case of viruses, productive infection refers to an infection resulting in the formation of progeny virus, while an abortive infection fails to complete that outcome. Entry alone is not proof of productive infection.

Dissemination refers to spread beyond the initial site. Spread may be restricted to neighboring cells, extend through nearby tissue, or involve transport to distant sites. The adjective local describes confinement to a limited anatomical region; systemic describes a much broader distribution, often involving distant parts of an organism. A systemic physiological response does not by itself establish systemic dissemination of the agent. For example, a change in whole-organism physiology could result from a soluble product or a host signaling molecule arising at a localized site. To classify the spread of infection, evidence must concern the location of the agent or its relevant biological activity, rather than the location of every observed consequence.

Disease is the outcome in which normal function or structure is disturbed. Injury can result from the agent, its products, the host response, or a combination of these. Some infection states produce little detectable injury. Other states may produce progressive functional disturbance. Therefore, a diagram showing exposure, adhesion, colonization, invasion and disease should contain branches, not just a single uninterrupted arrow. Clearance and non-damaging persistence are biologically meaningful alternatives. Some routes also bypass steps that are characteristic of different agents; for example, an intracellular viral replication cycle should not be forced into a bacterial surface-colonization model.

Time adds another independent dimension. The incubation period is the interval between infection or exposure, as operationally defined in a given setting, and the appearance of clinical disease manifestations. It is not a synonym for the duration of attachment, the duration of intracellular replication, or the period during which an agent can be transmitted. A symptom-free interval may contain substantial biological activity. Likewise, persistent detection may outlast a particular functional disturbance. Distinguishing the time of an event from the time at which it becomes observable is central to interpreting an infection time course.

The most useful examination approach is to ask which transition is directly supported by a stated observation. Detecting an agent at an exposed site supports presence at that site. Evidence that its association persists can support colonization in the appropriate context. Intracellular localization provides evidence about entry but not necessarily replication. A change in normal tissue function demonstrates injury but does not automatically establish the distribution of the causative agent. Combining these observations creates a more complete explanation than treating all positive findings as equivalent indications of infection.
