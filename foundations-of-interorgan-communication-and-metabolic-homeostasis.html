1. The biological problem: coordinating specialized organs

A multicellular animal faces a problem that a single free-living cell does not face in the same form. Nutrients enter through a restricted set of surfaces, whereas thousands of millions of cells consume them at different rates. In a mammal, the intestine absorbs molecules from food, but most skeletal muscle fibres are far from the intestinal epithelium. The liver can release glucose into the circulation, whereas erythrocytes continuously consume glucose and cannot replace glycolysis with mitochondrial oxidation. Adipose tissue stores a large reserve of chemical energy, but this reserve must be made available at the correct rate. An increase in local energy demand cannot always be satisfied from local storage. The organization of separate organs therefore creates a need for both distribution and regulation.

Consider the transition from resting before a meal to absorbing carbohydrate after eating. A rise in circulating glucose is useful because tissues can replenish their fuel supplies. If every organ continued operating according to its premeal instructions, however, hepatic glucose release could persist while new glucose was entering from the intestine. The sum of these inputs could exceed glucose removal. Conversely, if all tissues stopped releasing fuel during a prolonged interval between meals, cells with continuing glucose requirements would face a shortage. Coordinated regulation must alter both supply and use, rather than simply increasing or decreasing every metabolic reaction in parallel.

Homeostasis describes regulation that constrains an internal variable within a functionally suitable range despite disturbances. It is a dynamic process rather than a condition in which all reactions cease or every measurement remains constant. For example, glucose can enter and leave the extracellular fluid at substantial rates while its concentration changes very little. The rate of replacement, not just the amount present, is physiologically important. The term metabolic homeostasis encompasses regulation of the availability, distribution, storage and transformation of substrates across time and tissues. Some quantities, such as glucose concentration, can be measured directly; others, such as the capacity to respond to a sudden metabolic demand, require a perturbation and observation of recovery.

Interorgan communication occurs when activity in one organ influences another through a transferable signal or a connected neural pathway. This does not imply that every correlation between organs represents a purposeful message. Two organs may respond independently to the same circulating nutrient, or a change in blood flow may alter both without direct communication between them. A convincing explanation must specify a causal connection rather than merely drawing an arrow between organ names. The useful unit of reasoning is the sender, the stimulus that alters the sender, the signal, its route, the recipient and the measurable response.

Why specialization creates a coordination problem

A cell can control its own metabolic reactions, but it cannot independently determine the concentration of every extracellular nutrient. A hepatocyte encounters blood that has already passed through other vascular beds, and a muscle fibre encounters fuel delivered according to both cardiovascular function and the activity of other tissues. The capacity of the circulatory system to carry molecules is therefore distinct from the capacity of an individual tissue to use them. Delivery can be plentiful while cellular uptake is limited by a transporter or an enzyme. Conversely, a tissue may possess an active pathway for using a substrate but receive too little of that substrate to sustain its normal rate of metabolism. Systemic regulation must reconcile these two kinds of limitation.

The chemical energy that enters an organism is also not identical to ATP in its cells. ATP couples energy-releasing reactions to processes such as ion pumping, contraction and biosynthesis. It is continually regenerated locally; the liver does not normally supply ATP in bulk to power distant muscle fibres. Instead, organs exchange fuels from which their cells can regenerate ATP. The existence of a common circulation does not make every organ metabolically interchangeable. Mature mammalian erythrocytes lack mitochondria and obtain ATP by glycolysis, while a contracting skeletal muscle may adjust the relative contribution of glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidation. A single circulating pool can thus support very different metabolic strategies.

Storage creates a further problem of timing. Glycogen is a polymer of glucose residues that provides a rapidly accessible carbohydrate reserve, and triglyceride in adipose tissue is a more concentrated form of stored chemical energy. The tissue that contains a reserve may not be the tissue that needs its energy. Liver glycogen can contribute to circulating glucose because liver cells possess the machinery needed to release free glucose. In contrast, the usual role of muscle glycogen is to support the muscle itself. Even if total body glycogen is large, its anatomical distribution determines the fraction that can readily contribute directly to the circulating glucose pool. A prediction based solely on total glycogen therefore misses an important physiological constraint.

From the internal environment to distributed regulation

The historical idea of an internal environment associated with Claude Bernard helped establish the distinction between external conditions and the relatively protected chemical surroundings of cells. Walter Cannon subsequently developed the physiological concept of homeostasis, emphasizing coordinated processes that maintain internal stability. Neither idea requires the internal environment to be chemically unchanging. It is more useful to ask which changes can be tolerated, which would impair function and how quickly compensatory processes can operate. These questions connect the historical ideas to present-day metabolism: a rise in glucose after a meal is not necessarily a failure of regulation, but a persistent inability to remove or supply glucose appropriately signals a problem with control.

Early physiological investigations often inferred that an organ released an active substance before the identity of that substance was known. A key conceptual advance was the realization that a chemical made at one site could change activity at another site even without direct contact. The study of pancreatic hormones demonstrated that circulating factors could regulate fuel availability, while later work on fat-derived hormones challenged the idea that adipose tissue was merely a passive storage depot. These developments extended, rather than replaced, the concept of neural control. An animal uses multiple forms of communication because neither a single gland nor a single neuron can independently monitor all relevant variables and coordinate every metabolic response.

The word integration should not be confused with centralization. Some responses can occur locally without a signal from the brain. An increase in a muscle fibre's ATP demand alters local metabolism, and an increase in extracellular glucose can change the secretory activity of pancreatic beta cells. Other responses require signals between tissues, such as the redistribution of fuel from adipose tissue during an interval without food. Central neural circuits contribute information about the environment, behavior and the physiological state, but the regulatory network also contains peripheral sensors. This is a distributed system: its organization emerges from the interaction of several control processes with distinct locations, delays and capacities.

Imagine that two organs both increase their activity during exercise. Before identifying a signal, consider three possible explanations. A central neural command might independently influence both organs; a circulating hormone might act on both; or one organ might release a factor that influences the other. The observations are consistent with any of these structures. If a diagram shows only two organ names and one arrow, it silently selects the third explanation. The remaining sections develop the reasoning needed to discriminate these alternatives. Begin each mechanistic account by specifying what the receiving organ must do differently and what evidence links that change to the proposed sender.

2. Energy balance, energy homeostasis and glucose homeostasis

Energy balance is a statement of conservation of energy. Over a defined interval, the change in chemical energy stored by an organism equals the energy taken in and available to the body minus the energy expended. If the input is defined as metabolizable energy, losses that have already been excluded from that input should not be subtracted again. Expenditure includes the energy ultimately dissipated as heat and transferred to the surroundings as external work. Use the same units and interval for every term. A positive balance increases stored chemical energy, but does not uniquely specify which tissue or molecule contains that energy.

Suppose a hypothetical animal obtains 8.4 megajoules of metabolizable energy in one day and expends 8.0 megajoules. The difference is 0.4 megajoules, or 400 kilojoules. The value specifies a daily change in chemical energy, not a directly calculable mass of adipose tissue. A conversion to body mass would require assumptions about water, glycogen, protein and fat deposition. The same animal might use stored fuel overnight and replace it during the following day while achieving approximate energy balance over a longer interval. A single meal cannot determine the direction of the balance over an entire week.

Energy homeostasis refers to the regulatory processes that coordinate energy acquisition, expenditure and reserves with the requirements of the organism. A regulatory system may respond to changes in availability or reserve status without restoring exact balance immediately. Growing animals, pregnant animals and animals recovering from depletion may accumulate energy as part of normal physiology. An organism exposed to an unexpected shortage cannot manufacture energy; the response changes behavior and fuel allocation within conservation constraints. This distinction is especially important when interpreting changes in body weight, appetite or metabolic rate.

Glucose homeostasis concerns the concentration and availability of glucose, not the entire store of energy. An animal with ample triglyceride reserves can still require hepatic or renal glucose production during fasting. Fatty acids with even-numbered carbon chains do not provide a net source of glucose in mammals through conversion of acetyl-CoA to glucose; glycerol released from triglycerides can contribute to gluconeogenesis. Some tissues depend on glucose more strongly than others. Thus maintaining glucose availability is compatible with a negative whole-body energy balance, and an apparently normal glucose concentration is compatible with altered insulin secretion or altered substrate flux.

A minimal glucose balance model uses a pool amount M and rates of appearance and disappearance. Over a short interval with approximately constant effective distribution volume V, M equals V times concentration C. The rate of change of M is the total appearance rate minus the total disappearance rate. If both are expressed in millimoles per minute and V in litres, dividing their difference by V gives the rate of concentration change in millimoles per litre per minute. This idealized model ignores distribution delays and assumes the relevant pool is adequately mixed. It is useful for establishing direction of change but cannot identify which organ caused the change without additional measurements.

Defining the boundary of an energy balance

In a conservation equation the chosen system boundary determines which terms appear. If the boundary encloses the entire animal, transfer of fatty acids from adipose tissue to muscle is internal and cancels when accounting for the body as a whole. The same transfer is an output in the adipose balance and an input in the muscle balance. Oxygen consumption provides information about oxidation, but oxygen itself is not counted as dietary chemical energy in the standard nutritional accounting convention. A complete description of energy stores includes not only lipid but also carbohydrate and protein. Energy released by oxidation does not disappear; it is transformed, with heat and work leaving the chosen boundary. When an expenditure measurement already includes these outputs, they must not be added again.

Write the average energy balance over a day as ΔE = Ein − Eout. For a rate equation, dE/dt = Pin − Pout, the symbol P represents energy per time, not a nutrient concentration. A numerical value of 100 kilojoules per hour is equivalent to approximately 27.8 watts because a watt is a joule per second. This conversion does not make the energy balance a measurement of mechanical power output; the body can dissipate substantial energy as heat. As an illustrative example, metabolizable intake of 9.0 megajoules per day and expenditure of 9.3 megajoules per day imply −0.3 megajoules per day. If these rates persisted for four days, the accumulated change would be −1.2 megajoules. The calculation assumes the rates remain unchanged, which is not guaranteed when the organism adapts its activity or metabolism.

Energy homeostasis concerns the mechanisms that change those rates. In one situation, a decrease in intake might stimulate feeding; in another situation, an animal may be unable to obtain food even though the feeding response is strong. An endocrine signal that influences appetite can alter the input term, while a sympathetic signal that changes thermogenesis can alter the expenditure term. Both responses belong to energy homeostasis, but neither violates energy balance. In experimental animals it is important to measure activity, food intake, body composition and relevant environmental conditions separately, rather than assigning a change in stored energy to appetite or expenditure solely from body mass measurements. Temperature is especially important when comparing small mammals because maintenance of body temperature contributes substantially to energy demand.

Concentration versus amount and turnover

Imagine a well-mixed vessel whose volume is 5 litres and whose glucose concentration is 5 millimoles per litre. The amount in the vessel is 25 millimoles. If glucose enters at 2.5 millimoles per minute and leaves at the same rate, the pool remains at 25 millimoles even though half of that amount is equivalent to approximately five minutes of gross entry. No individual molecule is obliged to remain for that duration; the ratio of pool size to exit rate is a characteristic turnover time under the simple assumptions of steady mixing and constant rates. Now increase both rates to 5 millimoles per minute. The concentration remains the same, but turnover becomes faster. Measuring concentration alone cannot discriminate these states. This vessel is an illustrative model, not an estimate of the actual effective glucose distribution volume of a human.

The distinction becomes more important when several sources feed the same pool. Suppose intestinal appearance contributes 0.6 millimoles per minute, hepatic production contributes 0.9 and renal production contributes 0.1. The total appearance is 1.6 millimoles per minute. If total disappearance is 1.6, the pool is stable. A different physiological state could produce the same total appearance from the liver and kidney alone, with no intestinal entry. The measured concentration and even total appearance rate may match, yet organ-specific metabolism differs. Independent information about the sources is required, such as organ balance measurements or isotopic approaches. These techniques address distinct questions: one can quantify net exchange across a tissue, while another estimates the rate at which a labelled or unlabelled substance enters a defined pool.

If the pool volume changes, the simple concentration equation must be adjusted. Starting from M = VC, differentiation gives dM/dt = V dC/dt + C dV/dt. Subtract the volume-dependent term before dividing by V to obtain dC/dt. A concentration may decline because the amount falls, because the distribution volume increases, or because both happen. An examination question that reports only concentration and a change in fluid volume therefore cannot be interpreted as a direct measurement of metabolic removal. In intact animals the effective distribution volume is also a model parameter, because equilibration among compartments is not instantaneous.

What a stable value does not reveal

A healthy regulatory response may preserve extracellular glucose while cellular fuel selection changes substantially. For example, adipose triglyceride breakdown can supply fatty acids for oxidative tissues, thereby reducing the pressure to use glucose as fuel. The released glycerol can contribute carbon to hepatic glucose synthesis. Under these conditions the animal may be depleting its overall chemical reserves even while glucose is maintained. Blood glucose is an important variable because particular cells have limited alternatives, but it is not a universal meter of energy availability. The term glucose homeostasis should therefore not be extended to every change in circulating lipid, oxygen consumption or appetite.

The reciprocal problem is that a seemingly normal concentration may be achieved at greater regulatory cost. Consider two hypothetical animals with similar fasting glucose but markedly different insulin concentrations. If the second animal requires a greater insulin signal to maintain glucose disposal and restrain glucose production, its apparent glucose stability may conceal reduced tissue responsiveness. Alternative explanations, such as a difference in insulin clearance, must also be considered before assigning the mechanism. The observational pair does not by itself establish the cause. This example links the foundational distinction between a controlled variable and its controller to the later study of insulin resistance without introducing clinical diagnostic criteria.

3. Communication routes and target specificity

Signals are classified by their route and biological context, not solely by molecular identity. An autocrine signal alters the activity of the cell that released it. A paracrine signal diffuses or is otherwise transferred to nearby cells. Juxtacrine communication requires close cellular contact, for example through membrane-bound ligands or gap junctions. Endocrine signalling involves delivery through the circulation to responsive cells at distant sites. Neural communication uses electrical propagation along neurons and chemical or electrical transmission at specialized contacts. Neuroendocrine cells combine neuronal activation with secretion of a substance into the circulation.

One molecule can participate in more than one route. Noradrenaline released by sympathetic nerve terminals can act locally on an innervated tissue; catecholamines released by the adrenal medulla enter the bloodstream and reach distant organs. A cytokine secreted within adipose tissue can affect nearby immune cells and, under some circumstances, reach other tissues. Accordingly, a question that asks whether a particular molecule is always paracrine or always endocrine may contain a misleading premise. The location of release, route of delivery and target response are decisive.

The distribution of receptors gives a circulating hormone selectivity despite its broad exposure. A blood sample may contain insulin, glucagon and adrenaline simultaneously, yet each tissue interprets these signals using its own complement of receptors, transport proteins, intracellular enzymes and transcriptional programs. Even if two cell types express the same receptor, their downstream responses need not be identical. Skeletal muscle can accumulate glycogen in response to insulin, but cannot export free glucose from that glycogen to the blood in the manner of the liver. The distinction arises from organ-specific metabolic machinery as well as the hormone signal itself.

Signal concentration at a recipient is influenced by secretion, delivery, dilution, binding, degradation and clearance. Portal circulation supplies a particularly important example: material absorbed from the intestinal tract and hormones from the pancreas can reach the liver before mixing fully with the systemic circulation. Peripheral venous measurements therefore do not necessarily reproduce the concentration encountered by hepatocytes. A measured rise in circulating concentration can reflect slower removal rather than increased secretion. If a question provides only hormone concentrations, the underlying rate of secretion is not necessarily identifiable.

An endocrine message does not move instantaneously. It must enter the blood, be distributed, interact with a recipient and produce a response. Neural signalling can operate quickly and with spatial specificity, but its effects can also persist through changes in enzyme activity or gene expression. Hormones can likewise act quickly through phosphorylation or slowly through transcription. It is more useful to compare specific mechanisms than to claim that hormones are always slow and nerves always fast.

Route, range and the identity of a message

The classification of cell communication often begins with direct contact. Gap junctions allow small molecules and ions to move between adjacent cells through a continuous aqueous connection, while membrane-bound ligands may engage receptors on neighboring cells. Neither arrangement alone can connect two distant organs that lack direct contact. Endocrine communication solves distance through vascular transport, and neural pathways solve distance through cellular processes that extend from a neuronal cell body to remote targets. A neuroendocrine cell combines elements of these strategies: its activity is controlled by electrical and synaptic events, but its released product enters the circulation. Thus the anatomical nature of the route and the chemical nature of the message are separate classification criteria.

Paracrine and autocrine signalling remain important even when the subject is communication between organs. For example, communication among pancreatic islet cells can modify the amount and pattern of hormone entering the blood. The resulting endocrine message is partly determined by these local interactions. A distant organ might then respond by changing a substrate concentration, which in turn influences the original sender. An examination problem may ask whether an intervention affects the interorgan pathway or an upstream local control step. A reduction in circulating hormone after inhibition of a local islet signal does not make the original local signal endocrine; it changes the generation of an endocrine output.

Blood flow, binding and response thresholds

A soluble ligand released into the blood is carried by convection and can reach many tissues, but vascular exposure is not necessarily uniform. Tissue blood flow affects how much ligand is presented per unit time, capillary properties influence access to cells, and binding proteins can affect the fraction available for receptor interaction. The measured plasma concentration of a protein-bound hormone includes both bound and unbound forms unless a method specifically distinguishes them. Depending on the ligand and binding system, association can provide a circulating reserve or alter clearance, while receptor interaction depends on accessible ligand. These qualifications explain why a plasma measurement cannot always be used as a direct measure of receptor occupancy.

A simplified receptor model helps separate ligand concentration from receptor abundance. For a single class of independent binding sites at equilibrium, the fractional occupancy is L/(Kd + L), where L is the free ligand concentration and Kd is the dissociation constant. Both quantities must have matching concentration units. If L equals Kd, half the sites are occupied; if L is nine times Kd, nine tenths are occupied. The model assumes simple reversible binding without cooperativity or ligand depletion. It predicts a fraction, not necessarily the physiological effect, because downstream pathways may amplify a signal or become limited at another step. Two tissues can show identical fractional occupancy and different responses if the number of receptors or coupling efficiency differs.

Consider two recipient tissues with the same receptor affinity, but one expresses twice as many receptors per cell. At a ligand concentration equal to the dissociation constant, each has half of its receptors occupied, but the tissue with more receptors has twice the number of occupied receptors per cell. Even this does not guarantee twice the biological effect, because the amount of downstream enzyme may be limiting. Conversely, a ligand may produce a near-maximal response at a concentration that occupies fewer than half of the receptors if downstream amplification is large. Pharmacological dose-response experiments therefore distinguish binding parameters from effect parameters. This elementary distinction is useful in physiology because a normal endocrine concentration is not sufficient evidence of normal target sensitivity.

Secretion and clearance establish signal abundance

A model for the circulating amount A of a signalling molecule is dA/dt = S − kA, where S is its secretion rate in amount per time and k is a first-order clearance constant in inverse time. When S and k remain constant, the steady amount is S/k. If secretion is stopped abruptly, the amount decreases exponentially, and its half-life is ln(2)/k. For a hypothetical factor with k = 0.1 per minute and S = 5 nanomoles per minute, the steady amount is 50 nanomoles and the half-life after secretion stops is about 6.9 minutes. Halving k with unchanged secretion would double the steady amount. Such a change could be mistaken for increased secretion if only a single blood measurement were available. The example assumes linear elimination and constant distribution volume; actual hormones can have more complicated kinetics.

Changes in delivery and clearance can be consequences of regulation rather than incidental disturbances. The liver may extract a substantial fraction of a hormone delivered through the portal circulation; the kidney participates in clearance of various circulating molecules. A difference between arterial and venous concentrations may result from binding, uptake, degradation or release, depending on the molecule and tissue. In a multi-organ network, the sender of a hormone is not the only determinant of its circulating abundance. This principle is important whenever a candidate organokine is identified by comparing concentrations before and after an intervention. A measured rise must be followed by evidence about its source, kinetics and target effects.

The speed of a signal is also not determined solely by its route. Neuronal action potentials can propagate rapidly, but an autonomic effect that changes transcription may develop over a longer interval. A circulating peptide can act rapidly when it reaches cells equipped with an appropriate signalling pathway. Steroid-related changes in gene expression can require more time, although not all steroid effects depend on new transcription. The relevant timescale is the complete sequence from stimulus to altered tissue function. A correct mechanistic explanation distinguishes the delay in transmission from the delay in the effector mechanism and avoids classifying every endocrine response as slow.

4. Endocrine and neural coordination

Pancreatic endocrine cells illustrate how a local sensor can produce a systemic response. Beta cells sense changes in nutrient availability and release insulin through regulated exocytosis. The familiar glucose-dependent mechanism includes intracellular glucose metabolism, an increase in ATP relative to ADP, closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels, depolarization and opening of voltage-dependent calcium channels. Calcium entry promotes secretion of stored insulin. Additional nutrients, gut hormones, autonomic signals and local islet communication modify this basic response. It is therefore not appropriate to treat glucose concentration as the sole determinant of secretion.

Insulin binds its receptor tyrosine kinase and influences downstream signalling involving IRS proteins, phosphoinositide 3-kinase and Akt. In muscle and adipose cells, one important consequence is recruitment of GLUT4 transporters to the cell surface. In hepatocytes, insulin changes net glucose handling largely through regulation of metabolic enzymes and pathways rather than through insulin-stimulated recruitment of GLUT4. The liver is exposed to portal insulin and also responds to changes in substrates supplied by other organs. A single molecular intervention may consequently produce several direct and indirect changes in hepatic glucose output.

Glucagon acts predominantly on the liver to support glucose production, particularly when the balance of signals favours mobilization rather than storage. Its receptor is coupled to G proteins that can stimulate adenylyl cyclase, producing cyclic AMP and activating protein kinase A. The pathway can change the activity of enzymes involved in glycogen breakdown and glucose production. Insulin and glucagon should be understood as coordinated and context-dependent rather than as exact mirror images in every tissue. Amino acids, for instance, can stimulate glucagon release even in circumstances in which insulin is also secreted.

Neural communication can carry information from peripheral sensors to the central nervous system. Afferent fibres conduct information toward the central nervous system, whereas efferent fibres carry output toward targets. In visceral physiology these pathways include vagal and spinal afferents and autonomic efferents. A meal can generate neural information about mechanical distension and chemical composition before all nutrients have reached the systemic circulation. Central processing can alter autonomic activity, behavior and endocrine responses. The nervous system is therefore part of the same metabolic network as the pancreas, intestine and liver, not an alternative to endocrine regulation.

An anticipatory change in physiology triggered by a reliable sensory or contextual cue is feedforward control. It can prepare an organism for a disturbance before the regulated quantity changes substantially. Such a response need not be perfectly predictive. Sensory input associated with eating may evoke preparatory digestive and metabolic responses, but the magnitude and reproducibility of anticipatory insulin release depend on species and experimental conditions. Once absorption begins, nutrient-dependent feedback provides further regulation. Feedforward control reduces the initial disturbance; feedback responds to departures and limits the consequences of inaccurate predictions.

A secretion mechanism converts chemistry into information

A pancreatic beta cell is a useful example because its stimulus is both a nutrient and an indicator of changing nutrient availability. Glucose enters the cell through facilitative transporters, whose relative importance differs between species. In human beta cells GLUT1 and GLUT3 contribute substantially, whereas GLUT2 is prominent in the classical rodent model. Glucokinase phosphorylates glucose and is important for coupling extracellular availability to metabolic rate. As glucose metabolism increases, changes in cellular adenine nucleotides favor closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. The reduction in outward potassium conductance depolarizes the membrane. Depolarization opens voltage-dependent calcium channels, and the increase in intracellular calcium promotes fusion of insulin-containing granules with the cell membrane. At each stage there is a distinct measurable variable: substrate availability, metabolic activity, channel conductance, membrane potential, calcium and secretion.

This sequence provides a way to predict the effects of interventions. In an isolated beta cell, an intervention that maintains high potassium conductance despite an increase in glucose can prevent the depolarization needed for normal calcium entry. An intervention that blocks the relevant calcium channels can reduce secretion even if the membrane is depolarized. Direct depolarization might bypass a defect in upstream nutrient sensing, but it cannot bypass every possible downstream defect. The logic is similar to pathway ordering in genetics: if one experimental manipulation restores a response when another component is defective, the bypass helps locate the defect, although interpretation depends on the specificity of the manipulations. In a whole animal, changes in glucose and other hormones introduce additional compensatory effects that are absent from isolated cell experiments.

Insulin secretion has amplifying influences in addition to the triggering sequence. A rise in intracellular calcium is important but is not the only determinant of the amount released. Metabolic coupling, local signalling within the islet and intestinal endocrine signals can modify secretion for a given glucose stimulus. The point is not to learn every component of secretion in this introductory chapter; that belongs to pancreatic physiology and the later gut–pancreas chapter. Rather, the example demonstrates that a metabolic signal can be processed by several convergent pathways. A hormone concentration measured after a meal represents the combined operation of these influences and of clearance, not a direct measure of any one intracellular event.

How the recipient decodes a shared hormone

Insulin's receptor possesses intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity. Its activation changes the phosphorylation of intracellular substrates and leads to signalling through several pathways, including PI3K and Akt. In skeletal muscle and adipose tissue, appropriate signalling increases the presence of GLUT4 in the plasma membrane by altering the movement of intracellular transporter-containing vesicles. The increase in transport capacity is useful when glucose is abundant. In muscle, however, incoming glucose can be oxidized or stored depending on the state of the cell and its energetic requirements. Insulin does not command all molecules entering muscle to follow one metabolic fate. Understanding the receptor pathway is necessary for explaining uptake, but a complete prediction of substrate use also requires information about demand and intracellular metabolism.

Hepatic cells encounter the same hormone but respond with different emphasis. In the fed state, insulin contributes to the regulation of hepatic glycogen synthesis and glucose production. Because hepatocyte glucose transport is not governed by the classic insulin-stimulated GLUT4 mechanism, a student should not transfer the muscle model unchanged to the liver. Insulin can also alter liver metabolism indirectly by restraining the supply of fatty acids and glycerol from adipose tissue. The distinction between direct and indirect regulation is experimentally important: an intervention that changes circulating insulin may simultaneously alter liver signalling, adipose substrate release and other hormonal responses. A measured change in liver glucose output cannot automatically be assigned to direct hepatic insulin receptor activation.

Glucagon illustrates a different receptor class. Binding to its hepatic G protein-coupled receptor stimulates a pathway involving adenylyl cyclase, cyclic AMP and protein kinase A. In a physiological context favoring mobilization of reserves, these signalling events help increase hepatic glucose availability. Insulin and glucagon can produce opposing effects on some aspects of hepatic metabolism without being symmetrical opposites in every tissue. A rise in glucagon is not equivalent to a withdrawal of insulin, because the pathways, receptors and target distribution are different. Likewise, a ratio of circulating insulin to glucagon may describe the relative hormonal environment but cannot replace direct measurements of each hormone and tissue response.

Neural output and metabolic coordination

An afferent pathway conveys information from a peripheral sensor toward central neural structures. An efferent pathway conveys output toward a peripheral target. These terms refer to direction relative to the central nervous system and not to whether an effect increases or decreases a physiological variable. For example, neural signals from the digestive tract can convey information about the presence of a meal. Autonomic output can then modify digestive function, pancreatic secretion and activity of other metabolic organs. A single reflex may include both an afferent and an efferent limb, and interruption of either can alter its function. The same organ can also communicate by blood-borne signals, so neural interruption need not abolish all regulation.

Sympathetic output provides an example of targeted regulation that is not reducible to a single circulating catecholamine concentration. Noradrenaline released near effector cells can produce local actions, whereas adrenaline released by the adrenal medulla reaches targets via blood. During a challenge, these mechanisms may act concurrently. Activation of appropriate adrenergic receptors in adipose tissue can promote mobilization of lipid reserves, and autonomic regulation of blood flow can modify substrate delivery. The net response depends on receptor subtype, tissue and physiological context. A prediction made from the word sympathetic alone is therefore less reliable than a prediction that identifies the target and receptor-mediated effect.

Neural and endocrine influences often cooperate. Imagine an animal exposed to a cue that reliably precedes food. Neural pathways may prepare digestive functions before absorbed nutrients substantially change blood chemistry. Once nutrient absorption begins, hormonal feedback becomes increasingly important. The early response may reduce the magnitude of the later disturbance but is not necessarily sufficient to maintain glucose by itself. If the expected food is not delivered, the anticipatory response must eventually be adjusted. This illustrates a limitation of feedforward control: it relies on a prediction and may be inappropriate when the predicted event fails to occur. Feedback supplies information about the actual outcome.

5. Substrates, metabolites and immune-derived signals

The bloodstream transports substrates that are both material resources and possible sources of information. Glucose entering a beta cell is metabolized and contributes to the intracellular changes that promote insulin secretion. In this context the nutrient is not merely burned or stored; its metabolism is part of a sensory mechanism. Fatty acids and amino acids can also influence receptors or intracellular nutrient-sensitive pathways. A metabolite does not have to be a protein or classical gland-derived hormone in order to alter the activity of a distant cell.

The dual role of metabolites creates experimental complications. Suppose a compound released by exercising muscle increases oxygen consumption in a cultured recipient. This could occur because the compound is oxidized as fuel, because it binds a receptor that activates a pathway, or because it alters pH or osmotic conditions. A dose-matched substrate control, appropriate buffer controls, receptor perturbation and direct measurements of substrate oxidation would help distinguish these possibilities. Demonstrating uptake of a labelled metabolite does not by itself prove receptor-mediated signalling.

Lactate illustrates a second important distinction. Glycolytic tissues can release lactate and other tissues can oxidize it or convert its carbon skeleton to glucose. Lactate is thus a transferable substrate rather than simply an unusable waste product. In appropriate settings lactate can also influence signalling pathways. Evidence of a change in circulating lactate during exercise does not establish that a particular distant response is mediated by lactate signalling; supply, demand, acid-base changes and other concurrent signals need to be considered. The detailed physiological cycles of carbon exchange belong to Node 7.L.05.

Immune cells respond to infection, tissue damage and metabolic cues. Their products, including cytokines, can modify the behavior of adipocytes, hepatocytes, endothelial cells and other recipients. Tumor necrosis factor and interleukin-6 are examples of molecules with context-dependent metabolic effects. Sustained inflammatory signalling can interfere with normal insulin responses, but a transient rise of a cytokine during physiological exercise cannot be interpreted as equivalent to prolonged inflammatory exposure. Duration, source, coexisting signals and recipient sensitivity matter.

An adipose tissue sample contains mature adipocytes, stromal cells, vascular cells and immune cells. An increase in cytokine messenger RNA in the entire sample could reflect increased expression per cell, a change in the abundance of immune cells, or both. To establish a particular cellular origin, an investigator may compare separated cell populations or use cell-specific expression and loss-of-function approaches. The distinction between organ origin and cell origin is fundamental to the interpretation of interorgan signalling experiments.

Material exchange and information exchange can coexist

A circulating metabolite may participate in communication without being a classical gland-derived hormone. Its concentration can carry information about the activity of a tissue or the availability of a nutrient. A recipient can respond because the molecule is transported into the cell and metabolized, because it binds a receptor, or through a combination of the two mechanisms. A useful way to distinguish these possibilities is to ask whether the response requires conversion of the molecule to another compound. If it does, inhibition of the relevant metabolic conversion may remove the response. If the molecule acts through a surface receptor, a response may persist even when its use as fuel is prevented, provided receptor activity is preserved. Neither result alone excludes every alternative, because metabolic and receptor pathways can interact.

Fatty acids illustrate this duality. Adipose tissue releases non-esterified fatty acids during lipolysis, and much of the circulating non-esterified fatty acid is associated with albumin. Oxidative tissues can use this material as fuel, while the intracellular products of fatty-acid metabolism can influence regulatory processes. Some fatty acids also interact with specific sensing mechanisms. The amount delivered depends on mobilization, blood flow, binding and removal by recipient tissues. Measuring total fatty acids in a blood sample does not reveal the flux of any one fatty acid into muscle or liver, and an increase in fatty-acid oxidation after a change in availability is not by itself evidence of a receptor-mediated message. It may reflect increased substrate delivery to an existing metabolic pathway.

Lactate exchange provides a complementary example. A tissue with active glycolysis can convert pyruvate to lactate while regenerating NAD+ needed for continued glycolytic flux. Lactate can leave the cell through monocarboxylate transport mechanisms and be used by other tissues. The heart and oxidative muscle can oxidize lactate carbon, while the liver can use lactate as a precursor for glucose formation. These fates depend on transport, enzyme activities and the energetic state of the recipient. Lactate also can engage signalling mechanisms under appropriate circumstances. The important introductory distinction is between lactate as a transferable carbon substrate and lactate as a regulator of cell behavior; the existence of one role does not establish the other in a particular experiment.

The Cori cycle, introduced in metabolic biochemistry, helps explain why transfer of material is not necessarily transfer of net energy to the body as a whole. Conversion of glucose to lactate in a glycolytic tissue yields ATP locally, while resynthesis of glucose from lactate in the liver consumes energy. The process redistributes metabolic work between tissues and can help sustain glucose availability, but it cannot create energy without cost. If a student interprets an increase in lactate recycling as a new source of chemical energy, the conservation argument in the preceding section exposes the error. Detailed stoichiometry and physiological transitions are reserved for the integrated feeding, fasting and exercise node.

Immune signals and allocation of resources

Immune and metabolic processes are linked because immune activation changes both demand for nutrients and the signals that influence their distribution. Cytokines are secreted proteins that can act locally and, in some contexts, systemically. They are often named in relation to immune function, but many are also made by non-immune cells. During an acute response to infection, changes in cytokine concentrations can influence the liver, central nervous system, vascular tissues and other organs. These effects need not represent a pathological defect in homeostasis; redistribution of resources can be part of an adaptive response. The same general classes of signals can become associated with dysfunction when activation is persistent or inappropriate.

Tumour necrosis factor and interleukin-6 are useful examples of why the context of a cytokine matters. A cytokine can alter signal transduction, gene expression or cellular metabolic activities in a recipient. Persistent inflammatory stimulation may impair some insulin responses in susceptible tissues, whereas a transient response during exercise can accompany beneficial metabolic adaptation. One cannot infer the biological meaning of a cytokine merely by recognizing its name. Relevant information includes the producing cell type, duration and concentration of exposure, receptor system and the other signals present. Exercise-induced interleukin-6 is discussed here as an example of context dependence, not as a claim that every increase in IL-6 has the same origin or effect.

Whole tissue measurements are particularly challenging because tissues contain multiple populations. If an adipose sample shows a twofold increase in inflammatory gene expression, possibilities include increased expression per immune cell, increased abundance of immune cells, increased expression by adipocytes, or a combination. A careful investigation can compare separated or identified cell populations with the whole tissue and measure protein release as well as RNA abundance. Even increased protein in a tissue does not establish that the protein enters the circulation at a physiologically significant rate. Localization and transport evidence are necessary to move from a tissue observation to an interorgan mechanism.

A simple causal comparison highlights an additional distinction. Suppose cultured muscle cells respond to conditioned medium collected from activated immune cells. The medium contains many possible mediators, and its nutrient composition may differ from fresh medium. A control with matched nutrient composition helps distinguish depletion of fuel from a secreted regulatory effect. Neutralizing a candidate cytokine and comparing with a matched control tests its contribution, while adding back the purified cytokine at a physiologically plausible concentration tests whether the response can be restored. A result in culture establishes a possibility under those experimental conditions; establishing an endocrine effect in an animal requires evidence of delivery to the recipient and action at concentrations encountered in vivo.

6. Organokines: a source-based vocabulary

The term organokine is used for secreted factors associated with a particular organ and capable of influencing physiological processes. Adipokines are associated with adipose tissue, myokines with skeletal muscle, hepatokines with the liver and batokines with brown adipose tissue. These are source-based labels, not four chemically exclusive molecular families. An individual molecule may be secreted from more than one organ, and many factors have local effects in addition to any systemic action.

Leptin provides a strong example of the conversion of a storage tissue into a signalling organ. Adipocytes secrete leptin, whose circulating concentration is related to adipose reserves and current energy state. It acts through leptin receptors, including receptors in neural circuits involved in regulation of food intake and expenditure. A reduction in leptin during energy deficit can signal the need to conserve energy and increase the drive to obtain food. High leptin concentration is not synonymous with a strong effective leptin response, because receptor function and downstream responsiveness influence the outcome.

Adiponectin is another adipose-associated circulating protein. It is involved in regulation of lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity through receptor-dependent mechanisms. Its relationship to adipose tissue differs from that of leptin; concentration cannot simply be predicted from fat mass by assuming that all adipokines increase proportionally with adipocyte number. The properties of the adipose tissue, regulation of secretion and clearance are also relevant. For examination purposes it is valuable to compare the physiological functions rather than memorize the word adipokine as a synonym for a single effect.

Contracting skeletal muscle can release interleukin-6, which can participate in metabolic coordination during exercise. The same cytokine is produced by other cell populations in other contexts. Evidence that muscle releases a factor during contraction is not evidence that all circulating molecules of that type originated from muscle. Nor is a transient change during exercise sufficient to demonstrate an identical consequence during chronic disease. A useful experimental comparison includes a resting control, the time course of contraction and recovery, and measures of release across the muscle vascular bed.

Fibroblast growth factor 21 is an example of a factor associated with the liver that has endocrine metabolic actions and also can be produced outside the liver. Its secretion and actions depend on nutritional context and species. Signalling in recipient tissues requires appropriate receptor machinery, including the co-receptor beta-Klotho. The existence of a named hepatokine does not make the liver the exclusive site of synthesis or every tissue a recipient.

Brown adipose tissue consumes chemical energy to produce heat, particularly during thermogenic activation. It can also release molecules with possible local and distant regulatory effects. These molecules are termed batokines when considered in relation to brown fat as a source. The degree to which a particular candidate is necessary for whole-body regulation is a separate empirical question from whether its expression changes during cold exposure. For each proposed organokine, ask which cell releases it, under what conditions, in what quantity and whether a recipient response is demonstrable at physiological exposure.

Organokines classify sources, not exclusive molecular families

The source-based naming system reflects a change in the way physiologists view organs. A tissue traditionally described by a mechanical or metabolic function may also release molecules that alter distant physiology. Adipose tissue stores triglyceride, skeletal muscle produces force, liver transforms and distributes substrates, and brown adipose tissue dissipates chemical energy as heat. These functions create physiological states that could be communicated to other tissues. A signal derived from any of these organs may therefore carry information about storage, contraction, nutrient status or thermogenic activity. Such names are descriptive and not a formal biochemical classification. They do not specify whether a factor is a peptide, lipid, or another type of molecule, nor whether all of its effects are endocrine.

There are two important safeguards when naming a factor. First, detecting expression in an organ is not equivalent to demonstrating release from that organ. Second, a factor released from one tissue can also be released elsewhere. For example, the label myokine is appropriate when discussing interleukin-6 released by contracting skeletal muscle, but that usage does not redefine all interleukin-6 in the body as muscle derived. The term hepatokine describes a liver-associated secreted signal in a particular context. A factor with an extrahepatic source may still be a hepatokine when the liver is its demonstrated source. A rigorous explanation states the producing tissue and physiological stimulus instead of treating the label as proof of provenance.

Leptin and the logic of a reserve-related signal

A large store of triglyceride is spatially separated from neural circuits that influence food acquisition and energy expenditure. A circulating signal from adipose tissue can help connect these two parts of the system. Leptin is a protein hormone encoded by the LEP gene in humans and its corresponding gene in mice. Adipocytes are an important source, and circulating leptin generally reflects adipose stores, while also responding to shorter-term nutritional and hormonal conditions. Receptors in central neural circuits enable the signal to influence processes related to feeding and energy expenditure. The physiological role is best understood as part of a network rather than as an isolated appetite switch.

The distinction between signal deficiency and target insensitivity is central to the history of leptin. In the classical mouse models, an animal with a defect in the production of a circulating factor differs from an animal with a defect in responsiveness to that factor. Connecting circulations or supplying an active factor can potentially correct the first defect but need not correct the second. Positional cloning of the mouse obese gene by Zhang and colleagues in 1994 identified the gene associated with leptin. The earlier physiological observations and later molecular identification complement each other: one establishes a pattern consistent with a transferable signal and the other identifies a molecular candidate. Neither the phenotype of an individual mouse nor the concentration of leptin alone is sufficient to identify the defect.

In ordinary variation of adiposity, a high leptin concentration does not guarantee a proportionately large response in every target cell. Transport into relevant regions, receptor signalling and downstream circuit state can influence responsiveness. In addition, leptin concentration can fall during energy restriction before a large change in body fat has occurred. The hormone thus carries information related to both reserve status and the current physiological context. The detailed neural circuitry underlying its actions belongs to the next node; here the key principle is that a reserve-related signal helps coordinate behavior and expenditure with information from a peripheral organ.

Adiponectin, muscle signals and liver-derived factors

Adiponectin is a distinct adipose-derived protein and should not be interpreted as a duplicate of leptin. Its receptors and physiological effects differ. Evidence connects adiponectin signalling with regulation of lipid handling and insulin sensitivity in several tissues. Circulating adiponectin can show a relationship with adiposity different from that of leptin, illustrating that two factors released by the same organ need not encode the same information. The pattern of organokines depends on the functional state of the tissue and on the pathways governing expression, processing and release. An experiment that finds an increase in one adipokine cannot automatically infer a corresponding increase in another.

Muscle contraction changes intracellular calcium, ATP turnover, substrate use and mechanical conditions. These changes can influence the release of signalling factors, some of which act within muscle and some of which have proposed or established distant actions. Exercise-associated IL-6 is useful because studies have linked skeletal muscle activity to changes in its release. The response varies with exercise characteristics and physiological state. It cannot be interpreted by assuming that every increase in blood IL-6 reflects muscle production or that all distant responses to exercise require IL-6. Neural command, circulating catecholamines, mechanical activity and substrate exchange provide parallel routes by which exercise influences other tissues.

FGF21 illustrates nutrient-sensitive endocrine communication. It is a member of the fibroblast growth factor family whose metabolic actions involve receptor complexes including the co-receptor beta-Klotho in appropriate target tissues. Hepatic production is important in several nutritional and metabolic contexts, but expression is not restricted to the liver. Regulatory responses vary with the nature of nutritional challenges and across species. For introductory purposes it is enough to distinguish the hormone from the substrates whose availability helps alter its production, and to recognize the role of recipient receptor machinery. It would be incorrect to conclude from elevated FGF21 alone that a particular target response must be stronger: the concentration can be affected by both release and clearance, and tissue responsiveness may change.

Brown adipose tissue as an active participant

Brown adipose tissue contains cells specialized for thermogenic energy expenditure, in part through mitochondrial uncoupling protein 1. Thermogenic activity changes fuel requirements and can influence the exchange of nutrients between tissues. The tissue also releases candidate signalling factors sometimes called batokines. FGF21 is an example discussed in the literature in relation to several tissue sources, including brown adipose tissue in particular experimental contexts. Demonstrating that brown adipose cells can secrete a factor in culture is not equivalent to establishing that their secretion contributes substantially to circulating concentrations in humans. A tissue-specific perturbation, measurement of physiological exposure and demonstration of recipient response strengthen that conclusion. This distinction is especially valuable for emerging organokine research, where the existence of a secreted molecule may be well supported but its systemic contribution remains uncertain.

7. Extracellular vesicles and the evidence problem

Extracellular vesicles are membrane-enclosed particles released from cells. They can contain proteins, lipids and nucleic acids, and their composition may differ from that of the parent cell. Vesicles are distinct from soluble hormones and from lipoprotein particles, although these entities can coexist in the same biological sample and may be difficult to separate. The general term extracellular vesicle is preferred when the mechanism of vesicle formation has not been established. A small diameter alone does not justify identifying a particle as an exosome, which refers to a particular endosomal origin.

Vesicles may interact with recipient cells at the cell surface, enter by uptake pathways or in some cases deliver material that changes cellular activity. The chain from release to function contains several separate steps. First, the donor must release the relevant material. Second, it must reach the recipient in an effective form. Third, it must engage a target or deliver biologically active cargo. Finally, the measured response must depend on that interaction rather than another component of the preparation. Observing fluorescent material in a cell does not establish all four steps. Labelling reagents may dissociate, form particles or label nonvesicular material.

A proposed adipose-to-liver vesicle signal can be evaluated by comparing material from donor cells in two metabolic states. The recipient response should be compared with the response to appropriately processed control medium and nonvesicular fractions. If a candidate cargo is suspected, a selective manipulation of that cargo in donor cells should alter the recipient response while other relevant properties are controlled. Evidence that the response is restored by a carefully designed rescue increases confidence. However, a very large administered dose may establish biological capacity without demonstrating that naturally circulating concentrations have the same effect.

The MISEV2023 consensus guidelines emphasize characterization, separation and appropriate functional controls in extracellular vesicle research. At examination level, the essential lesson is to distinguish the presence of vesicles, their cellular association, their cargo, and the physiological function attributed to them. None of these observations is interchangeable with the others. Extracellular vesicles are a plausible and experimentally supported communication mechanism in particular systems, but a specific proposed metabolic effect requires its own evidence.

How a vesicle differs from a freely dissolved hormone

A peptide hormone ordinarily interacts with receptors while it is accessible in extracellular fluid. An extracellular vesicle is a membrane-bounded object, and its biological effects can depend on both its surface and the materials enclosed within it. The lipid bilayer separates internal contents from the surrounding fluid and may protect some cargo from extracellular enzymes. However, that physical property does not establish that any particular enclosed molecule becomes available to the appropriate intracellular target. A vesicle could bind a recipient without entering it, could be internalized and degraded, or could deliver a functional component. These possibilities have different experimental implications. A demonstration of particle entry establishes only one part of the causal sequence required for cargo-mediated regulation.

Vesicles can arise by outward budding from the plasma membrane or through intracellular membrane trafficking followed by release of vesicles from multivesicular compartments. An exosome is associated with the latter route, whereas the broader term extracellular vesicle does not specify biogenesis. Because size distributions of particles from different routes can overlap, a size measurement cannot by itself identify the route by which a particular particle formed. MISEV2023 recommends descriptive terminology when origin is not established and emphasizes careful separation and characterization. For the purposes of systemic physiology, the main question is whether a cell releases membrane-enclosed material that reaches and affects a distant recipient, not whether the investigator can attach the most specific possible subtype name to a particle.

Suppose a blood sample contains particles bearing a membrane-associated protein also expressed by adipocytes. This observation may be consistent with adipose release but is not conclusive, because other cells may express the same protein or contribute similar particles. Circulating material also includes protein aggregates, lipoproteins and soluble proteins that can be recovered alongside vesicles. An apparent biological effect in a vesicle-enriched fraction might therefore reflect a contaminating soluble factor. Separation methods enrich populations according to physical or chemical properties but are not perfect classifiers of biological origin. The strength of a claim increases when different forms of evidence converge: physical characterization, source-specific evidence, appropriate controls and a reproducible functional response.

From cargo abundance to a quantitative functional hypothesis

A proposed RNA-mediated effect illustrates why the quantity of material matters. A recipient gene may respond when a regulatory RNA is introduced experimentally in large amounts, yet physiological vesicles may deliver much less RNA. To infer that an endogenous route changes gene expression, the experiment must establish plausible exposure, functional access and an effect at the relevant concentration. Consider an illustrative calculation in which an enriched preparation contains one million particles and an average of one copy of a particular RNA per hundred particles. This implies ten thousand copies in the preparation on average, not one copy in every particle. If the material is distributed among one thousand recipient cells, there are ten copies per cell on average before accounting for loss, unequal uptake or degradation. A small fraction reaching the relevant intracellular compartment could make functional delivery much lower. This is a deliberately hypothetical calculation, not a measurement of actual extracellular vesicles or a universal threshold for RNA activity.

An informative experiment might compare a preparation from stimulated donor cells with material from unstimulated donors, controlling for cell number and collection conditions. If the recipient responds only to material from stimulated donors, the next question is whether the activity resides in vesicles or in another component of the preparation. A matched fraction depleted of vesicles can help test that distinction, although depletion itself may change other materials. If a candidate cargo is suspected, perturbing its abundance in the donor and measuring the recipient response can further test the hypothesis. The experiment should also determine whether the donor manipulation affects particle release or donor viability independently of the candidate cargo. A decrease in recipient response following donor cell injury would otherwise be difficult to interpret.

Fluorescence-based tracing is often attractive because it appears to reveal particle transport directly. Yet the label can dissociate, label other materials, or be transferred independently of intact vesicles. A labelled preparation therefore requires controls that reveal the behavior of the label in the absence of the relevant particles. Detection of label in an organ may also indicate retention or clearance by cells rather than delivery to the cell type responsible for the claimed physiological response. The distinction between an anatomical destination and a functional target is the same one encountered for a soluble hormone: exposure alone is insufficient. The question is whether the recipient engages a mechanism that changes physiology.

Compared with the established endocrine roles of insulin or leptin, many proposed vesicle-mediated systemic metabolic pathways are still being evaluated. This does not mean that extracellular vesicles have no physiological function. It means the evidence for different proposed source–target pairs is not equally strong. Students should be able to distinguish an established mechanism, a plausible candidate supported by experimental observations and a speculative mechanism not yet demonstrated at physiological exposure. This distinction protects against two opposite errors: dismissing a newer mode of communication entirely or presenting every proposed route as settled textbook fact.

8. Cellular nutrient sensing and feedback networks

A cell must match ATP production to ATP consumption, but the chemical concentrations involved are not direct readings of the energy reserves of the entire animal. ATP is continually regenerated, and changes in energy demand can alter the proportions of ATP, ADP and AMP. AMP-activated protein kinase, AMPK, is a central participant in cellular energy regulation. Adenine nucleotide binding influences its activation and regulation; upstream kinases phosphorylate an activating site. AMPK can inhibit energetically expensive processes and promote pathways that replenish usable energy. Its physiological effect depends on the type of cell and the circumstances.

Adenylate kinase catalyses a reversible reaction involving two ADP molecules and the formation of ATP and AMP. As ATP consumption increases, relatively small shifts in the nucleotide system can produce informative changes in AMP. This relationship helps explain the sensitivity of cellular energy sensing without requiring ATP concentrations to collapse. The adenylate system is only one part of AMPK regulation. Other inputs can influence the kinase, and a measurement of AMPK activity is not a universal measure of whole-body energy balance.

Mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1, mTORC1, integrates information about nutrient availability and growth-related signalling to regulate processes such as protein synthesis and autophagy. Amino acid availability and growth-factor signals contribute through distinct regulatory mechanisms. Under energy stress, AMPK can restrain mTORC1 signalling. It is misleading to reduce these systems to a rule that one is always on and the other always off. They receive multiple inputs and can operate differently in distinct tissues. Detailed molecular regulation belongs to the general signalling unit and the later chapter on metabolic flexibility.

Negative feedback exists when an initiated response tends to oppose the original disturbance in a regulated variable. Consider a glucose increase after a meal. An appropriate insulin response can increase glucose disposal and suppress endogenous glucose production; both effects tend to limit the rise. As the disturbance subsides, the stimulus for secretion changes. The negative sign is a property of the complete causal loop, not the sign of every arrow within it. Insulin can activate a signalling enzyme while the overall feedback loop reduces the original increase in glucose.

Positive feedback occurs when a response reinforces a disturbance or initiating process. It can be useful for events that require amplification but is not normally the sole mechanism maintaining a variable within a narrow range. Biological control systems contain interacting loops, delays and limits. If corrective responses are delayed, an overshoot may occur even when the loop is negative. Two systems with the same eventual glucose concentration may differ in their recovery time, overshoot and hormonal requirements. These differences contain physiological information that is lost in an isolated endpoint measurement.

Energy charge connects nucleotide chemistry with regulation

Energy-sensing pathways respond to the consequences of ATP consumption and production within a cell. The adenylate kinase equilibrium, 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP, explains why AMP can change markedly when the balance among ATP, ADP and AMP shifts. A convenient descriptive index is adenylate energy charge, defined as ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]). The brackets denote concentrations measured in the same compartment and expressed in the same units. The index ranges between zero and one because the numerator weights ATP fully, ADP by one half and AMP not at all. It is an accounting measure of the distribution of adenine nucleotides, not the fraction of total body energy stored in ATP.

For an illustrative cell with ATP, ADP and AMP concentrations of 4.0, 0.8 and 0.2 millimoles per litre, respectively, the total is 5.0 and the weighted numerator is 4.4. Its calculated energy charge is 0.88. Suppose a subsequent state has concentrations of 3.5, 1.0 and 0.5 in the same units. The total remains 5.0, but the charge is 4.0/5.0 = 0.80. ATP concentration has fallen modestly while AMP has increased substantially relative to its initial value. This example illustrates how a nucleotide-sensitive regulatory mechanism may respond to changes that would be less obvious if only ATP were measured. The numbers are hypothetical; they do not define physiological thresholds for the activation of AMPK in any specific cell.

AMP-activated protein kinase is a regulated enzyme complex rather than a direct meter that converts energy charge into a fixed response. Changes in adenine nucleotides influence its regulation, and phosphorylation of a regulatory site on the catalytic subunit is important for its activity. Other signals, including calcium-linked pathways in some contexts, can contribute to its activation. Once active, AMPK can modify enzyme activity and influence processes that help match ATP-consuming activities to energy availability. For example, during energetic stress it can favor pathways supporting ATP production while restraining selected ATP-consuming biosynthetic activities. The particular outputs depend on cell type and context. A simple rule that AMPK always inhibits every anabolic reaction or always stimulates every catabolic reaction would overstate the scope of the mechanism.

Mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 responds to inputs including amino acid availability and growth-factor signalling. Nutrients and growth factors can provide complementary information: the presence of amino acids suggests that building materials are available, while growth-related signals help indicate whether biosynthetic investment is appropriate. An energy deficit may limit biosynthesis even when amino acids are available. AMPK and mTORC1 interact in several contexts, but they are not interchangeable sensors or a single two-position switch. A cell can receive an anabolic signal from another organ while local energy constraints limit its ability to carry out the response. This explains why systemic endocrine information must be integrated with intracellular state rather than obeyed unconditionally.

Reading the sign of a feedback network

In a simple negative feedback loop, an increase in a regulated variable leads to a response that tends to reduce that increase. It is the product of the signs around the full closed loop that determines the feedback sign. For example, elevated glucose can stimulate insulin secretion, and insulin can promote glucose disposal while reducing glucose production. The first relationship is positive, because more glucose tends to increase insulin secretion, but the net effect of insulin on glucose can be negative. The overall loop is therefore negative in the relevant physiological range. A student who labels the entire loop positive because glucose stimulates insulin secretion has considered only one edge of the network.

A simple linear model illustrates the role of response strength. Let x represent the deviation of a concentration from a reference value, D a sustained disturbance expressed as concentration change per unit time, and kx a corrective rate with k positive and units of inverse time. The equation dx/dt = D − kx predicts a steady deviation x = D/k when the disturbance is constant. A stronger corrective response reduces the steady deviation, but does not necessarily make it exactly zero. If D is removed, the deviation decays toward zero in this idealized model. With k = 0.2 per minute and D = 0.1 millimoles per litre per minute, the predicted steady deviation is 0.5 millimoles per litre. Doubling k halves that deviation. The model assumes linear behavior and no delay; biological control is often nonlinear and involves multiple interacting processes.

Delay can change the behavior of a corrective loop. An intervention that responds to an earlier value may still be acting when the disturbance has already begun to resolve. A strong delayed response can produce overshoot, and repeated correction can produce oscillations in some systems. It does not follow that every oscillation represents pathological instability: biological rhythms and pulsatile secretion may be normal features of physiology. The prediction depends on the delays, strength of responses, other pathways and external inputs. In an examination dataset, a time series can therefore reveal information that a measurement at a single time point cannot reveal. A return toward the initial value after perturbation supports the presence of compensation, but identifying the components requires additional tests.

Feedforward and feedback can also operate at the same time. A sensory cue may stimulate early digestive or endocrine activity, while the subsequent arrival of nutrients stimulates a response that depends on actual concentrations. If the early cue accurately predicts a large meal, anticipation may reduce the size of the disturbance. If it predicts a meal that never arrives, feedback may oppose the inappropriate preparatory response. Positive feedback, by contrast, tends to amplify an initiating change and may serve particular physiological functions, but an amplifying mechanism cannot by itself ensure that a regulated concentration remains constrained. These distinct structures should be identified from causal direction and timing rather than from the names of the molecules in the network.

9. A distributed network of organ-specific responses

The brain integrates external information and internal signals, influences food-seeking and meal termination, and controls autonomic and endocrine output. The gut absorbs nutrients while supplying hormonal and neural information about incoming food. The pancreas senses the metabolic environment and adjusts endocrine output. The liver buffers the supply of glucose and processes nutrients delivered through portal blood. Skeletal muscle accounts for a variable demand for ATP, consumes circulating and locally stored substrates and changes its signals with contraction. Adipose tissue buffers lipid availability through storage and mobilization and provides endocrine information. The kidney contributes to glucose production in appropriate nutritional states, handles filtered glucose and influences clearance of circulating molecules. The organs do not form a simple one-way command chain.

A representative meal produces overlapping events: intestinal absorption increases nutrient delivery, endocrine and neural signals alter pancreatic output, the liver changes the balance between production and uptake, and muscle and adipose tissue alter glucose handling. The eventual blood glucose profile is the result of these combined rates. During fasting, nutrient absorption declines; endogenous production and mobilization become important for sustaining required functions. During exercise, muscle contraction can stimulate glucose uptake partly through mechanisms distinct from insulin action. Detailed time courses and fuel selection are developed in later nodes.

A disturbance in one tissue can propagate through the network. If insulin responsiveness in adipose tissue declines, suppression of lipolysis may be impaired. Increased delivery of fatty acids and glycerol can influence hepatic metabolism. The pancreas may alter secretion in response to the changing metabolic environment. A normal measured glucose value may persist temporarily if compensatory responses are effective. This is why a systems-level interpretation must distinguish the initial defect, compensatory response and final phenotype. The chapter on insulin resistance will examine these processes in detail.

Regulatory events span overlapping timescales. Neural impulses and ion-channel gating can occur rapidly; transporter redistribution, phosphorylation and hormone secretion can affect physiology over short intervals; gene expression, tissue remodelling and adjustments of reserve size can develop over longer periods. The boundaries are not absolute, and a slow change may be initiated by a rapid event. A time-course experiment that samples only once can miss the initiating signal or confuse a compensatory event with the original cause.

Reading the organ map as a set of connected balances

The digestive tract is an entry point for externally acquired nutrients, but not all absorbed materials take identical transport routes. Water-soluble nutrient products such as monosaccharides and many amino acids enter portal blood, giving the liver early access. Much of the long-chain dietary lipid is assembled into lipoprotein particles and transported initially through lymph before reaching the systemic blood. This anatomical difference helps explain why a meal changes the nutrient environment of organs in patterns that cannot be inferred from the word absorption alone. The gut also supplies sensory and endocrine information about the meal, allowing changes in nutrient delivery to be coordinated with downstream storage and use. Detailed intestinal transport and incretin mechanisms belong to the preceding digestive material and the subsequent gut–pancreas–liver node.

The pancreas contributes hormonal information that reflects the local and systemic nutrient environment. Its endocrine output can modify the activity of several organs simultaneously. The liver has a strategic position because it receives portal blood and systemic arterial blood, transforms many substrates and regulates the release of glucose and other metabolites. Adipose tissue can store incoming energy as triglyceride or mobilize reserves under different conditions. Skeletal muscle represents a large and variable metabolic demand: at rest its energy requirements differ markedly from those during contraction. The brain helps coordinate behavior and autonomic output but also depends on reliable substrate delivery. The kidney contributes to glucose conservation through reabsorption and can contribute to endogenous glucose production, with its relative role depending on the physiological state. These roles are complementary rather than exclusive.

To follow a meal through the network, start with the external input. Absorption introduces new substrates into the body, but the net circulating concentration depends on how quickly tissues remove them and how much endogenous production continues. Insulin signalling can contribute to restraint of endogenous glucose output and facilitate disposal in insulin-responsive tissues. Glucose entering muscle may replenish glycogen or support current ATP production. Fatty acids derived from dietary lipid may be oxidized or stored. At the same time, nutrient sensing in the intestine and signals reaching the brain can contribute to meal-related responses. These events overlap. A diagram that orders them into a simple chain is useful for tracing causal links, but it must not imply that all liver processing finishes before muscle or adipose tissue responds.

In an interval without food, the external entry term decreases, but energy demand continues. Endogenous sources become more important. Changes in insulin and counterregulatory influences can favor hepatic glucose production and mobilization of adipose reserves. Fatty acids can supply fuel to appropriate oxidative tissues, while glycerol and other suitable precursors contribute to gluconeogenesis. Renal glucose production may also participate, particularly as fasting progresses. The brain's fuel use changes with prolonged fasting, but erythrocytes retain their requirement for glycolytic ATP production. These observations show why a whole-body transition is not a uniform switch from glucose use to fat use in every cell. The response is a coordinated redistribution of substrates among tissues with different metabolic capabilities.

Exercise introduces a disturbance on the demand side. Contraction increases the rate of ATP consumption in muscle and can stimulate glucose uptake by pathways that are partly distinct from insulin signalling. Increased muscle demand can alter circulating substrate concentrations, while neural and hormonal responses can help sustain supply. At moderate intensities, several fuels can contribute simultaneously; their relative use depends on duration, intensity, nutritional state and training. If an experiment observes increased glucose disposal without increased insulin, it would be premature to conclude that all insulin-related processes are irrelevant. The observation is compatible with a contribution from contraction-mediated pathways, but interpretation requires information about baseline insulin, sensitivity, delivery and other changes. This illustrates how multiple communication routes can support the same physiological outcome.

Shared variables create indirect communication

A change in one organ can affect another without a newly secreted peptide. Consider adipose lipolysis, which supplies fatty acids and glycerol to the circulation. Increased release changes the amount of material available to the liver and other tissues. The liver may respond to the altered substrate environment even if its receptors for circulating hormones remain unchanged. If the change in liver metabolism subsequently alters circulating glucose, pancreatic endocrine output may change. Such a chain involves material transport and regulatory sensing in addition to endocrine signalling. A diagram should distinguish a substrate-flow arrow from a regulatory arrow, because their meanings are not identical. The presence of material transfer can help explain a downstream response but does not necessarily establish a distinct receptor-mediated signal.

The circulatory system itself can mediate indirect changes. During a physiological challenge, altered blood flow can modify the delivery of oxygen, substrates and hormones to recipient tissues. The concentration of a compound in arterial blood may remain unchanged while the amount presented to a tissue per minute changes because flow changes. A similar principle applies to clearance: if removal by a tissue changes, blood concentrations can shift even when the secreting tissue has unchanged activity. Cardiovascular and renal physiology therefore provide essential context for interorgan metabolic regulation. Treating these organ systems as passive pipes or filters would omit important determinants of the conditions encountered by metabolic cells.

Regulatory timescales overlap rather than forming strict categories. An ion-channel response can occur within seconds or less, changes in circulating hormones and transporter trafficking may alter metabolism over minutes, and changes in enzyme abundance or organ structure may develop over hours, days or longer. A short experiment can test a rapid effector response but may miss slower adaptation. A long experiment can reveal adaptation but makes it harder to distinguish the primary perturbation from secondary compensatory changes. When examining an organ-specific deletion, for example, a lifelong change in a gene can produce developmental compensation, whereas an acute intervention may reveal more immediate effects. Neither approach answers every question. The appropriate comparison depends on whether the hypothesis concerns rapid coordination or long-term regulation.

10. Experimental reasoning: from association to causation

Parabiosis provides a classical conceptual demonstration of a circulating regulatory factor. In this approach the circulations of two animals are connected, allowing blood-borne factors to influence both partners. Douglas Coleman's studies with obese ob/ob and diabetic db/db mutant mice provided evidence for a circulating factor involved in body-weight regulation. The ob/ob mutant could respond to the signal supplied by another animal but lacked its normal production, whereas the db/db mutant failed to respond appropriately. The later identification of leptin and its receptor explained key aspects of this result.

The inferential strength of this design is the separation of signal production from responsiveness. If an affected animal improves when supplied with a circulating factor from an unaffected partner, lack of a circulating product becomes plausible. If the affected animal does not respond while its partner shows a strong response, resistance to a circulating factor becomes plausible. The design does not by itself identify a chemical molecule, a receptor, or the exact downstream pathway. It also changes several shared circulating variables. Molecular identification and selective manipulations were needed to resolve the original physiological inference.

Another useful method measures net exchange across a tissue. Under steady conditions the net uptake rate is blood flow multiplied by arterial concentration minus venous concentration. If flow is 0.5 litres per minute and arterial and venous concentrations are 5.0 and 4.6 millimoles per litre, respectively, uptake is 0.20 millimoles per minute. A negative value indicates net release under this sign convention. Flow and concentrations must refer to compatible blood or plasma compartments. The calculation is a conservation statement and does not distinguish simultaneous production and consumption within the tissue.

A causal experiment should isolate a proposed communication link. If a factor X from organ A is hypothesized to activate a receptor in organ B, a useful design compares normal and donor-specific loss of X while monitoring nutrient availability, then tests whether recipient-specific receptor loss blocks the response. Restoration of X at a suitable exposure can help test sufficiency in the deficient condition. An observed association between circulating X and the response does not separate a direct effect from a shared stimulus. Conversely, failure to observe an effect after removal of X can be complicated by redundant pathways or developmental adaptation. The strongest inference is based on convergent evidence from different experimental designs.

Suppose a hypothetical experiment finds that an intervention raises muscle glucose uptake without changing measured insulin concentration. This result does not prove that insulin signalling is irrelevant. The intervention could increase sensitivity to existing insulin, change blood flow or activate a parallel pathway. A matched experiment with controlled insulin exposure and selective pathway perturbations would discriminate among these alternatives. Quantitative interpretation should begin with the variables actually measured, identify those inferred, and state what additional evidence would make the conclusion stronger.

Organ balance calculations and their limitations

The Fick principle for a substance passing through a vascular bed relates net tissue exchange to flow and the difference between incoming and outgoing concentrations. If Q denotes flow in litres per minute and Ca and Cv denote arterial and venous concentrations in millimoles per litre, Q(Ca − Cv) gives net uptake in millimoles per minute under suitable steady conditions. Positive values indicate net removal from the blood and negative values indicate net addition. The same choice of plasma or whole-blood flow and corresponding concentration must be used consistently. For a compound distributed differently between blood cells and plasma, mixing plasma concentrations with total blood flow can produce an incorrect rate. The method also assumes that the sampled vessel represents the relevant drainage and that the flow estimate corresponds to the same tissue bed.

Consider an illustrative pair of observations across a resting tissue and the same tissue during an intervention. Initially flow is 0.30 litres per minute and the arterial–venous glucose difference is 0.40 millimoles per litre, giving net uptake of 0.12 millimoles per minute. During the intervention flow increases to 0.60 litres per minute and the difference becomes 0.30 millimoles per litre. Net uptake is now 0.18 millimoles per minute, an increase of 50 percent even though the concentration difference became smaller. The example shows why a larger extraction difference does not necessarily imply a higher uptake rate. A tissue could also extract a smaller fraction of the delivered substrate while increasing total uptake if delivery rises sufficiently.

The liver requires particular care because it receives blood from two major inflows. In an idealized calculation, total glucose entering per minute is QpCp + QaCa, where the subscripts p and a represent portal and arterial inflows. The outgoing amount per minute is QhCh in the hepatic venous outflow, with Qh approximately equal to the sum of the inflows when net fluid exchange is negligible. The difference gives net hepatic uptake. For hypothetical values of 0.8 litres per minute at 6 millimoles per litre in portal blood, 0.2 litres per minute at 5 millimoles per litre in arterial blood, and 1.0 litre per minute at 5.5 millimoles per litre in hepatic venous blood, inflow is 5.8 millimoles per minute and outflow is 5.5. Net uptake is 0.3 millimoles per minute. Comparing hepatic venous glucose only with arterial glucose would misleadingly suggest net release in this example.

Net exchange does not reveal simultaneous opposing internal reactions. A tissue that takes up glucose can also release glucose under circumstances in which it has the required machinery. The net balance reports the difference between gross uptake and gross release. Distinguishing these processes may require tracer methods, which follow the dilution or fate of a labelled form of a substance. A stable-isotope label can help distinguish molecules introduced experimentally from molecules appearing through endogenous production. The calculation requires a model of mixing and knowledge of labelled input; under non-steady conditions simple steady-state formulas may be inappropriate. More detailed tracer calculations are reserved for subsequent nodes, but the conceptual point is essential: a measurement of net change does not by itself identify each constituent rate.

Testing necessity, sufficiency and physiological relevance

A strong interorgan signalling experiment asks whether a proposed signal is necessary for a response under specified conditions. If removal of the source factor prevents the response, the result supports necessity provided that the manipulation has not independently impaired other relevant processes. It also asks whether the factor is sufficient: supplying it under specified conditions should produce or restore the response if it can drive the mechanism. Necessity and sufficiency are separate properties. A signal may be sufficient when supplied experimentally but unnecessary during normal physiology because another pathway compensates. A signal may be necessary as one component of a combined response without being sufficient when supplied alone. Therefore an experimental statement should include the conditions under which the inference applies.

Consider a hypothetical four-group experiment testing a secreted factor from muscle on hepatic substrate use. In controls, contraction is associated with a liver response. In animals lacking the factor specifically in muscle, the response is reduced. Supplying the factor at a concentration that restores normal physiological exposure restores the response in those animals. However, the same replacement fails when the proposed receptor is absent from hepatocytes. Together, these results support a model in which the source and target receptor contribute causally to the response. Further controls are still needed: contraction intensity, substrate delivery and circulating hormones should be comparable, and the genetic manipulation should not have caused major developmental differences. The four results are illustrative and do not describe a published experiment for a specific organokine.

A less direct pattern might occur if removing the muscle factor changes activity in the central nervous system, which in turn changes hepatic metabolism. A receptor in liver might influence the response by altering baseline physiology rather than mediating the circulating factor's direct action. To discriminate these explanations, researchers can compare intact animals with isolated recipient cells or tissues, test whether the response requires the candidate receptor locally and measure plausible intermediate changes. Isolated preparations provide control over conditions but remove systemic influences that may be required for a normal response. The best interpretation usually comes from complementary experiments that constrain different alternatives rather than one technique alone.

Temporal ordering can support a proposed mechanism without proving it. A donor signal must be available before it can cause the recipient response, so a response that consistently occurs before the proposed signal arrives argues against that direct causal model. However, earlier appearance of the factor does not exclude a common cause. In a time-series experiment, frequent sampling may help identify the order of changes, but measurement sensitivity and transport delays matter. An apparently late rise in peripheral concentration may not reflect earlier exposure in portal blood or interstitial fluid. The strongest reasoning combines timing with interventions, source and recipient evidence, quantitative plausibility and careful consideration of shared stimuli.

These approaches explain the progression from historical physiology to present-day mechanistic experiments. Connecting circulations showed that transferable influences could alter phenotype; molecular identification established the nature of specific signals; receptor studies explained responsiveness; organ balance and tracer studies quantified substrate redistribution. Each approach answers a different part of the interorgan communication problem. No single measurement can substitute for the others. In a CSIR-NET Part C problem, identify the measured outcome, calculate the appropriate balance if relevant, specify alternative explanations and choose the comparison that would distinguish them. This strategy carries directly into the study of central appetite regulation and later integrated metabolic states.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Homeostasis means no change

Correction: A steady concentration can mask substantial turnover. Why it is tempting: A constant graph is often mistaken for absent movement. Examination reasoning: Compare two equal input/output pairs with different rates; use mass balance rather than concentration alone.

Energy balance and glucose balance are identical

Correction: Energy stores can decline while glucose is maintained. Why it is tempting: Both involve metabolic fuels, but they describe different quantities. Examination reasoning: Separate whole-body energy accounting from glucose pool accounting.

Positive energy balance specifies exact fat gain

Correction: Storage composition and water changes affect mass. Why it is tempting: Energy and mass are often converted with a fixed factor. Examination reasoning: Use the stated energy difference; do not infer fat mass unless composition is specified.

Hormones affect all tissues equally

Correction: Receptor abundance, coupling and local conditions determine effects. Why it is tempting: The bloodstream is shared, suggesting equal exposure and action. Examination reasoning: Distinguish exposure, binding and effect.

All endocrine actions are slow

Correction: Some occur rapidly through phosphorylation and transport changes. Why it is tempting: Neural and endocrine routes are taught as simple opposites. Examination reasoning: Compare the complete response mechanism and its timescale.

Glucagon acts equally on liver and muscle

Correction: Liver is a major glucagon target for glucose production; muscle glycogen is primarily local. Why it is tempting: Both tissues contain glycogen. Examination reasoning: Check receptor expression and capacity to export free glucose.

Hepatic uptake requires GLUT4

Correction: Hepatocytes have distinct transport and metabolic control. Why it is tempting: The muscle insulin model is overgeneralized. Examination reasoning: Identify the cell type before predicting the transporter response.

Peripheral hormone equals portal hormone

Correction: Portal delivery and extraction can create gradients. Why it is tempting: One blood sample seems representative of the whole body. Examination reasoning: Identify the sampling site and vascular route.

Lactate is merely waste

Correction: It can be oxidized or used for gluconeogenesis. Why it is tempting: Its association with glycolysis is mistaken for uselessness. Examination reasoning: Trace the carbon and recipient metabolic capacity.

IL-6 always indicates chronic inflammation

Correction: Origin, timing and target context matter. Why it is tempting: The cytokine name is associated with immune disease. Examination reasoning: Distinguish transient exercise-associated release from chronic stimulation.

Organokines have exclusive tissue origins

Correction: Names indicate the source under discussion, not exclusivity. Why it is tempting: Source-based words sound like molecular classes. Examination reasoning: Ask whether source-specific release was actually measured.

Every small EV is an exosome

Correction: Exosome requires evidence of endosomal origin. Why it is tempting: Size ranges overlap and terminology is frequently simplified. Examination reasoning: Choose the broad EV term unless biogenesis is demonstrated.

Fluorescent particle uptake proves RNA function

Correction: Label transfer and uptake do not establish functional cargo access. Why it is tempting: Images seem to directly show the claimed route. Examination reasoning: Require label-only controls, separation and a cargo-dependent functional test.

AMPK measures total body fat

Correction: AMPK integrates cellular energy-related inputs. Why it is tempting: Energy status is confused across scales. Examination reasoning: Identify the measured cellular nucleotide and regulatory state.

Correlation proves interorgan causation

Correction: A common stimulus can independently alter two organs. Why it is tempting: Simultaneous changes invite a causal narrative. Examination reasoning: Test source, route, recipient response and alternatives using interventions.

A smaller arteriovenous gradient proves lower uptake

Correction: Net uptake depends on both flow and gradient. Why it is tempting: Concentration difference is confused with amount per time. Examination reasoning: Multiply matching flow and concentration difference.

Any activating edge is positive feedback

Correction: The sign of a closed loop depends on the combined causal edges. Why it is tempting: A positive intermediate effect is mistaken for the sign of the loop. Examination reasoning: Trace the effect back to the original variable.

Equal plasma concentrations prove equal hormone secretion

Correction: Clearance and distribution can differ. Why it is tempting: Concentration is mistaken for production rate. Examination reasoning: Apply secretion–removal balance and compare kinetics.

1. Part B reasoning

Which quantity defines energy balance?

Answer and explanation

Difference between metabolizable energy intake and expenditure

Energy balance concerns chemical energy accounting, unlike glucose concentration or body mass alone.

The distinction is important because a normal glucose value can coexist with an energy deficit. The quantities must be compared over the same time interval.

2. Part B reasoning

What determines endocrine target specificity?

Answer and explanation

Receptors and intracellular response machinery

Circulating exposure is not sufficient for a response.

A receptor can be absent or its pathway can be poorly coupled even when ligand exposure is normal. Conversely, a receptor-expressing tissue can respond selectively to a circulating message.

3. Part B reasoning

What distinguishes feedforward from feedback?

Answer and explanation

Feedforward responds to predictive cues before the regulated variable is disturbed

Feedback depends on a change in the regulated system.

A cue may be misleading, so an anticipatory response does not necessarily restore the controlled variable. Feedback subsequently responds to the actual physiological change.

4. Part B reasoning

What channel change links beta-cell ATP production to depolarization?

Answer and explanation

Closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels

Reduced potassium conductance supports membrane depolarization and calcium entry.

Reduced outward potassium current allows the membrane potential to become less negative; opening of voltage-dependent calcium channels then promotes granule exocytosis.

5. Part B reasoning

What organ primarily responds to glucagon to increase circulating glucose?

Answer and explanation

Liver

Hepatic glucose production responds to glucagon; muscle glycogen is principally a local reserve.

A hepatocyte has the necessary capacity to mobilize glucose for circulation. Muscle glycogen principally provides local substrate rather than glucose directly to blood.

6. Part B reasoning

What term describes a skeletal-muscle-derived signalling factor?

Answer and explanation

Myokine

The name indicates source in a given context, not exclusive origin.

The designation does not imply that the same molecule is never made by other organs. Tissue-specific release, rather than gene expression alone, supports assignment of source.

7. Part B reasoning

Why is lactate not simply waste?

Answer and explanation

It can serve as oxidative fuel or gluconeogenic substrate

Lactate transfers carbon and chemical energy between tissues.

Oxidative tissues may use lactate carbon, and gluconeogenic tissues may return that carbon to glucose. Neither role establishes that every lactate effect is receptor-mediated.

8. Part B reasoning

What property defines a negative feedback loop?

Answer and explanation

The response opposes the initial disturbance

Individual steps may be activating even when the overall loop is negative.

Trace the entire closed loop: a positive link from stimulus to effector can be followed by an opposing effect of the effector on the initial variable.

9. Part B reasoning

Which kinase participates in cellular energy sensing?

Answer and explanation

AMPK

It integrates nucleotide and other regulatory inputs rather than measuring whole-body energy directly.

The enzyme is regulated by nucleotide-related and other signals. Local ATP stress should not be confused with organism-level energy reserves.

Extracellular vesicles

Exosome implies a specific origin not established by particle size.

A specific term such as exosome requires evidence of biogenesis. A size or fluorescence measurement alone cannot establish it.

Zero net change in the modelled pool, not zero turnover

Concentration reflects release relative to removal and distribution.

A cue predicts a meal and increases secretory activity before absorption. On a test day, the cue is presented but food is withheld. What distinguishes feedforward and feedback here?

  1. Neither pathway depends on physiological state
  2. Both require absorbed glucose
  3. The cue itself proves negative feedback
  4. The early cue-driven response is anticipatory; later correction can depend on actual nutrient availability
Answer and explanation

D. The early cue-driven response is anticipatory; later correction can depend on actual nutrient availability

Feedforward responds to predictive information, whereas feedback responds to the resulting physiological state. A misleading cue can reveal the distinction.

45. Part C reasoning

Portal inflow is 0.7 L/min at 7 mM, arterial inflow is 0.3 L/min at 5 mM, and venous outflow is 1 L/min at 6 mM. What is net hepatic glucose exchange?

  1. 0.4 mmol/min uptake
  2. 0.4 mmol/min release
  3. 1 mmol/min release
  4. Zero
Answer and explanation

A. 0.4 mmol/min uptake

Inflow is 0.7 × 7 + 0.3 × 5 = 6.4 mmol/min. Outflow is 6 mmol/min, so net uptake is 0.4. Arterial concentration alone is insufficient.

46. Part C reasoning

After selective deletion of a donor factor the recipient response disappears; physiological replacement restores it only if the recipient receptor is intact. What is supported?

  1. The factor is necessarily the only regulator in all animals
  2. A causal contribution of the donor factor and recipient receptor under the tested conditions
  3. Every cell expresses the receptor
  4. The factor does not circulate
Answer and explanation

B. A causal contribution of the donor factor and recipient receptor under the tested conditions

The loss, rescue and receptor dependence support the proposed causal link. Other regulators, indirect mechanisms and developmental effects still require consideration.

47. Part C reasoning

A tissue shows stable net uptake in two conditions. In one condition tracer data show both gross uptake and gross release have increased by the same amount. Which is correct?

  1. Tracer results violate conservation
  2. Net uptake must increase
  3. Stable net exchange can conceal increased bidirectional turnover
  4. Both gross rates are zero
Answer and explanation

C. Stable net exchange can conceal increased bidirectional turnover

Net uptake is gross uptake minus gross release. Equal increases cancel in the net quantity; the two measurements describe different properties of the system.

48. Part C reasoning

A 5 L model pool contains 25 mmol glucose. Volume increases to 6.25 L without amount change. What is the new concentration?

  1. 6.25 mM
  2. 5 mM
  3. 1 mM
  4. 4 mM
Answer and explanation

D. 4 mM

Concentration is amount divided by volume: 25/6.25 = 4 mM. The fall does not establish substrate consumption because amount is unchanged.

49. Part C reasoning

An animal lacking an adipose-derived factor eats more. Pair-feeding normalizes its body mass, but the recipient tissue response remains impaired. What does this suggest?

  1. Food intake alone does not explain the persistent tissue response defect
  2. The factor is not involved in any metabolic regulation
  3. Body mass establishes receptor occupancy
  4. Pair-feeding proves direct receptor action
Answer and explanation

A. Food intake alone does not explain the persistent tissue response defect

Matching intake and body mass can reduce a major confounder. Persistent differences support additional mechanisms but do not establish direct action without further experiments.

50. Part C reasoning

A proposed donor signal is first detected at 20 min, yet recipient response begins at 5 min. What is the strongest justified inference?

  1. The signal is definitely absent from all tissues
  2. The measured timing weakens the proposed direct explanation; earlier local exposure and detection limits should be examined
  3. The factor must act retroactively
  4. Correlation alone proves the mechanism
Answer and explanation

B. The measured timing weakens the proposed direct explanation; earlier local exposure and detection limits should be examined

A cause must be available before its effect. However, peripheral sampling may miss earlier local or portal exposure, so the result argues against the simple model without ruling out every possible route.

51. State SET application

Assertion: A negative energy balance can coexist with stable glucose. Reason: Internal glucose production and altered substrate use can compensate for reduced intake.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion

The body can mobilize stored fuels and maintain glucose despite declining total chemical energy. These are distinct balance variables.

52. State SET application

Assertion: A high concentration of an organokine proves increased secretion. Reason: Clearance can alter circulating concentration.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

D. Assertion false; reason true

A concentration reflects secretion, distribution and removal. Reduced clearance may elevate concentration without changing secretion; therefore the assertion is false.

53. State SET application

Which pair of observations most directly distinguishes endocrine signal abundance from responsiveness?

  1. Body weight and age alone
  2. Matched ligand concentration and a measured receptor-dependent response
  3. Food color and meal timing alone
  4. Total energy intake and blood volume alone
Answer and explanation

B. Matched ligand concentration and a measured receptor-dependent response

At matched exposure, differences in target response support differences in responsiveness. The other pairs do not directly establish receptor function.

54. State SET application

Which experimental comparison best addresses contamination by soluble proteins in a vesicle preparation?

  1. Compare two uncharacterized fluorescent samples
  2. Only measure donor RNA
  3. Compare characterized particle-enriched and particle-depleted fractions with matched controls
  4. Assume particle size establishes purity
Answer and explanation

C. Compare characterized particle-enriched and particle-depleted fractions with matched controls

Comparing fractions with appropriate controls helps attribute activity, though no separation is perfectly selective. Fluorescence and size alone cannot establish cargo-specific action.
