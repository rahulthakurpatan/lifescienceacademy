The animal as a habitat

The digestive tract of an animal has two apparently contradictory tasks. It must retain and digest food long enough to obtain nutrients, yet it must prevent uncontrolled growth of organisms entering with food and water. The intestinal epithelium establishes a boundary between internal tissue fluids and the lumen, but the lumen is neither chemically constant nor biologically empty. It is a changing habitat in which microorganisms encounter substrates, secretions, competitors and surfaces. Understanding animal physiology therefore requires attention to the properties of both the host and the microbial communities it sustains.

Consider a mammal consuming a meal rich in plant polysaccharides. Digestive enzymes belonging to the animal can hydrolyse many carbohydrates, but do not necessarily cleave every glycosidic linkage. Some material escapes digestion and reaches distal intestinal regions. A bacterium carrying an appropriate enzyme system can use that material as a resource. If another bacterium uses the released intermediate, the consequences of the original meal extend beyond a single microbial species. The biochemical capabilities of the host and the inhabitants of its gut therefore influence which substrates remain available, which microbial populations grow and which metabolic products accumulate. The next chapter examines the physiological fate of those products; here the question is why the community able to produce them takes a particular form.

A habitat includes its physical and chemical conditions. A microbial population consists of organisms belonging to a defined group in that habitat. The community consists of interacting populations, while the ecosystem encompasses the organisms and their nonliving surroundings. These levels apply just as readily to a colon as to a lake or soil sample. However, the animal actively modifies its habitat through epithelial secretions, absorption, immune responses and movement of digesta. The surrounding conditions are consequently not independent of the organisms living there. The community can also change those conditions by consuming oxygen and substrates or producing acids. Host and microbes are coupled components of a system, although each component retains its own biological interests.

The older image of a normal animal as an autonomous organism occasionally invaded by disease agents is insufficient. Classical microbiology showed how particular organisms cause infection, while studies of normal resident communities demonstrated that microbial presence need not constitute disease. The two perspectives complement one another. A microbe can be part of a stable association in one location and cause damage in another. Detecting a microorganism by microscopy or DNA analysis therefore answers a different question from establishing that it causes a physiological effect.

A useful distinction is between the anatomical individual and the functional system under study. The animal remains a multicellular organism descended from a fertilized egg. Its resident microbial cells have distinct histories and genomes. When the research question concerns nutrient conversion or community assembly, the wider host-associated system may be the appropriate object of analysis. This does not mean that the anatomical or evolutionary individuality of the animal has disappeared. The appropriate boundary depends on the question being asked.

Microbiota, microbiome, metaorganism and hologenome

Microbiota refers to the assemblage of microorganisms associated with a defined habitat. The gut microbiota is thus spatially specified: a sample from the large intestine need not represent the stomach, and a faecal sample need not represent every mucosal surface. The term can be qualified to identify the bacterial microbiota, archaeal community or fungal community. Such qualification matters because a method designed to detect bacteria may reveal almost nothing about fungi or viruses.

Microbiome is used in more than one sense in scientific literature. Some authors emphasize the collective microbial genomes; others describe the community together with its surrounding ecological context. A broad ecological usage is particularly helpful in physiology because cells, their genes, their products and the properties of the habitat jointly determine what happens. This chapter uses microbiota for the microbial assemblage and microbiome for the wider ecological system, while using metagenome for collective genetic material recovered from a community. When an examination question provides its own explicit definition, that definition should guide interpretation rather than a rigid assumption that all publications use the same convention.

The words metaorganism and holobiont emphasize the host in association with its microorganisms. Their boundaries and usage vary across fields. A holobiont can be described without assuming that every inhabitant benefits the host or that all partners are inherited together. Some microbes may be long-term residents; others may appear briefly, and pathogens can also interact with the assemblage. In a study of the entire animal, gut, skin and oral communities may be relevant; in a study of intestinal ecology, the chosen compartment is narrower. Any operational definition should state which associated populations and time scale are included.

The hologenome is the combined genetic complement of the host and its associated organisms. A microbial metagenome is not identical to that complement because it does not ordinarily include the entire host genome. Nor does a list of genes tell us which genes are expressed, whether enzymes are active or whether their products reach host tissues. Genetic potential, current activity and measured physiological consequences form distinct levels of explanation. A bacterial population may possess a pathway but lack the environmental substrate or regulation needed to express it. Conversely, two communities can carry different species but share biochemical capabilities because the relevant genes occur in several lineages.

The distinction also clarifies the evolutionary discussion. The holobiont is an ecological assemblage; the hologenome describes its combined genetic material. The stronger claim that the complete assemblage forms a stable unit of natural selection is a hypothesis requiring independent evidence. For selection on an association to generate cumulative evolutionary change, variation in the association must influence reproductive success and be transmitted with sufficient fidelity. Reliable association can arise through direct transmission or consistent environmental reacquisition, but neither follows merely from observing co-occurrence in an adult animal.

Imagine two host genotypes, H1 and H2, each able to acquire microbial strain M from the environment. M may enhance growth of both hosts. If H1 acquires M more reliably because it supplies a specific nutrient, selection may favour the host trait that promotes acquisition. M can simultaneously evolve traits that improve its transmission among hosts. This outcome may produce coadaptation without requiring the entire set of microorganisms in either host to be inherited as a single genome. Some members could be selected primarily for their own proliferation, even when their activities impose a cost on the animal. An evolutionary model must accommodate these conflicts as well as cooperation.

Historically, association between organisms has motivated several conceptual frameworks. Symbiosis supplied the vocabulary of close living relationships; microbial ecology introduced analysis of mixed populations; genome sequencing opened a way to investigate collective genetic potential. Modern holobiont terminology brings those perspectives together, but the methodological advances do not make a universal evolutionary conclusion inevitable. The important skill is to identify the level of explanation supported by the evidence.

Ecological interactions and changing fitness effects

A biological interaction is classified by its effect on the participants relative to a defined comparison. Mutualism occurs when both participants benefit, commensalism when one benefits and the other experiences no detectable net effect, and parasitism when one benefits at the expense of the other. Competition reduces the performance of both competitors relative to the situation in which the competitor is absent. Amensalism occurs when one population is inhibited while the other experiences no detectable effect. The signs are descriptions of outcomes under specified conditions, not permanent moral labels for species.

Benefits must be defined using a relevant endpoint. For a bacterial population, growth rate, survival and transmission are possible endpoints. For an animal, a biochemical improvement is not automatically equivalent to higher lifetime reproductive success. A substance produced by a bacterium may improve one physiological variable while having no effect on survival. In an experimental question, use the stated endpoints rather than translating every measured increase into an evolutionary fitness gain.

Competition for a resource is often indirect. Two populations can both consume a limited sugar without physically contacting one another. When one population removes the sugar, the concentration available to the other falls. This is exploitative competition. In interference competition, one organism actively inhibits another, for example by producing an antimicrobial compound. The same populations may compete under one diet and coexist under another if the abundance or type of limiting substrate changes.

Cross-feeding occurs when the product released by one population becomes a substrate for another. A primary degrader may release smaller carbohydrates that a second organism can transport. The second population can increase without possessing the enzymes required to attack the original polymer. If removal of the intermediate helps the first population maintain favourable reaction conditions, the relationship may benefit both. If it does not affect the first population appreciably, the relationship may be closer to commensalism. Demonstrating transfer of a metabolite establishes a material connection, not automatically the sign of the interaction.

Physical space introduces another layer. Attachment to mucus or food particles can limit loss from a flowing habitat. Competition for an attachment site may therefore occur even when freely available nutrient concentrations are high. Microbes may form spatial associations where diffusion distances determine which metabolites are accessible. The relevant ecological neighbourhood can be much smaller than the complete intestinal region represented by a stool sample.

Context-dependent pathogenicity illustrates why familiar categories should be applied cautiously. A resident intestinal bacterium that rarely damages an intact epithelium can cause disease if introduced into internal tissues or if host defences are impaired. The term pathobiont is often used for a resident organism with disease-promoting potential under particular circumstances. It does not mean that every carrier has disease or that the organism is sufficient to produce disease in every host. Different strains of a named species may have different capabilities; a taxonomic label alone does not establish virulence.

The host also participates in ecological interactions. Secretions may nourish selected bacteria, epithelial defence may suppress others, and motility can change the time available for multiplication. In return, resident populations may occupy space and consume resources that would otherwise be accessible to invading organisms. Such effects provide a conceptual basis for colonisation resistance, the ability of an established community to oppose establishment of outsiders. Its specific physiological mechanisms belong to the next chapter.

For a reasoning problem, distinguish direct competition from an indirect effect mediated by the environment. If population A acidifies the medium and population B declines, A may have inhibited B through a chemical change rather than through nutrient exhaustion. Buffering the medium while maintaining substrate availability can help separate the explanations. If B recovers when pH is held constant, acidity is implicated; if it does not recover, additional mechanisms should be investigated. This logic is more informative than simply describing A and B as negatively correlated.

The diverse biological members of gut communities

Bacteria are prokaryotic cells with membrane-bounded cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA. Many gut bacteria obtain energy by degrading organic matter, using fermentation or respiration according to the available electron acceptors. Their ability to tolerate oxygen varies. A single animal can contain many different bacterial lineages with distinct substrate preferences, oxygen requirements and growth strategies. Broad taxonomic groups commonly discussed in the human gut include Bacteroidota and Bacillota, also known in older literature as Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, respectively. These names identify lineages rather than fixed physiological roles. Species and strains within a lineage may differ markedly.

Archaea are prokaryotic organisms distinct from bacteria in evolutionary history and several cellular properties. Gut-associated methanogenic archaea can use products of microbial fermentation, including hydrogen in appropriate reactions. By consuming hydrogen, such organisms can alter the chemical environment experienced by fermenters. The quantity of methane ultimately produced depends on the availability of substrates, the abundance and activity of methanogens, competing hydrogen-consuming processes and habitat conditions. Not every animal or human carries the same number of active methanogens. Bacterial profiling alone is not a reliable estimate of archaeal diversity.

Microbial eukaryotes include fungi and protists. Fungi such as yeasts can occur in intestinal samples, but a detected fungal sequence may come from an established resident, a recently ingested organism or nonliving material. The fungal community is sometimes called the mycobiota or mycobiome, depending on the definition in use. Protists include organisms that consume other microorganisms, live in association with host tissues or act as parasites. In certain herbivorous animal systems, microbial eukaryotes play conspicuous roles in the processing of plant material. Their importance must be established for each host and habitat rather than inferred from human bacterial surveys.

Viruses are acellular infectious entities, not metabolically self-sufficient cellular microorganisms. Bacteriophages infect bacterial hosts and can influence bacterial population dynamics. In a lytic infection, viral replication leads to release of progeny and often lysis of the infected cell. Temperate phages can maintain their genetic material in a host cell as a prophage, with later induction into productive infection under appropriate conditions. A phage that reduces one bacterial population can indirectly affect competitors and cross-feeding partners. Eukaryotic viruses also occur in host-associated viral assemblages. The term virome encompasses the viral component and may be operationally defined by the nucleic acids detected.

Because bacteria, archaea, fungi and protists have different cell structures and genetic markers, the observed composition depends on the method used. A survey based on a bacterial ribosomal RNA gene cannot be assumed to capture the viral community. The absence of a detected signal may mean the organism is truly absent, but may also reflect poor recovery, low abundance, inappropriate primers or limited sampling. Similarly, DNA from a dead cell does not demonstrate active colonisation. These are conceptual limits on ecological inference, while the systematic comparison of laboratory approaches belongs to Node 7.K.03.

Taxonomic richness describes the number of distinct groups observed at the chosen resolution. Evenness describes how uniformly individuals or measured abundances are distributed among those groups. A community of ten equally common taxa differs from a community of ten taxa dominated by one. The biological significance of the difference depends on functions, interactions and the question being tested. A highly specialized beneficial symbiosis can involve very few species, whereas a diverse assemblage can contain harmful organisms. Diversity is not itself a universal measure of health.

A related distinction is between core taxa and core functions. Taxa shared among many individuals may be described as taxonomic core members according to a stated prevalence threshold. A function may be common even when its carriers differ among individuals. For example, two bacterial communities may each degrade a particular substrate using different strains. This property is called functional redundancy when more than one member can perform a relevant function. Redundancy is rarely complete: the organisms may differ in rate, regulation, product yield or response to disturbances. A shared pathway does not guarantee identical output.

Spatial ecology: gradients, niches and intestinal transit

A microbial niche is the set of conditions, resources and interactions under which a population can maintain itself. Habitat describes where it lives; niche describes how it persists there. A gut region is not a homogeneous flask. Longitudinal differences extend from the stomach through the small intestine to the colon, and radial differences extend from the epithelial surface through mucus into the central lumen.

The stomach imposes a strong acid barrier, although its acidity changes with meals and anatomical location. Organisms entering with food do not all survive. In the proximal small intestine, bile, pancreatic secretions, absorption and movement of digesta create additional filters. Rapid transit and host absorption can limit the opportunity for populations to accumulate. Distal intestinal regions tend to offer longer residence times and more material that escaped host digestion. The colon is consequently a major site of dense anaerobic microbial communities in many mammals. This is a general pattern, not a rule assigning an identical density or pH to every animal.

Oxygen enters intestinal tissues from the vascular supply. Cells and microorganisms consume oxygen, so concentrations can decline toward the lumen. The strongly reducing conditions found in much of the colon favour organisms adapted to low oxygen. Obligate anaerobes cannot grow normally in oxygenated conditions and may be damaged by oxygen exposure; facultative anaerobes can grow with or without oxygen, using different metabolic strategies; aerotolerant anaerobes tolerate oxygen but do not depend on aerobic respiration. The local balance of supply and consumption determines conditions. An intestinal compartment can therefore contain microhabitats with different oxygen availability even when the bulk luminal environment is anaerobic.

A gradient arises whenever transport of a chemical is not fast enough to remove differences established by localized sources and sinks. Consider oxygen entering from one boundary and being consumed throughout tissue and mucus. The direction of diffusion is toward lower concentration. In a simplified steady state, oxygen supplied at the boundary is balanced by oxygen consumption and transport away. Increasing microbial or epithelial consumption can steepen the gradient if supply remains fixed. Increasing supply can allow oxygen to penetrate farther if consumption does not rise equivalently. The actual shape depends on geometry, diffusion coefficients and biological consumption, not merely the distance from the bloodstream.

Nutrient gradients also arise because substrates enter from the diet or host secretions and are removed through digestion, absorption and microbial activity. Mucus contains host-derived glycans that are available to specialized populations. A bacterium living near mucus can therefore access a resource even if a dietary polysaccharide is temporarily scarce. A population associated with food particles instead encounters concentrated dietary material. Distinct resource distributions promote coexistence by allowing organisms with different metabolic capacities to occupy different niches.

The intestine is an open system with continuous input and output. If a free-living population grows slowly while the contents move quickly, it may be washed out. In an idealized well-mixed compartment of volume V litres and outflow Q litres per hour, the dilution rate D equals Q/V in inverse hours. For a freely suspended population with specific growth rate μ and no additional loss, the net rate is μ minus D. If μ is smaller than D, abundance decreases in the absence of immigration. If μ exceeds D, abundance can increase until nutrients or other constraints reduce growth. Real intestinal communities violate the well-mixed assumption because of attachment, spatial structure and variable flow. The model is useful precisely because it identifies a mechanism that those biological details modify.

For example, take a model compartment with a volume of 0.5 L and flow of 0.1 L per hour. Its dilution rate is 0.2 per hour. A population growing at 0.15 per hour experiences a net rate of minus 0.05 per hour under the stated assumptions. A population growing at 0.3 per hour instead has a net rate of 0.1 per hour. Attachment that effectively reduces loss could change the persistence of the first population without requiring it to divide faster. This example illustrates why anatomical structures and microbial adhesion can matter to community composition.

Mucosal surfaces are especially important because they provide spatially localized resources and encounter host defence. Mucus can act both as a separation layer and as a source of substrates. Its organization varies across the gastrointestinal tract and animal species. Microbes colonising outer mucus or lumen need not invade the epithelial cells themselves. If a barrier is disrupted, the physical relationships between populations and host tissue can change dramatically even without a large change in stool taxonomic composition. Location and abundance are therefore different ecological variables.

A faecal sample is a practical window into material leaving the distal gut. It is not a direct map of oxygen exposure, attachment, position within mucus or every upstream region. When two faecal samples differ, the result may reflect changes in population growth, transit, detachment or input from another region. When they appear similar, important spatial differences may nevertheless exist. The correct interpretation should remain at the level of the sampling design.

Community assembly and ecological succession

Community assembly begins with the arrival of potential inhabitants, but arrival alone does not establish residency. To colonise a habitat, an organism must reach it, survive its physical and chemical conditions, gain access to resources and remain long enough to reproduce or otherwise persist. The sequence can be regarded as dispersal followed by environmental filtering and interactions among established populations. Failure at any stage can prevent a potentially suitable organism from becoming a stable member.

The earliest residents can influence the success of later arrivals. This phenomenon is called a priority effect. A pioneer population may consume oxygen, release an intermediate metabolite or occupy available attachment sites. Its presence can facilitate later colonisation by some organisms while excluding others. Community history can therefore influence the outcome even when the final environment has the same externally imposed conditions. This is one reason why animals sharing a diet and host species need not converge on identical species compositions.

In early life, mammalian intestinal communities often change rapidly as exposure to microorganisms, feeding and gut development change. Sources of microorganisms include caregivers, food, other animals and the surrounding environment. Birth mode is associated with differences in early community development, but it is not the sole determinant and does not define an individual's microbiome indefinitely. Early milk feeding supplies a distinctive set of nutrients, including oligosaccharides that can favour organisms capable of using them. Introduction of complementary foods changes the resources offered to the community and can alter the relative success of microbial groups.

Succession describes directional change in community composition over time. It does not mean that every infant follows a fixed taxonomic sequence or that there is one universal endpoint. The direction and pace can vary with exposure, feeding, host development and geography. The increasing variety of dietary resources after weaning may allow additional metabolic niches to become occupied, but a change in diversity should not be interpreted independently of age and feeding stage. A low-diversity milk-associated community may be entirely appropriate for its developmental context.

The 2018 TEDDY study followed infant gut microbiome development using repeated stool samples and linked metadata. Such a longitudinal design is useful because it observes change within individuals rather than comparing unrelated children of different ages. The study documented age-associated development and feeding-related patterns. Its observational character is important: associations with feeding or delivery mode can be influenced by correlated exposures, and the study does not make every age-related species change a necessary causal step in human development.

Adult communities can fluctuate even without an obvious disturbance. Daily meals, stool transit and other exposures change the conditions experienced by microorganisms. A population that is consistently present may vary in abundance, while a population not detected on one day may reappear in a later sample. Stability should therefore be assessed against the natural range of variation observed over repeated measurements. Comparing a single pre-disturbance sample with a single post-disturbance sample can mistake ordinary variation for disruption.

Ecological succession differs from evolutionary change. In succession, changes in abundance or arrival of taxa alter community composition. Evolution involves heritable change within populations across generations. Both processes may occur simultaneously in microbes, but a time series of relative abundance does not by itself demonstrate genetic adaptation. A change from one lineage to another may simply reflect ecological sorting of pre-existing variants. Evidence of heritable changes in a lineage requires genetic or experimentally demonstrated inheritance information.

The concepts of dispersal and filtering also explain the distinction between microbial exposure and effective transmission. A strain present in the environment may never enter a particular host, and a strain encountered repeatedly may fail to establish. Transmission between generations can be direct from parent to offspring, indirect through shared surroundings or some combination. For evolutionary reasoning, the crucial question is whether the association is reliably reproduced across relevant generations, not merely whether the microorganism was once present in a parent.

Host variation, diet and disturbance

The host determines several ecological conditions through anatomy, digestive enzymes, secretions, immune characteristics and feeding behaviour. Herbivorous animals that rely heavily on microbial processing of plant polymers may possess enlarged fermentation compartments. The rumen of a ruminant provides a relatively retentive habitat where microbes process plant material before digesta passes farther through the gastrointestinal system. Hindgut fermenters instead conduct much fermentation after the small intestine. These anatomical arrangements influence where microbial products can be absorbed and how microbes are exposed to dietary material. They also demonstrate why it is misleading to impose the taxonomic expectations of the human colon on every animal.

Within a host species, diet influences resources and therefore changes selection among organisms. A polysaccharide useful to one microbial population may be unavailable to another because the latter lacks appropriate degradation enzymes. A change in food composition can favour existing organisms before any new strain arrives. If the original population was absent, however, supplying the substrate does not guarantee the required function will appear. Community response depends on both the ecological opportunity and the organisms capable of exploiting it.

Dietary effects can be direct or indirect. A compound may be metabolized directly by a bacterium; alternatively, the host may respond to the diet by changing bile secretion, transit or the amount of substrate reaching the colon. Such host-mediated effects can alter many taxa at once. A correlation between a food and bacterial abundance cannot distinguish these pathways without further evidence. Controlled feeding, measurements of intermediate variables and appropriate comparison groups can strengthen interpretation.

Medications introduce another type of perturbation. Antibiotics can reduce susceptible populations and change competition or resource availability for surviving organisms. A resistant or tolerant population may increase in relative abundance simply because other populations decline. This does not establish an increase in its absolute cell number. Some medication effects can also be indirect through altered motility or secretions. A microbiome study comparing patients with healthy controls must therefore consider whether medication exposure differs between the groups.

Resistance and resilience describe different responses to perturbation. Resistance is the extent to which a system changes little while exposed to a disturbance. Resilience is its capacity to recover after the disturbance is removed. A community may be strongly altered by an antibiotic but return near its original state, showing low resistance and substantial resilience. Another may change moderately and then remain altered. The relevant state variable must be specified: taxonomic composition, total cell density and a metabolic function may recover on different time scales.

Dethlefsen and Relman's study of repeated antibiotic exposure in a small number of adults illustrated that recovery can differ between people and can remain incomplete. Its dense time series is informative about individual trajectories. The small number of participants prevents direct estimation of the proportion of all people who would experience incomplete recovery. The scientifically defensible conclusion concerns the observed responses and the possibility of individual-specific trajectories, rather than a prediction that every exposure permanently changes the gut.

Recovery can occur through expansion of surviving populations, immigration from environmental sources or reseeding from protected microhabitats. Functional recovery can precede restoration of the original taxonomic composition if replacement organisms carry similar capabilities. Conversely, a taxonomic profile that returns near baseline can conceal changes at strain level or in gene expression. A robust investigation measures recovery at the level relevant to the physiological question.

A universal healthy taxonomic profile is unlikely because animals have diverse evolutionary histories, diets and intestinal anatomies, and even individuals of the same species differ. This does not imply that all community configurations are equally compatible with health. A specific organism or metabolic process can be harmful or beneficial in a defined setting. The distinction is between rejecting a single universal list and rejecting any relationship between microbial ecology and physiology. The latter would be unjustified.

Environmental factors can be confounded with host factors. If a wild animal population and a captive population have different gut microbiota, differences in diet, housing, contact networks, stress, medication or age may all contribute. Host genotype cannot be inferred as the cause merely from population membership. An appropriate experiment isolates one candidate factor or measures and accounts for plausible alternatives. Even then, a result from one species or captive setting may not generalize to another.

Comparative symbioses and evolutionary interpretation

Animals illustrate a continuum of microbial associations. The Hawaiian bobtail squid acquires its luminous bacterial partner from seawater after hatching. The interaction takes place in a specialized light organ, not the gut. It is valuable as a comparison because it demonstrates selective establishment of an association from an environmental microbial pool. The animal and bacterium possess traits contributing to recognition and persistence, so environmental acquisition need not mean an indiscriminate or unreliable relationship.

A key experimental advantage of the squid association is that researchers can compare animals with and without colonisation by their characteristic partner and investigate features of the host and bacterial cells during establishment. The review literature documents the sequential selection involved in the association. The example is not evidence that every intestinal microbial community behaves as a two-partner system. In the gut, many interacting organisms, dietary inputs and different host compartments complicate attribution of any effect to one population.

The aphid association with Buchnera provides a contrasting example of an intimate intracellular symbiosis. Buchnera is transmitted maternally and provides nutritional capabilities important for its host's restricted diet. Its relationship has a high degree of partner dependence relative to many environmentally acquired gut bacteria. Transmission fidelity and dependence create conditions under which selection can act strongly on characteristics of the association. They still do not imply that every genetic interest of the two partners is identical. Conflicts can remain possible even in tightly coupled systems.

Ruminant gut communities represent a multispecies nutritional association. Primary degraders, fermenters and organisms that use fermentation products form a network in a retentive compartment. Methanogenic archaea can compete with alternative hydrogen-consuming populations, and fungi or protozoa may participate in feed breakdown and trophic interactions. Community functions can arise through many combinations of members. Unlike the specialized squid light-organ association, there is no reason to assume one bacterial partner is sufficient to reproduce all ecosystem functions.

Compare host-associated and free-living microbial ecosystems by considering the origin of constraints. In soil, physical particles, moisture, organic matter and interactions among organisms establish gradients and resource patches. In the gut, analogous processes operate, but the host supplies regulated secretions, controls transit and responds immunologically to organisms. Both environments are shaped by microbial activity. The distinction is the strong biological regulation imposed by the host, not the presence versus absence of ecology.

The hologenome debate becomes clearer when these examples are placed together. High-fidelity inheritance and mutual dependence in a specialized symbiosis can make the association an important level for evolutionary explanation. An environmentally assembled gut community may contain organisms with divergent transmission routes, variable membership and competing fitness interests. Host-mediated selection and microbial evolution can both occur, but evidence for collective selection on the whole assemblage must not be inferred from physiological integration alone.

Suppose a microbial variant grows faster within an animal but reduces the animal's reproductive output. Selection within hosts may favour the variant, whereas selection among host-associated systems may oppose it if more productive hosts contribute more descendants. The outcome depends on the relative strength of the processes and the way variants reach new hosts. This elementary multilevel reasoning shows why cooperation cannot simply be presumed and why a broad ecological concept must be distinguished from a strong evolutionary claim.

Quantitative evidence and examination reasoning

Microbiome abundance is commonly reported as a proportion of measured community members. If a population has abundance N and the total abundance of all counted populations is T, its relative abundance is N/T. Multiplying by 100 expresses the result as a percentage. Both numerator and denominator matter. For example, if a population remains at two million cells while total cells decrease from ten million to four million, its relative abundance rises from twenty to fifty percent. Its absolute abundance has not changed. The increase in proportion reflects the decline of other members. This simple arithmetic is central to interpretation of community profiles.

An observed relative abundance can be affected by the measurement process. Counts based on sequence reads need not equal counts of cells because extraction, gene copy number and amplification can influence detection. A hypothetical dataset using direct cell counts avoids these particular issues, but a real molecular survey requires calibration or complementary measurements to support an absolute abundance claim. Node 7.K.03 develops these limitations in greater detail.

Diversity has multiple dimensions. Richness is the number of detected taxa at a given resolution. Evenness considers their distribution. Suppose community A consists of four taxa with 25 percent abundance each, while B contains the same four taxa at 85, 5, 5 and 5 percent. Both have richness four, but A has greater evenness. If taxon D disappears from B, richness decreases to three even if the remaining populations become more even. Different diversity indices may respond differently because they assign different importance to rare and dominant taxa. The existence of a single numerical index does not remove the need to specify the ecological question.

To assess succession, consider an illustrative set of repeated counts from a developing gut. Early samples show a group of facultative organisms predominating; subsequent samples show increasing numbers of obligate anaerobes. This is compatible with environmental modification through oxygen consumption or changing substrate supply, but the counts do not directly identify the cause. A measurement of oxygen and a controlled change in oxygen availability would strengthen the oxygen explanation. If substrate composition changes simultaneously, it remains an alternative explanation. The correct Part C answer should identify the evidence required to distinguish the models.

Correlation between two organisms may arise without direct interaction. Both might increase when the same nutrient becomes available. Alternatively, one may supply the other with a metabolite. To distinguish shared environmental preference from cross-feeding, compare growth of the second organism in the presence of the substrate alone and in a medium conditioned by the first organism, with suitable pH and nutrient controls. Evidence that a specific product is consumed by the recipient and that its addition restores growth would strengthen the cross-feeding mechanism. It would not establish that the relationship is mutualistic unless a benefit to the producer were also demonstrated.

A community profile can be a consequence rather than a cause of a host condition. If an inflammatory change increases oxygen availability near a mucosal surface, organisms tolerant of oxygen may increase. This association does not establish that those organisms initiated inflammation. They may be responding to it, or their proliferation may reinforce it. Temporal observations and controlled perturbations are needed to distinguish initial causes, secondary responses and feedback.

The same logic applies to tests of host evolutionary association. If two related animal species harbour similar microbial populations, shared host traits may filter the communities similarly, but similar diets or environments can also explain the observation. Demonstrating parallel host and microbial phylogenies is suggestive in some systems, yet strict co-divergence requires additional evidence of transmission and lineage history. A defensible answer never confuses taxonomic resemblance, functional similarity, causal interaction and evolutionary individuality.

Estimating the scale and distribution of microbial life

A useful way to understand the magnitude of a microbial community is to distinguish density, volume and total abundance. If a sample contains n cells per gram of material and the mass of the corresponding compartment is m grams, then a first estimate of its cell number is n multiplied by m. The estimate is valid only if the sampled density is representative. If the habitat is heterogeneous, the calculation must be carried out separately for regions with different densities. Multiplication of a density obtained from the colon by the entire gastrointestinal volume can therefore produce a misleading estimate.

The calculation was important in the history of the widely repeated claim that microbial cells outnumber human cells ten to one. Sender, Fuchs and Milo revisited assumptions about bacterial densities and human cell counts and obtained estimates closer to parity for a reference adult in their 2016 analysis. The revised result does not diminish the physiological significance of microbial activity. It demonstrates that a frequently repeated numerical statement is not a universal biological constant. Body composition, intestinal contents and the boundaries of the calculation all matter. Bacterial cell number is not synonymous with microbial biomass, total genetic diversity or number of expressed enzymes.

Consider a teaching model containing three intestinal compartments. The first holds 50 g of contents at 10 million microbial cells per gram, the second 100 g at 100 million cells per gram and the third 300 g at 100 billion cells per gram. The estimated numbers are 500 million, 10 billion and 30 trillion cells respectively. The distal compartment overwhelmingly dominates the total estimate, although it contains only two thirds of the mass. This is an illustrative arithmetic exercise, not a measured human distribution. It shows why a minor error in estimating density or volume in the most heavily colonised region affects the total more than a comparable relative error in a sparsely colonised region.

An organism may be present at a small proportion yet remain ecologically significant. A taxon accounting for one tenth of one percent of a very large community may still contain numerous active cells. Conversely, a large percentage within a low-biomass sample may represent few cells. When interpreting samples from different locations, the absolute magnitude of the habitat population therefore deserves separate attention. In an exam question, a high relative abundance in a stomach sample does not automatically imply a higher total cell number than a modest percentage in a colon sample.

The scale of the community also affects how ecological changes propagate. A large population can generate many descendants and therefore opportunities for genetic variation, but population size alone does not guarantee persistence. A population can experience severe bottlenecks when it is transmitted between hosts. An association that is abundant during adult life may be represented by only a small subset of strains in an offspring. Genetic diversity can consequently be lost at the transmission stage even when the adult habitat supported a large number of organisms.

Biological mass introduces another distinction. A microbial eukaryote is often much larger than a bacterial cell. A small count of eukaryotic cells can therefore represent a greater biomass contribution than its numerical percentage suggests. Viruses add another complication because viral particles are not cells. Comparing viral particle numbers with bacterial cell counts is meaningful for questions such as opportunities for encounter but does not produce a meaningful percentage of total cellular organisms. Define the counting unit before constructing a ratio.

The same care is needed when interpreting relative gene counts. A bacterium can carry several copies of a marker gene, and the amount of DNA per cell may vary with genome size or replication status. A host cell and a bacterial cell contribute different amounts of genetic material. A ratio of detected sequences is not necessarily a ratio of cells. A taxon that appears to increase after a change in extraction conditions may simply have become easier to detect. These examples establish why accurate ecological descriptions begin with a statement of what was actually counted.

For physiological inference, even absolute cell numbers may be inadequate without a rate measurement. If two samples each contain one million cells of a species, but the cells in one sample consume substrate five times faster, their total contribution to resource turnover differs fivefold. A snapshot of community size is thus complementary to measurement of its chemical activity. The connection between abundance, activity and host responses is developed in the following node.

Energy availability, resource partitioning and coexistence

The ecological consequences of microbial metabolism become clearer when the resources are separated into energy sources, carbon sources and electron acceptors. Organic molecules can provide carbon for cell synthesis as well as energy for metabolism. An electron acceptor permits electrons released during oxidation to be transferred to another molecule. Oxygen supports aerobic respiration when the cell has the necessary metabolic machinery, whereas some microbes use alternative acceptors or ferment substrates without an external terminal acceptor. The gut environment does not supply these possibilities equally everywhere.

A resource can be abundant in total quantity yet inaccessible to a particular organism. Large dietary polymers must be cleaved before their components can enter many microbial cells. Some microbes produce enzymes at the cell surface or in the surrounding medium; others depend on already released products. An organism that lacks the ability to use intact starch may nevertheless grow on sugars released by a starch degrader. The environmental quantity of starch alone is therefore not sufficient to predict the growth of every population in the system. The enzymatic capabilities and activities of neighbouring organisms determine the effective resource supply.

Resource partitioning refers to the division of available ecological opportunities among organisms. Consider two populations living in the same intestinal compartment. One primarily uses a dietary polysaccharide, while the other uses a host-secreted glycan. Each can maintain a population even if the other consumes its preferred substrate. If the diet changes, the first population may decline while the second remains supported by the host-derived resource. Their coexistence does not require direct cooperation; it may result from incomplete overlap in their limiting resources.

The opposite scenario is illustrated by two populations that depend on exactly the same limiting sugar under the same conditions. If they have no additional differences in nutrient use, spatial position, loss rates or environmental responses, the population that maintains growth at lower sugar concentration may tend to exclude the other in a simple steady environment. A fluctuating and spatially organized gut can weaken such a prediction. Resource pulses, differential adhesion, phage predation and interactions with other organisms can all provide alternative routes to persistence. Competitive exclusion is a useful ecological principle, not an assertion that every pair of gut bacteria using the same substrate must show complete exclusion.

A microbial guild is a group of organisms performing a similar ecological function or using a related resource. The members of a polysaccharide-degrading guild need not be closely related by ancestry. A taxonomic classification answers an evolutionary question about lineages, whereas a guild classification answers a functional question about ecological activities. These classifications may overlap but should not be substituted for each other. If an examination presents two different bacterial genera that both hydrolyse a particular substrate, they can be assigned to the same functional guild despite their taxonomic separation.

In a food web, the material processed by one organism may become available to several others. A primary degrader can release two fermentation intermediates that support different secondary consumers. Secondary consumers can in turn release products used by other populations. Such branching creates indirect dependencies: a decline in the primary degrader can reduce several other populations even if it never contacts them. Conversely, an increase in one secondary consumer can redirect resources away from another, creating competition farther along the network. Identifying which connections involve actual exchange rather than statistical correlation is central to mechanistic ecology.

The relationship between a hydrogen-producing fermenter and a hydrogen-consuming microbe illustrates how resource exchange can change the physical feasibility of metabolism. If a product accumulates, the energetics of the reaction producing it can become less favourable. A consumer that removes that product can help maintain conditions supporting continued fermentation. This type of interdependence is commonly discussed as syntrophy when organisms obtain benefits from coupled metabolic activities that would otherwise be energetically constrained. Not all instances of cross-feeding meet the stronger definition of syntrophy. For example, a bacterium consuming surplus sugar released by another without changing the producer's performance can participate in cross-feeding without such obligatory interdependence.

A useful experimental prediction follows. In a culture system, compare a fermenter grown alone with a fermenter grown together with a hydrogen-consuming partner. Measure substrate disappearance, product accumulation and growth of each partner. If hydrogen accumulation falls in the combined culture and the producer's substrate use increases, the result is consistent with removal of an inhibitory or energetically limiting product. A separate condition in which hydrogen is removed without the partner can test whether the physical change is sufficient to explain the effect. The partner may also provide other metabolites, so co-culture alone cannot identify the specific mechanism.

The importance of a resource depends on turnover as well as standing concentration. A metabolite can remain at a low concentration because it is produced slowly or because it is produced rapidly and consumed rapidly. A single low measurement cannot distinguish these situations. In a gut ecosystem with multiple producers and consumers, flux describes transfer of material per unit time, whereas concentration describes the amount per unit volume. This distinction connects microbial ecology with the biochemical concept of steady state. At steady state, concentration can remain unchanged despite substantial opposing rates of production and consumption.

A conceptual balance for a luminal metabolite is production plus input minus microbial consumption, host absorption and outflow. If these terms are expressed in micromoles per hour, their sum gives the change in total metabolite amount per hour. For example, if microbial production is 80 micromoles per hour, microbial uptake is 30, host absorption is 40 and outflow is 10, the amount remains constant in the absence of other processes. Increasing microbial uptake by 10 without changing any other rate initially reduces the amount by 10 micromoles per hour. Over time, rates can change as substrate concentrations change. The calculation makes clear why an unchanged concentration does not establish that microbial activity is unchanged.

Habitat pH is another property shaped by opposing processes. Fermentation may produce organic acids, but the resulting pH depends on buffering, movement of digesta and absorption as well as acid production. Because pH is logarithmic, a decrease of one pH unit represents a tenfold increase in hydrogen ion activity under the usual definition. The biological response of an organism depends on the pH range in which its transport systems and enzymes operate effectively. A population favoured by acidification in one habitat may be disadvantaged by the same degree of acidification in another. An ecological argument should therefore identify the direction and magnitude of environmental change, not merely whether acid-producing microorganisms are present.

Resource partitioning, cross-feeding and chemical modification are jointly important. The removal of one population can release a resource for its competitors while depriving its consumers of a product. The same perturbation may therefore cause increases and decreases in different members. This explains why a disturbance targeted at a small part of the community can have broader consequences. The next chapter traces the resulting products into host physiology; the present concern is the ecological origin of those products.

How host characteristics filter and organize microbial communities

The host can favour or exclude organisms without recognizing every member as an individual species. An ecological filter is a habitat condition that permits some biological traits to support persistence more effectively than others. An acidic compartment, for instance, favours organisms that tolerate its acidity. A mucus-rich surface favours organisms that can attach to it or obtain nutrients there. These consequences follow from interactions between microbial traits and host properties; they need not require a separate receptor for every species.

The intestinal wall performs a dual function as a transport interface and as a barrier. Nutrients can pass toward the host under regulated conditions, while whole organisms are normally restricted from unrestricted entry into tissues. Mucus, epithelial continuity and local defence contribute to that separation. These features influence microbial niches even when the host does not eliminate resident microorganisms. A host can tolerate close association while retaining spatial control over the location of microbial cells. This is a crucial distinction between tolerance of residents and absence of defence.

A microbe near the epithelial surface may encounter host-derived nutrients, antimicrobial molecules and a different oxygen concentration from a microbe in the central lumen. Its ecological success reflects multiple conditions simultaneously. Suppose a strain possesses a strong ability to attach to mucus but is sensitive to a host antimicrobial product present near the surface. Its adhesion trait alone may not be sufficient for persistence. A second strain with weaker adhesion but greater tolerance can occupy a different spatial niche. The relative fitness of those traits can change with the physiological state of the host.

Host diet affects the microbial habitat partly through digestive processes. If a host absorbs most of a simple sugar in the proximal small intestine, little of that sugar may reach distal communities even though it is abundant in the meal. A complex polymer resistant to host digestion may instead become an important distal resource. Two foods containing similar total carbohydrate can therefore differ in their effects on gut microbial resource availability. The relevant quantity for a distal bacterial population is the substrate that arrives at its location in usable form, not the amount listed as total carbohydrate in the ingested meal.

The host's movement of contents changes the relationship between growth and physical loss. A region with rapid transit imposes a stronger washout pressure on freely suspended organisms than a region with long retention, other factors being equal. Host contractions also mix contents and change contact with mucus. For a population attached to food particles, the movement of those particles can determine retention. Consequently, measurements of faecal abundance may reflect stool transit as well as underlying community differences. A study of variation among people should distinguish these possibilities whenever its design allows.

Consider a simplified experiment in which the same microbial mixture is introduced into two otherwise comparable model habitats with different residence times. If a slow-growing strain persists only in the longer-retention condition, the result supports transit as a filter. If attachment opportunities differ between the habitats, however, that explanation is not isolated. A stronger design matches surface properties, temperature and nutrient inputs and varies flow alone. In the animal, changing transit may also modify exposure to secretions and nutrient absorption. The model and animal experiments answer complementary questions rather than providing identical evidence.

Different host species can possess different filtering traits even when they encounter the same pool of environmental microorganisms. The shape of the gut, available fermentation chamber, temperature, feeding pattern and immune properties differ. A strain successful in one host can fail to colonise another because the required niche is missing. This ecological host specificity does not prove that the microbial lineage originated with that host lineage. Demonstrating co-divergence requires historical genetic evidence in addition to a pattern of present-day specificity.

Within a species, host age modifies the habitat. The developing intestinal epithelium, dietary transitions and maturation of host defences change the opportunities available to microbes. A strain that grows well in a milk-fed infant may not have an advantage in an adult consuming diverse plant and animal substrates. It may nevertheless persist at lower abundance or occupy a localized niche. During aging, changes in diet, medications, motility and living environment may modify microbial communities, but chronological age alone cannot be isolated from these factors by a simple comparison of younger and older people.

Host genetic effects can be investigated conceptually by comparing different host genotypes maintained with similar food, housing and microbial exposure. A reproducible difference in colonisation under these conditions suggests a contribution from host genotype or genotype-dependent physiology. To identify a specific mechanism, the candidate trait should be measured and ideally manipulated. For example, if one genotype produces a different mucosal glycan profile, a strain's ability to use those glycans can be tested. This reasoning separates a broad genetic association from a defined biochemical pathway.

The host can also modify microbial ecology as a consequence of stress or illness. If an altered physiological state changes nutrient delivery, movement, secretion or local oxygen, microbial changes may follow. Such changes can feed back on the host because altered populations produce different compounds. A one-directional diagram from microbes to disease or from disease to microbes may therefore be incomplete. At the ecological level, coupled changes can be analysed by specifying which variable changes first, what intermediate mechanism links it to another variable and which perturbation would test the proposed causal direction.

Reading landmark studies of spatial structure and dietary change

An ecological mechanism should generate observable predictions. If oxygen supplied by intestinal tissues contributes to radial microbial organisation, then oxygen measurements at different positions should reveal a spatial gradient. In addition, organisms with differing oxygen requirements should show nonrandom distributions across the same positions. Establishing that these two predictions hold is stronger than noting that the colon contains many anaerobes. However, association of a taxon with a position does not alone prove that oxygen is the sole cause of its distribution.

Albenberg and colleagues investigated intestinal oxygen gradients and microbial spatial partitioning in humans and mice in a study published in Gastroenterology in 2014. Their work combined measurements of oxygenation with investigation of microbial locations. Evidence that oxygen reaches the lumen from tissue and that bacterial distribution varies radially supports the ecological gradient model. The study also highlights the importance of mucus as a local resource. Because multiple conditions vary with distance from the epithelium, one should not convert a spatial correlation into the claim that oxygen explains every radial difference.

A simple follow-up hypothesis is that oxygen sensitivity and mucus utilization make independent contributions to spatial location. A model experiment could compare two strains with matched oxygen tolerance but different abilities to consume mucus-derived substrates, and separately compare strains with matched substrate use but different oxygen tolerance. The first contrast isolates a nutritional trait under the stated assumptions, while the second isolates a physiological tolerance trait. Measuring growth and localization in each contrast allows the contributions to be separated conceptually. Real strains may differ in many additional properties, so carefully matched comparisons or genetic alterations are needed for stronger causal attribution.

Dietary experiments pose a related problem because diets change multiple variables together. David and colleagues reported rapid changes in human microbial community characteristics during controlled short-term dietary interventions, published in Nature in 2014. The study supports the idea that microbial communities respond over relatively short time scales to large dietary changes. It does not establish that every dietary component produces an independent effect or that short-term changes are permanent. It also does not make the community associated with either diet universally desirable for every physiological endpoint.

Imagine a study in which participants consume diet A for five days, return to their usual diet and later consume diet B. Repeated measurements within each participant help control stable differences such as many genetic and long-term environmental factors. Nevertheless, the order of interventions and adequacy of the interval between them remain relevant. If the first diet produces a lasting community change, the second intervention begins from a different baseline. Randomizing the order and including adequate baseline and recovery measurements help reveal whether observed differences depend on the intervention or its sequence.

Even a controlled diet comparison cannot isolate a particular nutrient unless the diets differ only in that nutrient or the design independently varies the candidate components. If diet A differs from diet B in fibre, fat, protein and food-associated microbes, a microbiome difference may involve any combination of these features. A follow-up study can match the other components while changing fermentable substrate availability. Measuring residual substrate, microbial abundance and a relevant product would help connect exposure with mechanism. This illustrates the difference between a dietary pattern effect and an identified substrate-specific effect.

The Human Microbiome Project Consortium's 2012 investigation of healthy people is useful for interpreting interindividual variation. The researchers characterised microbial communities from multiple body sites, allowing comparisons among habitats and individuals. Their results demonstrate that healthy human-associated communities need not share an identical taxonomic structure. The study provides a reference range of observed configurations rather than a universal diagnostic rule. Its conclusions should be considered in relation to the cohort sampled, the methods used and the body sites examined.

A student can distinguish two biological questions within that research framework. First, do samples from the same type of body habitat tend to share more community characteristics than samples from different habitats? Second, do different taxonomic communities carry comparable capacities for important functions? The first concerns ecological filtering by habitat. The second concerns functional overlap. Positive results for both can coexist: habitats can impose strong constraints on overall community structure while leaving room for variation in the identity of species that perform a particular function.

Studies of gut ecology frequently use faecal samples because repeated collection is more practical than repeated access to intestinal tissues. A longitudinal stool-based study is therefore powerful for following changes in excreted community material. It is less direct for testing location within the intestinal wall or local conditions in the small intestine. By contrast, tissue-associated sampling can provide spatial information but is often harder to repeat over long periods. An evidence-based argument should combine these strengths rather than treating either method as a complete representation of the gut ecosystem.

The key lesson from the three research examples is that descriptive measurements, perturbation studies and comparative surveys answer different questions. A descriptive gradient suggests a mechanism for niche partitioning; a controlled dietary change tests the responsiveness of a community to a changed input; a survey of multiple healthy people establishes the extent of observed variation. No one study answers every question about the holobiont. The student's task is to select the appropriate evidence for the claim being evaluated.

From species labels to ecological traits

The ability to name a microbe is different from the ability to predict its behaviour. Species are taxonomic categories, and microbial cells belonging to the same species need not have identical genomes. A strain is a distinguishable lineage within a species, usually identified by genetic or other reproducible properties. Strain differences can affect carbohydrate use, adhesion, resistance to environmental stress, production of metabolites and sensitivity to phages. A community profile reported only at the genus level may therefore hide differences that are decisive for particular ecological functions.

Consider two hypothetical strains of the same species, both able to use glucose. Strain P additionally possesses an enzyme system allowing growth on a particular complex carbohydrate, while strain Q lacks that system. In a medium containing glucose alone, the strains might show similar growth. In a habitat in which the complex carbohydrate is the principal available substrate, P may have a substantial advantage. A genus-level community profile cannot distinguish replacement of Q by P if the total abundance of the genus remains unchanged. Nevertheless, the replacement can change the capacity of that population to use available substrates.

Microbial genomic differences may arise through mutation, loss of genes or acquisition of genetic material. Horizontal gene transfer provides a mechanism by which a genetic trait can spread among cells without ordinary parent-to-offspring descent. Transformation involves acquisition of extracellular DNA, transduction involves phage-mediated transfer, and conjugation involves transfer through contact-dependent processes. These mechanisms were introduced in fundamental molecular biology. Here their ecological implication is that a trait can be distributed differently from the lineages used to classify organisms. The presence of a metabolic gene should not be inferred solely from a broad taxonomic label.

Selection for a trait requires a difference in reproductive success under the conditions of interest. A resistance gene can benefit its carrier when a particular antimicrobial agent is present, but that advantage may be absent when the agent is removed. Maintenance of the gene can impose a cost in some genetic and environmental backgrounds, although a cost is not obligatory. It follows that the ecological response to antimicrobial exposure may involve enrichment of previously existing resistant strains, altered frequencies of resistance genes or both. An immediate increase in resistance-associated sequences does not establish that a new mutation occurred during the observation period.

One way to distinguish changes at different biological levels is to ask what remains constant. If the number of cells assigned to a species stays constant but the genetic variants associated with those cells change, the result is compatible with strain replacement or evolution within that species. If the strain composition stays constant but transcripts associated with a pathway increase, altered regulation is a possible explanation. If transcript abundance stays constant but substrate turnover changes, the availability of substrate or other biochemical conditions may be responsible. Each observation constrains a different part of the causal chain.

Taxonomic identity, genetic potential and expressed traits are especially important in the interpretation of so-called beneficial microbes. Evidence that a particular strain produces a useful metabolite cannot automatically be generalized to all members of its genus. Likewise, a pathogenic strain does not prove that all other strains with the same species name cause disease. If an exam describes a newly isolated strain, the answer should depend on the experimentally demonstrated properties of that strain rather than the reputation of its taxonomic group.

A microbial ecosystem can also contain a reservoir of traits maintained at low abundance. Under an environmental change, carriers of those traits can become more successful. An apparently new community function may emerge from expansion of an existing population rather than immigration or de novo evolution. To distinguish these explanations, sensitive baseline measurements and genetic comparisons with organisms acquired from the environment are useful. A failure to detect a trait at baseline is not conclusive proof that the trait was absent.

This trait-centred perspective prepares the student for the study of the microbiome as an ecological system. It also prevents a common oversimplification: functional predictions do not become precise merely because a large number of taxonomic names have been measured. A short list of experimentally characterized traits may explain a particular ecological response better than an extensive list of organisms whose relevant capabilities remain unknown.

Predation, viral infection and non-bacterial interactions

Microbial ecosystems include consumers as well as organisms that directly obtain resources from diet and host secretions. A protist may ingest bacteria, reducing populations of susceptible prey and releasing nutrients through its own metabolism. Whether the prey declines depends on its rate of reproduction, the feeding preferences of the consumer and the spatial probability of encounters. In a physically structured environment, bacteria protected in an inaccessible location may experience less predation than freely exposed bacteria of the same lineage.

Fungal cells can interact with bacterial populations through the consumption or release of metabolites, competition for substrate and effects on local conditions. A yeast able to persist in a particular gut region may compete for an easily assimilated carbohydrate with neighbouring bacteria. Conversely, a fungal product could be used by a bacterial consumer. The existence of such possible interactions does not establish that they occur at important rates in every gut. Measurements of fungal abundance, activity and spatial association are necessary before assigning an ecological role.

The role of bacteriophages can be understood using the encounter between a susceptible bacterial cell and an infectious viral particle. Successful infection requires compatible cellular structures and permissive intracellular conditions. A virus cannot reproduce independently of a suitable host. Therefore, a phage may be abundant only while a susceptible population is available, although its particles can persist for some time after host abundance falls. A bacterial species with several strains may contain both susceptible and resistant populations, making the species-level abundance a poor indicator of how many cells are available for infection.

Lytic phages can cause density-dependent losses of bacteria. As a susceptible bacterial population grows, encounters with phages may become more frequent, potentially increasing viral replication and subsequent losses. If the bacterial population declines, viral replication may later decrease because suitable hosts are scarce. These feedbacks can produce time-dependent changes in both populations. The actual trajectories depend on infection efficiency, latent periods, environmental removal, host resistance and other ecological interactions. It is not necessary to assume a perfectly regular predator–prey oscillation to recognize the importance of density-dependent infection.

A temperate phage provides an alternative relationship because its genome can persist in the bacterial lineage. When a prophage is maintained during cell division, the viral genetic material is replicated together with the host's genetic material. Under conditions favouring productive infection, the phage can enter a lytic cycle. The ecological consequences depend on the proportion of susceptible bacteria, the prevalence of lysogeny and the conditions affecting induction. The discovery of viral genetic sequences in a bacterial genome does not by itself demonstrate an ongoing episode of cell lysis.

An indirect effect of phage activity can be illustrated with three populations. Bacterium A degrades a dietary polymer, bacterium B uses a product released by A, and bacterium C competes with A for another substrate. A phage selective for A can reduce A's contribution to the food web. B may decline because its resource supply decreases, whereas C may benefit from reduced competition. The phage need not infect B or C for either population to change. A multi-population ecological response therefore cannot be interpreted solely from the phage's host range.

A conceptual experimental approach would compare otherwise similar communities with and without a phage known to infect A, measuring A, B and C over time and measuring the intermediate supplied by A. If A decreases before the intermediate and B decline, while C increases, the sequence is consistent with the proposed indirect mechanisms. A phage-resistant variant of A, if otherwise comparable, would provide a useful additional comparison. The experiment would still need controls for any material introduced with the phage preparation and for differences in initial community composition.

Animal gut systems vary in the prominence of non-bacterial interactions. In the rumen, microbial eukaryotes are often conspicuous participants in plant-material processing and consumption of other microbes. In the human distal gut, bacterial surveys dominate much of the literature, but this reflects both the biological abundance of bacteria and the historical emphasis of measurement methods. A bacterial survey is not a complete census of all members of the ecosystem. When interpreting a graph or classification table, the denominator must specify whether it refers to bacteria, cellular microorganisms or a broader collection of detected genetic material.

The term trophic interaction describes transfer of energy or material through feeding. Microbial predation, cross-feeding and phage infection each involve such transfers, but their effects on the populations are not identical. A phage requires a compatible host for reproduction; a protist can sometimes ingest multiple bacterial prey types; a cross-feeding bacterium can use a metabolic product without killing its producer. These distinctions help explain why ecosystem structure can change through mechanisms not captured by a simple list of bacterial species.

Testing priority effects and developmental succession

A priority effect is an experimentally testable property of a community, not merely a synonym for succession. Succession refers to change through time; a priority effect requires evidence that the order or timing of arrival changes the subsequent outcome. Suppose populations A and B eventually occupy the same habitat in all animals, but A is commonly detected first. The observation establishes a pattern of arrival, yet it does not demonstrate that arriving first gives A an advantage. A may simply be suited to conditions that occur early in development.

To test the effect of order, start with comparable habitats and expose one group to A before B, another to B before A and a third to both populations together. If final abundance differs according to the order of introduction despite equivalent final conditions, the results support a priority effect. The design should match the number of organisms introduced and the intervals between observations. It should also distinguish elapsed time since inoculation from the time spent in the habitat by each population. Otherwise, the apparent priority effect might reflect the longer opportunity for growth of the first arrival.

Priority effects can arise through niche pre-emption or niche modification. In pre-emption, the early colonist occupies sites or consumes a resource that later organisms also require. In modification, it changes the environment in ways that favour or inhibit later arrivals. Pre-emption predicts that increasing the supply of the contested resource or providing additional sites may reduce the advantage of the first arrival. Modification predicts that a habitat conditioned by the early colonist can influence a later organism even when the early colonist itself is no longer present.

Consider a hypothetical experiment using identical intestinal model chambers. When A enters first, the final abundance ratio A:B is 80:20. When B enters first, the ratio is 25:75. When they enter simultaneously, the ratio is 50:50. These data suggest that arrival order changes the community outcome. Now consider a second experiment in which the chambers are supplied with abundant additional attachment sites. If the difference between sequential introduction groups becomes smaller, site occupation is implicated. If the difference remains large, chemical modification or another mechanism may be involved. The second experiment is important because it addresses why order matters rather than merely documenting that it matters.

The concept has special relevance to early development. A milk-fed infant receives a different set of substrates from an adult, and the available source pool of microorganisms changes as contacts and diet diversify. An early organism that consumes a nutrient or modifies its environment can influence the establishment of later colonists. Nevertheless, a sequence seen in a group of infants may partly reflect a shared developmental environment rather than direct modification by earlier microbes. Evidence of priority effects in a controlled model helps establish plausibility, but observations from infants are needed to determine the extent of their contribution to natural development.

Milk-associated oligosaccharides illustrate the link between host resource supply and community assembly. These molecules are structurally diverse carbohydrates, many of which are poorly digested by the infant's own enzymes. Organisms with suitable utilization pathways can gain a nutritional advantage in a milk-associated environment. Different strains vary in the range of oligosaccharides they can use. A change in the type of milk or transition to complementary foods changes available ecological niches; it does not simply introduce a uniform increase in food quantity.

An important distinction is that dietary transition is accompanied by host maturation. For example, if the abundance of a bacterial group changes near the time of weaning, both dietary substrates and host developmental factors may have changed. A longitudinal study can locate the change in time, but separating its drivers requires measurement of those factors or experimental manipulation. In a controlled animal model, the age of animals and the composition of diet can be varied separately within ethical and physiological constraints. The comparison is most informative when microbial exposures and other conditions are similar across groups.

A simple succession dataset can help develop this reasoning. Suppose an early sample contains 60 percent facultative anaerobes, 30 percent obligate anaerobes and 10 percent other detected groups. At a later time the corresponding values are 20, 70 and 10 percent. The change is consistent with increased availability of anaerobic niches, but total cell density has not been supplied. If the early sample contains one million cells and the later sample ten million, facultative organisms have actually increased from 600,000 to two million cells despite their reduced relative share. Thus a declining percentage in a developmental profile does not necessarily mean elimination of the early population.

Succession can also involve stochastic events. If very few cells of a potential colonist arrive, chance survival or failure to reproduce may affect establishment. Two hosts exposed to broadly similar microbial sources can consequently acquire different founding strains. As those strains occupy niches and modify conditions, the initial differences may be maintained or amplified. A deterministic filter and stochastic arrival are not mutually exclusive explanations. The former determines which traits are compatible with the habitat; the latter influences which compatible organisms actually reach and establish in a particular host.

The most useful prediction in a developmental question follows from separating these processes. If exposure is the primary constraint, providing a missing colonist should improve establishment when the habitat is suitable. If habitat filtering is the primary constraint, repeated exposure alone may fail until the relevant conditions change. If priority effects dominate, introducing the same organism before rather than after a competitor may alter success. These outcomes are distinguishable in principle and offer different explanations for the same observational absence.

Disturbance, recovery and alternative community trajectories

A disturbance is an event or change in conditions that alters the community from its previous trajectory. Some disturbances are brief, whereas others persist. A transient dietary interruption may temporarily change substrate availability. A sustained change in diet can establish a different resource regime. These two cases should not be assessed using exactly the same expectation of recovery. Return toward the original community is most informative after the external conditions have returned near their original state.

Resistance can be evaluated by measuring the magnitude of change during the disturbance. Resilience can be evaluated by following the subsequent recovery. These properties require a baseline and a specified measure of state. For example, a study might define similarity to the pre-disturbance community on a scale from zero to one, where one means identical by that particular metric. A smaller decline during exposure indicates greater compositional resistance under the chosen measure. A subsequent increase toward one indicates compositional recovery. Neither measure automatically tells us whether a relevant physiological function was preserved.

In an illustrative study, community X has similarity values of 1.0 before exposure, 0.30 immediately afterward and 0.90 one month later. Community Y has values of 1.0, 0.75 and 0.78 at the same times. Y has greater immediate resistance because its similarity decreases by only 0.25 rather than 0.70. X shows greater observed recovery over the follow-up because its similarity increases by 0.60 rather than 0.03. A simple normalized recovery fraction can be calculated as the increase in similarity divided by the initial decline. X recovers 0.60/0.70, approximately 86 percent of its lost similarity, while Y recovers 0.03/0.25, or 12 percent. This illustrative fraction is not a universal standardized resilience index; it is a clearly defined way of comparing recovery in the given problem.

Recovery is not equivalent to the disappearance of a perturbation. If all susceptible organisms in one niche were removed, a suitable replacement must arrive or grow from a remaining reservoir. Restoring the original nutrient supply does not itself supply the missing population. Similarly, if surviving organisms fill the newly available niche, a previously common strain may face greater competition when reintroduced. This explains how the history of a disturbance can influence the state reached after conditions are restored.

Protected reservoirs can assist recovery. Organisms attached within particular microhabitats may experience conditions different from those in the freely flowing lumen. A surviving subpopulation can expand after a disturbance has ended. Alternatively, populations from other host-associated sites or the external environment can contribute to recolonisation. Demonstrating a reservoir requires tracing the relevant lineage through the disturbance and into the recovering community. A taxon detected before and after the event may represent survival of the same strain or acquisition of a similar but distinct strain.

Functional recovery depends on whether the ecological activities of interest are restored. Suppose the original polymer-degrading guild included strains A and B. After a disturbance, A disappears but B expands sufficiently to restore the same total substrate turnover. Taxonomic composition has changed, but that particular function has recovered. If A also produced a metabolite not produced by B, another function may remain altered. A conclusion about functional resilience must therefore state which function has been measured.

A related concept is an alternative stable state. In its strict ecological sense, the idea requires more than observing an altered community that persists for a short time. Under comparable external conditions, feedbacks or interactions may maintain different configurations depending on the previous history of the system. To demonstrate this, an investigator would need evidence that the conditions are genuinely comparable, that the different states persist over a relevant period and that the difference is not merely slow recovery or continuing exposure to an unmeasured factor. Persistent differences after a perturbation are compatible with this idea, but not sufficient proof of it.

A repeated disturbance may produce a different response from the first disturbance even when the external treatment is identical. The initial event can change the composition, genetic traits and interactions of the community. The second event therefore acts on a different starting system. In the repeated-antibiotic study cited earlier, the individuality of responses makes this a useful example of why recovery should be followed with repeated sampling rather than inferred from an average of unrelated participants.

Population trajectories can be summarized with a graph, but the time scale must be interpreted carefully. A sample collected one week after a disturbance may show little recovery simply because the relevant colonists grow slowly or have not yet arrived. A later observation could reveal substantial recovery. Conversely, a rapid initial increase in similarity might plateau far below baseline. A well-designed follow-up includes measurements during exposure, soon after removal and at later intervals appropriate to the biological question. The exam trap is to classify a system as permanently altered from a single short-term observation.

Finally, recovery need not imply that an original state was ideal. The word resilience describes the maintenance or restoration of a defined state or function. It is not a moral judgement or a synonym for health. A harmful configuration can be resistant to change, and a beneficial configuration can be fragile. The health implications of community disturbances require specific evidence about host outcomes, which is considered in Node 7.K.06.

Evaluating evolutionary claims about holobionts

Ecological interdependence and evolutionary integration are related but distinct concepts. Interdependence means that the current activities of one participant affect another. Evolutionary integration requires a history of heritable associations and selection on their consequences. A nutritional benefit provided by a microorganism can lead to selection on host traits promoting access to the microorganism. It can also lead to selection on microbial traits that improve access to host resources. These two processes can operate without identical evolutionary interests or simultaneous inheritance of every associated microbial lineage.

To analyse an evolutionary hypothesis, identify the variable trait, its transmission and its fitness effect. Suppose animals differ in their ability to retain a particular symbiont. If retention is determined by a heritable host trait and symbiont retention increases host reproduction, the host trait can increase in frequency. The microorganism may be acquired anew from the environment by each generation. In that case, the heritable feature under selection may primarily be the host's acquisition mechanism rather than the direct inheritance of the particular microbial cells present in its parent.

Now suppose instead that a microbial lineage is directly transmitted to the offspring of its host. If transmission is highly faithful and its effect on host reproduction is positive, the reproductive successes of the two partners can be more tightly coupled. Host lineages supporting successful transmission and symbiont lineages supporting productive hosts may both be favoured. Such coupling creates an opportunity for selection on properties of the association. The strength of the coupling depends on transmission failures, opportunities for the microorganism to reproduce outside the host and differences among competing strains.

A simple numerical teaching example illustrates conflict between levels of selection. Consider two hosts, each initially carrying 100 bacterial cells. In the first host, a microbial variant increases to 200 cells but reduces host reproduction to one surviving offspring. In the second host, a different variant remains at 100 cells but allows four surviving offspring. If each offspring acquires the corresponding variant faithfully and starts with 100 cells, the first association produces one next-generation host carrying 100 cells and the second produces four carrying 400 cells. The within-host growth advantage of the first variant does not guarantee greater transmission across host generations. The calculation is deliberately simplified: if the first variant also spreads horizontally among many unrelated hosts, the outcome can change. It is the transmission route, not merely the within-host abundance, that determines the relationship between bacterial and host success.

The same reasoning shows why competition between microbes can matter even when the community as a whole performs a valuable function. A strain that does not contribute to an energetically costly shared product may benefit from products released by other strains while saving the cost of its own contribution. Its growth advantage within the community can conflict with the maintenance of the shared function. Spatial association, preferential access to resources, host filtering or other interactions may counter such a tendency. These are mechanisms to investigate, not guarantees that cooperative traits will always be maintained.

A comparison between host phylogenies and microbial community similarity is sometimes described using the term phylosymbiosis. Broadly, more closely related host lineages may exhibit more similar communities under a specified analysis. This observation does not establish that every microbial lineage diverged at the same time as its host. Related hosts can share diets, anatomy and immune characteristics that favour similar ecological traits. To demonstrate co-divergence between specific lineages, their evolutionary histories and transmission patterns need to be assessed more directly. Community similarity is a pattern; co-divergence is one possible explanation for some patterns.

In the aphid–Buchnera association, the dietary problem and transmission mechanism make nutritional dependence a particularly informative example. Plant phloem sap is nutritionally imbalanced relative to the animal's requirements for certain essential amino acids. The symbiont contributes biosynthetic capacity that complements host metabolism. Evidence that loss of symbionts reduces host performance, together with nutritional evidence, supports an important functional relationship. Maternal transmission provides a strong link between host reproduction and the transmission of the bacterial partner. The example has greater relevance to evolutionary integration than a simple finding that two unrelated intestinal bacteria coexist in a stool sample.

A different evolutionary arrangement occurs in the squid association described earlier. The host's specialized organ and its interaction with the bacterial partner enable a highly specific association even though acquisition occurs from the environment. This demonstrates that vertical transmission is not the only route by which a reliable association can be assembled. Nevertheless, reliable environmental reacquisition must be distinguished from direct maternal inheritance. One can ask whether the host selects a particular species or strain and how consistently that relationship is established under natural conditions.

A broad holobiont framework is useful for describing an association without prejudging which evolutionary processes dominate. The claim that a particular association acts as a unit of selection is more specific. Evidence should establish that relevant differences in the association affect reproductive success and persist or recur across generations. It should also account for competing processes at the level of host and microbial lineages. Criticism of a universal hologenome model is not criticism of symbiosis, coevolution or microbial effects on physiology. It is a demand that stronger evolutionary claims be matched by appropriate evidence.

Comparing animal microbial habitats through experimental reasoning

Animal species differ not only in the microorganisms detected in them but also in the problems that their microbial associations help solve. A ruminant consumes plant material containing structural polysaccharides that require microbial processing for efficient utilization. An aphid consumes a specialized liquid diet whose nutritional composition requires complementary biosynthetic capabilities. A squid uses a specialized bacterial association in a light organ for a function unrelated to digestion. These cases should not be grouped together simply as examples of microorganisms being beneficial. Each has a different resource environment, anatomical location and transmission strategy.

The large foregut fermentation chamber of a ruminant allows microorganisms time to process plant material before digesta enters later digestive compartments. Feed particles provide surfaces on which microbial populations can assemble. A population attached to particles experiences resource concentrations and retention conditions that may differ from those of free organisms in the liquid phase. Mixing, replenishment of feed and removal of fermentation products help shape the habitat. These conditions explain why the rumen is an ecological community rather than a simple collection of independently acting digestive enzymes.

The global rumen census reported by Henderson and colleagues in 2015 investigated microbial communities from animals across multiple geographic settings. The study found community differences associated with host and diet as well as widespread shared members. A core component and variable composition can therefore coexist. The core is defined relative to the sampled hosts, detection methods and prevalence criterion; it does not mean that every individual carries every detected strain or that the core is identical to the human gut microbiome.

A comparison of foregut and hindgut fermentation emphasizes the effect of anatomical sequence. In a foregut fermentation system, microbial processing occurs before passage through much of the host's digestive tract. In a hindgut fermentation system, material reaching the distal compartment has already passed through the major upstream digestive and absorptive regions. This distinction affects substrate delivery, opportunities for microbial biomass digestion and absorption of microbial products. Animals can use different ecological architectures to obtain nutritional benefit from microbial metabolism. A taxon common in one architecture may be less successful in the other because environmental conditions differ.

A herbivorous animal's microbial functions can be conserved without conserving precisely the same species. For example, a polymer-degrading guild may be present across several hosts but contain different representatives adapted to differences in temperature, retention and food. Conversely, the same broad bacterial group may participate in different functional interactions in two animals because other community members differ. A comparison of communities should therefore assess both taxonomic and functional properties when making predictions about host physiology.

The ecological properties of a microbial habitat can also be inferred from perturbations. Suppose two animal species consume the same experimental diet but have different rates of substrate breakdown in their fermentation compartments. Several hypotheses are possible: the communities may contain different numbers of degraders, individual degraders may have different activity, or the host may provide different residence times or chemical conditions. Comparing community samples under matched model conditions can test whether differences persist without the host environment. If they disappear, the host environment is implicated; if they persist, community properties may contribute. Neither result alone identifies the responsible strain or pathway.

Host switching or transplantation provides another conceptual test. If a community from animal A is introduced into an otherwise comparable recipient environment from species B, organisms suited to the new habitat may persist while others are lost. Failure to establish can reflect habitat incompatibility, insufficient viable inoculum or competition with resident organisms. Such results cannot be interpreted as proof that the original community was intrinsically inferior. The specific principles of germ-free and defined-colonisation models are the subject of Node 7.K.04; here the relevant inference is the ecological filtering of a microbial assemblage.

Comparison with free-living habitats further clarifies the meaning of ecological selection. A soil aggregate can contain oxygenated outer regions and poorly oxygenated inner regions because microorganisms consume oxygen faster than it diffuses inward. Similar physical principles operate in host-associated systems. However, the gut receives nutrients through regulated host feeding, digestion and secretion, and its contents are removed by motility. The host can change the environment in response to microbial products. Host-associated ecosystems are thus subject to strong physiological feedback in addition to general ecological processes.

A productive comparative question asks which features are analogous and which are different. A specialized organ supplying a resource to one characteristic symbiont is analogous to a gut niche in that both select organisms from possible colonists. It differs in the diversity of participants and the complexity of resource exchange. The aphid's inherited symbiont is analogous to a persistent resident in that both can provide functions important to the host. It differs in transmission fidelity and cellular location. Such comparisons are more informative than memorizing a disconnected list of animal–microbe pairs.

The complete conceptual framework for the next chapter follows from these distinctions. Host anatomy and physiology create a habitat; microbial populations with suitable traits colonise that habitat; interactions among them determine the rate and direction of material transformations; and the resulting products can act on host tissues. Ecological composition is one link in the chain, not the final physiological explanation. Investigating that chain requires both community evidence and measurements of the chemical and biological processes it supports.
