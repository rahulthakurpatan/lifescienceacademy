1. The biological problem of coordination

A multicellular animal faces a problem that a solitary cell can often solve locally. A change in the surroundings may be detected at one surface of the body, yet the appropriate response must be produced by muscles or glands at another location. The response may also need to be adjusted according to hunger, previous experience, internal temperature or the current position of the limbs. Chemical communication through tissue fluid and blood is valuable for many such tasks, but its distribution and timing are not always suited to rapid, spatially precise coordination. Nervous systems provide a complementary solution: specialised cells establish communication pathways through which information can be collected, transformed and delivered to selected targets.

Consider an animal that touches a damaging surface. A response requires several logically distinct events. A change in the environment must first alter a sensory structure. This alteration must be converted into a change in cellular electrical activity. Information from the stimulated region must then reach cells that can influence muscles. Some muscles may be activated while others must relax. Finally, sensory feedback about the resulting movement may modify the response. It is not enough simply to transmit a signal rapidly; the destination, timing and interpretation of that signal determine whether it is useful.

A neuron is a cell specialised for receiving, processing and transmitting information. Its plasma membrane contains proteins that allow electrical and chemical conditions to influence its activity. Many neurons possess elongated processes that bring distant regions into functional contact. However, an individual neuron does not constitute a nervous system. The organisation of neurons into circuits, together with the activities of non-neuronal cells, makes it possible to perform coordinated functions. The same broad cellular principles are used in circuits that produce a simple withdrawal and circuits that coordinate breathing, posture or more elaborate behaviour.

A useful distinction is between a signal and a substance transported to maintain the signalling apparatus. The electrical disturbance travelling along an axon can reach a distant target long before proteins synthesised in the cell body are delivered to that same target. The electrical event depends on local membrane properties along its route; it does not require a protein molecule to move the entire length of the axon each time a message is sent. Nevertheless, the proteins responsible for that electrical activity must be renewed, and terminals must receive organelles and membrane components. Understanding the nervous system therefore requires simultaneous attention to information flow and material flow.

The nervous and endocrine systems should not be viewed as isolated competitors. A neuron may influence endocrine secretion, and a circulating hormone may alter neuronal activity. Their characteristic differences concern delivery and time course rather than absolute chemical versus electrical identity. Most neurons use chemical communication at some of their contacts. Conversely, some endocrine cells are electrically excitable. Neural signals can have prolonged consequences, and some hormonal effects develop quickly. The advantage of a neural pathway lies in its anatomical connectivity and ability to transform patterns of activity, not in an absolute prohibition against chemical messages.

The nervous system must also preserve the environment in which its signals occur. An extracellular change in potassium concentration can alter membrane voltage; accumulation of transmitter can distort communication between cells; loss of an insulating sheath can interrupt communication between intact neurons. These examples explain why the study of neuroglia is inseparable from the study of neurons. A neural circuit is not merely a wiring diagram. It is living tissue whose electrical properties depend on transport, metabolism and cellular cooperation.

2. Comparative organisation: distributed nets and centralised circuits

A nervous system can be organised without a brain in the vertebrate sense. In a cnidarian such as Hydra, neurons form interconnected networks distributed through the body. Local stimulation can recruit activity in the surrounding network and produce movements of the body column or tentacles. This arrangement suits an animal that encounters stimuli from several directions. A diffuse organisation does not imply that all neurons are identical, that every connection is random or that every signal travels equally in every direction. Modern observations reveal differentiated neural populations and coordinated patterns even in animals without a single central brain.

The architectural advantage of a nerve net is that communication can be distributed near the structures it controls. If one part of the network remains connected to an effector, it may be capable of local activity without requiring a command from a distant central organ. A distributed system nevertheless faces constraints when a behaviour requires the simultaneous integration of sensory information from widely separated regions. The relevant question is not whether a nerve net is inferior to a brain, but which organisation is appropriate for the animal's body plan and activities.

Bilateral symmetry often accompanies directed locomotion. An animal moving head first repeatedly encounters objects and food near its anterior end. Concentrating sensory structures and neural integration near that end can reduce the distance between major sensory inputs and processing circuits. This concentration is termed cephalisation. Centralisation is a related but different concept: neural processing elements are gathered into defined structures rather than distributed uniformly. A centralised circuit may be located away from the head, and a head region may contain several distinct centres. These concepts describe spatial organisation, not a single evolutionary scale.

Planarians illustrate a useful intermediate architectural comparison. An anterior pair of cerebral ganglia is connected with longitudinal nerve cords, and transverse connections give the system a ladder-like appearance in simplified diagrams. The ganglia bring many cell bodies and their processes into proximity, permitting information from sensory structures to be integrated. The longitudinal cords allow communication with more posterior regions. A transverse connection provides a route between the left and right sides. Such an arrangement can coordinate directional movement while retaining distributed neural elements.

In annelids and arthropods, a ventral nerve cord commonly links ganglia associated with body segments. Segmented circuits can generate or modify local movements, while communication among segments supports whole-body coordination. For example, coordinating locomotion requires the activity of one segment to be appropriately phased relative to neighbouring segments. A ganglion that can generate local activity need not function independently during normal movement. Descending signals, sensory feedback and intersegmental communication can adjust its output. Thus distributed and centralised processing coexist within a single nervous system.

Molluscs illustrate the diversity possible within a phylum. Some have distinct ganglia connected by nerves, whereas cephalopods possess a highly developed central nervous system together with substantial peripheral neural processing. An octopus arm contains extensive circuitry, so a command from the central brain need not specify the state of every muscle independently. Local sensory and motor networks can handle parts of the control problem. This is a useful example of why increasing centralisation does not necessarily eliminate peripheral autonomy.

Echinoderms, including sea stars, demonstrate another arrangement. A nerve ring is associated with radial nerves that extend into the arms. This pattern should not be confused with the ladder-like planarian arrangement, despite the fact that both permit communication between regions of the body. The adult radial organisation is compatible with activity in several directions. Vertebrates, in contrast, possess a dorsal central nervous system consisting of the brain and spinal cord, connected to peripheral structures through nerves and ganglia.

These examples describe present-day anatomical diversity. They must not be arranged into a supposed chain in which modern cnidarians turn into modern flatworms, then into modern annelids and finally into vertebrates. Each lineage has its own evolutionary history. Similar problems can favour comparable circuit properties in distantly related organisms, and different arrangements can achieve similar outcomes. Centralisation can reduce certain connection lengths while creating other requirements for communication between specialised regions. Neural complexity is therefore not adequately measured by the presence of a single brain or by the number of ganglia.

A comparative experiment can separate local processing from long-distance coordination. Suppose a preparation retains a sensory input, a local ganglion and a muscle, while a connection to a distant integrating centre is reversibly interrupted. If the local muscle still responds to stimulation, the local elements are sufficient for that response under the test conditions. If coordinated responses involving several segments disappear, the distant connections may be necessary for those particular patterns. Failure to respond is not automatically evidence that a central command is indispensable: injury, altered metabolism or loss of tonic inputs could also suppress activity. Appropriate controls must establish that the local circuit remains viable.

3. The vertebrate central and peripheral nervous systems

In vertebrates, the brain and spinal cord together form the central nervous system, abbreviated CNS. Neural structures beyond these organs comprise the peripheral nervous system, or PNS, with conventional special cases such as the optic nerve, which is developmentally and histologically a CNS tract. The anatomical distinction identifies locations and tissue organisation, not whether a structure carries sensory or motor information. Both central and peripheral regions contain pathways involved in both kinds of processing.

The peripheral nervous system includes nerves that connect sensory receptors, muscles, glands and visceral organs to central circuits, as well as ganglia that contain neuronal cell bodies. A nerve is a bundle of axons organised with supporting cells and connective tissue. It is not an elongated neuron. A peripheral ganglion contains cell bodies and associated cellular structures, whereas a CNS nucleus is generally a collection of neuronal cell bodies defined by anatomical or functional relationships. A CNS tract is a bundle of axons within the central nervous system. The term nucleus can also mean the DNA-containing organelle of a cell, so context matters.

Grey matter and white matter describe differences in tissue composition. Grey matter contains abundant neuronal cell bodies, dendrites, synapses, glia and many axonal processes. White matter contains a high proportion of myelinated axons, along with glia, blood vessels and some other cellular elements. The two names do not represent pure cell populations. In the spinal cord, much grey matter lies centrally and white matter surrounds it. In the cerebral hemispheres, grey matter forms an outer cortex and deep nuclei, while substantial white matter lies beneath the cortex. Detailed regional anatomy belongs to later chapters.

An afferent pathway conveys information towards a specified reference centre, usually the CNS when discussing peripheral nerves. An efferent pathway carries information away from that centre. These terms are relational. A fibre can be afferent to one brain region and efferent from another. In a mixed peripheral nerve, sensory afferent and motor efferent axons may occupy the same connective tissue bundle. They are nevertheless separate cellular processes with distinct connections. Cutting the entire mixed nerve therefore differs from selectively interrupting its sensory component.

A somatic motor pathway controls skeletal muscle through motor neurons. Visceral motor pathways regulate smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands, generally through autonomic circuits. The sensory side also includes information from both the external environment and internal organs. Somatic does not simply mean conscious, and autonomic does not mean that the CNS is uninvolved. A spinal reflex can activate skeletal muscle without a conscious decision, while central circuits continuously regulate autonomic activity. The enteric nervous system contains local circuits of the gastrointestinal tract that can operate with considerable autonomy while remaining subject to extrinsic regulation.

The distinction between sensory receptor, sensory neuron and sensory organ is equally important. In some cases, the sensory neuron itself possesses a specialised receptive ending. In other systems, a separate receptor cell detects a stimulus and transfers information to a sensory neuron. The organ can contain many supporting cells and several classes of receptor. A receptor protein, a receptor cell and a receptor organ are different levels of organisation. Later chapters will examine the conversion of particular stimuli into electrical responses; here the important principle is that afferent information begins at a specialised interface with the environment or internal tissues.

4. Neuronal polarity and the organisation of a single cell

Most textbook illustrations depict a multipolar neuron with a cell body, several dendrites and one long axon. This is a useful starting model because it makes the separation of receiving, integrating and transmitting regions visually clear. Yet the drawing should not be interpreted as a universal blueprint. Neurons differ enormously in size, shape and molecular organisation, and the common model is most valuable when the reasons for each region are understood.

The cell body, or soma, contains the nucleus and much of the biosynthetic machinery. Transcription supplies RNA, and ribosomes translate proteins needed for cellular maintenance and signalling. Rough endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and vesicular sorting pathways are especially important because proteins produced in the soma may ultimately be used far from it. Nissl staining reveals RNA-rich structures associated with rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes in the soma and proximal dendrites. The axon hillock and mature axon generally lack the prominent Nissl bodies characteristic of the soma. That observation must not be extended into the absolute assertion that no protein synthesis ever occurs in any axonal region.

Dendrites extend the area over which a neuron can receive information. A branching tree allows contacts to be distributed at different distances and on different branches. Dendritic spines, where present, provide specialised small protrusions that host many excitatory synapses in certain neurons. Not every dendrite has spines, and not every excitatory synapse is located on one. The geometry of a dendrite influences the transfer of local electrical changes towards other parts of the cell. A distant input may have a different influence from an otherwise identical input close to the soma, depending on membrane properties and active channels.

The axon hillock is the region in which the soma tapers into the axon in a conventional multipolar neuron. The axon initial segment is a molecularly specialised proximal axonal domain, often positioned just beyond the hillock. The two terms are related but not interchangeable. In many neurons the initial segment has a high density of voltage-dependent channels and is an important site of action-potential initiation. Its specialised cytoskeleton also contributes to maintaining differences between axonal and somatodendritic membranes. Some sensory neurons initiate impulses near peripheral endings rather than near a central soma, demonstrating why the functional rule must not be reduced to one universal geometric location.

An axon carries signals to other cells, sometimes over distances very large compared with the diameter of its soma. It may form collaterals along its course, allowing one neuron to influence several targets. A terminal branch can contain multiple boutons. The number of axonal branches is therefore not the number of neurons, and the number of synapses is not necessarily the number of distinct target cells. One neuron may contact the same partner at several points, while a single target cell may receive thousands of contacts from different sources.

The presynaptic terminal contains specialised machinery for communication with another cell. In a typical chemical synapse, a change in terminal membrane voltage can induce calcium entry and trigger the release of transmitter from vesicles. Receptors on the receiving cell then respond. Electrical synapses instead provide a more direct route for ionic current through gap junctions. Their existence does not contradict the neuron doctrine: separate cells can communicate through specialised intercellular channels without forming an unrestricted continuous cytoplasm.

Polarisation is not equivalent to one-way movement of all substances within a neuron. Conventional information flow often proceeds from receptive regions towards axonal outputs, but membrane voltage can spread in other directions, and intracellular cargo travels both towards and away from the soma. Dendrites can release signals, and some neurons possess unusual arrangements of receptive and output sites. Functional polarity is therefore an experimentally testable pattern of specialised domains rather than an inflexible prohibition on reverse communication.

The historical evidence for cellular individuality came in part from a staining method developed by Camillo Golgi. When only a small proportion of neurons in a densely packed tissue are impregnated, the entire shape of individual cells can become visible against relatively unstained neighbours. Santiago Ramón y Cajal used this approach to trace processes and argue for distinct neural cells linked by contact. Golgi favoured a different interpretation involving a reticular organisation. Their shared Nobel Prize in 1906 recognised contributions to nervous-system structure despite the disagreement. Light microscopy alone could not resolve every narrow intercellular boundary; later ultrastructural evidence helped establish the separation of cells at chemical synapses. The history illustrates the difference between a visible staining pattern and a conclusion about membrane continuity.

5. Classifying neurons by shape and function

Two independent classification schemes are especially useful. Functional classification asks what role a neuron performs in a circuit. Morphological classification asks how its processes emerge from the soma. The schemes often correlate, but one does not uniquely determine the other. A student who memorises that a motor neuron is multipolar should also recognise that many multipolar cells are interneurons.

Sensory neurons convey information from receptors towards circuits that interpret or respond to it. In many mammalian dorsal root ganglia, the sensory neuron is pseudounipolar. A single process leaves the cell body and divides into a peripheral and a central branch. Both branches are axonal in important structural and physiological respects, even though the peripheral branch conveys sensory information towards the CNS. An impulse can travel from the peripheral branch through the junction into the central branch without first passing through the soma as a serial processing station. The soma remains essential for metabolism and maintenance of both branches.

A bipolar neuron has two principal processes emerging from opposite or distinct regions of the soma. Bipolar organisation is encountered in specialised sensory pathways such as olfactory receptor neurons and certain retinal neurons. The retinal bipolar cell is not itself the photoreceptor: it relays information from photoreceptors towards other retinal circuits. Thus a shared morphological category can contain cells with distinct functional positions. True unipolar arrangements are common in various invertebrate nervous systems. Pseudounipolar and unipolar should not be treated as identical descriptions of developmental origin.

Multipolar neurons have multiple dendritic processes and usually one axon. Spinal motor neurons are familiar examples. The dendrites receive inputs from numerous sources, and an axon carries output towards muscle. Pyramidal neurons of cerebral cortex possess characteristic apical and basal dendritic arrangements, while cerebellar Purkinje cells possess expansive flattened dendritic arbors. These shapes are related to the spatial organisation of their inputs. A diagram showing a large arbor indicates potential receptive area, not a direct measurement of how many functional synapses are present.

Interneurons are neurons that connect components of neural circuits. Many operate locally, but the terminology is not universally restricted to a fixed physical distance. Projection neurons send axons to distant targets within a neural system. Excitatory and inhibitory describe effects on the activity of target cells, which depend on the receptors and physiological conditions as well as the transmitter. Morphology alone cannot establish the sign of a synaptic effect. A long axon, for example, can carry excitatory or inhibitory output depending on the cell type.

A neuron with very little obvious distinction between axon and dendrites is sometimes described as anaxonic. This term does not establish that the cell lacks all output mechanisms. Some retinal interneurons have elaborate local processes that participate in both input and output. A physiological description of a cell should specify which membrane regions receive signals, where the cell generates its output and which target structures it influences.

A functional circuit can be analysed by tracing connectivity rather than by identifying every morphological feature. In a simple sensory-to-motor response, the receptor initiates afferent activity, central connections influence motor neurons, and muscle fibres respond to the efferent signal. Interneurons can introduce inhibition, select competing responses or distribute sensory information to other pathways. If the sensory neuron is pseudounipolar and the motor neuron multipolar, the difference helps explain their respective geometries but does not by itself establish the mechanism of the reflex. That requires knowledge of synapses and connectivity.

6. Neuroglia as active partners in neural function

Neurons cannot maintain effective communication in isolation. Neural activity changes the ionic and chemical composition of the extracellular space, consumes metabolic substrates and imposes continuous demands on membranes and organelles. Glial cells help regulate these conditions. The traditional description of glia as merely a supporting glue obscures several essential physiological functions, including myelination, transmitter clearance, immune surveillance and maintenance of specialised tissue environments.

Astrocytes possess processes that interact with synapses, neuronal surfaces and blood vessels. One major function is regulation of extracellular potassium. During neuronal activity, potassium can accumulate outside neurons. If the increase is not controlled, it can modify membrane voltage and alter the excitability of nearby cells. Astrocytes take up potassium and can redistribute ions through coupled cellular networks. The exact contribution of uptake, redistribution and membrane transport depends on local activity and tissue properties. It is therefore inappropriate to identify a single universal route for all potassium regulation.

Astrocytes also express transporters that remove extracellular glutamate. Glutamate is a major excitatory transmitter, but persistent extracellular accumulation can lead to excessive receptor activation and cellular injury. Transport into astrocytes helps terminate transmitter exposure and contributes to recycling. Within astrocytes, glutamate can be converted to glutamine, which is released and can be used by neurons as a precursor for transmitter synthesis. This cycling links neurotransmission with metabolism. It should not be assumed that all glutamate is removed by astrocytes or that a synapse cannot function unless an astrocytic process surrounds every part of it.

At cerebral blood vessels, astrocytic endfeet contribute to the neurovascular environment. The principal restrictive interface of the blood–brain barrier is the specialised endothelium, with tight junctions and regulated transport, supported by interactions with pericytes, basement membranes and astrocytes. Astrocyte processes are not themselves the continuous endothelial sealing layer. This distinction is a common source of incorrect examination answers. Astrocytes also help regulate metabolic exchanges and responses to injury, but their actions vary by region and state.

Oligodendrocytes are the myelinating cells of the CNS. A mature oligodendrocyte can extend several processes, each forming a myelin internode around an axonal segment. Schwann cells provide peripheral myelin; an individual myelinating Schwann cell forms one internode on one axon. The distinction refers to the number of internodes formed per cell, not the number of impulses carried by the axon. Nonmyelinating Schwann cells can enclose several small axons within cytoplasmic pockets without forming compact multilamellar sheaths around them.

Microglia are resident immune cells of the CNS, developmentally distinct from the major neural-derived macroglial lineages. Their processes survey local tissue, respond to changes in cellular integrity and remove debris. During development and some physiological conditions, microglia contribute to remodelling synaptic connections. Activation is not a single invariant state; responses depend on the tissue context and stimulus. A large number of activated microglia near a damaged region demonstrates an association with injury, not necessarily that the microglia initiated the damage.

Ependymal cells line the cerebral ventricles and central canal. Many possess cilia that contribute to movement and organisation of cerebrospinal fluid at ventricular surfaces. The specialised choroid plexus epithelium is the main secretory interface for cerebrospinal fluid. Ordinary ependymal lining cells must not all be assigned identical secretory roles. The epithelial arrangement, ciliary activity and fluid interface distinguish ependymal cells from oligodendrocytes and from the specialised cells surrounding peripheral ganglia.

Satellite glial cells surround neuronal somata in peripheral sensory and autonomic ganglia. Their location is different from the axon-associated arrangement of Schwann cells. Satellite cells help regulate the chemical environment around ganglion neurons and participate in responses to injury. A peripheral neuron may consequently depend on one population of glia near its soma and another along its axon. This anatomical segregation resembles the separation of local functions in CNS glia but should not be used to assign all CNS astrocyte properties automatically to satellite cells.

The relative abundance of neurons and glia varies greatly between regions. Azevedo and colleagues reported in 2009 approximately 86 billion neuronal nuclei and approximately 85 billion nonneuronal nuclei, on average, in the adult male human brains they examined. The estimates were obtained using a counting approach in which tissue was converted to a suspension of nuclei and neuronal identity assessed with a marker. The nonneuronal category includes cells other than glia, and regional ratios differ. The result therefore challenges the older universal claim that every human brain contains ten glial cells for each neuron, without providing a single exact glia-to-neuron ratio for every structure.

7. Myelin, internodes and nodes of Ranvier

An axon is surrounded by its own plasma membrane, often termed axolemma. In a myelinated axon, this membrane is additionally surrounded at intervals by layers of glial membrane. These layers are produced by wrapping and compaction of the processes of a myelinating cell. Myelin is lipid-rich, but it also contains important proteins. It is an organised cellular structure rather than a freely deposited coating of fat.

The multilamellar sheath changes the electrical behaviour of the axonal membrane. As an introductory model, consider the membrane as a barrier through which charge can leak and across which charge can be stored. Increasing the effective insulating thickness generally reduces capacitive load and increases resistance to transverse current leakage. More of the electrical disturbance can then influence distant portions of the axon before being dissipated. In a living axon these effects operate together with specialised channel distributions, axon diameter and the geometry of the sheath. Myelination is not simply a change in membrane resistance with all other properties fixed.

A myelinated segment is an internode. Between successive internodes lie short regions called nodes of Ranvier. These regions lack compact myelin and contain high concentrations of voltage-gated sodium channels in typical mature myelinated axons. The action potential is actively regenerated at successive nodes; electrical current spreads along and through the intervening axonal circuit. The term saltatory conduction describes this spatially discontinuous pattern of regeneration. It does not mean that ions physically jump across the extracellular gap from one node to another, or that the axon is disconnected between nodes.

Immediately adjacent to the node are paranodal domains where terminal loops of myelin contact the axon. Farther beneath the sheath are juxtaparanodal regions associated with particular potassium channels. The arrangement helps segregate different membrane proteins into functional domains. Damage to the sheath or the junctions can alter this arrangement even if an axon remains structurally continuous. An observed deficit in signal transmission may therefore result from loss of axons, alteration of channels, disorganisation of myelin domains or several changes together.

The number of nodes encountered along an axon depends on the length of the myelinated portion and the length of its internodes. Longer internodes reduce the number of places at which the impulse must be regenerated, but increasing their length without limit would not be beneficial: the disturbance must remain large enough at the next node to trigger regenerative activity. The useful spacing depends on diameter, insulation, channel organisation and other factors. Myelin thickness must likewise be interpreted relative to the underlying axon. One widely used geometric measure is the g-ratio, defined as axon diameter divided by total fibre diameter including myelin. For a circular cross section with axon diameter 6 micrometres and total diameter 9 micrometres, the g-ratio is 6 divided by 9, or approximately 0.67. The radial sheath thickness in this idealised example is (9 minus 6) divided by 2, or 1.5 micrometres. The g-ratio is dimensionless and does not by itself determine conduction velocity.

Unmyelinated fibres can transmit signals without compact myelin. In vertebrate peripheral nerves, many small fibres are associated with nonmyelinating Schwann cells. Large unmyelinated axons also provide an alternative strategy for improving conduction speed through reduced internal resistance. The squid giant axon is a classical example of the use of a very large diameter rather than vertebrate-style compact myelin. The comparison highlights a trade-off: increasing the diameter of each axon consumes space, while constructing a sheath requires specialised glia and metabolic investment. These are alternative anatomical contributions to a physiological problem.

When peripheral axons are injured, Schwann cells can adopt a repair-associated state, help remove damaged myelin and provide a supportive pathway for regenerating processes. Recovery is conditional on survival of the neuronal soma, the extent of damage and access to an appropriate target. CNS repair is commonly more restricted, but it is incorrect to state that every peripheral injury heals completely or that remyelination never occurs centrally. New oligodendrocytes can arise from precursor populations and contribute to repair under suitable conditions. This chapter uses repair only as evidence for glial functions; detailed clinical management falls outside its scope.

8. Axonal transport and the maintenance of distant terminals

A long axon creates a logistics problem. Most of the machinery that produces and sorts proteins is concentrated near the soma, but a terminal may be tens of centimetres away. Synaptic vesicle proteins, ion channels, mitochondria and other components must reach and be maintained at distal sites. Damaged material and information about conditions at the terminal must also reach locations where they can influence cellular maintenance. Simple diffusion is generally inadequate for the delivery of large cargoes over these distances.

Diffusion and directed transport have different scaling properties. In a simplified one-dimensional diffusion model, the mean squared displacement is twice the diffusion coefficient multiplied by time. If the characteristic displacement increases by a factor of ten, the characteristic diffusion time increases by a factor of one hundred for the same diffusion coefficient. Directed movement at constant mean velocity instead has a travel time proportional to distance. This distinction becomes decisive when a structure extends many orders of magnitude beyond the size of a molecular cargo. The diffusion equation describes statistical displacement and not a guaranteed delivery time; it is used here to explain the scaling problem rather than to calculate the actual movement of a particular organelle.

Axonal transport is the movement of cellular material along the axon. Anterograde means away from the soma and retrograde means towards it. These names are defined by the position of the soma, not by the direction of an impulse travelling in a sensory neuron. Microtubules provide polar tracks within the axon. In typical mature axons, most microtubules are oriented with their plus ends directed distally. Dendritic microtubule organisation can be more mixed, making it unsafe to transfer an axonal direction rule directly to every dendrite.

Many kinesin-family motors move towards microtubule plus ends and participate in anterograde transport. Cytoplasmic dynein, working with associated regulatory and adaptor proteins, moves towards minus ends and supports retrograde transport. These proteins use the chemical energy of ATP to produce directed movement. Not every kinesin-family member is a plus-end-directed cargo transporter, so the general axonal rule should be stated with its biological context. Motor proteins bind cargo either directly or through adaptors, allowing the cell to regulate which objects move and where movement is initiated or stopped.

Membrane-bound cargoes such as vesicle precursors and many organelles are frequently transported in the fast component. Cytoskeletal proteins and many soluble proteins participate in slower net transport. Slow transport need not involve a motor that always walks slowly. Intermittent movement, long pauses, assembly states and changes in direction can produce a small long-term average displacement despite much faster individual movement episodes. Distinguishing instantaneous velocity from net transport rate is essential when interpreting data obtained from tracking a fluorescent particle.

Consider a hypothetical axonal cargo that travels towards a terminal at a mean net speed of 200 millimetres per day. If the terminal is 600 millimetres from the soma, the idealised transit time is 600 divided by 200, or three days. At a net rate of 2 millimetres per day the same distance would require 300 days. These are illustrative calculations, not reported velocities for a particular neuron. The calculation assumes that the stated net speed is appropriate to the full route and that no additional delay occurs at the source or destination. It demonstrates why cargo identity and transport mode matter to cellular maintenance.

Retrograde transport carries material towards the cell body, including signalling endosomes formed when extracellular factors interact with receptors in distal regions. Signals associated with these endosomes can influence gene expression and neuronal survival. Retrograde transport also contributes to the turnover and redistribution of organelles and cellular material. The existence of anterograde and retrograde motor systems does not require cargo to move steadily in only one direction throughout its lifetime. Individual organelles, particularly mitochondria, may pause, reverse or remain anchored at sites of local demand.

A classical way to infer transport direction is to interrupt movement along an axon and examine where cargo accumulates. Cargo moving from soma to terminal tends to collect on the proximal side of a transport block. Cargo moving towards the soma tends to collect on the distal side, provided movement continues on the corresponding sides of the interruption. The interpretation depends on the survival of the preparation and on the identity of the measured cargo. Accumulation by itself does not prove the identity of the motor; a perturbation that damages microtubules, inhibits ATP production or disrupts cargo binding could produce similar changes.

The experiments of Vale and colleagues in 1985 connected directed organelle movements in squid axoplasm with a distinct microtubule-associated motor, named kinesin. Reconstituted assays separated the movement mechanism from the intact complexity of the neuron. With polar microtubule arrays and suitable cargo or bead assays, motion could be related to track orientation. These observations supported the idea that opposite transport directions use distinct molecular machinery. Such assays demonstrate motility under defined conditions; they cannot by themselves establish the full regulation of trafficking in a living mammalian axon. Later studies identified the participation of cytoplasmic dynein and multiple cargo-specific regulatory factors.

Fluorescent labelling allows modern experiments to follow individual cargoes in intact cultured neurons. In a position-versus-time representation, movement away from the soma produces a positive slope if position is measured in the distal direction; retrograde movement produces a negative slope. A stationary particle produces zero slope. A steeper absolute slope corresponds to greater speed only when the graph axes and units are unchanged. Observing fewer moving cargoes after a motor perturbation suggests impaired transport but requires controls for cargo labelling, cell health and microtubule organisation. A reduction in fluorescence could otherwise be mistaken for a reduction in actual movement.

9. How comparative anatomy becomes experimental evidence

A drawing of a nervous system can establish where cell bodies and processes are concentrated, but not necessarily how an animal uses those structures during behaviour. To progress from anatomy to physiology, it is necessary to ask whether the identified structures contribute to a measured function. In a segmented animal, for example, several ganglia may appear similar in a stained preparation. They might nevertheless perform different tasks according to the segment in which they occur. The presence of anatomical repetition is not itself proof of physiological equivalence. Researchers can compare sensory inputs, electrical activity and outputs across segments while controlling for the type and strength of stimulation.

An anatomically distributed circuit may still be highly organised in time. Suppose an animal has three successive body segments, each with a local sensory-motor circuit. A stimulus to the first segment could activate all three segments in a reproducible order. The order may result from signals moving along longitudinal connections, differences in the intrinsic properties of each local circuit, or both. Selectively interrupting the longitudinal connection between the first and second segment, while retaining the local sensory and motor elements of each segment, can test whether the connection is necessary for the propagation of that pattern. If the first segment still responds and the more distant segments do not, the result supports a role for the interrupted pathway.

It is equally important to compare the response to direct local stimulation after the interruption. If the second segment still generates its usual response when stimulated directly, loss of the transmitted response is less likely to reflect death of its neurons or muscles. If it cannot respond even to direct stimulation, the experiment has not cleanly isolated the role of the connection. The most persuasive physiological experiments combine the original stimulus, a control stimulus, a reversible or selective perturbation where possible, and independent measures of the viability of the tissue. This logic applies to nerve nets, ganglion-based networks and vertebrate neural circuits alike.

A similar principle helps distinguish the contribution of central and peripheral neurons in animals with extensive local processing. Imagine a preparation in which movement of one appendage is tested with and without communication with a central brain. Local responses to stimulation may persist even when coordinated responses involving several appendages are lost. The correct conclusion is not that the brain normally plays no role in appendage movement. Instead, the observations identify a set of conditions under which local circuitry can generate some responses and a separate set of conditions requiring communication between regions. Intact-animal behaviour integrates both levels.

Comparisons between animals should also control for scale. An animal with a larger body may require longer connections even if its nervous system has a similar pattern of regional organisation. A larger ganglion might contain more neurons, larger neurons, greater proportions of neuropil or some combination. Neuropil refers to regions containing dense interwoven neuronal processes and synaptic connections, often with relatively few cell bodies. A large neuropil may indicate an extensive local network of processes, but its volume is not a direct measurement of the amount or complexity of information processed. Cell counts, connectivity and physiological measurements provide complementary information.

The same caution applies to cephalisation. In a simplified diagram, a head containing dense neural tissue may be assumed to control every body region in detail. However, an anterior concentration can perform sensory analysis and selection of actions while distributed circuitry handles implementation. These functions are not incompatible. A central neural system can send relatively abstract commands to circuits elsewhere, reducing the number of distinct signals that must cross long distances. An analogy is a local controller receiving an instruction to start a pattern rather than receiving a separate instruction for every individual contraction. The analogy is useful only to the extent that it distinguishes levels of control; biological circuits remain continuously influenced by feedback and are not fixed software modules.

A centralised architecture also has vulnerabilities. Disruption of a shared pathway can affect many functions simultaneously because multiple signals depend on the same anatomical route. In a distributed architecture, some local functions may persist when another region is damaged. But distributed organisation also requires mechanisms for coordinating distant parts. The examination-relevant reasoning is to relate the effect of a perturbation to the specific organisation and connectivity of the animal described, rather than assume that centralised or distributed systems are universally better.

10. Cellular compartments, protein sorting and metabolic supply

Neuronal polarity develops and persists because different parts of the same cell acquire and maintain different molecular compositions. All neuronal plasma membranes consist of a lipid bilayer with associated proteins, but the population of proteins at the soma is not identical to that at the axon terminal. A channel, receptor or adhesion protein must arrive at an appropriate domain and remain there long enough to perform its function. A structural description of the neuron is incomplete unless it explains why the same membrane can support several physiologically distinct regions.

A membrane protein destined for a terminal can be translated at the rough endoplasmic reticulum, enter the secretory pathway and undergo processing in the Golgi apparatus. It may then be packaged in a transport carrier. The carrier moves through the axon, interacts with the target region and can deliver its contents to the membrane by fusion. A protein positioned on the surface may be retained through interactions with local scaffolding molecules, removed by endocytosis or recycled. The relative rates of delivery, retention and removal determine how much protein remains in that domain. This general framework follows from cell biology rather than from a unique pathway for every neuronal protein.

The cytoskeleton has several complementary functions in this process. Microtubules support long-distance cargo movement and contribute to the organisation of cell processes. Actin filaments are particularly important near cell surfaces, in growing processes and in small regions such as dendritic spines and terminals. Neurofilaments, a class of intermediate filaments, contribute to the structural organisation of many mature axons and are associated with the maintenance of axonal calibre. It would be inaccurate to assign all transport exclusively to microtubules, because shorter-range movements and membrane dynamics also involve actin-associated motors. However, for long-distance axonal cargo delivery, the microtubule system is the central mechanism at this level of study.

An axonal initial segment provides a clear example of domain organisation. Scaffolding proteins help cluster membrane proteins and associate them with the underlying cytoskeleton. A barrier to free redistribution of some components helps maintain differences between axonal and somatodendritic surfaces. If a mutation disrupts the ability of a channel to localise to the initial segment, a neuron may retain approximately the same total quantity of the channel while having less channel activity at the specialised region where it is needed. Measuring total protein in a whole-cell sample could therefore miss a serious physiological defect. Measuring the spatial distribution is essential.

A similar distinction arises in presynaptic terminals. An axon can remain physically connected to its target while the terminal loses components required for transmitter release. Conversely, the terminal may contain intact release machinery but receive no effective electrical signal from the upstream axon. Both conditions can reduce transmission, but they represent different failures. Measuring activity at the soma and terminal, together with the postsynaptic response, helps identify the affected step. A student should not treat reduced output as a sufficient explanation of the mechanism producing the reduction.

Mitochondria supply ATP and participate in cellular calcium handling, among other activities. Their distribution is therefore particularly important in regions with recurrent energetic demand. Ion pumps maintain the gradients on which electrical signalling depends, and vesicle trafficking requires energy. A terminal receiving repeated activity can consume metabolic resources more rapidly than a less active region. Some mitochondria move through the axon, while others become stationary for prolonged periods. Stationary mitochondria are not necessarily defective: their position may allow them to support persistent local demands. Thus a healthy system is not one in which every organelle moves continuously.

Local protein synthesis provides a further qualification to a strictly soma-centred model. Dendrites contain RNA and translational machinery that can support synthesis of selected proteins near their sites of use. Local translation also occurs in particular axonal contexts, including development, regeneration and some mature axons. This does not remove the requirement for axonal transport. A neuron needs both locally regulated processes and long-distance delivery of many components. If a question asks whether the axon contains conventional prominent Nissl bodies, the expected answer differs from a question asking whether any axonal protein synthesis is possible.

The compartmental model explains how a neuron can respond selectively to a perturbation. Loss of a sorting signal may redistribute a particular protein without destroying the cell. Disruption of the cytoskeleton may interfere with both domain organisation and cargo movement. Metabolic inhibition can affect ion gradients, ATP-dependent transport and synaptic function simultaneously. Therefore a single general metabolic perturbation cannot uniquely identify one mechanism unless additional tests distinguish the possible consequences.

11. Neuronal geometry and quantitative structure–function relationships

The surface of a neuron is not merely a passive boundary. Membrane proteins and lipid composition determine how that surface exchanges ions and responds to stimuli. Geometry determines how much membrane is present relative to the cytoplasm beneath it. To isolate geometric effects, consider a cylindrical unbranched segment of axon with diameter d and length L. Ignoring the two end faces, the surface area is πdL and the volume is πd²L/4. Dividing area by volume gives 4/d. The ratio has units of inverse length. For a long thin cylinder, decreasing diameter increases the amount of lateral membrane per unit volume.

Suppose one idealised unmyelinated axon has diameter 1 micrometre and another has diameter 4 micrometres. Their lateral surface-to-volume ratios are 4 and 1 per micrometre respectively. The thinner axon has four times as much lateral membrane per unit cytoplasmic volume. This result does not by itself predict a specific electrical response, since membrane channel density, membrane resistance and surrounding cells can differ. It does show why changes in diameter have consequences for material exchange and why electrical geometry cannot be inferred from axon length alone.

The internal resistance of a uniform cylindrical conductor can be approximated as R = ρL/A, where ρ is resistivity, L is length and A is the cross-sectional area. Resistivity is measured in ohm metres when lengths and areas are expressed in SI units, giving resistance in ohms. For a circular axon, A = πd²/4. If resistivity and length are held constant, internal resistance varies inversely with the square of diameter. Doubling the diameter makes the cross-sectional area four times larger and the axial resistance one-quarter as large. Lower axial resistance helps local electrical disturbances influence more distant regions, although actual propagation also depends on the membrane and other variables.

The following example makes the units explicit. Compare two ideal cylinders of equal length, 1 millimetre, and equal resistivity, with diameters 2 and 6 micrometres. The cross-sectional areas are π and 9π square micrometres respectively. In SI units these become approximately 3.14 × 10⁻¹² and 2.83 × 10⁻¹¹ square metres. The ratio of their axial resistances is nine to one. A numerical value for resistance itself cannot be calculated without assigning resistivity. Reporting a resistance in ohms from geometry alone would introduce an unsupported physical parameter.

Dendrites provide a different geometry problem. A branching structure may receive information at many spatially separated locations. If the cell receives inputs on several branches, the electrical effects at the soma depend on the transfer properties of those branches. Branch diameter, distance, local channels and the location of other active inputs can all matter. A cell with a more elaborate dendritic arbor has greater potential for distributed contacts, but an anatomical branch count does not determine the amplitude of the somatic response to any given input. The quantitative relationship between voltage and passive spread will be developed in the next chapter.

A simple synaptic counting model also shows why structural measurements require interpretation. Assume a neuron has 200 synaptic contacts distributed equally over ten branches, with one contact per presynaptic cell for simplicity. Each branch then receives twenty contacts. If the contacts are instead concentrated on two branches, the total contact count is unchanged but their spatial arrangement is different. The two cells need not integrate information identically. If several contacts come from the same presynaptic cell, the number of unique input cells may be smaller than 200. The model is not a realistic census of a particular neuron; it separates the variables that a measurement of total synapse count cannot resolve.

Axon branching creates a related distinction between path length and total membrane investment. A single axon extending 100 millimetres and then dividing into three terminal branches each 20 millimetres long contains 160 millimetres of total branch length. A signal travelling to any one terminal passes through only 120 millimetres. If the velocity along every segment were the same, arrival time would depend on 120 millimetres, whereas the approximate membrane area at fixed diameter would depend on 160 millimetres. This distinction is useful for reasoning about energetic demands and communication delays in branching networks. Actual branches may differ in diameter and conduction velocity, so the uniform model is illustrative.

These geometric arguments do not substitute for direct electrophysiology. They explain why two cells with the same channel proteins can behave differently if their dimensions and distribution of membranes differ. They also explain why an experimental comparison of cells should account for size rather than interpret every difference in total current, fluorescence or protein content as a change in molecular concentration. Normalisation to membrane area or cell volume may be appropriate depending on the question being asked.

12. Testing glial contributions to neuronal physiology

A cell type is most convincingly assigned a physiological function when several lines of evidence converge. Its location and morphology may suggest a function; the proteins it expresses may indicate a capacity for that function; and a selective perturbation may demonstrate its contribution to the performance of living tissue. For astrocytes, a process near a synapse provides anatomical evidence of an opportunity for interaction, while expression of a transmitter transporter provides molecular evidence of a mechanism. Neither observation alone establishes how much transmitter is removed by that cell during a particular pattern of activity.

Consider a hypothetical experiment comparing extracellular glutamate after identical brief stimulation in two preparations. In the control, the concentration returns rapidly towards baseline. In a second preparation, uptake by astrocytic transporters is selectively reduced, and the measured concentration remains elevated for longer. This pattern supports a contribution of astrocytic uptake to clearance. The interpretation requires evidence that transmitter release has not also increased, that the measurement is calibrated and that the neurons remain viable. A prolonged extracellular signal can arise from a larger initial release or slower removal, and a single measurement taken at a late time cannot distinguish these explanations.

A time course makes the distinction more informative. If the initial measured peak is similar but the subsequent decline is slower, impaired clearance becomes a plausible explanation. If both peak and decline differ, altered release and altered clearance may contribute. The extracellular space is a physical compartment in which substances can diffuse; local geometry and extracellular volume also influence measured concentration. Receptor activity may be affected even when the total amount of transmitter released remains unchanged. Hence the relationship between release, concentration and receptor response must be kept explicit.

The same experimental logic applies to potassium homeostasis. A local recording electrode sensitive to extracellular potassium could measure the concentration before, during and after a defined period of neural activity. A selective reduction in astrocytic potassium uptake might prolong the recovery of extracellular concentration after stimulation. However, reduced uptake could also change neuronal excitability during the stimulation, thereby changing the total amount of potassium released. Simultaneous neuronal recordings and a consistent stimulation protocol help determine whether the perturbation changes the source of the extracellular ions, their removal, or both.

Glial markers provide a means to identify cell populations but have important limits. A marker may be expressed more strongly in some cells or physiological states than in others. Increased fluorescence for a marker can reflect increased expression per cell, an increase in cell number or changes in cell shape that alter the area occupied by the labelled processes. Counting all fluorescent pixels is not necessarily equivalent to counting cells. A careful study may combine identification of nuclei, cell-type markers, morphology and measurement of specific proteins to answer different questions.

The study of microglia illustrates the difference between a response to tissue change and a cause of tissue change. If microglia accumulate near degenerating axons, they may be removing debris or responding to signals released by damaged tissue. To establish that a microglial action is necessary for a particular injury outcome, it would be necessary to alter that action and measure the outcome under suitable controls. Even then, the interpretation must consider whether the intervention also affected other cell populations. The mere presence of an immune cell in a lesion does not establish the direction of causation.

Similarly, astrocytic endfeet around vessels do not establish that astrocytes themselves constitute the tight barrier layer. A tracer introduced into the vascular compartment can help identify the location of the interface restricting movement into neural tissue. Microscopic examination of the endothelial junctions and adjacent cellular processes can then link restricted permeability to anatomy. An experimental alteration in endothelial junction integrity would be expected to influence passage of certain tracers, but not every substance crosses by the same route. The presence of specific transport proteins means that barrier function is selective rather than equivalent to complete impermeability.

The nuclei-counting approach used in the human brain study by Azevedo and colleagues also illustrates measurement design. Dissociating tissue into a suspension of nuclei allows counts to be estimated without tracing each branched cell in an intact section. A neuronal marker distinguishes a major subset of neuronal nuclei from other nuclei. The method sacrifices information about the original locations and connections of those cells, and classification depends on marker properties. A cell count answers a question about abundance; it cannot answer how many synaptic connections the tissue contains or how active it is. The appropriate method therefore depends on which biological property is being tested.

13. Myelin architecture, quantitative interpretation and perturbation

Myelin can be studied at several scales. A transverse view of a fibre reveals the axon and surrounding sheath, while a longitudinal view shows the sequence of internodes and nodes. A light microscope with suitable staining can reveal patterns of myelinated fibres across tissue. Electron microscopy resolves the layered organisation of individual sheaths more clearly. Neither orientation alone gives a complete description. A transverse section may provide information about sheath thickness but cannot establish the spacing of nodes along an axon, while a longitudinal sample may show nodes without accurately revealing the true cross-sectional geometry.

The geometric g-ratio provides one measure of the relationship between sheath and axon. Let d represent the diameter of the axon and D the outer diameter of the entire myelinated fibre. For concentric circular cross sections, the thickness is (D − d)/2. The cross-sectional area occupied by the sheath is π(D² − d²)/4. Its fraction of the total cross-sectional area is 1 − (d/D)². The derivation follows by dividing the sheath area by πD²/4. The formula uses diameters measured in the same units and gives a dimensionless fraction. It assumes a concentric circular profile and excludes other tissues around the fibre.

For example, an axon diameter of 8 micrometres surrounded by a sheath with outer diameter 10 micrometres gives a g-ratio of 0.8 and a sheath thickness of 1 micrometre. The sheath occupies 1 − 0.8² = 0.36, or 36 percent of the idealised fibre cross-sectional area. If the same axon diameter is surrounded by a sheath with outer diameter 12 micrometres, the g-ratio falls to about 0.667, the thickness increases to 2 micrometres and the sheath occupies about 55.6 percent of the area. The second example demonstrates greater relative sheath thickness, not necessarily a greater conduction velocity in every biological setting. The spatial arrangement of nodes and the characteristics of the axonal membrane remain important.

A comparison between fibres with equal outer diameters demands different reasoning. If two fibres both have a total diameter of 10 micrometres but one has an axon diameter of 8 micrometres and the other 6 micrometres, the second has a lower g-ratio and thicker sheath. However, its smaller axonal cross section is associated with a larger axial resistance if resistivity is identical. Changing the relative allocation of space to axon and sheath therefore affects more than one property. A question that supplies only the g-ratio does not provide enough information to calculate the speed of impulse conduction.

Internode measurements also require a defined sampling convention. If ten consecutive internodes each measure 1 millimetre, their combined internodal length is 10 millimetres. The total length of the sampled region also includes the small nodal intervals. If a region begins and ends at nodes, ten complete internodes are bounded by eleven nodes. If a diagram instead begins within an internode and ends within another, counting visible nodes and internodes can yield different numbers. The distinction is primarily geometric and prevents off-by-one mistakes in schematic reasoning.

To test whether myelin contributes to effective communication, conduction can be compared in a fibre before and after a controlled change to the sheath. If the fibre remains intact but propagation becomes slower or fails, the result supports a role for the sheath and associated domains in maintaining propagation. The interpretation becomes stronger if the integrity of the axon and its membrane proteins is also assessed. A destructive intervention might remove myelin and damage the axon simultaneously, making it impossible to identify which change caused the functional deficit. Comparing fibre anatomy, electrical activity and molecular organisation gives a more complete account.

Myelin disorders illustrate a further distinction between initial injury and subsequent consequences. A sheath disturbance can impair communication before the axon degenerates, yet chronic loss of glial support may eventually affect the survival of the axon itself. Restoring insulation may not restore function if the target axon has already been lost. Conversely, an axon that remains structurally present may still transmit poorly while its nodal domains are disorganised. These distinctions are physiological rather than diagnostic and can be used to interpret a hypothetical experiment without requiring specialist disease classifications.

14. Reading axonal transport data and distinguishing alternative mechanisms

A fluorescently labelled organelle can be followed at successive times to measure its position along an axon. Define x as distance from a reference point, increasing towards the terminal. The signed average velocity over an interval is the final position minus the initial position, divided by the elapsed time. The sign indicates net direction relative to the chosen axis, and the magnitude represents the displacement per unit time. A particle that travels away from the soma and later returns to its starting position has zero net displacement, but it has still travelled a nonzero distance. Consequently average speed, obtained from total path length, may be greater than the magnitude of average velocity.

For a hypothetical particle observed at times 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 seconds, suppose its positions are 0, 4, 8, 8 and 4 micrometres. The first two intervals each show movement of 4 micrometres in 2 seconds, corresponding to anterograde velocity of 2 micrometres per second. During the third interval the particle pauses. In the final interval it moves 4 micrometres towards the soma, corresponding to velocity of −2 micrometres per second. The net displacement across eight seconds is 4 micrometres, so the signed average velocity is +0.5 micrometres per second. The total path length is 12 micrometres, giving an average speed of 1.5 micrometres per second. These are simulated observations, not published measurements.

This example explains why different methods can report different transport rates without contradicting each other. A technique that measures the slope of uninterrupted individual runs may yield the motor-associated movement speed. A technique following the same cargo over a long period may include pauses and reversals and therefore yield a lower net rate. A pulse labelling approach tracking a population of newly synthesised proteins can measure the displacement of a broad labelled population over days, rather than the movement of a single visible particle over seconds. The appropriate comparison is between measurements with similar definitions and timescales.

A graph of population fluorescence along an axon presents a distinct interpretation problem. A peak of label at a distal position can move as labelled molecules travel, but its height can also change because of degradation, dispersion or variation in the labelling process. Loss of peak intensity does not prove that all labelled cargo has stopped moving. If the peak shifts while becoming wider, the labelled population may include objects travelling with different net velocities. Quantifying both the distribution and the total signal is more informative than reporting peak location alone.

An experiment designed to test the role of kinesin could compare the movement of identified cargoes in untreated neurons and neurons in which a specific kinesin-dependent pathway is impaired. A prediction is that certain anterograde movements may decrease. It would be unsafe to conclude that all anterograde movement must disappear, because several kinesin family members and cargo adaptors can contribute to transport. Moreover, disruption of one direction can indirectly change the other direction if fewer cargoes reach the distal region from which retrograde movement normally begins. Interpretation requires knowledge of cargo identity, timing and localisation of the perturbation.

A related question concerns ATP. Motor-mediated movement uses ATP, so depletion can impair transport. But ATP depletion also affects ion pumps and many other reactions. If an experiment measures loss of terminal activity after metabolic inhibition, the observation cannot establish that interruption of axonal transport is the sole cause. Testing motor activity in a defined cell-free system, or comparing a selective motor perturbation with broader metabolic inhibition, helps separate these mechanisms. The distinction between demonstrating an energy requirement and identifying a particular molecular motor is central to experimental reasoning.

The polarity of microtubules offers a direct test of a proposed transport rule. In a reconstituted assay, if the orientation of the track is known independently, the direction of motor-driven motion can be expressed relative to its plus and minus ends. Merely observing movement towards the right side of an image is insufficient because the image may be rotated or the tracks may have different orientations. Experiments with oriented microtubules therefore distinguish motor polarity from the arbitrary orientation of a microscope field. The relationship between the molecular direction and the anatomical direction follows only after the organisation of microtubules in the cell is established.

The problem of transport after axonal injury integrates these concepts. A distal region separated from the soma no longer receives its normal supply of cell-body-derived material, even if it retains some membrane proteins and mitochondria for a limited interval. A proximal stump can remain connected to the soma but may undergo changes in transport and growth. The immediate loss of a continuous electrical pathway differs from the delayed changes in maintenance of disconnected regions. The time course of functional decline can therefore help distinguish a direct interruption of signalling from subsequent failure of cellular maintenance, although neither process occurs in complete isolation.

A quantitative comparison of diffusion and directed transport helps explain why axonal length imposes a special constraint on cellular maintenance. For free diffusion along one spatial dimension, the mean squared displacement of an ensemble is twice the diffusion coefficient multiplied by time. The coefficient has dimensions of length squared per time; it is not a velocity. Suppose an illustrative soluble molecule has an effective diffusion coefficient of ten square micrometres per second. The characteristic time for a root mean squared displacement of one hundred micrometres is one hundred squared divided by twenty, or five hundred seconds. For a displacement of one millimetre, equivalent to one thousand micrometres, the corresponding time is fifty thousand seconds, approximately fourteen hours. These estimates refer to the width of a displacement distribution rather than the guaranteed arrival time of an individual molecule. Binding, obstacles, cytoplasmic crowding and degradation can further alter the result. Membrane bounded organelles cannot be assumed to share the diffusion coefficient of this illustrative soluble molecule.

In contrast, a cargo moving continuously at an illustrative speed of one micrometre per second would cover one millimetre in one thousand seconds, approximately seventeen minutes. Pauses, reversals and transfer between motors can make its net delivery time substantially longer. For a comparison on a larger scale, consider a one metre axon conducting an electrical disturbance at fifty metres per second. The disturbance traverses this distance in twenty milliseconds, whereas continuous motor movement at one micrometre per second requires one million seconds, or about eleven and a half days. These numbers are illustrative rather than representative measurements for every fibre. They make clear that electrical signalling and protein delivery solve different biological problems on radically different time scales. Electrical conduction depends on the propagation of local changes in membrane potential; it does not require the same charged particle to travel from the cell body to the terminal. A protein or organelle, by contrast, must actually be displaced if it is to be delivered from one compartment to another.

The distinction also predicts different responses to an experimental perturbation. Blocking a motor on otherwise intact microtubules may initially preserve the electrical response because membrane channels already present in the axon can still function. With sufficient time, declining replacement of those channels, synaptic vesicle proteins or mitochondria can impair the response. Conversely, blocking channels necessary for regenerative electrical activity can abolish conduction rapidly without immediately stopping every molecular motor. Measuring the time course of both cargo displacement and electrical responses, while checking membrane integrity and ATP availability, is more informative than observing a single failed response. The timing alone does not prove a mechanism, because drugs may have multiple effects, but it provides a testable way to separate immediate electrical failure from progressive maintenance failure.

15. From individual cells to nervous tissue and nerves

The classification of neural structures becomes particularly useful when examining a real tissue preparation. An isolated neuronal diagram suggests that the soma, dendrites and axon can all be viewed at once. In a section of tissue, however, most long cellular processes extend out of the plane of the section. A circular profile might be a neuronal soma, a glial cell nucleus, a blood vessel or a cross section of an axon, depending on its dimensions and staining characteristics. Recognition requires several features rather than one shape. A neuron may display a conspicuous nucleus, a nucleolus and cytoplasm with prominent synthetic machinery, while many smaller glial nuclei appear among its processes. These are general histological guides, not criteria that correctly identify every cell in every preparation.

Consider a transverse section of a peripheral nerve. Individual axons are embedded in a local environment containing Schwann cells and connective tissue. The endoneurium surrounds individual nerve fibres and their associated Schwann cell units. Groups of fibres are collected into fascicles surrounded by perineurium, and the epineurium forms a connective tissue investment around the larger nerve and its fascicles. This hierarchy permits many separate axons to travel together while maintaining structural and chemical organisation. The perineurial barrier is important for the environment within the fascicle. The connective tissue layers are not additional layers of myelin: myelin is a glial membrane structure immediately associated with particular axons.

A peripheral nerve section may contain both large myelinated fibres and small unmyelinated fibres. The number of circular myelin profiles does not equal the total number of axons in the nerve. Small unmyelinated axons may share a nonmyelinating Schwann cell arrangement, and their identification may require higher resolution. An axon profile also cannot be assigned confidently to a sensory or motor neuron solely from the presence of a sheath. Both sensory and motor systems contain myelinated fibres. The anatomical destination and source, or selective labelling, are needed to identify the functional class of an individual fibre.

A peripheral ganglion should be distinguished from a nerve fascicle even if both lie along the same anatomical pathway. A dorsal root ganglion contains sensory neuronal somata with surrounding satellite cells. The cell bodies are situated beside the pathway connecting peripheral endings and the spinal cord. An autonomic ganglion instead contains neuronal somata that receive synaptic inputs from preganglionic neurons and send their own axons towards target tissues. This difference matters: a dorsal root ganglion is not normally a relay station at which incoming peripheral sensory axons terminate on a second neuron before entering the spinal cord. A ganglion can therefore represent a collection of cell bodies without being a synaptic relay of the same type as every other ganglion.

The CNS has its own arrangements of supporting elements. Oligodendrocytes form compact myelin segments, astrocytes extend processes within grey and white matter, and microglia occupy distributed territories. Axons within a CNS tract are not enclosed in the same pattern of peripheral nerve connective tissue layers. A peripheral nerve and a central tract can both convey impulses over a long distance, yet their cellular environments and repair responses differ. The distinction helps explain why an anatomical name such as tract or nerve carries information beyond the direction in which signals travel.

Grey matter is often rich in neuropil. In such regions, the cell bodies visible in a stained section are only a portion of the neural machinery. Many dendrites, axonal terminals and glial processes occupy the space between them. A region containing fewer visible cell bodies than another region is not necessarily less functionally important; it might receive or transmit many connections. White matter likewise contains living glia and axons requiring energy and maintenance. It is not an inert conduit connecting active grey matter. The exchange of signals between regions depends on the health of both.

Histological interpretation benefits from combining stains. A technique highlighting myelin can establish the location of many myelinated fibres; another highlighting neuronal cell bodies provides information about the distribution of somata; a marker for particular glial populations provides a different view of the same tissue. Differences between images may reflect the biological selectivity of the stain rather than a contradiction. A positive marker is meaningful only when the target and limitations of the method are understood. A negative result could reflect genuinely absent material, insufficient sensitivity, inadequate preservation or an inaccessible target in the preparation. These methodological distinctions are central to examination questions that ask what a micrograph actually demonstrates.

16. Anatomical connectivity and the interpretation of tracing experiments

The shape of a neuron does not reveal the complete identity of its synaptic partners. A long axon may cross several structures before reaching its principal targets, and a passing axon can be mistaken for one that terminates in a region. To establish connectivity, experimental approaches must identify where neuronal processes originate, where they terminate, and whether they communicate with the suspected target. These are three related but separable questions.

An anterograde tracer introduced into or taken up by neurons at a defined origin can be transported along their axons. Label observed in a distant region then provides evidence that processes of labelled neurons reach that region. A retrograde tracer taken up at a destination can travel towards neuronal somata, allowing identification of cells projecting there. Both approaches exploit the cellular capacity for transport, but tracer properties determine precisely what is labelled. Some tracers are taken up by fibres passing through the injection region, some cross particular intercellular connections, and others remain largely within the originally labelled cells. The interpretation must therefore specify which type of tracer is used.

Suppose a hypothetical anterograde experiment labels neurons in region A and reveals labelled axons and boutons in region B. This supports a projection from A towards B. A separate retrograde tracer placed in B might label somata in A, providing convergent anatomical evidence. Neither observation alone determines whether activation of the projection excites or inhibits the receiving cells, whether every labelled bouton is a functional synapse, or whether the pathway is essential for a particular behaviour. Functional measurements are required for those conclusions.

The converse experiment illustrates a common error. If a retrograde tracer placed in B labels neurons in A, the data identify neurons whose processes reach B. They do not establish that a projection also runs from B to A. Reciprocal connectivity is possible, but it requires evidence for the opposite direction. Confusing the direction of tracer movement with the direction of the anatomical projection can reverse the conclusion: a retrograde tracer moves from a destination back towards the cell bodies of neurons that supply it.

Multiple tracer colours can distinguish overlapping projection populations. Suppose a red tracer placed in target B labels 40 somata in region A, while a green tracer placed in target C labels 30 somata. If ten somata carry both labels, a simple inclusion-exclusion calculation gives 40 + 30 − 10 = 60 uniquely labelled somata. Thirty are red only, twenty are green only and ten are double labelled. Under assumptions of selective uptake and reliable detection, the double-labelled population supports the existence of neurons with processes reaching both targets. The numbers are hypothetical. They do not demonstrate that every double-labelled neuron makes equally strong synapses in both regions, or that every unlabelled neuron lacks projections.

Anatomical and physiological experiments can complement one another. If selective stimulation of A produces a rapid change in B, the anatomical tracing evidence strengthens the case for a pathway between them. Nevertheless, a rapid effect might be mediated through intermediate neurons, and a connection demonstrated anatomically may not be active under the conditions of the physiological recording. An investigator may compare responses with and without disruption of the proposed pathway, control for stimulation of fibres passing through the region, and determine whether the response timing is compatible with the proposed circuit. This reasoning establishes how multiple independent observations can support or weaken a biological model.

The same distinction applies at a cellular scale. A neuron with a dendritic branch physically adjacent to an axonal terminal may receive input from that terminal, but adjacency alone does not prove communication. Electron microscopy can reveal membrane specialisations characteristic of synapses, and electrophysiological or optical recordings can establish functional effects. No single measurement automatically answers all questions about anatomy, transmission and behaviour. The chapter therefore introduces tracing as an extension of neuronal polarity and axonal transport, rather than as a substitute for the detailed study of synaptic mechanisms in a later node.

17. Linking glia, metabolism and neuronal excitability

The extracellular fluid surrounding a neuron is small relative to many of the ionic movements occurring during repeated activity. Changes in that environment can influence nearby cells, even without a newly formed synaptic connection. Glial uptake and buffering, vascular exchange and the intrinsic transport systems of neurons cooperate to limit such disturbances. The word homeostasis should not imply that every variable is held absolutely constant. A physiological system maintains variables within ranges compatible with function, while transient changes can themselves contribute to regulation.

An illustrative mass-balance model clarifies the difference between an extracellular concentration and an amount of material. Let Q denote the quantity of an ion or other solute in a small extracellular compartment and V its volume. Its concentration is C = Q/V. If Q is expressed in picomoles and V in microlitres, C is in micromoles per litre because one picomole per microlitre is one micromole per litre. Suppose 12 picomoles of a solute are added to a 2 microlitre compartment without immediate removal. The resulting concentration increase is 6 micromoles per litre. If the same amount is added to a 1 microlitre compartment, the increase is 12 micromoles per litre. These numbers are deliberately illustrative and are not measurements of brain extracellular space.

A second model describes a substance removed from a compartment at a rate proportional to its excess concentration. If the compartment volume and other conditions are constant, the excess can be described by C(t) = C₀ exp(−kt), where C₀ is the excess at time zero, k is a positive removal constant measured in inverse time and t is elapsed time. The product kt is dimensionless, as required for the exponential. The half-time is ln(2)/k. This follows from setting C(t)/C₀ equal to one-half and solving for time. Such a model is appropriate only for a simplified regime in which removal is approximately first order; biological uptake can saturate, and multiple compartments can create more complicated time courses.

For example, suppose two hypothetical extracellular solute transients begin at the same excess concentration. The control has a half-time of 2 seconds and the altered tissue has a half-time of 4 seconds. After 4 seconds the control has one-quarter of its initial excess, whereas the altered tissue has one-half. The altered tissue retains twice the excess concentration at that time. An experiment with equal initial peaks but different recovery half-times suggests altered net removal. The inference does not uniquely identify a glial transporter, since diffusion, extracellular volume or other removal mechanisms might also change. Additional perturbations and measurements would be necessary.

Energy supply is equally relevant to membrane physiology. The maintenance of gradients across neuronal membranes depends on metabolic processes that produce ATP. Glial cells participate in the local availability and handling of energy substrates, and myelinating cells support the axons they ensheath. It would be incorrect to conclude from this cooperation that neurons are incapable of taking up or metabolising glucose themselves. The relative contribution of different metabolic routes depends on cell type, region and activity. At the examination level, the essential idea is that electrical communication requires continuous metabolic support even though an electrical signal travels much faster than nutrients or vesicles are transported over the entire cell.

The consequences of glial impairment can occur on more than one timescale. A disturbance in extracellular ionic control may influence excitability rapidly. Changes in myelin can alter the transfer of signals along axons even without immediate death of the neurons. A failure of metabolic or trophic support may have progressively damaging effects. Because the resulting symptoms may overlap, the timing of a change and the type of measurement can help distinguish possible explanations. A rapid alteration in local neuronal firing during a potassium transient does not provide the same mechanistic evidence as a gradual loss of axonal terminals after a long-term disruption of transport.

A particularly important teaching distinction is that most glia do not use the same conventional action-potential-based long-distance communication as neurons. This does not mean that they are physiologically silent. Glial cells express receptors, transporters and channels, undergo changes in intracellular signalling and respond to their environment. Some glial lineage cells express voltage-dependent conductances. The division between neurons and glia is based on specialised cell functions, morphology and development, not simply the presence or absence of electrical phenomena at the membrane.

This perspective also helps interpret interventions in mixed tissue. If a drug applied to a preparation changes a neuronal response, the drug may have acted directly on neuronal membrane proteins, indirectly through glial regulation, or through several cell types. A cell-type-selective intervention, when appropriately validated, offers stronger evidence about the responsible population. A drug effect observed in isolated neurons and absent in a glial-only preparation might support a direct neuronal mechanism, but isolation changes the cellular environment. Results from isolated cells and intact tissue should therefore be interpreted together rather than treated as equivalent.

18. Integrated reasoning: separating signal, structure and supply

A coherent explanation of nervous system organisation must distinguish three connected dimensions. The first is the anatomical route: which cells connect which regions? The second is information transfer: how does activity in one region influence the next? The third is cellular maintenance: how are the membranes, proteins, organelles and extracellular conditions required for that transfer preserved? A single observable deficit, such as the disappearance of a muscle response, can arise from failure in any of these dimensions. A well-designed experimental question asks the student to discriminate among them.

Imagine a preparation containing a motor neuron with a long myelinated axon and a muscle target. In condition I, stimulating the neuron produces an action potential at the soma and a signal at the distal axon, followed by muscle contraction. In condition II, the somatic response persists but the distal signal is delayed or absent. In condition III, the distal axon signal is preserved but the muscle no longer contracts. The pattern in condition II directs attention towards propagation along the axon, although an altered recording or physical interruption must be excluded. Condition III directs attention towards the terminal, synapse or muscle itself. Direct stimulation of the muscle would help distinguish a failure of neuromuscular communication from a failure of the contractile apparatus.

Now imagine a fourth condition in which all three responses are initially preserved after a selective perturbation of cargo delivery, but terminal output gradually declines over subsequent days. The time dependence is consistent with progressive depletion or deterioration of components at the terminal. It does not prove a transport defect unless the perturbation and cargo movements are independently verified. An immediate electrical response can survive temporarily on existing membrane proteins and organelles, whereas maintenance depends on the replacement and regulation of those components. Separating acute from chronic effects is often the key to explaining data in experimental physiology.

A sensory neuron introduces another instructive case. A pseudounipolar dorsal root ganglion neuron has a soma located to the side of the principal signalling route from peripheral ending to spinal cord. Suppose a manipulation alters protein synthesis in the soma while leaving the axonal branches temporarily intact. The immediate passage of electrical signals may continue, but long-term health of both branches can be affected. Conversely, an interruption in the peripheral branch can prevent natural receptor signals from reaching the CNS even while the soma and central branch remain structurally intact. The geometric arrangement explains why loss of a particular branch and loss of the cell body are not physiologically equivalent.

The effects of glial damage similarly depend on location and cell type. Loss of a myelinating oligodendrocyte can affect several internodes, potentially on more than one axon. Loss of a myelinating Schwann cell directly removes one internodal unit on one axon, although local injury can involve several neighbouring cells. A disturbance in a satellite glial cell population is centred around peripheral ganglion somata, whereas a disturbance in nonmyelinating Schwann cells concerns axons in peripheral tissues. The predicted site of dysfunction follows from the spatial relationship between the glial cell and the neuron.

An integrated prediction may require recognising that one perturbation affects more than one variable. Removing myelin alters transverse insulation, effective capacitance and the organisation of nodal proteins. Disrupting microtubules affects transport and cell architecture. Reducing ATP production impairs ion pumps, motors and many biosynthetic processes. It is tempting to select the single most familiar mechanism and attribute the entire phenotype to it. Scientific reasoning instead identifies the set of plausible effects and then uses controls to distinguish them. This approach is particularly valuable in Part C questions where several alternatives are biologically possible but only one is justified by the observations provided.

Finally, the cellular foundations explain why the next stage of neural physiology begins with the membrane potential. A neuron has a specialised geometry, a distribution of membrane proteins and a regulated extracellular environment. These properties establish the conditions under which ionic gradients can produce an electrical potential across the plasma membrane. The next chapter will consider the electrochemical forces and selective permeabilities quantitatively. Later chapters will establish how regenerative activity, synapses and neural networks transform these cellular properties into coordinated responses. The organisation described here supplies the anatomical and metabolic context for those electrical mechanisms.

19. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Incorrect: A nerve net is random and cannot coordinate behaviour. Correct: Distributed networks can contain specialised populations and reproducible activity. Reasoning: Simplified drawings omit circuit structure. Exams may contrast absence of a brain with absence of coordination; infer function from connectivity and experiments.

  2. Incorrect: Modern animals form a linear progression from nerve nets to vertebrate brains. Correct: Extant lineages have separate evolutionary histories; centralisation describes organisation, not a ladder. Reasoning: Comparative textbook diagrams are often arranged by morphology. Avoid inferring ancestry from diagram order.

  3. Incorrect: Every ganglion is a synaptic relay. Correct: A ganglion contains peripheral somata; dorsal root ganglia normally lack an obligatory neuron-to-neuron sensory relay. Reasoning: The autonomic ganglion model is overgeneralised. Trace actual cell bodies and processes.

  4. Incorrect: A nerve is one long neuron. Correct: A nerve bundles many axons with glia and connective tissue. Reasoning: The terms are casually interchanged. A mixed nerve may contain many independent sensory and motor axons.

  5. Incorrect: Every action potential begins at the soma. Correct: The initial segment often initiates spikes; sensory axons can initiate near peripheral endings. Reasoning: A conventional multipolar diagram is mistaken for a universal rule. Locate the relevant specialised membrane.

  6. Incorrect: The hillock and initial segment are identical. Correct: The hillock is a geometric transition; the initial segment is a molecularly specialised axonal domain. Reasoning: They lie close together in common diagrams. Distinguish morphology from molecular localisation.

  7. Incorrect: A pseudounipolar peripheral process is simply a dendrite. Correct: Both major branches have important axonal properties. Reasoning: Direction of sensory information is mistaken for dendritic identity. Follow structure and physiology rather than direction alone.

  8. Incorrect: All multipolar neurons are motor neurons. Correct: Many multipolar cells are interneurons or projection neurons. Reasoning: Shape and functional categories are confused. Identify the connectivity and target independently.

  9. Incorrect: No axonal protein synthesis is possible. Correct: Prominent Nissl bodies are absent from the typical axon, but local translation occurs in particular axonal contexts. Reasoning: A histological generalisation becomes an absolute molecular claim. Read exactly what is measured.

  10. Incorrect: Glia are passive packing material. Correct: Glia regulate chemical conditions, myelinate axons, participate in immunity and support tissue function. Reasoning: A historic name obscures physiology. Predict effects of cell-type-selective disruption.

  11. Incorrect: Astrocytic endfeet are the main tight-junction barrier layer. Correct: Specialised vascular endothelium forms the principal restrictive interface; astrocytes help support it. Reasoning: Anatomical proximity is confused with the sealing structure. Distinguish support from the physical interface.

  12. Incorrect: All nonneuronal cells are glia, with a fixed ten-to-one ratio. Correct: Nonneuronal counts include other cell types, and ratios vary by region and species. Reasoning: An often-repeated estimate is treated as universal. Ask how cells were identified and counted.

  13. Incorrect: A Schwann cell myelinates many separate axons in the same way as an oligodendrocyte. Correct: A myelinating Schwann cell makes one internode; a nonmyelinating Schwann cell can ensheath several small axons. Reasoning: Ensheathment is confused with compact myelination. Identify both cell type and sheath type.

  14. Incorrect: Saltatory conduction means ions leap through empty gaps. Correct: The axon is continuous; local current spreads and spikes regenerate at nodes. Reasoning: The word saltatory is interpreted literally. Separate current spread from regenerative membrane events.

  15. Incorrect: A lower g-ratio guarantees faster conduction. Correct: Relative sheath thickness is only one determinant; axon diameter, internodal length and channel organisation also matter. Reasoning: One geometric variable is mistaken for a full physiological model. Check what other parameters are held constant.

  16. Incorrect: All kinesins travel to plus ends and all moving axonal particles move steadily. Correct: Many cargo kinesins are plus-end-directed, with exceptions in the family; cargoes pause or reverse. Reasoning: A useful introductory rule is turned into a universal statement. Distinguish motor polarity, track polarity and net movement.

  17. Incorrect: Retrograde tracer movement shows a projection in the same direction as tracer motion. Correct: A retrograde tracer travels towards the soma, opposite the conventional soma-to-terminal projection direction. Reasoning: The arrow in a tracer diagram is confused with the projection arrow. Specify both directions separately.

  18. Incorrect: A rise in glial fluorescence proves the number of glia increased. Correct: Expression per cell, morphology and cell count can all affect fluorescence. Reasoning: An indirect marker is mistaken for a census. Request counts and per-cell measurements.

    2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
    3. Assertion true, reason false
    4. Assertion false, reason true
    Answer and explanation

    A. The same sensory neuron connects peripheral and central regions via its bifurcated axonal process; its soma lies in the ganglion. The reason explains the absence of an obligatory intervening relay.

  2. Assertion: Compact myelin is a glial membrane structure. Reason: In the vertebrate CNS it is formed by myelinating Schwann cells.

    1. Both true, reason explains assertion
    2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
    3. Assertion true, reason false
    4. Assertion false, reason true
    Answer and explanation

    C. Myelin consists of glial membrane layers, but CNS compact myelin is produced by oligodendrocytes rather than Schwann cells.

  3. Assertion: A retrograde axonal tracer can reveal somata that project to its uptake site. Reason: Retrograde transport proceeds towards the soma.

    1. Both true, reason explains assertion
    2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
    3. Assertion true, reason false
    4. Assertion false, reason true
    Answer and explanation

    A. The direction of transport explains why a suitably selective tracer at a destination can reveal the projecting cell bodies.

  4. A set of statements: I. A nerve may contain afferent and efferent axons. II. White matter contains living glia. III. Every sensory neuron is bipolar. Which are correct?

    1. I only
    2. I and II
    3. II and III
    4. I, II and III
    Answer and explanation

    B. I and II are true. III is false because many somatic sensory neurons are pseudounipolar and neuronal morphology varies across sensory pathways.

  5. A set of statements: I. Astrocytes contribute to transmitter homeostasis. II. Microglia participate in immune surveillance. III. Satellite cells form all CNS myelin. Which are correct?

    1. III only
    2. I and III
    3. I and II
    4. II and III
    Answer and explanation

    C. Astrocytic clearance and microglial surveillance are established roles. Oligodendrocytes, not satellite cells, form CNS compact myelin.

  6. A population of particles includes 12 moving distally, 8 moving proximally and 5 stationary at the time of observation. What percentage is stationary?

    1. 5%
    2. 25%
    3. 40%
    4. 20%
    Answer and explanation

    D. Total particles = 25; stationary fraction = 5/25 = 0.2 or 20%. A snapshot does not imply that those particles remain stationary throughout their lifetime.

  7. Which experimental observation best supports the claim that a local ganglion can produce a specific movement without a central command during the test?

    1. The ganglion is large in a fixed specimen
    2. The movement persists after reversible central disconnection while local circuit viability is confirmed
    3. The brain contains many neuronal nuclei
    4. The axons have thick myelin
    Answer and explanation

    B. A functional manipulation directly tests whether the movement persists without that connection. Size and morphology alone do not establish autonomous function.

  8. A selective transport perturbation leaves early electrical communication intact, but vesicle proteins and output decline over days. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

    1. Transport and electrical propagation are identical
    2. The soma was necessarily destroyed immediately
    3. The delayed phenotype is compatible with a cellular maintenance defect, requiring confirmation of cargo delivery and viability
    4. The result proves glia are irrelevant
    Answer and explanation

    C. Existing local machinery can support early function while later depletion affects output. Independent cargo and viability measurements are needed to identify the cause.

23. Revision and mastery

Chapter synthesis

Neural systems solve coordination problems by establishing specialised routes for information processing. Distributed nerve nets, ganglion-based organisation and centralised systems represent different arrangements rather than a single ranking of animals. In vertebrates the brain and spinal cord form the CNS, while nerves and peripheral ganglia link central circuits to the body. Neurons are polarised cells with specialised receptive, integrating and output domains. Morphology and circuit function are independent classifications. Glial cells maintain extracellular conditions, immune surveillance, myelin and fluid interfaces. Microtubule-associated motors deliver and retrieve cargo along the axon, allowing local electrical communication to persist over long distances.

High-yield distinctions

Ganglion versus nucleus; nerve versus tract; afferent versus efferent; hillock versus initial segment; pseudounipolar versus bipolar; astrocytic support versus endothelial barrier; myelinating versus nonmyelinating Schwann cell; internode versus node; anterograde versus retrograde transport; instantaneous velocity versus net transport.
