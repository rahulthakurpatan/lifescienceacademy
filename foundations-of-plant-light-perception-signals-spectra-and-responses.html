1. Why a plant needs light as information

A seedling buried beneath the soil faces two distinct problems. It must obtain carbon and energy in order to maintain its cells, but it must also decide which organs to develop and where to direct growth. Before it reaches the surface, an angiosperm seedling can often sustain respiration using reserves that were deposited in the seed. It cannot yet carry out appreciable photosynthesis because useful radiation does not reach its buried shoot. Nevertheless, its tissues remain capable of detecting the arrival of light. A small exposure can initiate a developmental change even when it contributes negligibly to the carbon budget. This observation separates sensory photobiology from photosynthetic bioenergetics.

Consider two seedlings of the same genotype with ample stored reserves. One develops in darkness and one receives adequate light. In a typical Arabidopsis comparison, the dark-grown individual elongates its hypocotyl, maintains an apical hook and retains relatively closed cotyledons. In light, hypocotyl elongation is restrained, the hook opens and the cotyledons expand and green. These are different developmental programs, not simply unequal rates of a single program. The elongated shoot in darkness can help the seedling reach the illuminated surface while reducing expenditure on broad photosynthetic organs before they can function. Once light is detected, the allocation of growth changes.

A photoreceptor is a molecular system whose state changes in response to absorbed radiation and whose altered state affects cellular behavior. The receptor cannot understand its surroundings consciously. Its molecular state encodes a physical property of the incoming light. In a signaling pathway, other proteins respond to that state, sometimes modifying transcription, sometimes altering enzyme activities and sometimes reorganizing cellular structures. The output therefore depends on which tissues contain the receptor and the signaling machinery, and on the developmental state of the plant.

This arrangement explains how light might inhibit elongation in one organ while encouraging expansion in another. There is no contradiction if the responding cells deploy different growth programs downstream of the same sensory input. A plant also receives chemical, mechanical, thermal and water-status signals. Light is consequently not an isolated command: its meaning emerges through interaction with other environmental and internal conditions. A seed that is too dry to germinate will not necessarily germinate simply because a photoreceptor has been activated. To understand a light response, identify the input, the receptor, the transduction process and the measured output separately.

Separating a signal from a resource

A resource contributes material or usable energy to a process. The carbon dioxide entering a leaf is a resource for carbon fixation; the photons absorbed by its photosynthetic apparatus contribute energy. An environmental signal, in contrast, changes the regulation of a biological process. It can do this even if the amount of energy it supplies is negligible compared with the energy required to execute the response. The distinction is functional, not a distinction between two physical kinds of photon. A particular red photon absorbed by a photosystem and another red photon absorbed by a phytochrome can have the same energy. What differs is the absorbing molecular machinery and the consequence of absorption.

Consider the amount of cellular work associated with emergence of a seedling. Cells must synthesize wall polymers, maintain ion gradients, produce proteins, and exchange metabolites. These processes require sustained metabolic energy. A photoreceptor exposed to a very short light pulse can influence subsequent synthesis and development through pathways powered largely by the plant's existing metabolic resources. There is no violation of energy conservation because the photon does not supply all the energy needed to make the new structures. It initiates a state change that regulates the allocation of cellular energy. An analogy is an enzyme regulator that changes the rate at which a substrate is consumed without becoming the substrate itself. Unlike many enzyme regulators, the photoreceptor initially responds to electromagnetic radiation rather than to a dissolved molecule.

A useful experiment compares reserve-supported seedlings grown on equivalent media and exposed either to a brief effective light pulse or maintained in darkness. The endpoint should be a specific developmental trait rather than simply survival. A change following the pulse may support a sensory interpretation if the total photon energy is insufficient to account for the developmental change by direct carbon gain. However, the experiment cannot show that every later metabolic effect is independent of photosynthesis. Light-induced development can prepare the plant for subsequent photosynthesis. Sensory and metabolic functions may be causally linked even though they are experimentally distinguishable.

Sensory outputs have different timescales

A change in the state of an absorbing molecule can occur rapidly after photon absorption. A physiological response may appear much later because the molecular state must influence a pathway, and the pathway must bring about a measurable change. The relevant timescale depends on the output. An ion-transport response can use existing membrane proteins; transcriptional changes require changes in RNA production or turnover; anatomical changes may require sustained cell expansion and differentiation. Consequently, it is possible to detect a biochemical response before a seedling's shape visibly changes.

This difference determines how an experiment should be sampled. A single early measurement of hypocotyl length may miss an eventual developmental effect. A single late measurement of protein state may miss a transient light-induced signal that initiated the change. A time series sampling the receptor state, an intermediate response, and a morphological endpoint provides more informative evidence. Temporal precedence is necessary for a simple causal claim: if a proposed initiating event consistently occurs after the physiological change, the event cannot account for initiation of that change. Precedence alone is not sufficient, because two events could both result from a third factor. Perturbing the candidate pathway provides a complementary test.

Light sensors also differ from energy sensors in an important practical way. A plant experiencing reduced photon input may become carbon limited and change growth because of metabolic constraints. It may simultaneously detect the altered light environment and activate a regulatory response that anticipates future competition. In real habitats these mechanisms often operate together. Controlled experiments separate them by independently manipulating spectral composition, carbon availability where appropriate, photon dose and genotype rather than treating the entire shade phenotype as one mechanism.

2. Photons, wavelength and the units of a light stimulus

Light is electromagnetic radiation. Its wavelength is the distance between successive equivalent points of a wave, measured in metres or, commonly in photobiology, nanometres. A nanometre is one billionth of a metre. In vacuum, wavelength multiplied by frequency equals the speed of light. The energy of an individual photon is proportional to frequency and therefore inversely proportional to wavelength. With Planck's constant h and vacuum speed of light c, the photon energy E is hc divided by wavelength. The relation concerns the energy of one photon; it does not specify how many photons fall upon a leaf.

At 450 nm, a photon has an energy of approximately 4.41 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. At 660 nm the corresponding energy is approximately 3.01 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Thus an equal number of blue and red photons does not transport equal radiant energy. Blue photons carry roughly 1.47 times the energy per photon in this comparison. Conversely, a red beam and a blue beam delivering the same radiant energy contain different numbers of photons. This distinction is indispensable for interpreting a comparison of plant responses to monochromatic treatments.

Radiant irradiance is radiant power incident on a unit area, usually expressed as W m⁻². One watt is one joule per second. Photon flux density counts incident photons per area per time, usually expressed in µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹. A mole of photons represents Avogadro's number of photons. Multiplying energy per photon by Avogadro's constant yields the energy per mole. Consequently, dividing monochromatic radiant irradiance by molar photon energy gives the molar photon flux density, which may be converted to micromoles per square metre per second by multiplication by one million.

Suppose two ideal narrow-band sources each supply 1 W m⁻², one centred at 450 nm and the other at 660 nm. Using h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and the Avogadro constant 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, their molar photon energies are approximately 266 and 181 kJ mol⁻¹ respectively. The two photon flux densities are approximately 3.76 and 5.52 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Even if the receptors had the same probability of successful activation per photon, the equal-power experiment would not supply equal photon doses. The ratio arises from the physical units rather than the molecular characteristics of a receptor.

These calculations assume approximately monochromatic radiation and a measurement plane with a specified area. For a broadband lamp the total photon flux requires a wavelength-resolved calculation, because every spectral interval contributes a different amount of energy per photon. Measuring at the plant rather than at the lamp also matters: distance, optical filters, leaf angle and scattering alter the radiation that reaches the tissue. Illuminance in lux weights wavelengths by the sensitivity of human vision; it is generally unsuitable as a direct measure of plant photoreceptor stimulation. Two lamps can appear equally bright to a person while supplying very different photon distributions to a plant.

Reading the dimensional analysis

The equation E = hc/λ is straightforward only if the units are consistent. Planck's constant has units of joule seconds and the speed of light has units of metres per second. Their product has units of joule metres. Dividing by wavelength in metres gives joules per photon. If a wavelength given as 450 nm is entered into this equation as 450 without converting it to metres, the result is wrong by a factor of one billion. The error is not a subtle biological uncertainty but a dimensional error that can be detected before any numerical calculation.

Molar photon energy is useful because physiological instruments report the arrival rate of large numbers of photons using micromoles. For 550 nm light, conversion gives λ = 5.50 × 10⁻⁷ m. Multiplication of h and c gives approximately 1.986 × 10⁻²⁵ J m, and division by λ yields 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Multiplying by the number of photons in a mole gives approximately 217.5 kJ mol⁻¹. A photon flux density of 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at this wavelength corresponds to an irradiance of about 21.75 W m⁻², since 100 micromoles equals 0.0001 mol. The magnitude of this value is a useful check: the answer must be far smaller than 217.5 kW m⁻² because the rate involves only a tiny fraction of a mole per square metre per second.

Equal photon flux is often preferable when investigating how efficiently different wavelengths drive a photochemical response. Even equal photon flux does not imply equal absorption by the receptor, however. If one wavelength is transmitted through the tissue or has a lower probability of absorption, fewer incident photons may actually excite the relevant receptor. A receptor's wavelength dependence is therefore not eliminated by choosing equal photon doses. Rather, the equal-dose design eliminates a simple difference in the number of incident photons so that biological spectral sensitivity can be studied.

Incident, absorbed and productive photons

An incident photon is one that reaches the measurement surface. It may be reflected, transmitted or absorbed. If absorbed, its energy may initiate the relevant photochemical event, or it may be dissipated or lead to another outcome. A useful conceptual sequence is therefore incident photons, absorbed photons, and successful photochemical events. The number of successful events cannot generally exceed the number of photons absorbed in a simple one-photon process, although each successful event may trigger many downstream biological changes. Distinguish the efficiency of photoconversion from the magnitude of the final physiological response.

For a hypothetical receptor preparation, imagine that 1,000 photons reach the sample at wavelength A, 400 are absorbed by the receptor, and 200 cause the monitored conversion. The fraction absorbed is 0.40 and the probability of monitored conversion per absorbed photon is 0.50 under these simplified conditions. The conversion per incident photon is therefore 0.20. If wavelength B has the same conversion probability per absorbed photon but only 100 of 1,000 incident photons are absorbed, it gives 50 successful events. The resulting difference in effective sensitivity comes from absorption, not from a change in the post-absorption conversion probability.

These numbers are illustrative and do not represent a measured receptor. The model assumes the events are counted without complication from reverse reactions, bleaching or multiple receptor states. In intact tissues, several light-absorbing molecules coexist and may have overlapping absorption spectra, so total absorption cannot be assigned to a particular receptor without additional evidence. In a sufficiently simple system, however, the distinctions help explain why incident photon dose, optical absorption and biological output cannot be treated as equivalent measurements.

3. Spectral quality, quantity, duration and direction

A light environment cannot be adequately described by a single value of irradiance. Spectral quality refers to the wavelength distribution of the radiation. A plant outdoors can encounter direct sunlight, skylight, radiation transmitted by leaves, and reflected light from adjacent vegetation. These environments differ both in the total number of photons and in the relative numbers arriving at particular wavelengths. Blue light occupies approximately the 400–500 nm range; red light occupies roughly 600–700 nm; far-red extends beyond the conventional 700 nm limit of photosynthetically active radiation. UV-A and UV-B occupy shorter wavelength intervals, approximately 315–400 nm and 280–315 nm, respectively. Such boundaries are conventions and are not sharp biological switches.

Leaves preferentially absorb several visible wavelengths while transmitting and reflecting relatively more far-red radiation. Beneath or near vegetation, the red to far-red photon ratio can therefore fall. This change can provide information about neighboring plants even before severe carbon limitation develops. An experimentally useful red to far-red ratio must specify the wavelength bands or narrow windows used to compute it. Ratios from different instruments are not necessarily interchangeable. A low ratio can arise because red flux decreases, far-red flux increases, or both change. Each situation can produce different absolute receptor conversion rates even when the ratios are identical.

Quantity describes the rate of photon arrival. Duration describes how long the stimulus is delivered, and temporal pattern describes when exposures occur and how they are separated. Plants may respond differently to continuous illumination and brief pulses even when the time-integrated photon doses are identical. This happens when signaling intermediates decay between pulses, when receptors recover toward another state in darkness or when other time-dependent processes modify responsiveness. The biological clock adds another level: a light pulse arriving at one phase of an endogenous rhythm may cause a different output from an identical pulse arriving at another phase.

Direction supplies spatial information. A shoot receiving a beam from one side can develop unequal signaling across its tissues. Differences in elongation on opposite sides produce curvature, whereas uniform elongation changes shoot length without necessarily changing its orientation. The angle of incident radiation affects photon interception by leaves and therefore can influence both directional sensing and energy capture. The information supplied by direction is especially apparent in a seedling assay using a small unilateral source. However, gradients in temperature or water loss may accompany an unshielded lamp, so carefully controlled geometry and temperature are necessary to attribute bending to light.

The four dimensions can be varied separately in a controlled experiment. To investigate quality, maintain equal photon flux and exposure duration at multiple wavelengths. To investigate quantity, vary photon flux at a fixed wavelength and duration. To investigate duration, keep flux constant but vary exposure time. To investigate direction, compare unilateral and symmetric exposure with carefully characterized photon delivery. These are operational distinctions rather than claims that the plant possesses an independent receptor for each dimension.

Measuring the ecological signal

The same total photon flux can be produced by a spectrum dominated by blue radiation, a spectrum dominated by red radiation, or a broad mixture of wavelengths. These treatments are biologically distinct because their probabilities of exciting the different sensory systems differ. A spectral plot normally displays wavelength on the horizontal axis and a wavelength-resolved irradiance or photon flux quantity on the vertical axis. The quantity integrated over a spectral interval represents the contribution of that interval to the total. A single sensor reporting an integrated number cannot recover the entire wavelength distribution.

A common environmental comparison involves full light and canopy light. Green foliage absorbs a substantial fraction of visible radiation through photosynthetic pigments, and far-red is relatively less absorbed by those pigments. Therefore a plant near foliage may encounter a lower red to far-red ratio than an isolated plant. The direction of the incoming radiation can also change: reflected radiation from a nearby plant may provide lateral spectral information. The sensory significance lies in the change in probabilities of phytochrome photoconversion, rather than a requirement that the neighboring plant completely block sunlight.

The conventional photosynthetically active radiation interval is 400–700 nm. It is a practical measurement convention, not a statement that radiation outside the interval has no physiological effects. Far-red can participate in photosynthetic function in appropriate spectral mixtures, and it clearly contributes to plant photoreceptor signaling. UV radiation may induce protective responses and, at sufficiently damaging exposures, cause molecular injury. Therefore the terms photosynthetic light and sensory light should not be assigned strictly to disjoint wavelength ranges. A photon within the conventional interval can participate in either function depending on which molecule absorbs it.

Consider two hypothetical growth chambers with the same measured conventional photon flux density. Chamber A provides relatively little far-red radiation, while chamber B supplies supplemental far-red without changing the photon flux measured between 400 and 700 nm. The conventional photon flux reading alone would suggest that the two chambers are equivalent. A phytochrome-dependent response could nevertheless differ. The experimenter needs wavelength-resolved information extending beyond the conventional interval in order to interpret the comparison.

Direction is not merely light quantity

A horizontally uniform light field and a one-sided light field may illuminate a plant with the same total amount of radiation while producing distinct spatial patterns of absorption. A directional response requires information that differs across a spatial coordinate or a system that can compare exposure over time as geometry changes. In a simplified shoot, the left and right sides may receive different doses. Differential receptor activation can then initiate spatial differences in regulation. Growth curvature follows when the longitudinal extension of one side exceeds that of the other.

Suppose a shoot segment bends toward a source on its left. The side facing the light becomes the shorter, inner side of the resulting curvature, while the opposite side becomes the longer, outer side. It is therefore incorrect to infer that positive phototropism means that illuminated cells necessarily elongate faster. The visible direction of movement is determined by relative growth across the organ. Other movements, such as reversible changes in cell turgor, can change orientation without requiring the same differential elongation mechanism.

To investigate this spatial logic experimentally, characterize the irradiance at the tissue position, not only at the lamp. Shielding one part of a shoot should not create an unintended temperature gradient or physically restrain growth. A transparent control covering can help distinguish mechanical effects from exclusion of radiation. In a directional growth experiment, a time series of curvature and elongation on both sides is more informative than a final photograph because a loss of curvature may reflect a failure of perception or a failure of growth. The observation that both sides fail to grow at all has a very different interpretation from normal total elongation without directional asymmetry.

4. Absorption spectra and action spectra

An absorption spectrum describes how much radiation a sample absorbs as wavelength changes. For a purified photoreceptor, a spectrophotometer can compare the intensity transmitted through a sample with that through a reference solution. Absorbance is the base-ten logarithm of the incident to transmitted intensity ratio. Under appropriate dilute-solution conditions, the Beer–Lambert relation expresses absorbance as molar absorptivity multiplied by optical path length and molar concentration. The spectrum is a property of the material and its physical and chemical state under the measurement conditions.

An action spectrum instead quantifies the effectiveness of different wavelengths in causing a specified biological response. The experimenter might expose equivalent populations of seeds to narrow-band light, then score germination; or expose seedlings to several wavelengths, then measure the degree of hypocotyl inhibition. At a chosen dose the relative response can be plotted against wavelength. In another approach, the photon dose needed to produce a defined submaximal response is determined at each wavelength; its reciprocal measures relative sensitivity. The method must be specified because the shape of a fixed-dose response curve may depend on saturation and other nonlinearities.

If a molecule absorbs strongly in the same spectral region in which an intact organism responds efficiently, that correspondence provides evidence that the molecule could participate in sensing. It does not alone prove that the molecule is the receptor. Multiple pigments may absorb overlapping wavelengths, tissues may filter incoming light, and more than one receptor can contribute to a common output. At high doses, several weakly absorbed wavelengths may produce a similarly saturated response. Thus a peak in a biological response cannot be interpreted as a direct chemical fingerprint in every circumstance.

A stronger identification strategy combines spectra with genetics and molecular measurements. Imagine that wild-type seedlings show inhibition of hypocotyl elongation under blue light, while a candidate receptor mutant exhibits little inhibition at moderate blue photon flux. If the mutant grows normally in darkness and displays a comparable red-light response, generalized growth impairment is less likely. If a normal copy of the candidate gene restores the blue response, the genetic evidence strengthens. Measuring the purified protein's absorption and light-induced state changes then addresses whether the gene product is itself photosensory or merely a downstream component. A downstream transcription factor can be necessary for the blue response without absorbing blue photons.

The action spectrum of photosynthetic oxygen evolution is also an action spectrum: it measures a defined biological process rather than direct pigment absorption. Its interpretation involves light collection by photosynthetic pigments, energy transfer, electron transport and other constraints. The action spectrum of a light-regulated developmental output involves distinct receptor-dependent processes. Both can contain activity in blue and red regions because multiple pigment systems absorb there. Matching the color of two effects is therefore not enough to establish a shared causal pathway.

Constructing a reliable biological spectrum

A useful action-spectrum experiment begins by defining a response that can be measured quantitatively. For germination, the endpoint might be the proportion of viable seeds with emerged radicles after a specified interval. For a hypocotyl assay, it might be the reduction in mean length relative to a dark control. The choice of endpoint determines which components of plant biology contribute to the measured spectrum. A germination spectrum and a hypocotyl spectrum need not be identical, even when the same receptor family contributes to both, because the tissues and downstream thresholds differ.

The experimenter next selects a series of sufficiently narrow wavelength bands. A light source described as blue may emit radiation across several tens of nanometres; its emission spectrum needs to be measured when accurate wavelength assignment matters. Optical filters may transmit unwanted wavelengths in addition to the desired band. This is especially important if a small contaminating component is highly effective at initiating the response. A strong response to an apparently weakly absorbed wavelength might actually be caused by leakage from another spectral region. Measuring the spectrum at the sample plane reduces this uncertainty.

For each wavelength, a range of photon doses can be delivered to comparable biological samples. A dark control establishes the baseline. At very low doses, the measured output may be indistinguishable from noise or spontaneous response. At very high doses, the physiological output may saturate. The intermediate range often offers the best resolution of relative sensitivity. If two wavelengths both produce near-maximal hypocotyl inhibition at a high dose, that observation gives little information about the dose needed to reach that response. A full dose-response series can reveal that one wavelength is considerably more effective even though their high-dose outcomes are similar.

Suppose that at wavelength A a dose of 8 µmol m⁻² produces a chosen response level, and at wavelength B the required dose is 32 µmol m⁻². If relative effectiveness is defined as the reciprocal dose, A is four times as effective as B in the experiment. Normalizing the effectiveness of A to 1 gives B a value of 0.25. This calculation is valid for the specified response level. If the curves have different shapes, the ratio obtained at a different response level may change. The action spectrum must therefore report how effectiveness was operationally defined.

What a spectrophotometer actually measures

A spectrophotometric absorption measurement is conceptually different from the experiment just described. An optical reference supplies a baseline incident or transmitted intensity. The sample attenuates the light, and the detector estimates transmission. If the transmittance is 0.1, the absorbance is 1; if transmittance is 0.01, absorbance is 2. Absorbance is logarithmic, not a linear fraction of photons absorbed. Under the simple assumption that all attenuation is absorption, absorbance 1 corresponds to 90 percent of incident light being absorbed, while absorbance 2 corresponds to 99 percent. The second sample does not absorb twice the fraction absorbed by the first.

For a pure molecular solution, the Beer–Lambert law can connect absorbance to concentration. A crude extract or intact leaf may scatter light and contain several pigments, which complicates assignment of measured attenuation to one molecule. Changes in absorption can also result from changes in the proportions of different molecular states. If a purified receptor can exist in two photointerconvertible forms, the observed absorption spectrum is a weighted combination of their spectral contributions. Measurements before and after appropriate light treatments provide a way to detect state-dependent changes without relying exclusively on physiological outputs.

The strongest molecular interpretation is obtained when spectral measurements, photochemical state transitions and genetic evidence converge. A spectrum from purified material shows that the molecule can absorb radiation under the test conditions. It does not necessarily show that it is present or functional in the relevant cell at the relevant developmental stage. Conversely, the absence of an organismal response after a mutation may reflect a requirement for the gene in downstream signaling rather than photon capture. An experiment combining gene expression or protein localization with biochemical photochemistry and phenotype rescue can distinguish these alternatives more effectively than any single assay.

5. From chromophore excitation to photoreceptor signaling

Many photoreceptors consist of a protein and a light-absorbing chemical group. The protein without its necessary chromophore is called the apoprotein. The associated, chromophore-containing form is often called the holoprotein. A chromophore has an electronic structure that permits absorption of radiation in particular wavelength regions. Its interaction with the protein environment can shift or modify its spectral properties. This means that a free pigment's spectrum need not be identical to that of the same pigment embedded in a protein.

Absorption excites an electronic state. That excitation may be followed by a change in chemical configuration, electron or proton transfer, formation of a transient bond, or a change in protein conformation. The mechanisms differ among receptor families. The critical sensory event is that absorption influences the distribution or lifetime of molecular states that can interact differently with signaling partners. The excited electronic state itself is often much shorter lived than the subsequent signaling state.

In higher plants, phytochromes are the principal red and far-red sensory receptors and contain a linear tetrapyrrole chromophore, phytochromobilin in seed plants. Cryptochromes sense blue and near-ultraviolet light and contain flavin adenine dinucleotide. Phototropins possess flavin mononucleotide-binding LOV domains coupled to a protein kinase. Although cryptochromes and phototropins respond to overlapping wavelengths, their protein structures and downstream functions differ. A blue photon does not specify which family was excited; absorption probabilities, receptor abundance, cellular location and the dynamics of the two pathways determine the output.

The ultraviolet-B receptor UVR8 illustrates an important exception to the expectation of a separate small-molecule chromophore. Specific tryptophan residues of its protein contribute to UV-B perception. Its inclusion here is limited to recognizing the diversity of plant light sensors. Plant members of the ZEITLUPE family also bind flavin and contribute to light-dependent timing processes. These examples show that the three families central to this subunit are not an exhaustive list of every plant photoreceptor.

A receptor must be distinguished from its downstream target. A kinase can transfer a phosphate group to another protein; a transcription regulator can change expression of a gene; an ion channel can modify membrane transport. Some light receptors have intrinsic enzyme activity, as phototropins do, while others primarily influence interactions with signaling proteins. A protein involved in light-regulated transcription is not necessarily a photoreceptor. Conversely, a photoreceptor need not bind DNA directly to alter gene expression. These distinctions become experimentally useful when a mutant has normal photochemistry but defective physiological output.

A minimal vocabulary of molecular states

A protein's conformation refers to the three-dimensional arrangement of its atoms. It can exist in more than one energetically accessible state, and the relative abundance of those states may change when the protein interacts with a ligand or undergoes a chemical modification. A photoreceptor uses light to alter this molecular state distribution. The change may involve a chromophore's geometry, the strength of an interaction within the protein, or the accessibility of a region that binds another protein. The changed molecular configuration can thus influence the rest of a signaling pathway even after the initial electronic excitation has ended.

Do not imagine the receptor as a permanent switch that remains activated indefinitely once it absorbs a photon. Many photoreceptors have reversible or transient signaling states. Reversal may occur through light absorption by a different state, through thermal reactions, through protein turnover, or through combinations of these processes. In some circumstances the light-induced protein state becomes less abundant because signaling-active molecules are degraded or because interacting proteins change their localization. This introduces a memory of recent illumination whose duration depends on the molecular system.

The concept of specificity also needs careful treatment. A chromophore may absorb a broad range of wavelengths rather than one precise color. Wavelength ranges associated with receptor families represent regions of major biological sensitivity. There can be overlap and responses outside the most prominent region. A receptor assigned to blue-light sensing is not thereby proven to have zero absorption at every longer or shorter wavelength. When predicting an experiment, the relevant properties are the spectral distribution of the source, the receptor absorption and photochemistry, and the sensitivity of the measured pathway.

Distinguishing the three core receptor families

The core families in this subunit illustrate three ways in which light perception becomes regulation. A phytochrome contains a bilin whose light-induced changes couple to the protein's signaling state. Red and far-red radiation can shift the balance between states. Cryptochromes contain a flavin chromophore and use blue-light-dependent changes in protein state and interactions to influence regulatory processes. Phototropins contain flavin-binding LOV domains coupled to kinase activity, providing a mechanism that connects light-dependent changes directly to phosphorylation. These introductory descriptions identify the common principles without replacing the detailed molecular pathways of the next three nodes.

A comparative experiment could express the three receptor proteins in suitable biochemical preparations and measure their spectral properties. This would establish whether each preparation absorbs the expected wavelengths. A different comparison could test hypocotyl growth, directional curvature and chloroplast positioning in a series of receptor mutants. The second experiment addresses physiological contribution rather than directly measuring photochemistry. The two approaches together help determine which receptor's physical sensing properties are relevant to a particular biological output.

Multiple receptors may influence one developmental trait. For example, hypocotyl elongation integrates responses to more than one part of the spectrum. A mutant lacking one receptor may therefore retain substantial light inhibition of growth when other receptor systems remain functional. This does not imply that the missing receptor has no role. The observed phenotype depends on the illumination used, on receptor abundance, and on how downstream pathways overlap. Under broad-spectrum light, multiple pathways may compensate for one another more readily than under a carefully selected narrow-band treatment.

From receptor to amplified output

When a receptor changes the activity of a signaling enzyme, one activated enzyme molecule can modify more than one substrate molecule during the lifetime of its active state. Likewise, regulation of transcription can alter the production of many RNA molecules. Such processes allow a small change in the initiating sensory signal to produce a larger downstream change. Amplification is not the same as amplification of photon energy: biochemical work is powered by cellular energy stores and metabolism. It is the information represented by the receptor state that is propagated through a network.

A pathway can also contain negative feedback or opposing signals. An increase in light-dependent activation may be counteracted by the accumulation of inhibitory components. A hormone signal may alter the sensitivity of the tissue to the photoreceptor-controlled pathway. The observed response may consequently show a threshold, a delay or a plateau. Without knowledge of these intermediate processes, the magnitude of a physiological output cannot be converted directly into the number of active photoreceptors.

6. Etiolation and de-etiolation as measurable development

Photomorphogenesis refers to the regulation of plant development by light. Skotomorphogenesis describes the developmental program characteristic of dark-grown seedlings in relevant species. In young Arabidopsis plants, the hypocotyl is the region between the root and cotyledons. Cotyledons are embryonic leaves, and the apical hook protects the emerging shoot apex as the seedling grows through soil. Under continuous darkness the hypocotyl typically extends, the hook remains bent and cotyledons do not expand fully. Etioplasts in appropriate dark-grown angiosperm tissues are plastids prepared for a subsequent transition to chloroplast development.

Exposure to light leads to de-etiolation. The seedling reorganizes the relative activities of cell elongation, organ expansion and plastid differentiation. The timing of these changes is not identical. Receptor photoconversion occurs before appreciable morphological change can be observed. Changes in gene expression can precede accumulation of proteins required for chloroplast development, while opening of the hook and expansion of cotyledons occur over longer intervals. A single snapshot cannot resolve the sequence of these events.

Dark growth is an active biological program. In Arabidopsis, the COP1-containing ubiquitin ligase system and phytochrome-interacting factors contribute to repressing or counteracting light-promoted developmental outputs in darkness. Upon illumination, sensory pathways modify the activities or abundance of regulatory proteins. The details differ across receptors and are developed in later chapters. At this stage it is enough to distinguish the repression of a light response in darkness from the absence of any developmental regulation.

A constitutively photomorphogenic mutant can display aspects of light-grown seedling development even in darkness. This phenotype does not demonstrate that the mutant is generating light or operating photosystems in the dark. It suggests that a regulatory restraint has been altered so that parts of the light-development program can proceed without their usual environmental trigger. Chlorophyll accumulation and functional photosynthetic machinery may still have light-dependent requirements. The visible phenotype should therefore be decomposed into separate measured traits rather than labeled simply as light-grown or dark-grown.

A reliable assay measures hypocotyl lengths in seedlings of matching age and genotype under defined conditions. Seed position, seed batch, medium composition, temperature and germination timing can introduce variation. Measuring only the largest seedling is biased. Independent biological replicates and a specified sampling scheme make the comparison more interpretable. Because final length incorporates growth over time, time-lapse measurements can additionally separate an early change in growth rate from a late developmental consequence.

Developmental allocation in darkness and light

An angiosperm seedling emerging from soil must allocate finite reserves among different growing tissues. Before emergence, extensive expansion of photosynthetic leaf surfaces offers little immediate advantage because light is limited. A developmental strategy favoring extension of the emerging shoot may increase the probability of reaching an illuminated environment. This is one reason the dark-grown phenotype has biological significance rather than representing a simple laboratory abnormality. It is important not to treat this explanation as a guarantee of survival: seed reserve size, depth of burial, species-specific morphology and soil conditions determine whether a particular seedling can successfully emerge.

The apical hook in many dicotyledonous seedlings illustrates regional control of growth. A curved structure helps protect delicate emerging tissues as the seedling passes through soil. Hook formation and maintenance depend on unequal growth across its sides. When illumination signals emergence, the regulation of differential growth changes and the hook opens. The transition is integrated with hormonal control and mechanical conditions. A dark-grown seedling with a defective hook may have a defect in a growth-regulating process rather than a defect in light perception itself.

Cotyledon expansion has a different relationship to growth than hypocotyl extension. Under light, cotyledons commonly expand while rapid extension of the hypocotyl is inhibited. The statement that light inhibits seedling growth is therefore misleading unless the organ and growth measure are specified. A light-grown seedling may have a shorter hypocotyl but larger cotyledons and eventually more photosynthetic capacity. The relative allocation of expansion has changed. Measuring only total seedling mass or only one organ's length cannot capture this entire developmental transition.

Plastid differentiation and the visible phenotype

Etioplasts contain membrane structures and biochemical components that can support transition toward chloroplast development when appropriate light becomes available. Chloroplasts subsequently develop organized photosynthetic membranes and photosynthetic pigment systems. In many dark-grown angiosperms, a light-requiring step in chlorophyll biosynthesis limits normal greening. Other plant lineages can differ in their capacity for dark chlorophyll synthesis, so the common angiosperm seedling description should not be elevated to an absolute rule for all photosynthetic organisms.

Light-induced greening involves both direct biochemical requirements and changes in gene regulation. Photoreceptor signaling can increase expression of genes contributing to chloroplast development, but transcriptional induction does not automatically guarantee that every required biochemical conversion can occur in darkness. A constitutive developmental mutant can open cotyledons without normal greening. This distinction matters because green color is a convenient visual endpoint and can tempt students to treat it as a complete measure of photomorphogenesis.

A careful phenotyping scheme separates at least four endpoints: hypocotyl length, hook angle, cotyledon expansion and chlorophyll accumulation. A mutant might be abnormal in one and normal in others. Such a pattern indicates that different parts of the developmental response have been affected. It provides a basis for more targeted experiments. If a mutant is normal in darkness but fails to inhibit hypocotyl growth in blue light, the next question is whether other blue outputs are similarly impaired. If chloroplast movement is intact, the defect may affect only some receptor pathways or downstream processes. A common wavelength does not require a common mechanism for every output.

How to interpret seedling length

Final hypocotyl length is the accumulated result of growth rates over an interval. If two seedlings are both 5 mm at the end of an experiment, they may nonetheless have experienced different growth dynamics. One could have grown slowly throughout, and the other could have initially elongated rapidly but stopped after illumination. Time-lapse imaging allows growth rates to be estimated from length changes over known intervals. It can reveal whether a treatment changes the onset, duration or magnitude of growth inhibition.

In a hypothetical experiment, seedlings have equal initial lengths of 2 mm. A dark control reaches 8 mm after 12 hours, while a light-treated group reaches 5 mm. Mean extension during the interval is 6 mm in darkness and 3 mm in light. The reduction in extension is 50 percent, whereas the reduction calculated using final lengths is 37.5 percent. Neither computation is inherently wrong, but they describe different quantities. The experimenter must specify whether the endpoint is final organ size or growth during the observation interval. Questions that present initial as well as final measurements may require the second calculation.

7. Fluence, rate dependence and temporal integration

Photon fluence is the time integral of photon flux density. When the flux density is constant and the measurement geometry is fixed, the incident dose is simply flux density multiplied by duration. A beam providing 20 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for five seconds supplies 100 µmol m⁻². Another providing 2 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for fifty seconds supplies the same dose. Both treatments might produce the same response if the relevant process integrates photon arrivals with negligible loss of signaling state over the exposure.

This approximate equivalence is called reciprocity. It is an empirical behavior observed under particular conditions, not a universal law of plant physiology. A receptor may be converted to a signaling state by light and may also return toward a different state through thermal or light-driven processes. In a short exposure, reversal or relaxation can be negligible. During a much longer exposure, these opposing reactions can become important. Equal incident photon numbers can then yield different final receptor-state distributions.

An elementary mathematical model illustrates this distinction. Let x be the fraction of a hypothetical receptor in a signaling state. Suppose illumination produces activation at a rate proportional to photon flux I and the inactive fraction (1 − x), with proportionality constant k. Suppose the activated state also decays in darkness and light at first-order rate d. The rate of change of x is kI(1 − x) − dx. At constant I, the stationary fraction in this simplified model is kI divided by (kI + d). If d is negligible during exposure, the solution depends on the product I times time for a receptor initially inactive. If d is appreciable, the time course and steady state depend on I separately. This hypothetical model is not a full molecular description of phytochrome photochemistry; in particular it omits wavelength-dependent reverse photoconversion.

Low-fluence responses in classical phytochrome experiments can often be induced by a short red exposure and antagonized by subsequent far-red light. Very-low-fluence responses may be triggered by much smaller activation fractions and may not be reversed by ordinary far-red treatment. High-irradiance responses characteristically depend on prolonged illumination and fluence rate. These are categories of response behavior, not names for three different pigments. Distinct members of the phytochrome family contribute differently to those behaviors, and experimental thresholds vary with the organism and trait.

A threshold inferred from an assay is the dose at which a defined response can be reliably detected under the chosen conditions. It is not necessarily the minimum dose capable of changing any receptor molecule. A downstream output may require sufficient signal accumulation, and measurement noise obscures small effects. Likewise, a plateau in a response-dose plot does not prove that every receptor molecule is activated. The physiological output, rather than receptor occupancy, may have reached its limit.

Deriving the simplified activation law

The elementary model introduced earlier is useful for understanding why photon dose can sometimes be sufficient to predict a response. Let x range from zero to one, and suppose that the total number of receptor molecules is constant. The inactive fraction is then 1 − x. If illumination activates inactive molecules at a probability per unit time proportional to I, the activation contribution to the rate of change of x is kI(1 − x). The quantity kI has units of inverse time. A decay process acting independently on each active molecule contributes −dx, where d also has units of inverse time.

Rearranging gives dx/dt = kI − (kI + d)x. With constant I, this is a linear first-order differential equation. The stationary fraction is obtained by setting the derivative to zero. The difference between x and its stationary value decays exponentially at rate kI + d. Thus, if the initial active fraction is x₀, the fraction after time t is x_ss + (x₀ − x_ss) exp[−(kI + d)t]. The model predicts faster approach toward the stationary state at larger activation rates, even though the stationary value itself also changes. It is a teaching model for reversible state populations, not an exact description of the full phytochrome system.

When d is zero and the preparation starts with x₀ = 0, the equation simplifies to x = 1 − exp(−kIt). The exponent is a function of fluence It. Under these assumptions, two treatments giving the same dose produce the same receptor-state fraction. For small kIt the exponential can be approximated by 1 − kIt, giving x approximately kIt. In this limited low-dose range, activation is approximately proportional to dose. As the inactive fraction is depleted, activation becomes nonlinear and ultimately approaches one. Saturation of the molecular state can thus arise even without any downstream saturation.

Now consider nonzero decay. In a hypothetical system, suppose kI = 1 s⁻¹ for one treatment and 0.1 s⁻¹ for another. Let d = 0.1 s⁻¹ in both cases. The corresponding stationary fractions are 1/1.1, approximately 0.91, and 0.1/0.2, exactly 0.50. If the high-rate exposure lasts 1 s and the low-rate exposure lasts 10 s, the two activation-rate–time products are equal. Starting from x = 0, the high-rate treatment gives approximately 0.91 × (1 − e⁻¹·¹) = 0.61, whereas the low-rate treatment gives 0.50 × (1 − e⁻²) = 0.43. Thus equal activation opportunities integrated over time need not give equal final fractions when relaxation occurs throughout exposure.

Pulses, adaptation and experimental boundaries

Two brief pulses separated by darkness are not necessarily equivalent to one continuous exposure with the same total fluence. In the simple model, x decays during darkness at rate d. A long gap can therefore remove much of the state produced by the first pulse before the second pulse arrives. If the downstream response itself integrates receptor activation over time, the prediction becomes more complicated: two treatments that give the same final receptor fraction may have had different integrated activities. The measured biological endpoint determines which feature of the time course is most important.

A high-irradiance response should not be interpreted merely as a low-fluence response to which more photons were added. Some physiological responses require sustained illumination and depend strongly on the rate and spectral composition of the input. In classical phytochrome photobiology, the response categories were distinguished experimentally by dose and exposure behavior. A strong response under sustained far-red illumination does not contradict far-red antagonism in an appropriate low-fluence red/far-red pulse experiment. The receptor family members, molecular cycles and downstream programs involved differ between the cases. Later treatment of phytochrome A and phytochrome B provides the molecular basis for this distinction.

An exam problem may present a table of responses under equal dose and different rates. Before deciding whether reciprocity has failed, check whether duration, intensity and spectral quality were actually measured, whether the seedlings had equivalent developmental states and whether the response was observed at comparable times. If the experiments differ in temperature or in the interval between treatment and measurement, those factors could produce unequal responses without an intrinsic failure of receptor-level reciprocity. A well-designed experiment varies one physical factor while holding relevant alternatives constant.

8. How experiments established sensory signaling

In studies reported in The Power of Movement in Plants in 1880, Charles Darwin and Francis Darwin investigated directional responses of grass seedlings. Shielding particular regions of the coleoptile, the protective sheath surrounding a young grass shoot, allowed the experimenters to distinguish the site of light sensitivity from the site of bending. Shielding the tip interfered with curvature, whereas the exposed lower region could still elongate. Comparisons with suitable uncovered and differently shielded seedlings supported the inference that a signal from an illuminated sensory region influenced growth elsewhere.

The significance of these experiments is spatial causation. If the lower region bends because light directly changes its local growth independently of the tip, preventing light from reaching the tip should not eliminate the response while leaving lower tissues illuminated. A tip-dependent response instead suggests communication between regions. The experiments did not identify the modern molecular photoreceptor or the complete hormonal mechanism; those questions required subsequent physiological and biochemical investigations. It would be anachronistic to say that the Darwins demonstrated a particular phototropin gene.

A second landmark experiment concerns lettuce seed germination. Borthwick and colleagues reported in 1952 that different sequences of red and far-red illumination could reversibly change the germination response. In the classical light-sensitive system, red exposure promoted germination, and a subsequent far-red treatment could reverse that promotion. Another red exposure could restore promotion. The outcome was not explained simply by adding the radiant energy of all pulses together: an additional pulse could antagonize the effect of an earlier one.

This result supports a light-sensitive regulatory system capable of occupying interconvertible states. The names Pr and Pfr later became associated with phytochrome forms preferentially absorbing red and far-red radiation respectively. Red light generally drives net conversion toward Pfr in classical low-fluence experiments; far-red favors the opposite direction. These states are not perfectly wavelength-exclusive, because their absorption spectra overlap. Under sustained light a mixture of states is established, and dark reactions can change it afterward.

To design a photoreversal test, divide a uniform seed batch among darkness, red, far-red, red followed by far-red, and red followed by far-red followed by red. Hold temperature, water availability, treatment duration where relevant and post-treatment conditions constant. Compare replicate germination percentages after a common interval. The simple last-effective-pulse prediction applies to an appropriate low-fluence reversible response when exposures establish sufficiently different receptor states before the downstream decision becomes irreversible. Seeds that have committed to germination need not be reversed by a later pulse. Some species or physiological states have different light requirements, and very-low-fluence or high-irradiance responses do not necessarily follow this simple pattern.

Why the classical experiments were decisive

A historical experiment is useful not merely because it introduced a name but because it narrowed the range of plausible mechanisms. In the phototropism studies, one possible explanation for bending was that light directly stimulated or inhibited elongation wherever it struck the growing organ. Another possibility was that a specialized region detected the light and communicated with other regions. The shielding experiments were capable of distinguishing these explanations because the physical input could be delivered to the presumed growth region while withholding it from the tip. The dependence of the directional response on the tip was therefore evidence against a purely local interpretation of growth in that experimental preparation.

The details of a control are crucial. Cutting away a tip would remove light-sensitive cells, but it could also remove tissue required for normal growth, cause a wound response or alter the supply of growth-regulating substances. Shielding the tip while leaving it attached reduces these alternative explanations. A covering that permits light transmission but otherwise has comparable mechanical properties would further help distinguish exclusion of light from a physical obstruction. The goal of such controls is not to show that light is the only influence on growth; it is to establish whether the particular difference in directional response is attributable to the manipulated light input.

The Darwins' conclusion that the upper region can influence the lower region anticipated an important principle of plant signaling: the site of perception and the site of response need not coincide. The distance involved can be small, as in the different regions of a coleoptile, or extend between organs. Later experiments on the movement of growth regulators established additional links between these spatially separated events. The earlier results alone do not determine the chemical identity of the mobile influence. This distinction illustrates why a historical result must be interpreted in the context of what was actually measured, rather than described as if the modern molecular pathway had already been discovered.

The lettuce experiments addressed a different biological question. Instead of determining the location of reception, they examined whether wavelength and the sequence of exposures could control a developmental decision. If the benefit of the first light exposure depended simply on energy accumulation, an additional light exposure would ordinarily be expected to add energy, not to specifically undo its effect. A reversal under a different wavelength supplied evidence that the wavelengths had opposing influences on a regulatory state. It was also important that the response could be restored by another exposure of the original spectral quality. A single treatment that inhibited germination might have represented toxicity; alternating promotion and antagonism made a reversible regulatory interpretation more persuasive.

The classical result is often abbreviated as a rule about the final light treatment. This is an experimental pattern, not a universal definition of phytochrome. The first pulse changes the molecular state distribution. The second pulse changes it in the opposite direction. The germination output reflects that distribution and the sensitivity of the downstream developmental program, provided the system has not already passed an irreversible decision. If the exposures are very weak, the successive treatments may not shift the receptor-state distribution sufficiently to give the familiar pattern. If the post-treatment interval is long, thermal relaxation and signaling events can also change the prediction.

Interpreting a reversal dataset without overstating it

Imagine an illustrative classroom experiment in which six groups of one hundred viable seeds each are tested. The dark control has 12 germinating seeds; a red-treated group has 82; a far-red-treated group has 14; red followed by far-red gives 16; red followed by far-red followed by red gives 79; and red followed by far-red followed by red followed by far-red gives 15. These are hypothetical observations constructed to illustrate the expected pattern, not numerical values reproduced from Borthwick's paper. The groups ending with red have relatively high germination and those ending with far-red resemble the low dark baseline.

It would be tempting to subtract every value from the red treatment and interpret the difference as the number of receptor molecules returned to Pr. That would be incorrect. Germination is counted as a discrete output for each seed, and each individual seed may have its own sensitivity, physiological state and threshold. A fraction of germinating seeds is not the fraction of activated receptor molecules in the entire group. A change in the distribution of receptor states could move many seeds across a developmental threshold without causing an equally large proportional change in the mean receptor state.

The table would also need true biological replication. A hundred seeds in one shared dish do not necessarily constitute a hundred independent experimental units because they share microenvironment, handling and treatment. If each treatment is represented by a single dish, any unexpected difference between dishes can be mistaken for a light effect. Independent dishes across randomized experimental blocks strengthen inference. Reporting the mean response and its variation across independent replicates distinguishes a reproducible treatment effect from an apparently striking difference that occurred only once.

A related issue is seed viability. Failure to germinate under a particular light treatment does not necessarily mean the seed is dead. It may remain dormant or may lack another condition required for germination. A viability check or subsequent permissive treatment can help distinguish suppression of a developmental transition from loss of viability. If far-red treatment specifically alters a reversible sensory state, appropriately timed red illumination can restore promotion in a suitable system. If the seeds were physically damaged, recovery under red light would not ordinarily be expected.

Connecting opposing wavelength effects to state populations

A two-state representation of the phytochrome system helps explain why the spectral effects can oppose one another. Let a population contain molecules in states A and B. A particular light spectrum may drive A toward B at an effective rate a, and B toward A at rate b. Both rates can be nonzero under the same spectrum because the absorption bands of the two states overlap. If the total receptor population remains constant, the steady state is determined by the balance between conversion in the two directions.

The ratio of the state populations is not simply the ratio of incident red and far-red photon counts. Each conversion rate depends on absorption by the initial state and the probability that absorption produces the relevant conversion. Broad-spectrum illumination additionally contributes across many wavelengths. A red-rich treatment can favor the Pfr form because its net photoconversion properties differ from those of a far-red-rich treatment. The terms red-absorbing and far-red-absorbing identify the preferential spectral properties of the two forms, not exclusive absorption bands.

If a simple model has forward rate a = 3 arbitrary inverse-time units and backward rate b = 1 in the same units, the stationary fraction in B is a/(a+b), or 0.75. Under a second spectrum with a = 1 and b = 4, it is 0.20. These are illustrative effective rates, not measured phytochrome constants. The calculation shows why a sequence of sufficiently long treatments can move the state population toward different values. It does not imply that the fraction of seeds germinating under those treatments must be exactly 75 and 20 percent. A downstream nonlinear response separates receptor state from biological outcome.

When darkness intervenes, photoconversion due to incident radiation ceases, but that does not guarantee the state distribution is frozen. Thermal relaxation, protein turnover and downstream signaling can continue. The simple last-pulse logic is therefore most informative when the treatment and observation intervals have been defined in relation to the relevant response. The full phytochrome mechanism, including the biological differences between family members, is the subject of the next chapter rather than a prerequisite that must be memorized here.

Comparing two kinds of historical evidence

The tip-shielding and pulse-reversal experiments share a methodological principle despite addressing different biological phenomena. In each case the light input was manipulated in a way that produced different predictions for competing mechanisms. Tip shielding changes the location of reception without necessarily preventing the growth region from receiving light. Red and far-red alternation changes the spectral history without requiring an interpretation based solely on cumulative photon energy. The outcome constrains the explanation because the experiment was designed around a contrast between hypotheses.

These are examples of how physiological evidence can guide molecular discovery. Neither type of experiment needs a complete genome sequence to establish a meaningful mechanistic principle. At the same time, neither type can by itself identify a specific receptor gene or every downstream protein. Modern genetics and spectroscopy can test molecular candidates that satisfy the physiological constraints. A student evaluating an examination question should therefore distinguish what the original observations support from what was established by subsequent work. The distinction avoids assigning evidence to the wrong historical experiment and improves causal reasoning in unfamiliar datasets.

9. Connecting receptor inputs to plant physiology

A plant uses spectral information to modify many different outputs. In a vegetated habitat a reduced red to far-red ratio can indicate nearby leaves. An elongation response may improve access to future light, but it also consumes resources and can compromise other functions. Shade avoidance is therefore an environmentally conditioned adjustment rather than an invariant response to reduced photosynthetic rate. Shade-tolerant plants may respond less strongly, and the responses of individual organs are not identical. An experiment comparing neutral-density shade with a treatment selectively enriched in far-red light helps distinguish quantity-dependent effects from changes in spectral composition.

Directional growth provides another type of output. When light is delivered from one side, phototropin-mediated sensing can initiate an unequal growth response across a shoot. An organ can bend without the illuminated side being the side that elongates most. For positive shoot phototropism, the differential growth pattern normally makes the shoot curve toward the source. Subsequent lessons examine the auxin redistribution and cell-wall response underlying this change. The distinction between reception and growth response is more important here than memorizing the direction of every intermediate transport step.

Chloroplast movement is an intracellular light-regulated behavior. Under relatively weak light, chloroplast accumulation at positions favoring interception can improve light capture; under strong blue light, avoidance movements can reduce exposure. These responses occur on different spatial scales from growth of an entire shoot. A chloroplast that repositions within a cell need not imply that the whole leaf has changed its angle. The receptor-controlled repositioning is also distinct from a photosystem using absorbed energy for electron transport.

Light can regulate the stomatal aperture, affecting gas exchange between a leaf and the atmosphere. Blue-light sensing by guard cells and effects associated with photosynthetic activity can both contribute to opening. Their relative importance changes with experimental conditions. Water stress, carbon dioxide concentration, internal timing and hormones can override or modify light-dependent effects. Thus the presence of a blue-light response does not imply that all stomata must open whenever blue light is present.

Light acts as a timing signal in addition to regulating immediate responses. A light-dark cycle can synchronize an endogenous circadian oscillator. The oscillator can persist with an approximately daily period in constant conditions, whereas a response that merely follows the imposed alternation may disappear when the alternation is removed. Photoperiodic development, including flowering in many species, depends on the relationship between illumination and internal timing. A pulse during the dark interval can be biologically important even though its contribution to daily carbon assimilation is small. These temporal examples complete the distinction between light as information and light as an energy resource.

Shade avoidance as a prediction rather than a simple response to darkness

In a developing stand of plants, the light reaching a seedling can change before its photosynthetic apparatus experiences a large decrease in total useful radiation. Radiation reflected from nearby leaves can modify the red to far-red ratio. A seedling capable of detecting this change can alter growth in anticipation of future competition. The phenotype often includes elongation of stems or petioles, depending on species and developmental stage, and may include changes in leaf orientation and flowering time. These traits are collectively associated with shade avoidance, but not every species exhibits all of them.

A prediction that elongation will improve access to light has limitations. Elongated structures require carbon and nutrients, can alter mechanical stability and may compete with allocation to roots or storage tissues. In a habitat where neighboring vegetation is persistent and escape through elongation is unlikely, a different strategy may be more successful. Shade-tolerant plants are not necessarily insensitive to light; they may use different response thresholds or allocate growth differently. This ecological perspective explains why the magnitude of a shade response should not be treated as a universal measure of receptor abundance.

A simple experiment can use a high and a low red to far-red ratio at each of two photon flux levels, with growth measured in the same genotype. For example, a hypothetical result might show short hypocotyls at high ratio and high flux, longer hypocotyls at low ratio and high flux, intermediate lengths at high ratio and low flux, and the longest hypocotyls at low ratio and low flux. This pattern would suggest that spectral composition and photon availability both contribute. It would not establish that the mechanisms are independent at the molecular level, because they could converge on the same growth regulator.

To test the receptor contribution, compare the treatment effects in a phytochrome mutant and a reference genotype. A reduced difference between spectral treatments in the mutant would support a role for the affected receptor in that response. A residual effect would not necessarily invalidate the role: multiple phytochromes and other sensory pathways can contribute to canopy responses. A mutant may also have a long baseline hypocotyl under the high-ratio treatment, limiting the observable additional elongation under low ratio. This ceiling effect needs to be considered before concluding that the plant is completely unable to detect spectral change.

Directional growth and spatially separated signaling

The bending of a shoot toward a source is a problem of spatial coordination. If every cell along both sides of the shoot elongates by the same proportion, the organ becomes longer without bending in a preferred direction. Curvature requires a difference in longitudinal growth across the organ. A directional light field supplies a potential source of asymmetry, but the plant must translate that asymmetry into a spatial difference in growth regulation.

Phototropins are important blue-light receptors in this process. Their photochemistry occurs in individual proteins, while the response involves multiple cells and tissues. The initial difference in sensory activity can influence the distribution of signaling components and growth regulators. Auxin-dependent differential growth is a central downstream mechanism in well-studied shoot phototropism systems. The key introductory principle is that the receptor does not mechanically pull the organ toward the lamp. Instead it initiates regulatory changes that alter the relative expansion of the two sides.

A simplified geometric example illustrates the relation between growth and curvature. Consider two sides of a short shoot segment initially equal in length. After exposure, suppose the inner side extends by 1 mm while the opposite side extends by 2 mm. The unequal final lengths are compatible with curvature toward the shorter side. The precise angle depends on organ geometry, tissue properties and the distribution of growth along the segment. It cannot be calculated from the two extension values alone without additional information. This is a useful reminder that knowing the direction of the growth difference does not automatically determine the magnitude of the final curvature.

To interpret a mutant with weak phototropism, first ask whether the shoot can elongate. A mutant that cannot grow under the experimental conditions cannot execute normal growth-mediated curvature even if its sensory machinery is intact. If total elongation is normal but the difference between sides is absent, a failure of asymmetric regulation becomes a stronger possibility. If an experimentally imposed growth-regulator asymmetry restores bending, the defect may lie before the execution of the growth response. If the tissue fails to bend even when the asymmetry is imposed, a downstream growth defect may be involved. These are conditional predictions, not a universal diagnostic for every species.

Intracellular movement and a change in the useful light environment

Chloroplast repositioning provides an example in which the responding structure is an organelle rather than a whole growing organ. In cells capable of movement, chloroplasts can occupy different regions depending on the illumination. Accumulation in positions favorable for interception under relatively weak light can increase the opportunity for light absorption, while avoidance under sufficiently strong blue light can reduce exposure. The same broad sensory class can therefore contribute to qualitatively different responses depending on stimulus strength and receptor composition.

A student should distinguish chloroplast movement from changes in chloroplast number, pigment synthesis and photosynthetic electron transport. If a microscope image shows a different distribution of chloroplasts after a light treatment, movement of existing organelles is one possible explanation. A time series tracking the same cell is much stronger evidence for movement than images of different cells at unrelated times. If pigment content changes but organelle position does not, the process is different. If oxygen evolution changes without observable movement, photosynthetic regulation may have changed independently of organelle repositioning.

The relationship between movement and photosynthetic performance illustrates sensory–metabolic integration. A receptor-mediated movement can alter the probability that photons reach photosynthetic pigments, thereby affecting energy capture. The sensory process is not identical to the photosynthetic process; it modifies the conditions under which photosynthesis occurs. An analogy is changing the angle of a solar panel relative to the sun. The mechanism is very different in a plant because a living cell uses biochemical regulation and intracellular structures rather than a mechanical motor controlled by an external device. The analogy only illustrates how positioning can influence interception.

Stomata integrate several meanings of illumination

A stoma is a pore bounded by guard cells. Its aperture influences the exchange of carbon dioxide and water vapor between the leaf interior and the atmosphere. Illumination can signal that the photosynthetic tissues will need carbon dioxide. Light can also activate guard-cell sensory pathways that modify ion transport and cell turgor. Opening of stomata therefore integrates a metabolic context and direct sensory regulation, rather than following a single universal switch.

A useful physiological comparison holds red background illumination constant and adds a relatively small blue-light component. The red background helps establish a photosynthetic context, while the additional blue light can reveal a blue-sensitive opening response. Such an experiment still requires careful interpretation because adding blue photons changes the total spectral input and may influence photosynthesis as well. Additional controls, such as appropriate photon-dose comparisons, carbon dioxide monitoring and receptor-mutant comparisons, help assign the contribution of direct blue-light sensing.

Guard-cell opening depends on the ability of cells to change their osmotic state and take up water. A light signal cannot produce the normal opening response if essential transport processes are unavailable or if counteracting signals dominate. Under water-limited conditions, a plant may conserve water despite illumination. The net response is the result of multiple regulatory influences, and a receptor may be activated even when the pore does not visibly open. In a mechanistic experiment, measuring an early guard-cell signaling response separately from aperture can help distinguish failure of sensory perception from inhibition of the final physiological response.

Not all species use identical stomatal schedules. Plants with crassulacean acid metabolism can have substantial nocturnal carbon dioxide uptake, illustrating how metabolic strategy and timing alter the relation between external light and gas exchange. Even among plants that commonly open stomata during the day, drought, internal carbon dioxide concentration and the circadian system can modify the response. The detailed ion-transport and turgor mechanism belongs to Node 6.E.05, while the water-potential framework belongs to Subunit 6.F.

Day length, clock phase and the distinction between a cue and a timer

A daily change in illumination provides a predictable environmental pattern. Plants can respond immediately to the onset or removal of light, but they can also possess an endogenous system that continues to oscillate when the environment becomes constant. This internal oscillator allows biological processes to be organized relative to anticipated environmental changes. Light can adjust the oscillator's phase without being the source of every subsequent oscillation.

Imagine that a leaf moves upward every morning and downward every evening under a twelve-hour light and twelve-hour dark schedule. These observations alone cannot distinguish an endogenous clock from a direct response to the imposed schedule. To investigate endogenous timing, transfer comparable plants to suitable constant conditions and continue measuring leaf movement. Persistence of near-daily cycles for several periods would support an internal oscillator. Immediate disappearance of cycling would be more compatible with direct environmental driving, although the conditions might themselves suppress the observable output. It is therefore important to use a suitable constant environment and to interpret the behavior of the particular trait being measured.

Period is the duration of one cycle; phase is the position within the cycle relative to a reference time; amplitude describes the magnitude of the oscillation. A light pulse can shift phase without permanently changing the intrinsic period. For example, two groups may both show approximately daily cycling but differ in the time at which the maximum occurs after an appropriately timed pulse. If phase shifts depend on the phase at which the pulse is delivered, light is interacting with a temporally organized system rather than simply increasing the energy available for movement.

Photoperiodism concerns responses to the duration and temporal arrangement of illumination and darkness. In many flowering plants, internal timing determines whether a light-dependent signal coincides with a permissive phase. Two treatments with the same daily total photon dose may differ in their effects on flowering because their light intervals occur at different times. A short interruption of the night can therefore alter a photoperiodic response with little contribution to daily energy assimilation. The details of circadian circuitry and photoperiodic flowering are reserved for Nodes 6.E.06 and 6.E.07. The present foundation is the distinction between stimulus timing, endogenous timing and the output regulated by their interaction.

10. Experimental reasoning and causal identification

Arabidopsis thaliana is a useful genetic model because researchers can compare identified mutants with a reference genotype and test the effect of restoring particular genes. A simple factorial experiment can compare a wild type, a candidate photoreceptor mutant and a complemented line under darkness, blue light and red light. The primary response might be mean hypocotyl length at a fixed seedling age. Darkness supplies a baseline for each genotype. The red treatment tests whether the defect is limited to the blue-responsive range. Complementation tests whether introducing a functional copy can rescue the phenotype.

For an illustrative dataset, suppose wild-type seedlings have mean hypocotyl lengths of 12 mm in darkness, 5 mm under blue illumination and 6 mm under red illumination. A mutant has corresponding means of 12, 10 and 6 mm. The complemented line has means of 12, 5.5 and 6 mm. Relative blue-light inhibition for the wild type is (12 − 5)/12 × 100, approximately 58.3 percent; for the mutant it is approximately 16.7 percent; for the complemented line approximately 54.2 percent. These invented values illustrate interpretation, not results from an actual experiment. The results suggest impairment of the blue-dependent growth response and rescue by the gene, but the absence of dispersion estimates prevents statistical inference. They also do not by themselves establish that the gene product directly absorbs radiation.

To distinguish a sensory defect from a downstream output defect, molecular and physiological experiments can be combined. Suppose both wild-type and mutant tissues contain the same amount of a photoreceptor, and both show the expected receptor-state change immediately after illumination. If a downstream marker fails to change in the mutant, a defect after reception becomes plausible. Conversely, an apoprotein lacking its necessary pigment may be detectable by an antibody yet unable to respond normally to light. Protein abundance alone is insufficient evidence of receptor function.

Several experimental controls are particularly important. An unilluminated control reveals spontaneous development. Matched photon fluence allows comparison of wavelengths, while a measured dose-response series checks saturation. Identical medium and temperature prevent metabolic or thermal differences from masquerading as spectral effects. A reference genotype helps separate a mutation-specific effect from general growth variation, and a restored genotype can provide evidence that the mutation accounts for the phenotype. Multiple independent experiments guard against chance differences or systematic variation between plates.

The logic of sensory photobiology ultimately depends on separating four levels of observation. The first is the physical stimulus arriving at the organism. The second is the molecular receptor state. The third is the transduced signal and its integration with other regulatory inputs. The fourth is the measurable physiological or developmental outcome. Changes at one level can be inferred from another only when experimental evidence supports that inference. This framework prepares the reader to examine phytochrome photoconversion, blue-light signaling, guard-cell physiology and biological timing in the succeeding chapters.

Distinguishing necessity, sufficiency and direct perception

Genetic perturbation can address several different causal questions. A gene is necessary for a normal response under specified experimental conditions if removing its function prevents or reduces that response. Necessity under one condition does not mean that the gene is indispensable under every possible condition. Another pathway may contribute under a different wavelength, a different dose or a different developmental stage. A mutant experiment must therefore report the environment and endpoint rather than making an unrestricted statement about the gene.

Sufficiency is a different question. If activation of a particular downstream pathway in darkness produces a response that ordinarily requires illumination, that pathway may be sufficient to produce the measured response in the experimental context. It does not follow that light normally acts only through that pathway. Nor does it establish that the manipulated protein is the receptor. Artificial activation may bypass the normal receptor-dependent step. In a textbook reasoning problem, necessity and sufficiency should be evaluated using the actual manipulation and result rather than treated as interchangeable descriptions of a causal relationship.

Complementation strengthens the assignment of a mutant phenotype to a gene. Suppose a mutant lacks normal blue-light-induced hypocotyl inhibition. Introduction of a functional version of the candidate gene restores the response. This supports the conclusion that loss of that gene's function was responsible for the defect, especially if the lines share a genetic background and appropriate controls are included. It still leaves open whether the protein directly absorbs blue light, stabilizes another receptor, transports a chromophore or executes a downstream regulatory step. Direct molecular evidence is required to discriminate among these possibilities.

Localization provides another constraint. A candidate receptor proposed to act in a particular sensory tissue should be present in a form capable of functioning there, or a plausible intercellular signaling mechanism must connect its location to the observed response. An experiment expressing a functional gene only in a defined tissue can test whether that tissue's expression is sufficient to restore an output. Such results must be interpreted carefully because the expression pattern may not exactly reproduce the normal abundance or timing of the gene. The introductory principle is that the location of a receptor-dependent requirement and the location of the visible response are experimentally separable.

Reading a quantitative phenotype without confusing statistics and biology

The length measurements in the preceding illustrative dataset are group means. A mean is a summary of the central tendency of a set of measurements. It provides no direct information about the spread of individual measurements unless variation is also reported. For example, two groups may have means of 5 and 6 mm, but the biological interpretation depends partly on the variation within and among independent experiments. If measurements are highly variable, the apparent difference may be poorly resolved; if they are consistent across well-designed independent replicates, a similar mean difference may be much more convincing.

In a simple seedling experiment, the experimenter should distinguish biological replicates from repeated measurements of the same experimental unit. Measuring the same hypocotyl ten times can improve confidence in the precision of its measured length but does not provide ten independent seedlings. Likewise, seedlings growing together on one plate may share environmental conditions. The appropriate analysis depends on how treatments were assigned and how variation is structured. For examination purposes, identify the independent unit and consider whether the observations support the strength of the stated conclusion. Formal statistical procedures are covered elsewhere in the curriculum.

A useful derived quantity is relative inhibition compared with the dark control for the same genotype. It reduces the influence of baseline length differences when comparing responsiveness, but it does not eliminate every confounder. Suppose one genotype grows 20 mm in darkness and 10 mm in light, while another grows 10 mm in darkness and 5 mm in light. Both show 50 percent inhibition by this definition. The absolute differences are 10 and 5 mm respectively. A claim that the genotypes have identical light-dependent growth mechanisms would be unjustified because the relative endpoint is compatible with different underlying growth rates or signaling states. The calculation answers a specific comparative question and should not be mistaken for a complete mechanistic analysis.

Sometimes a treatment changes the developmental timing of germination. If seedling length is measured a fixed number of days after seeds were placed on a plate, a group that germinated earlier may be longer simply because it had more time to grow. To test post-germination elongation, the experimenter can align observations to a defined germination stage or measure growth over a controlled interval after emergence. This adjustment can substantially change the biological interpretation of a length difference. It also illustrates why experimental comparisons require a common biological starting point, not merely a common date on a clock.

Testing competing explanations with a factorial design

A factorial design varies two or more experimental factors in combinations. In sensory photobiology, useful factors include genotype and wavelength, genotype and dose, or spectral ratio and total flux. Consider a simple study of genotype and wavelength using a wild type and a candidate mutant exposed to red or blue illumination. If both genotypes respond similarly to red but differently to blue, the effect of genotype depends on wavelength. Such a pattern is more informative about spectral specificity than a comparison under only one kind of light.

Add a dark control for both genotypes, because the difference under blue illumination could reflect a general baseline difference in elongation. If the mutant is longer than the wild type even in darkness, the comparison of final blue-treated lengths alone is ambiguous. If their dark lengths are similar but their blue lengths differ strongly, a response-specific defect becomes more plausible. Adding a complemented line further tests whether the candidate mutation accounts for the difference. Each factor answers a different part of the causal question.

An experiment may also include a molecular marker of an early response. If the wild type and the mutant differ in final length but show the same early marker response, the difference may arise at a later regulatory or execution step. If the early marker is absent in the mutant as well, the defect may lie earlier in the pathway, although the marker itself must be well characterized. An experimental series following events at several levels can narrow the location of a defect without requiring complete knowledge of every molecular component.

A similar design applies to spectral ratio and light quantity. At a fixed red to far-red ratio, changing total photon flux can test quantity-dependent behavior. At a fixed quantity, changing the spectral ratio tests sensitivity to composition. If the effect of ratio is much stronger at one flux level than another, the interaction suggests that the two properties are not simply additive determinants of the output. Such interactions are biologically plausible because receptor-state conversion, relaxation and downstream responsiveness depend on absolute rates as well as the spectral mixture. A student should be able to infer these qualitative patterns from a table without treating every difference as proof of a separate receptor.

A complete reasoning sequence for an unfamiliar light-response problem

Begin by identifying the response variable. A problem about oxygen evolution differs from one about germination, even if both use the same wavelengths. Determine whether the measurements describe an incident physical input, a receptor's absorption, a molecular signaling state or an organismal output. If the graph has wavelength on the horizontal axis, read the vertical axis before deciding whether it is an absorption or action spectrum. The interpretation of a peak depends on what is being measured.

Next identify the variables held constant. If radiant irradiance rather than photon flux is held constant, different wavelengths deliver different photon numbers. If the total photon dose is constant but the rates differ, a response difference may reveal time dependence. If both dose and wavelength are matched but timing differs, the state of the organism can modify the output. If direction differs, spatial asymmetry rather than a difference in total dose may be the relevant manipulation. The equation or conceptual principle must follow from the design.

Then identify the alternative explanations that remain possible. In a wavelength-dependent mutant phenotype, the affected gene may encode a receptor or a downstream component. In a germination assay, a low percentage may reflect dormancy, inviability or another missing requirement. In a curvature assay, weak bending may arise from a general growth defect rather than a sensory defect. Appropriate controls are those that distinguish these alternatives. A proposed conclusion should be no stronger than the contrast actually supports.

Finally connect the result to established receptor biology. Red and far-red sensitivity directs attention toward phytochromes in the standard seed-plant model; blue-light sensitivity may involve cryptochromes, phototropins or other flavin-containing systems depending on the output. A directional response, a transcription-dependent developmental phenotype and a clock-related response can involve different receptor contributions even in the same spectral region. A useful prediction identifies both the plausible receptor class and the downstream process requiring further evidence. This disciplined sequence provides a transferable framework for the receptor-specific chapters that follow.

11. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Light is only a source of photosynthetic energy

    Reserve-supported seedlings can respond to a photon pulse without meaningful carbon assimilation. Both roles involve absorption; ask whether the measured endpoint is energy conversion or a regulated developmental decision.

  2. Blue photons are more numerous at equal radiant power

    Blue photons carry more energy individually; equal power delivers more red photons. Students confuse energy per particle and particle number. Convert irradiance using hc/λ before comparing treatments.

  3. Absorption and action spectra are identical by definition

    Absorption describes optical properties; action measures a biological endpoint. Both are plotted against wavelength, so identify the y-axis and experimental method.

  4. A matching action peak proves receptor identity

    Spectral correspondence suggests a candidate, not proof. Overlapping pigments and downstream saturation can mislead; seek genetic rescue and direct photochemistry.

  5. Fluence and fluence rate are interchangeable

    Fluence is integrated photon dose; rate is photons per area per time. Check seconds in the units and whether reciprocity has been established.

  6. Equal fluence guarantees equal responses

    State decay, saturation and internal timing can violate reciprocity. Examination problems often hold dose constant while varying duration; inspect dynamics.

  7. Every red response is reversed by far-red

    Only appropriate reversible response modes follow classical photoreversal. Recognize VLFRs, HIRs and downstream commitment before applying last-pulse logic.

  8. Pr absorbs only red and Pfr only far-red

    Their absorption spectra overlap. Names indicate preferential absorption, not exclusivity; a photostationary mixture is expected.

  9. Low R:FR means zero photosynthesis

    It is a spectral ratio and can change independently of total flux. Compare absolute photon numbers and ratio separately.

  10. Cryptochrome and phototropin are interchangeable

    Both can sense blue light but have different protein architectures and characteristic outputs. A blue phenotype alone cannot identify a receptor; examine the particular trait and molecular evidence.

  11. All plant receptors require an external organic cofactor

    UVR8 senses UV-B through intrinsic protein tryptophans. Do not generalize the apoprotein–chromophore model beyond its scope.

  12. Dark growth is developmental inactivity

    Skotomorphogenesis is an active growth program. A long dark hypocotyl is not evidence of absent regulation.

  13. De-etiolation is identical to greening

    Multiple developmental traits and chloroplast differentiation have distinct timing and requirements. Score hypocotyl, cotyledons and pigment separately in mutant questions.

  14. A downstream response factor is a photoreceptor

    Direct light-dependent molecular state change distinguishes reception. Genetic necessity for a response does not establish photon absorption.

  15. Light always opens stomata

    Water status, internal CO₂ and hormonal signaling can oppose light-dependent opening. A closed stoma under blue light does not alone establish a receptor defect.

  16. A rhythm observed in a light-dark cycle proves a biological clock

    Persistent free-running rhythmicity is needed to distinguish endogenous oscillation from direct environmental driving. Transfer to suitable constant conditions and observe several cycles.

1. Part B — Which property of a photon increases as wavelength decreases?

  2. Energy
  3. Avogadro constant
  4. Duration of the light pulse
Answer and explanation

B. E = hc/λ; the speed in vacuum is unchanged. Neither the number of particles per mole nor exposure duration is a photon energy property.

2. Part B — Which measurement most directly describes the number of incident photons per area per second?

  1. Radiant energy per mole
  2. Absorbance
  3. Lux
  4. Photon flux density
Answer and explanation

D. Photon flux density counts incident photons per unit area per unit time. Lux weights visual sensitivity; absorbance is a logarithmic optical measure.

3. Part B — An absorption spectrum records which quantity as a function of wavelength?

  1. Optical absorption or absorbance
  2. Plant height
  3. Circadian period
  4. Germination fraction
Answer and explanation

A. Absorption is a molecular or material optical measurement. Height, germination and period are biological outputs that may be used to construct action spectra.

4. Part B — Which pair is correctly matched?

  1. Phototropin–phytochromobilin
  2. Phytochrome–FMN
  3. Phototropin–FMN
  4. Cryptochrome–chlorophyll a
Answer and explanation

C. Phototropin LOV domains bind FMN. Seed-plant phytochrome binds phytochromobilin, and plant cryptochromes contain FAD.

5. Part B — The pigment-free protein component of a chromophore-dependent receptor is called its:

  1. Photosystem
  2. Holoprotein
  3. Transcript
  4. Apoprotein
Answer and explanation

D. The apoprotein lacks the chromophore; the holoprotein is the associated chromophore-containing form. A transcript is RNA, not a pigment-free protein.

  1. Early photosynthetic carbon fixation
  2. Complete inhibition of shoot extension
  3. Elongated hypocotyl
  4. Broad green cotyledons

C. Dark growth favors hypocotyl elongation and restrained cotyledon expansion. Green expanded cotyledons characterize light-promoted development.

  3. Plasmolysis
  4. Photorespiration

A. De-etiolation is a developmental transition. The other terms refer to photosynthetic impairment, oxygenation-linked metabolism and osmotic shrinkage respectively.

  4. No change in neighboring vegetation

B. Leaves change the relative red and far-red fluxes through selective absorption, reflection and transmission. The ratio does not determine soil nitrate or blue flux.

  2. Chlorophyll reaction centers only
  3. Phytochromes only
  4. UVR8 only

A. Phototropins are blue-light sensors important in directional growth. Other receptors can modulate development but are not the primary blue directional sensors in the standard model.

  2. Infrared thermal radiation
  3. UV-B
  4. Far-red

C. UVR8 mediates UV-B sensing through protein tryptophan residues. The other bands are not its defining inputs.

  2. The receptor directly senses light; the downstream factor may respond to signals

  4. A light receptor must bind DNA

B. Direct photon-dependent state change defines sensory function. A transcription factor can control expression without itself being photosensitive.

  3. ATP and reducing equivalents
  4. A change in flowering time only

  4. The plant's complete genome
Answer and explanation

B. Action spectra are defined for specific responses and methods, and their shapes can depend on exposure and physiology.

49. SET — What is a reasonable interpretation when identical pulses differ in effectiveness after a long dark interval?

  1. Receptor or downstream signaling states may have relaxed
  2. Speed of light has changed
  3. Avogadro's constant has changed
  4. The photon wavelength must be zero
Answer and explanation

A. Inter-pulse relaxation can alter state and responsiveness. Basic physical constants do not vary in the experiment.

50. SET — Which statement best describes a plant light response?

  1. Every light response decreases elongation
  2. All responses are photosynthetic
  3. The response depends on receptor, tissue, developmental state and other inputs
  4. Every light response increases elongation
Answer and explanation

C. Different tissues integrate multiple pathways and produce different growth, movement or physiological outputs. No universal sign of elongation applies.

51. SET — Assertion: Equal radiant irradiance at two wavelengths does not guarantee equal photon flux. Reason: Photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength.

  1. Both statements are true; the reason correctly explains the assertion.
  2. Both statements are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. Assertion true; reason false.
  4. Assertion false; reason true.
Answer and explanation

A. At fixed radiant power, a larger energy per photon means fewer photons per unit time. Substituting E = hc/λ gives photon flux proportional to wavelength, directly explaining the assertion.

52. SET — Assertion: Red/far-red reversibility is expected for every phytochrome-mediated response. Reason: High-irradiance responses can depend on sustained exposure and fluence rate.

  1. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both are true, but the reason does not explain it.
  3. Assertion true; reason false.
  4. Assertion false; reason true.
Answer and explanation

D. Classical reversible low-fluence responses are not representative of every phytochrome output. Sustained illumination can drive high-irradiance responses with different behavior.

53. Part C — Consider four statements about the equal-dose experiment: I. The treatments deliver equal incident photon fluence. II. They necessarily produce equal receptor-state fractions. III. Relaxation during the longer exposure may alter the output. IV. A difference proves that no receptor absorbed light. Which statements are supported?

  1. II and IV only
  2. I and III only
  3. I, II and III only
  4. III and IV only
Answer and explanation

B. The dose is equal by integration, but receptor-state relaxation can make the final fractions unequal. Neither universal reciprocity nor absence of photon absorption follows from different physiological outputs.

54. Part C — In the illustrative germination graph, red gives 82% and red followed by far-red gives 16%; dark gives 12%. Relative to the red-induced increase above the dark baseline, how much of that increase is removed by far-red?

  1. 16/82, about 19.5%
  2. 66/82, about 80.5%
  3. 66/70, about 94.3%
  4. 70/12, about 583%
Answer and explanation

C. The red-associated increase above dark is 82−12 = 70 percentage points; the sequential treatment leaves 16−12 = 4. The removed increment is 70−4 = 66, so 66/70 ≈ 94.3%. This is an output calculation, not a receptor-state fraction.

13. Revision and mastery

Separate incident radiation, receptor state, signal transduction and physiological output. At constant photon flux, fluence is flux multiplied by exposure time. Compare spectra only when the ordinate, dose and method are specified. Light can supply photosynthetic energy and independently regulate developmental programs. Receptor-dependent outcomes may be modified by other environmental signals and internal time.

High-yield terminology and equations

  • Photon energy: E = hc/λ; convert nm to m.
  • Photon fluence: H = It for constant photon flux density I and duration t.
  • Absorbance: A = log₁₀(I₀/I); A = εcl for a suitable dilute solution.
  • Action spectrum: biological effectiveness versus wavelength.
  • Photoreceptor: a molecular light sensor; chromophore: its light-absorbing group.
  • Skotomorphogenesis and photomorphogenesis: contrasting developmental programs.
  • Reciprocity: equal response at equal dose within valid conditions.
