1. From primary metabolism to plant chemical diversity

Every growing plant must construct proteins, nucleic acids, membranes and cell walls while obtaining sufficient energy to maintain living cells. Yet it must also cope with excess light, herbivores, pathogens and competition. The carbon fixed in a leaf may become starch, cellulose or respiratory fuel, but it can also become a volatile terpene, a coloured flavonoid or a nitrogenous compound involved in ecological interactions. Specialized metabolism is the network of biochemical transformations that connects these outcomes to central metabolism.

Historically, compounds directly supporting fundamental growth and reproduction were called primary metabolites, while compounds with restricted distributions or poorly understood functions were called secondary metabolites. The modern term specialized metabolism avoids suggesting that the latter products are inferior, unnecessary or waste. Many affect plant fitness and some contribute directly to structural or physiological functions. Primary pathways are broadly conserved, whereas specialized pathways often vary between lineages, tissues and developmental stages.

The boundary is not absolute. Phytosterols have an isoprenoid origin but are essential components of membranes. Carotenoids contribute to photosynthesis and photoprotection. Lignin, a phenylpropanoid polymer, is necessary for the mechanical and hydraulic performance of many vascular plants. Plant hormones share precursors with specialized compounds but regulate development and physiology. A compound's chemical ancestry does not establish whether its function is primary, specialized or regulatory.

Key distinction

Primary versus specialized is an overlapping functional classification. Terpenoid, phenolic and alkaloid refer to chemical features or biosynthetic origins. Do not treat these classifications as equivalent.

2. Carbon supply: photosynthesis, glycolysis and shared precursors

In the chloroplast, light-driven electron transport supplies ATP and NADPH to the Calvin–Benson cycle. Assimilated CO₂ becomes organic carbon. Triose phosphates can be used for starch formation or exchanged with the cytosol, where carbon enters sucrose synthesis. Sucrose transported through phloem supplies sinks such as young leaves, roots, fruits and seeds. These organs need not fix their own carbon in order to synthesize specialized metabolites.

Glycolysis and interconnected carbohydrate pathways distribute carbon among several branch points. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP or G3P) is a C3 sugar phosphate; phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and pyruvate also carry three carbons. Erythrose-4-phosphate (E4P), a C4 sugar phosphate, is supplied by pentose phosphate and related carbohydrate reactions. Pyruvate oxidation can form acetyl-CoA, which carries an activated C2 acetyl group. Acetyl-CoA also arises by other metabolic routes. Its pools in mitochondria, cytosol and plastids are not freely interchangeable.

PEP may continue through glycolysis or enter aromatic amino acid synthesis. Pyruvate contributes to respiration, amino acid synthesis and the plastidial isoprenoid pathway. Acetyl-CoA supplies fatty acid synthesis and the mevalonate route in appropriate cellular pools. The same precursor may have several possible fates rather than a single assigned purpose.

Acetyl-CoA carboxylase consumes ATP and bicarbonate to generate malonyl-CoA. Although the malonyl group carries three carbons, a typical decarboxylative condensation transfers only two into the growing skeleton; the remaining carboxyl carbon leaves as CO₂. Malonyl groups support plastid fatty acid biosynthesis through acyl carrier protein intermediates and provide cytosolic building blocks for many specialized metabolites.

Separate carbon, energy and reducing power

Carbon supplies atoms, ATP drives energy-requiring reactions, and reducing equivalents such as NADPH support reduction. Extra sucrose does not necessarily increase final product formation if an enzyme, transport capacity or reducing power is limiting. A precursor pool can decrease because consumption increases, or accumulate because consumption slows. Concentration alone does not establish metabolic flux.

3. Nitrogen assimilation and amino acid allocation

Nitrate and ammonium are common root nitrogen sources. Nitrate reductase and nitrite reductase convert nitrate to ammonium. The GS–GOGAT cycle incorporates ammonium into organic nitrogen: glutamine synthetase forms glutamine, and glutamate synthase transfers nitrogen to 2-oxoglutarate to generate glutamate. This requires a carbon skeleton, ATP at the GS step and reducing power at the GOGAT step. Symbiotic nitrogen fixation can supply additional reduced nitrogen to host plants.

Transamination distributes amino groups among carbon skeletons. Amino acids enter protein synthesis but also support specialized pathways. Phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan are aromatic precursors; ornithine, arginine and lysine contribute to certain alkaloid families. Methionine contributes to some glucosinolates. Caffeine, however, is a purine-derived alkaloid, showing that amino acid derivation is not universal for this family.

The nitrogen atom need not remain in the product. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) removes ammonia from phenylalanine to produce cinnamate. Its C9 skeleton is retained in the phenylpropanoid pathway while the nitrogen can be reassimilated. A nitrogen-containing precursor can therefore generate a nitrogen-free phenolic product. Many alkaloids and betalains retain nitrogen; glucosinolates contain sulfur as well, connecting their synthesis with sulfur assimilation.

Carbon and nitrogen allocation change with genotype, environment and development. Mild nitrogen shortage can favour relative accumulation of certain carbon-rich products in some species, but severe starvation can limit photosynthesis, enzyme synthesis and precursor supply, decreasing specialized products as well as growth. Nitrogen deficiency does not universally increase phenolics or reduce every alkaloid. Product nitrogen content is not the same as nitrogen dependence of the producing cell.

Exam distinction

Trace the atom rather than just the precursor name: PAL removes the amino nitrogen of phenylalanine while retaining the carbon skeleton in cinnamate.

4. Four major precursor routes

The map below organizes entry points into specialized metabolism. It is a conceptual network, not one linear sequence or a single compartment. In a living plant these pathways can operate simultaneously, and individual products may combine several routes.

1. Isoprenoid pathways

The mevalonate (MVA) pathway typically occurs in the cytosol and associated compartments, starting from acetyl-CoA. The methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway occurs in plastids, starting from pyruvate and GAP. Both converge on the activated five-carbon building blocks isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP). Intercompartmental exchange may occur. The two routes use different enzymes and starting substrates, but their downstream carbon building blocks are identical.

Joining C5 units forms prenyl diphosphates that can supply C10 monoterpenes, C15 sesquiterpenes, C20 diterpenes and larger terpene skeletons. MVA typically contributes to many sesquiterpenes, triterpenes and sterols; MEP commonly supplies monoterpenes, diterpenes and carotenoids. These compartment associations are useful generalizations, not absolute rules. Modified final structures may depart from simple multiples of five carbons.

2. Shikimate and aromatic amino acid pathway

PEP (C3) combines with E4P (C4) to initiate shikimate pathway metabolism, eventually yielding chorismate. Chorismate is a branch point in the synthesis of phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan. PAL diverts phenylalanine carbon toward cinnamate and phenylpropanoids, while tyrosine and tryptophan provide starting material for other specialized branches. The detailed shikimate reactions belong to 6.G.04.

3. Acetate–malonate route

Plant type III polyketide synthases use suitable starter and extender substrates. Chalcone synthase uses a phenylpropanoid-derived C9 p-coumaroyl-CoA starter and three malonyl-CoA extenders. Each extender contributes two retained carbons, so the resulting scaffold contains 9 + 3 × 2 = 15 carbons. Three carboxyl carbons leave as CO₂. Flavonoids thus demonstrate mixed phenylpropanoid and acetate–malonate origin. The carbon count applies to the scaffold, not to additional sugars or other modifications.

4. Amino acid and nucleotide-derived metabolism

Amino acids and nucleotides contribute to diverse nitrogen-containing compounds through reactions such as decarboxylation, oxidation, condensation and methyl transfer. Some pathways combine amino acid and isoprenoid-derived components. Alkaloids, glucosinolates, cyanogenic glycosides, non-protein amino acids and betalains are among the nitrogenous specialized metabolites. Their common nitrogen content does not imply a common pathway.

5. Classifying compounds without false boundaries

A plant chemical may be described by its structure, its biosynthetic ancestry and its biological function. These categories are complementary. A yellow pigment and a fragrant volatile differ in observable properties, but neither colour nor smell identifies a pathway with certainty.

Terpenes and terpenoids

Terpenes traditionally denote hydrocarbons with isoprenoid skeletons; terpenoids include modified derivatives such as oxygenated forms, though the terms often overlap. Limonene is a monoterpene hydrocarbon; menthol is an oxygenated monoterpenoid. Carotenoids and sterols share isoprenoid ancestry despite having different biological roles.

Phenolics and phenylpropanoids

Phenolics are broadly characterized by hydroxyl groups attached directly to aromatic rings in their defining structural forms. Phenylpropanoids generally originate from an aromatic amino acid-derived C6–C3 skeleton. Many phenylpropanoids are phenolic, but cinnamate itself lacks a phenolic hydroxyl group, and many phenolics arise outside the PAL-derived route. Gallic acid can originate from shikimate-related intermediates. Lignin is a polymer of phenylpropanoid-derived monolignols; flavonoids typically have phenolic character and mixed phenylpropanoid–malonate origins.

Alkaloids and other nitrogenous products

Many alkaloids are basic, nitrogen-containing natural products with nitrogen in a heterocyclic ring, but exceptions exist. Traditional categories such as true alkaloids, protoalkaloids and pseudoalkaloids have classification caveats. Glucosinolates and betalains contain nitrogen without being ordinarily classified as alkaloids. Monoterpene indole alkaloids illustrate mixed amino acid and isoprenoid ancestry.

Glycosylation attaches a sugar to a compound, often altering its solubility and storage behaviour. The resulting glycoside can retain the underlying scaffold classification. Anthocyanins, for example, are glycosylated anthocyanidins and belong to the flavonoid group. A glycosylated terpenoid does not necessarily share the biosynthetic scaffold of a glycosylated phenolic.

6. Metabolic branching, compartmentation and resource allocation

At a branch point, two or more pathways consume a common intermediate. The partition of flux depends on substrate supply, enzyme kinetics and abundance, regulatory signals, compartmentation and downstream removal. Concentration measures an amount per mass or volume; flux measures a rate, often normalized by biomass. A large precursor pool may reflect a downstream bottleneck rather than greater throughput.

For a simplified pool P, dP/dt = vin − vgrowth − vspecialized − vother. At steady state, dP/dt = 0. If 100 nmol/h enters and growth and other pathways consume 65 and 15 nmol/h respectively, then 20 nmol/h enters specialized metabolism. The calculation assumes a single defined pool, comparable units, no unaccounted transport and negligible accumulation.

If the specialized branch increases its consumption while input stays fixed, competing outputs must decrease or the pool will decline. Plants can instead change carbon fixation, mobilize stored carbohydrate or adjust allocation to maintain growth. Growth–defense tradeoffs are not universal fixed relationships. Reduced growth can increase product concentration through reduced biomass dilution without any increase in synthesis.

Where a reaction occurs is important

Plastids supply MEP and much aromatic amino acid biosynthesis. Cytosol and endoplasmic reticulum host many phenylpropanoid reactions. Mitochondria support respiration, but mitochondrial acetyl-CoA cannot freely cross the inner membrane. Specialized products may accumulate in vacuoles, extracellular secretions, trichomes or cell walls, away from the original biosynthetic site.

A flower with normal pigment biosynthetic enzyme activity may still show altered pigmentation if transport into the vacuole is defective. Vacuolar pH can alter the observed hue of anthocyanins. The phenotype may reflect chemical modification, synthesis, storage, transport or degradation. Increasing expression of a branch enzyme is similarly insufficient to guarantee higher product yield if precursor supply or downstream steps are limiting. These concepts prepare for regulation (6.G.09) and metabolic engineering (6.G.10).

7. Evolutionary diversification from shared precursors

Central pathways are broadly conserved, whereas specialized pathways vary widely among plant lineages. New metabolic functions may arise when existing enzymes are recruited to additional substrates, or when gene duplication permits one copy to maintain the ancestral role while another acquires altered activity or regulation. Catalytic promiscuity, meaning weak activity on additional substrates, can provide a starting point for evolution. Not all duplicates acquire new functions, and innovation can occur without duplication.

A novel compound may improve plant fitness in a particular environment. If genetic variants increase its beneficial production with acceptable costs, selection may favour those variants. Changes in regulatory sequences may restrict production to flowers, roots, trichomes or damaged leaves. Other variants can be lost through drift or selection. Transporters and modifying enzymes can evolve alongside the scaffold-producing enzymes.

Different terpene synthases can generate diverse skeletons from a shared prenyl precursor; oxidation, methylation and glycosylation add further structural diversity. Evolution of precursor supply in primary metabolism can also enable new specialized routes. Similar ecological functions may evolve independently in different lineages, so similarity in product chemistry does not establish identical pathway ancestry.

Evidence matters

Sequence similarity supports hypotheses about ancestry; enzyme assays support biochemical capacity; genetic perturbation helps establish in vivo contribution; controlled fitness comparisons test ecological function. A compound induced after stress is not automatically proven to confer protection.

8. Stable isotope labeling and experimental reasoning

Stable isotopes such as 13C and 15N can trace atoms through metabolism. They differ in mass from the most abundant isotopes, allowing isotope incorporation to be measured by appropriate mass spectrometry. Early transfer from a labeled precursor to a product supports metabolic connectivity, but carbon and nitrogen can redistribute through central pathways. Precursor enrichment, time course, label position, compartmentation and turnover matter.

Worked carbon-label calculation

Consider an idealized C15 flavonoid scaffold. If all nine carbons of p-coumaroyl-CoA are labeled and the three malonyl extenders are unlabeled, nine labels remain (M+9, considering only the tracer). If the starter is unlabeled and all three malonyl groups are uniformly 13C-labeled, six labels remain (M+6) because each C3 extender loses one carbon as CO₂. This assumes no label scrambling, no additional sugar and fully labeled supplied substrates. Actual plant tissues may exhibit mixtures of isotopologues.

More Y is consistent with diversion of precursor, but may instead reflect reduced degradation or transport. Complementation strengthens the genetic association. Compare precursor labeling and time-resolved product isotopologues with enzyme activity and pool measurements before inferring a flux change.

Tracing nitrogen

15N-enriched nitrogen can enter amino acids through assimilation and label nitrogen-retaining products. Phenylalanine-derived nitrogen is removed at PAL, while its carbon skeleton can enter phenylpropanoids. Thus 13C-phenylalanine may label a nitrogen-free product but 15N-phenylalanine cannot contribute nitrogen directly to that same molecule. Nitrogen can be recycled and incorporated elsewhere; detecting label in a different product does not identify the exact amino acid donor.

For meaningful comparisons, match tissue development, measure precursor enrichment, quantify absolute labeled product as well as labeled fraction, and examine several time points. A higher fractional labeling may arise from weaker dilution by older unlabeled product, not higher net synthesis. A strong explanation specifies the measured variable and alternative mechanisms.

9. CSIR-NET and SET practice

Original syllabus-aligned practice questions, not claimed previous-year questions. Attempt each before expanding its explanation.

1. Part B

Which pair initiates MEP?

A Acetyl-CoA + malonyl-CoA
B PEP + E4P
C Pyruvate + GAP
D Glutamate + glutamine

Answer and explanation

C. Pyruvate and GAP enter MEP; PEP and E4P enter shikimate.

2. Part B

Which statement is correct?

A All phenolics are phenylpropanoids
B All N products are alkaloids
C Specialized products are waste
D Some terpenoids are indispensable

Answer and explanation

D. Sterols and carotenoids have essential physiological roles.

3. Part B

Which enzyme removes the amino nitrogen of phenylalanine?

A PAL
B DXS
C GS
D ACC

Answer and explanation

A. PAL produces cinnamate and ammonia.

4. Part B

Which shikimate starting substrate contains four carbons?

A IPP
B E4P

Which extender contributes two retained carbons in chalcone formation?

A Malonyl-CoA
B Glutamine
C DMAPP
D Chorismate

Which nitrogenous pigment is not ordinarily an alkaloid?

Typical MVA and MEP locations respectively?

B Both mitochondria
C Cytosol, plastid

B All alkaloids

9. Part C

A C15 scaffold incorporates three uniformly ¹³C-labeled malonyl groups and unlabeled C9 starter. How many labels remain?

A 3
B 6

A 20
B 30

B. 120 − 70 − 20 = 30, assuming no other outputs.

B P unrelated to X
C X branch impairment; Y flux uncertain
D Photosynthesis stopped

C. Pools reflect production and removal; flux requires additional measurements.

A PAL removes nitrogen
B Product is alkaloid
C N fixation absent
D Carbon cannot enter specialized metabolism

Answer and explanation

A. PAL retains carbon skeleton and removes ammonia.

14. Part C

What best demonstrates adaptive defensive function?

A Gene duplication alone
B Induced transcript alone
C Controlled compound-dependent fitness benefit during herbivory
D Similarity to a defense compound

Answer and explanation

C. Fitness evidence tests ecological contribution, unlike expression alone.

15. Assertion–reason

Assertion: Flavonoids have mixed biosynthetic origin. Reason: A C9 starter combines with malonyl-derived C2 units.

A Both true, reason explains
B Both true, reason does not explain
C A true, R false
D A false, R true

Answer and explanation

A. The precursor contributions establish mixed origin.

16. Assertion–reason

Assertion: Increasing concentration always increases flux. Reason: Catalytic capacity and turnover can limit flux.

A Both true, reason explains
B Both true, reason does not explain
C A true, R false
D A false, R true

Answer and explanation

D. Concentration and flux are distinct.

17. Analytical

Trace carbon fixation to flavonoid and isoprenoid scaffolds.

Draw both branches.

Answer and explanation

CO₂ → triose phosphates → PEP + E4P → chorismate → phenylalanine → p-coumaroyl-CoA; acetyl-CoA supplies malonyl extenders. Acetyl-CoA → MVA or pyruvate + GAP → MEP → IPP/DMAPP.

18. Analytical

Why may severe nitrogen deficiency decrease nitrogen-free phenolics?

Explain in terms of enzyme synthesis and carbon fixation.

Answer and explanation

Nitrogen deficiency can reduce photosynthetic proteins and biosynthetic enzymes, limiting carbon, energy and precursor supply despite lack of nitrogen in the product.

19. Numerical

Input 90 nmol/h, growth output 50 and specialized output 25; no other output. What is the change after two hours?

Assume constant rates.

Answer and explanation

90 − 50 − 25 = +15 nmol/h. The pool increases by 30 nmol over two hours. It is not at steady state.

20. Analytical

Two tissues have equal product pools but different fractional isotope labeling. Is greater net synthesis established?

Give alternatives and further measurements.

Answer and explanation

No. Precursor enrichment and dilution by existing unlabeled product may differ. Measure precursor enrichment, time-resolved absolute labeled product and turnover.

10. Rapid revision and curriculum progression

  • Central metabolism supplies carbon, nitrogen, ATP and reducing equivalents to specialized pathways.
  • Acetyl-CoA → MVA and malonyl-CoA; pyruvate + GAP → MEP; PEP + E4P → shikimate.
  • MVA and MEP both supply IPP and DMAPP, with different typical cellular locations.
  • PAL removes phenylalanine nitrogen; phenolics and phenylpropanoids are overlapping rather than identical categories.
  • Flavonoid C15 skeleton = C9 starter + three C2 retained malonyl units.
  • Not every nitrogenous product is an alkaloid.
  • Flux is a rate, pool size is an amount or concentration; consider compartmentation and turnover.
  • Gene recruitment, duplication, regulatory changes and precursor availability can diversify plant metabolism.
