1. The biological problem: environmental change and plant performance

A young wheat plant growing in a field cannot move into the shade when the afternoon becomes hot. Nor can it leave a patch of soil when the concentration of dissolved salts rises around its roots. Yet it is not merely a passive victim of its surroundings. Its leaves can alter their orientation and gas exchange, its roots can change the regions in which they grow, and its cells can adjust metabolism. The scientific problem is to explain how these responses protect living functions, when protection is sufficient, and why it sometimes fails. Stress physiology begins with that problem rather than with a catalogue of stress-induced genes.

The ordinary operation of a plant already involves continual adjustment. During the morning, light intensity increases and photosynthesis becomes more active. Water evaporates from moist cell walls inside the leaf and passes to the atmosphere through stomata. Roots absorb water from soil and transport it toward the shoot. At night the balance between carbon gain and respiratory carbon expenditure changes. These repeated transitions are not necessarily harmful. Plants have regulatory systems that accommodate the ordinary variation characteristic of their habitats. An environmental change becomes important as a stressor when it challenges those regulatory systems or impairs functions under the conditions being considered.

The word stressor denotes the causative environmental agent or condition. A reduction in soil water availability, an unusually high temperature, excessive salt, and invasion by a pathogenic fungus are examples. The physiological state produced by exposure may be called stress, while the measurable departure of a biological variable from its reference condition can be called strain. Terminology differs among textbooks and researchers, particularly concerning whether stress names the external factor or the internal condition. We shall keep cause, internal disturbance, response and injury separate even when the literature uses the same word for more than one of them. The distinction is useful because the same external challenge can induce different internal disturbances in different organisms.

Imagine two plants exposed to the same dry air. One closes its stomata promptly and retains much of its leaf water. The other loses water more rapidly, and its leaf cells lose turgor. The external vapor pressure deficit may be identical, but the internal water deficit is not. Now consider a third plant whose leaves reach the same low water content as the second plant but continue to retain membrane integrity. Its tissues may withstand dehydration more successfully. Without measuring both exposure and internal state, an experiment cannot reliably distinguish protection from reduced exposure and protection in the presence of exposure.

Plant performance is not represented by one universal number. A leaf may remain green after a period of water shortage while its expansion rate is very small. A plant may continue growing during moderate heat yet produce sterile pollen when the temperature rises at flowering. A seed may cease visible development during unfavorable conditions and germinate successfully when conditions improve. Growth rate, carbon assimilation, reproduction, tissue integrity and survival are related but not interchangeable. A sound stress diagnosis identifies a relevant function and a meaningful reference condition.

The reference condition itself requires thought. A temperature of 15 degrees Celsius can be suitable for a species from a cool habitat and detrimental to a tropical species. The significance of darkness differs between a buried seed and an expanded photosynthetic leaf. Even within one plant, the temperature and oxygen conditions tolerated by a mature leaf can differ from those tolerated by an actively dividing meristem. Stress is therefore relational rather than an intrinsic property of a single numerical value. Statements such as a temperature is stressful must specify the organism, tissue, developmental context, duration and measured consequence.

A useful framework treats the plant as an open system exchanging energy and matter with its surroundings. It absorbs photons, water and nutrients, exchanges gases, produces organic molecules and releases heat. The maintenance of living organization requires energy expenditure. A disturbance can alter an incoming resource, change the rate of an outgoing flux, interfere with a metabolic reaction or destroy a physical barrier. An excess is sometimes as consequential as a shortage: too much light can overload photochemistry, too much water can restrict oxygen supply to roots, and too much of an essential ion can be toxic.

Consider how this connects to the earlier chapters on photosynthesis and transport. The rate at which a chloroplast uses absorbed energy depends on the availability of carbon dioxide and the ability of metabolism to consume ATP and reducing equivalents. A dry atmosphere can lead to stomatal closure, which limits carbon dioxide entry. A leaf may then absorb more light than its restricted carbon metabolism can use. An initial disturbance of water balance consequently becomes a challenge to chloroplast energy balance. This is one reason why seemingly different stresses converge on several common protective processes, although their initiating events are not identical.

The history of the subject explains why definitions matter. Earlier physiological work often compared injured plants with healthy controls and focused on the environmental conditions under which functions declined. Jacob Levitt's influential treatment of plant environmental stress organized the subject around external challenges, internal strain and mechanisms of resistance. Later experimental work expanded the explanation to include rapid regulation, gene expression and the costs of protection. This progression did not make the older physiological measurements obsolete. Molecular information becomes biologically interpretable only when it is related to the performance and physical condition of the organism.

An exam question that reports a stress-responsive transcript but no physiological measurements illustrates the distinction. The transcript may identify an early response, a compensatory reaction, an indicator of injury or a consequence of slowed development. Its presence establishes neither that the corresponding protein is active nor that the response improves survival. To investigate function, compare a perturbation of the relevant gene with appropriate controls and examine the internal stress state and biological outcome. The resulting explanation should connect the molecular event with the actual problem the plant experiences.

2. Abiotic and biotic disturbances

An abiotic stress originates principally from a nonliving environmental factor. Water deficit, excessive salinity, high and low temperature, oxygen shortage, excessive irradiance and unfavorable mineral conditions belong to this category. A biotic stress arises from interactions with living organisms, such as pathogenic bacteria, fungi, viruses, nematodes and herbivorous insects. Competition with neighboring plants can also create resource limitation, although the immediately experienced limitation may be a shortage of light, water or nutrients. The distinction classifies the origin of the disturbance; it does not imply that every abiotic response differs from every biotic response.

Water availability is governed by more than the amount of liquid present. Water moves according to differences in water potential, a measure of the thermodynamic tendency of water to move. Dry soil can make uptake difficult because water becomes held more strongly by the soil matrix. Soil containing abundant dissolved salts can also have a low water potential even while it appears wet. On the atmospheric side, dry air can promote rapid evaporation. The plant must therefore balance the supply of water through roots and xylem with loss from exposed surfaces. The detailed components of water potential and the transport pathways belong to 6.F; the central stress concept here is that exposure is not synonymous with the water content of the soil.

Salinity illustrates an additional distinction. Dissolved salts reduce the water potential of the external solution and may therefore limit the initial uptake of water. Over longer periods, particular ions can accumulate in tissues and disrupt metabolic and ionic balance. The two components need not arise with the same timing or severity. If a plant experiences sodium chloride and a nonionic osmotic treatment adjusted to a comparable external water potential, a shared early response is consistent with a common osmotic component. Later injury specific to the salt treatment may involve ions. Such a comparison is informative but not perfect: nonionic osmotic agents can have other effects, and equal external water potential does not ensure equal internal hydration.

Temperature acts directly on reaction rates, molecular motion and the physical state of membranes. Warming within a suitable range can accelerate processes, but beyond that range destabilization of proteins, changes in membrane behavior and imbalances among metabolic pathways become important. Low temperature slows many reactions, and freezing adds the possibility of ice formation and water redistribution. Chilling injury can occur without ice, particularly in chilling-sensitive species. Thus heat, chilling and freezing should not be treated as opposite points on a single linear damage scale. Their mechanisms overlap in some respects and differ in others.

An oxygen deficit can occur when soil pores fill with water. Oxygen diffusion through water is much slower than through air, so the rate of oxygen delivery to respiring roots can become insufficient. Mitochondrial respiration is constrained, and cells may increase their dependence on glycolysis and fermentation to maintain a limited supply of ATP. A flooded root experiences an energy problem even though water is abundant. This example prevents an overly simple classification in which all unfavorable water conditions are assumed to be forms of dehydration. The fate of the shoot depends partly on how well roots retain transport functions and how the plant redistributes resources.

A pathogen creates disturbances by establishing itself in or on plant tissue, obtaining resources, altering cell functions or destroying living structures. A biotrophic pathogen depends on living host cells for much of its nutrition, whereas a necrotrophic pathogen can exploit killed tissue. These distinctions help explain why death of a host cell is not invariably an effective defense. A virus differs from a fungus in its mode of reproduction and dependence on host-cell machinery. Herbivorous insects remove tissue, withdraw cell contents or phloem sap, and can deliver signals or introduce pathogens. An examination question should identify the particular interaction rather than assume every biological attacker produces the same type of injury.

The plant response can begin before any visible symptom appears. Molecules from a microorganism may be recognized by receptors on the cell surface, and damaged host tissue may release signals that activate protective processes. These processes form part of innate immunity, a pre-existing capacity to recognize and respond to biological threats. Plants do not possess the vertebrate system of antibody-producing lymphocytes. Their defenses combine structural barriers, chemical protection and regulated cellular responses. Later chapters explain the molecular organization of pattern-triggered and effector-triggered immunity; this introduction establishes why the response must be interpreted relative to both the attacker and the host.

The distinction between a pathogen and an organism that is simply present is also essential. Roots normally interact with diverse microorganisms, some of which provide benefits or modify plant growth. A microorganism detected near a root is not automatically a pathogen. Evidence of a detrimental interaction requires appropriate observations, such as altered function, tissue invasion or reproducible disease symptoms under defined conditions. Similarly, a defense-associated signal can be induced during interactions that do not ultimately cause disease. Recognition and successful infection are different events.

Abiotic and biotic stressors frequently coexist. High temperature can influence pathogen multiplication and plant defense, while herbivore damage may alter water loss and carbon availability. A droughted plant may have fewer resources for growth or defense, but this does not establish that drought always increases disease. Changes in tissue chemistry, pathogen life cycle, microclimate and plant signaling can lead to different outcomes. Combined-stress experiments must measure the response to each challenge alone and to both together. This permits the investigator to ask whether the combined effect differs from a defined expectation rather than merely noting that two adverse conditions occurred.

A classification is useful when it directs measurement. For low soil water availability one may examine leaf water status, conductance and growth; for heat one may examine tissue temperature, viability and reproductive function; for infection one may examine pathogen abundance, damage and host performance. Some measurements are shared, such as membrane integrity, biomass or photosynthesis. But a shared endpoint does not identify the original disturbance. When a salt-treated plant shows reduced photosynthesis, the result alone does not reveal whether carbon dioxide diffusion, photochemical damage, metabolic inhibition or a combination caused the reduction.

3. Homeostasis, regulatory responses and the cost of protection

Homeostasis means the maintenance of internal conditions within ranges compatible with living function despite variation in the surroundings. A plant does not maintain every variable at a perfectly constant value. Guard-cell turgor, soluble carbohydrate concentration and hormone abundance can change substantially over a normal day. A regulated response often involves changing one variable to protect another. Closure of stomata, for example, lowers the conductance of the pore and can reduce water loss, while simultaneously restricting diffusion of carbon dioxide. The change in conductance may be a means of preserving tissue hydration rather than an indication that regulation has failed.

A feedback system contains a regulated variable, a way of detecting relevant changes and an effector capable of modifying a process that influences that variable. Negative feedback describes a relationship in which the response opposes the original deviation. If water loss lowers leaf water status and the resulting response reduces further water loss, this has a negative-feedback character. It does not require a single central control organ. Local sensing and communication among tissues can jointly produce regulation. The existence of feedback also does not mean that the regulated variable returns immediately to its original value; a persistent external disturbance may produce a new operating state.

Some responses have a feedforward component. A plant can alter stomatal behavior in response to environmental information before leaf water content has fallen appreciably. This may reduce the magnitude of a later disturbance. Circadian regulation of metabolism can prepare tissues for predictable transitions between day and night. Anticipatory regulation is not conscious prediction; it is the operation of inherited and environmentally responsive biochemical systems. Distinguishing feedback from feedforward reasoning requires observing the order of events and testing whether an internal change is necessary to elicit the response.

At the cellular level, membrane transport is a major contributor to homeostasis. The plasma membrane separates the cytoplasm from the external medium, while the tonoplast separates the cytoplasm from the vacuole. Selective transport allows cells to maintain suitable concentrations of ions and metabolites in distinct compartments. Proton pumps use energy to build gradients that can drive coupled transport. If a disturbance reduces ATP supply or increases ion leakage, the gradients may become difficult to maintain. Protection can involve reducing leakage, altering transport activity or changing the demand on energy metabolism. These outcomes are meaningful only when related to the affected compartment.

Metabolic networks also depend on a balance between production and consumption. During photosynthetic carbon limitation, the supply of reducing power may exceed the capacity of downstream reactions to consume it. Photoprotective processes can dissipate excess excitation energy and reduce the probability of damaging reactions. Reactive oxygen species are not simply waste products; at controlled levels and locations they participate in signaling. When formation exceeds the capacity for regulation and detoxification, oxidative injury may develop. The subsequent node on oxidative stress explains individual species and enzymatic protection, while the present chapter emphasizes the difference between a regulated signal and a damaging imbalance.

A physiological response can be protective, harmful or neutral with respect to a specified outcome. Reduced leaf expansion during moderate water shortage may preserve resources and limit the development of additional transpiring surface. It also reduces the area available to capture light later. If the shortage is transient, conserving water may be beneficial; if the shortage is mild and persists for an entire growing season, the accumulated cost of reduced leaf area can be substantial. It is therefore misleading to label every stress-induced growth reduction as injury or every growth-promoting response as protection.

The costs of protection include energy, material and opportunity. Synthesizing protective proteins consumes amino acids and requires energy for transcription and translation. Producing specialized metabolites diverts precursors that could participate in other functions. Repairing damaged membranes requires components and metabolic activity. Some changes, such as stomatal closure, have an opportunity cost even without large construction expenditure because they reduce carbon acquisition. Whether the expenditure improves lifetime performance depends on the threat and on the capacity of the plant to recover. The resources committed to defense should not be imagined as a fixed budget independent of metabolism: successful protection can preserve future resource acquisition, thereby increasing the budget available later.

This distinction clarifies a familiar experimental result. A genotype that accumulates more protective solute during drought may maintain better growth, but accumulation alone is not proof that the solute caused protection. Higher solute abundance could arise because the genotype has greater initial metabolic reserves, because water loss concentrates existing solutes, or because it experiences more severe internal stress. A causal test needs a relevant genetic or physiological perturbation, direct measurement of water status, and a comparison of function at comparable conditions. A correlation can guide investigation without resolving the mechanism.

Regulation has limits. Pumps cannot preserve a membrane gradient indefinitely if the membrane becomes highly permeable, and repair cannot restore tissue that has lost viability. A plant may sustain essential functions at the expense of nonessential growth for some time, yet ultimately exhaust its reserves or suffer accumulating damage. The existence of stress-response pathways is therefore not a guarantee of resistance. One goal of stress physiology is to determine the range of environmental conditions in which regulation succeeds and to identify the transitions at which maintenance, repair and recovery become inadequate.

4. Primary disturbance, secondary injury and reversibility

When a plant is exposed to an unfavorable condition, not all of the resulting changes occupy the same position in the causal sequence. Primary injury refers to injury directly associated with the initial action of the stressor, whereas secondary injury develops because the initial disturbance disrupts other processes. The distinction is useful for tracing mechanisms but depends on the causal level of the explanation. In heat-stressed tissue, thermal destabilization of a protein can be direct, while metabolic failure arising from loss of that protein's activity can be indirect. The latter may eventually produce membrane injury through changes in energy supply or redox balance. A membrane protein can itself be a primary target of heat, so the word membrane does not automatically designate secondary injury.

Dehydration provides a particularly clear sequence. A decline in the availability of water alters the tendency for water to move into roots. If supply becomes smaller than loss from the shoot, leaf water status falls and turgor can decline. Leaf cell expansion depends in part on pressure exerted against a yielding cell wall, so loss of turgor can reduce expansion without killing cells. As dehydration becomes severe, the physical and chemical environment within cells changes. The increased concentration of solutes, altered membrane organization and disturbance of metabolism may contribute to actual tissue injury. The earliest reduction in expansion and the later destruction of cellular structures should not be regarded as the same event.

A similar distinction applies to salinity. In the short term a lower external water potential can reduce the water status and expansion of a young leaf. Sodium and chloride delivered to the shoot over time may accumulate in particular tissues and compartments. If the plant cannot sufficiently limit the concentration of harmful ions in sensitive regions, metabolic and ionic disturbances develop. Damage to older transpiring leaves can reduce total photosynthetic area, and reduced carbon delivery to roots can in turn compromise uptake and growth. Thus an initially local disturbance can have a whole-plant consequence that returns to aggravate the original problem.

Physiological inhibition can be reversible without requiring replacement of the affected cells. A stomatal pore that closes in response to a short dry period can reopen when conditions become favorable. An enzyme reaction that slows at a low but noninjurious temperature can accelerate after warming. A viable seed held in unsuitable conditions can resume germination when its requirements are met. In all three examples the measured rate may be close to zero at one time while the capacity for later function remains. A rate measurement is therefore not identical to a measurement of functional capacity.

Injury can also be repairable even when it is not immediately reversible. A plant exposed to excessive light may temporarily lose a fraction of its photosystem II reaction-center activity and subsequently replace damaged components. The observed recovery requires metabolism and time; it is not merely the instantaneous removal of the original disturbance. Repair implies that some structure or function was impaired, whereas reversible regulation can occur without such impairment. Both may contribute to the same observed recovery curve, and separating them experimentally requires measurements that distinguish functional state from structural damage.

Irreversible injury is injury that cannot be restored in the affected biological entity under the recovery conditions considered. Dead cells cannot regain viability after water is supplied. A killed leaf cannot resume photosynthesis, although the plant bearing that leaf may survive by producing new foliage. A sterile flower cannot regain its lost opportunity to set seeds merely because the next day becomes cooler, even if the plant as a whole remains healthy. It is therefore essential to specify whether irreversibility concerns a molecule, cell, organ, individual or a particular reproductive event.

The distinction between repair and replacement is especially important for modular organisms such as plants. A shoot apex can die while an axillary bud remains viable and later forms a new shoot. The reappearance of green leaves establishes recovery of whole-plant growth, not recovery of the dead apex. Similarly, a tree may lose distal branches after drought yet survive through the tissues that retained sufficient hydraulic and metabolic function. A survival assessment must define its criterion, and an account of physiological recovery should identify which tissue is being measured. Otherwise a newly formed organ can be mistaken for repair of the original organ.

Some cellular measurements help establish whether structural integrity has been impaired. If membrane permeability increases, ions can escape from tissue into a surrounding solution, raising its electrical conductivity. Electrolyte leakage therefore provides an operational indicator of membrane injury. However, the experiment must standardize the amount and handling of tissue, solution volume, incubation duration and temperature. Cutting a leaf disc can itself release solutes, and total electrolyte content differs among tissues. Comparing initial leakage with total leakage after membrane disruption helps normalize the result, but does not identify which membrane or molecular process was initially damaged.

Vital stains and observations of subsequent growth provide complementary evidence. A tissue may show reduced metabolism yet remain viable, and some viability assays depend on the metabolic activity of living cells. A weak metabolic stain can therefore be ambiguous when cells are temporarily inactive. An assessment combining membrane integrity, metabolic capacity and recovery is more informative than relying on one test. For plants undergoing programmed loss of a tissue, it is also necessary to distinguish ordinary developmental senescence from premature deterioration caused by the imposed treatment.

Temporal ordering helps identify primary effects. If a reduction in leaf water potential consistently precedes an increase in membrane leakage, the data are compatible with dehydration contributing to later injury. They do not by themselves prove that dehydration is the sole cause, since a third process could influence both measurements. If maintaining leaf water potential through an independent manipulation reduces later leakage under the same external treatment, the case for causal involvement becomes stronger. If leakage persists despite maintained hydration, additional processes need investigation. A careful answer moves from a pattern in time to a test of alternative mechanisms.

Recovery must be evaluated against an appropriate control. A plant treated for five days may resume its original rate of growth after the treatment, yet its untreated counterpart continued growing during those five days. Even complete recovery of current growth rate does not imply elimination of the accumulated biomass deficit. An experiment can measure the instantaneous rate, cumulative growth and final yield separately. These endpoints answer different biological questions. Restoration of immediate function concerns physiological resilience; the remaining cumulative loss concerns the history of exposure and its consequences.

A practical classification of observations is consequently more informative than a rigid division of all stress into two phases. A decrease in turgor that reverses promptly after rewatering suggests a reversible physical disturbance. Reduced stomatal conductance may represent active regulation, whereas persistent low conductance after hydration has recovered may involve altered signaling or injury. A marked rise in electrolyte leakage suggests impaired membrane function. A failure to regrow despite suitable recovery conditions raises concern about loss of viability. Each interpretation should be qualified by the measurement method and the specific tissue being examined.

5. Intensity, duration, rate of onset and dose–response relationships

A stress response depends on how far conditions depart from a relevant operating range, how long the departure lasts, and how rapidly it develops. Intensity describes the magnitude of a specified challenge, such as the external salt concentration or temperature above a selected reference. Duration describes the time over which the challenge acts. A sudden transition can produce a different response from a gradual transition to the same final intensity because the plant has different opportunities to adjust transport, metabolism and gene expression. A duration without an intensity and an intensity without a duration are incomplete descriptions of most treatments.

The classic graphical approach begins with a response variable plotted against environmental intensity. Suppose a student grows a species at several constant temperatures and measures the production of dry biomass over a fixed interval. Biomass production may rise from a low-temperature region toward a broad optimum and fall at higher temperatures. The optimum is the region that maximizes the chosen response under the specified conditions, not necessarily the optimum for every physiological process. Germination, pollen development, photosynthetic carbon fixation and root growth may have different thermal optima. A curve for net growth should not be interpreted as a curve for a single enzyme reaction.

When a stress increases in one direction from a suitable reference condition, the response may initially change little because protective regulation compensates. As intensity increases, performance can decline, and at high exposure viability may be lost. This familiar pattern is a useful conceptual model but not a universal curve. Different endpoints can have different shapes. A protective transcript may rise while growth declines, membrane leakage may remain low until injury develops, and survival may remain high over a range in which yield is reduced. A low-intensity stimulus may even improve a selected performance measurement in certain circumstances. Such stimulation does not imply that mild exposure is universally beneficial or that its consequences are beneficial for every organ.

It is tempting to speak of a single threshold at which stress begins. In practice a threshold is an operational boundary defined using a particular measurement. If a researcher defines a growth threshold as the intensity at which growth falls to ninety percent of the control, that threshold can differ from the intensity at which membrane leakage becomes detectable. It also depends on experimental precision and treatment duration. A biological response that is gradual may appear abrupt when the assay cannot resolve small changes. A threshold should therefore be reported with the variable, exposure protocol and decision rule that define it.

Dose–response studies can compare genotypes by examining the entire response curve rather than a single treatment. A genotype may perform better near the optimum but lose performance more rapidly when exposure increases. Another may have a lower maximum but retain function over a wider range. The ranking depends on the environment and on whether absolute or relative performance is of interest. A single concentration can conceal differences at lower or higher intensities. It can also lead to an erroneous claim of universal resistance when the measured advantage is limited to a narrow interval.

A relative performance index can be calculated as R = 100 × Xs/Xc, where Xs is the measured value under stress and Xc is the corresponding control value for the same genotype and duration. Xs and Xc must have the same units and Xc must be positive. R is expressed as a percentage of the control; the corresponding percentage reduction is 100 − R. If untreated plants produce 12 grams of dry mass and stressed plants produce 9 grams, R equals 75 percent and the reduction equals 25 percent. This normalization describes proportional retention of performance, not necessarily the amount of material produced.

Consider two genotypes. Genotype A produces 20 grams under control conditions and 12 grams under a moderate challenge; genotype B produces 10 grams under control conditions and 8 grams during the same treatment. The relative performance indices are 60 and 80 percent, respectively. B retains a greater fraction of its own control performance, while A produces more biomass in the stressed environment. Selection for crop productivity in that environment may favor A, whereas investigation of proportional sensitivity may focus on B. The comparison shows why a numerical index cannot substitute for a defined biological objective. It also shows why both control and stress values should be reported.

A half-response intensity, sometimes expressed as an EC50 in a dose–response study, is meaningful only when the effect is defined. In a simple inhibition experiment it may denote the treatment intensity producing a specified fifty percent effect relative to the chosen reference. In a fitted curve with nonzero upper and lower asymptotes, it often refers to the midpoint between those asymptotes rather than fifty percent of untreated performance. A lethal exposure estimated for half of a population is a different endpoint, commonly termed an LD50 if dose is appropriate or an LT50 if a temperature or time is being specified with an explicit convention. A student should identify the meaning from the experimental definition rather than infer it solely from the subscript.

To see why duration matters, suppose seedlings receive a moderate heat treatment for one hour or eight hours. At first they may induce protective responses while retaining membrane integrity. Continued exposure can consume reserves, impair proteins and accumulate damage even if the temperature remains unchanged. Conversely, the additional time might allow a plant to establish effective protection in some moderate conditions. Duration is not always equivalent to accumulating a fixed amount of injury per hour. The damage rate can change as the physiological state changes.

In simple teaching models, an accumulated exposure index may be written D = integral of S(t) dt, where S(t) is the nonnegative intensity above a defined reference and t denotes time. If the intensity is constant, D equals S multiplied by duration. For a temperature excess measured in degrees Celsius over hours, D has units of degree-hours. Two hours at five degrees above a reference and five hours at two degrees above it both give ten degree-hours. This is an exposure index, not a universal prediction of injury. If damaging reactions accelerate nonlinearly with temperature, if the reference is not biologically appropriate, or if acclimation and recovery occur, equal degree-hours can produce different effects.

A better conceptual model recognizes that the state of the plant changes over time. The current injury burden is influenced by the rate of damage formation and the rate of repair. If damage accumulates more rapidly than repair, injury increases. If a stressor is removed and viable tissues retain repair capacity, the injury burden may decrease. If the intensity is so high that it compromises repair machinery, the decline in repair capacity can accelerate injury. This reasoning explains why the duration needed to reach a particular degree of damage may vary greatly with intensity and prior history. It also explains why an intermittent stress can be different from one continuous exposure with the same total time.

A gradual onset provides an opportunity to modify physiological state before peak intensity is reached. Slow soil drying may permit adjustments in stomatal behavior, root development and cellular solute composition. Rapid transfer to a highly concentrated solution can impose an abrupt osmotic disturbance that does not allow the same sequence of responses. To distinguish onset rate from total exposure, a researcher must specify both the treatment history and the endpoint conditions. A gradual treatment may take longer and thus have a different total duration; the history needs to be included in interpretation rather than assuming the two treatments differ in only one respect.

The recovery interval between episodes can change the outcome of a repeated stress. During an interval without the stressor, repair and replenishment of reserves may occur. Certain induced proteins and regulatory states may also persist into the next episode, changing the response. However, incomplete repair may make a later episode more damaging. Repeated exposure therefore need not have a single directional effect. A comparison between one exposure and several exposures should match total treatment and elapsed time as far as the research question permits, include an appropriately scheduled control and measure the state of the plant before each episode.

6. Why developmental stage, genotype and environment alter sensitivity

Developmental stage is a major determinant of the biological consequences of a stress. A seed, a recently germinated seedling, a vegetative plant and a flowering plant have different requirements and different capacities for protection. Mature dry seeds of many species can tolerate substantial water loss as part of normal development. Germinating seedlings generally become more dependent on continuous water availability as cells expand and metabolism increases. This does not mean that every seed is desiccation tolerant: recalcitrant seeds of some species are damaged by drying, and even an orthodox seed can lose its tolerance during germination. The appropriate comparison therefore specifies both species and developmental state.

An actively expanding leaf contains cells that must maintain sufficient turgor and wall extensibility for expansion. A fully expanded leaf no longer depends on those processes to the same degree for increasing surface area, although its photosynthetic and transport functions still depend on viable hydrated cells. A stress imposed during expansion may therefore reduce final leaf area without producing the same immediate change in an already mature leaf. Measuring only final area cannot distinguish fewer cells from smaller cells or changes in developmental timing. A combination of growth trajectories and cellular observations is more informative.

The reproductive stage introduces endpoints that cannot be inferred from vegetative appearance. High temperature or water shortage during sensitive periods of pollen formation, anthesis or fertilization can reduce seed set even when leaves remain green. Later, restrictions on photosynthetic carbon supply or assimilate transport can alter seed filling. The yield of a crop can therefore respond to the timing of stress as much as to its average intensity over a season. An early stress that reduces leaf number may have a different effect from a late stress that reduces seed mass. Recording phenological events alongside environmental measurements helps identify the affected developmental process.

Tissue-specific vulnerability also follows from spatial organization. Roots encounter soil salinity, mineral conditions and oxygen availability directly; shoots experience radiation, atmospheric temperature and evaporative demand. Young growing tissues depend on the import of resources, whereas mature photosynthetic leaves often act as sources of assimilates. A loss of root function can reduce water and nutrient supply to the shoot even when the shoot is not directly exposed to the causal agent. Conversely, severe leaf damage can reduce carbon delivery to roots. Whole-plant stress physiology must therefore connect organ-specific measurements with the exchange of water, nutrients and assimilates among organs.

Genotype refers to inherited genetic constitution. Genetic differences can influence structural features, the abundance and regulation of transport proteins, developmental timing, metabolic capacities and the response to environmental signals. A genotype with a deeper root system may encounter a different effective water supply than a shallow-rooted genotype growing in the same field. A genotype with different membrane composition may respond differently at the same tissue temperature. These examples distinguish a genetically determined difference in exposure from a genetically determined difference in the ability to withstand a given internal disturbance.

Phenotype is the observable state produced through the interaction of genotype, environment and developmental history. Phenotypic plasticity is the ability of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. It can involve changes in morphology, developmental timing, physiology or metabolism. Not every plastic change is protective. A smaller leaf in a stressful environment may represent adaptive limitation of water loss, unavoidable restriction of expansion, or both. To determine whether a plastic response is advantageous, compare relevant outcomes in the environments in which the response occurs and, where possible, alter the response without altering unrelated traits.

A reaction norm plots the phenotype of a genotype across environmental conditions. If two genotypes have parallel reaction norms, their difference in the chosen trait remains constant on the plotted scale as the environment changes. If the curves differ in slope or cross, the environmental effect differs between genotypes on that scale. This is a genotype-by-environment interaction. Crossing curves are especially important because the ranking of genotypes changes. The absence of a crossing does not exclude interaction; different slopes can produce interaction even when one genotype has a higher value everywhere.

Suppose plants of genotype A produce 10 grams of dry biomass in favorable conditions and 6 grams in a stressful environment. Genotype B produces 8 grams and 7 grams, respectively. A is larger under favorable conditions, but B is larger under stress. On the absolute scale the treatment effects are a loss of 4 grams for A and 1 gram for B, giving a difference of 3 grams between the treatment effects. The crossing demonstrates that a ranking derived in a favorable growth chamber need not predict the ranking in a stressful field. Statistical support for an interaction requires replication and an estimate of variability; the numerical example illustrates the concept rather than proving significance.

Even individuals with identical genotypes can differ in sensitivity because of their growth history. A plant previously exposed to a moderate challenge may possess protective proteins or altered physiological properties when a later challenge arrives. Another individual of the same age may lack these changes. Differences in seed size, maternal environment or initial plant size can also influence subsequent performance. Controlled studies often use genetically similar material and standardize age, size and pre-treatment conditions, not because variation is unimportant but because the purpose is to identify its causes.

External conditions interact with each other in determining internal exposure. Air temperature alone does not determine leaf temperature; absorbed radiation, air movement and evaporative cooling affect the leaf energy balance. Soil water content alone does not determine plant hydration; the water retention properties of soil and the atmospheric demand for water matter. A salinity measurement in an irrigation solution does not directly specify the concentration inside a leaf cell. A well-described experiment measures biologically relevant variables rather than assuming a nominal treatment exactly represents the condition experienced by each tissue.

These considerations have practical significance for experimental design. A randomized comparison of genotypes under the same nominal treatment is valuable, but interpretation still requires baseline measurements and information about actual internal state. Genotypes of very different sizes can dry their pots at different rates if watering is stopped simultaneously. The larger plant might then experience a more severe decline in soil water availability. A comparison at matched soil water status or matched tissue hydration answers a different question from a comparison of time until wilting. Both can be useful provided the investigator states which aspect of resistance is being assessed.

7. Escape, avoidance, tolerance and resistance

Plants can persist in environments that periodically or continuously impose unfavorable conditions through several kinds of strategy. Classification becomes clearer when we ask where the causal chain is interrupted. A plant may complete a sensitive activity before the stressor becomes severe. It may remain in the environment but reduce the internal disturbance generated by external exposure. It may experience a substantial internal disturbance yet maintain viable tissue and function. It may also repair damage or replace affected tissue after an episode. These possibilities are neither mutually exclusive nor equivalent. An individual plant may rely on several of them over the course of a season.

Escape is the reduction of temporal overlap between a sensitive biological activity and an unfavorable period. An annual plant may germinate after rainfall, grow rapidly and complete seed production before a predictable dry season. The adult shoot need not survive the subsequent months of water shortage for the population to persist. Seeds or another surviving life stage carry the life cycle forward. Escape describes the timing relationship; seed protection during the unfavorable period is a separate property that may contribute to survival. An early-flowering annual is not necessarily more capable than a late-flowering annual of maintaining viable leaves at the same low tissue water potential.

The effectiveness of escape depends on the environment being sufficiently predictable and on the time required to complete the relevant developmental process. If rainfall stops unexpectedly before seed maturation, accelerated flowering may not be sufficient. If favorable conditions persist much longer than usual, a plant that flowers early may forgo opportunities for vegetative growth and greater seed production. A short life cycle can therefore confer an advantage in a seasonally stressful habitat while reducing potential yield in a longer favorable season. The value of the strategy depends on the distribution of environmental conditions rather than on the maximum severity of the stress alone.

Seasonal dormancy and the persistence of underground organs also illustrate how life stages can differ in exposure. A perennial may lose aboveground tissues during winter while protected buds remain near or below the soil surface. The active shoot does not maintain summer rates of metabolism throughout the winter; it is absent or inactive during the unfavorable period. Yet the protected buds may still require cold tolerance if their temperature falls substantially. Dormancy can contribute to escape from an active growth requirement, while physical insulation and cellular protection contribute to avoidance and tolerance. Assigning only one label to the whole organism can hide the mechanisms.

Avoidance means limiting the internal disturbance experienced during external stress. In drought physiology this is commonly called dehydration avoidance. Water can be retained through reduced loss, increased access to available water or a combination of these. Stomatal closure reduces the conductance for water vapor under otherwise comparable conditions. A thick or less permeable cuticle can reduce nonstomatal water loss. Root placement can allow access to deeper or spatially separated water supplies. These mechanisms are grouped together because they influence tissue hydration, although the structures and signals involved are different.

Avoidance should be distinguished from simply being exposed to a less severe environment by chance. A plant that grows in a shaded microsite may experience less heat than one in full sunlight, but the comparison alone does not establish a genetically controlled avoidance mechanism. An experiment that places genetically similar plants under a common external challenge and alters leaf orientation or transpiration can examine whether a trait changes tissue temperature. If the modified trait reduces internal exposure, it provides evidence of an avoidance contribution. The external environment and internal state both need measurement.

Transpirational cooling demonstrates that avoidance of one stress can aggravate another. Evaporation consumes energy, so a leaf that loses water can remain cooler than it otherwise would be. Maintaining stomatal opening during heat may reduce leaf temperature when sufficient water is available. During simultaneous drought, the same behavior may accelerate tissue dehydration. Closing stomata saves water but can reduce evaporative cooling and carbon dioxide uptake. A trait cannot be classified as unconditionally protective merely because it alleviates one component of stress in isolation. Combined challenges can alter the balance of costs and benefits.

In saline conditions, exclusion of a potentially harmful ion from sensitive tissues is a form of avoidance at the level of those tissues. Roots can limit net uptake or influence the delivery of ions to shoots. Retrieval from the transport stream may also reduce accumulation in leaves. The resulting advantage depends on which ion, organ and compartment are considered. A plant can have high total sodium in a leaf while keeping cytoplasmic sodium relatively low by placing much of the ion in vacuoles. At the cytoplasmic level this reduces exposure; at the whole-leaf level it can contribute to tolerance of high tissue salt content. The apparent classification changes because the reference compartment changes.

Tolerance refers here to the capacity to maintain viability or a defined function despite a substantial internal disturbance. A dehydration-tolerant tissue can withstand a lower hydration state with less injury than a sensitive tissue under comparable conditions. Cellular mechanisms may include changes in solute composition, stabilization of macromolecules and membranes, regulated metabolism and effective repair. Heat tolerance can involve protection and restoration of protein function at a given tissue temperature. The molecular details are developed in later stress-specific nodes; the foundational criterion is continued function or viability despite comparable internal challenge.

Avoidance and tolerance are best separated by choosing experimental comparisons at two levels. First, expose two genotypes to the same external treatment and measure the internal disturbance. A genotype that maintains a higher relative water content may avoid dehydration more effectively. Second, compare injury at matched internal water status. If that genotype also suffers less injury at the same water status, it possesses a tolerance advantage according to the endpoint. The second experiment must take account of how the matched state was reached, because a slowly dehydrated leaf and a rapidly dehydrated leaf can have different physiological histories even at the same measured hydration.

A simple illustrative experiment makes the distinction concrete. Three plants are grown under a common external drying treatment. At the end, A retains eighty-five percent relative water content and twenty percent electrolyte leakage; B reaches sixty percent relative water content and twenty percent leakage; C reaches sixty percent relative water content and fifty percent leakage. A experiences less dehydration than B and C. At the matched hydration state of B and C, B has lower leakage and therefore shows better retention of membrane integrity in this comparison. The fact that A and B have equal leakage does not demonstrate equal dehydration tolerance because their internal states differ. A separate matched-hydration experiment is needed for that comparison.

The broad term stress resistance is often used for the ability to withstand an environmental challenge, encompassing avoidance and tolerance and sometimes escape. In plant pathology and herbivory research, resistance is often used more narrowly to denote traits that limit infection, pathogen proliferation, herbivore attack or the extent of damage. Tolerance in that context denotes the capacity to retain performance despite a given burden or amount of damage. These conventions are compatible if their scope is stated explicitly. The same term should not be silently switched from a broad abiotic meaning to a narrow biotic meaning within a single experimental argument.

For example, one plant may accumulate a smaller bacterial population following inoculation than another plant under the same challenge. This is evidence of greater resistance to bacterial multiplication under those conditions, assuming appropriate controls. A different plant may support a large bacterial population but retain more growth than a sensitive plant with a comparable population. It may show greater disease tolerance. Neither conclusion follows from symptom severity alone. Symptoms can reflect pathogen action, host responses or both, and two hosts with similar symptoms can support different pathogen burdens. Resistance and tolerance should therefore be measured independently.

Herbivory provides an analogous distinction. A plant with deterrent surface structures may be consumed less, while a plant with abundant viable buds and stored reserves may regrow more effectively after comparable tissue removal. The first property can contribute to resistance and the second to tolerance. Strauss and Agrawal's review of herbivory tolerance emphasizes that compensation after damage can influence reproductive performance and that resistance and tolerance need not be mutually exclusive. A leaf damage percentage is an intermediate measurement, not a direct measurement of lifetime fitness. The amount of reproductive output produced after damage is often a more relevant ecological endpoint.

Compensation can be partial, complete or, in some circumstances, greater than the performance of undamaged controls. If undamaged plants produce one hundred seeds and damaged plants produce sixty, compensation is incomplete with respect to seed production. If both produce one hundred, compensation is complete for that measurement. If damaged plants produce more than one hundred, overcompensation may be observed under those experimental conditions. Such a result requires replication and appropriate controls and does not imply that herbivory is generally beneficial. Changes in branching, allocation and competition among growing tissues can contribute to compensation, but an apparent increase can also arise from initial differences between groups.

The difference between resistance and tolerance can matter beyond the plant individual. A disease-tolerant host may maintain productivity while supporting substantial pathogen populations. A resistant host may reduce pathogen reproduction or transmission, depending on the mechanism. These outcomes have different implications for crop protection and neighboring plants. Tolerance can be valuable without eliminating a pathogen; resistance can reduce the infectious burden without guaranteeing high yield if defense is costly or another limitation dominates. The biological objective determines which property is desirable.

A recovery strategy becomes important when prevention and tolerance do not eliminate all injury. Living meristems, stored carbohydrates and intact transport pathways can permit replacement of lost organs after an episode. This is often described as resilience at the organismal level. Resilience terminology varies: some definitions include the speed of restoration, whereas others emphasize the degree of restoration or persistence of system function. We will specify the measured recovery endpoint instead of assuming a universal resilience index. Rapid restoration of photosynthesis is not necessarily equivalent to rapid restoration of reproductive output.

The categories are consequently best understood as experimental questions. Did the organism reduce overlap with the challenge? Did it reduce the internal disturbance under the same external challenge? Did it retain function despite a comparable disturbance? Did it reduce the abundance or damage caused by an attacker? Did it restore or replace affected structures after the challenge ended? The answers may all be positive for a successful genotype, but each requires different observations. This framework prevents a common error in examination questions: treating all forms of successful survival as evidence for one cellular tolerance mechanism.

8. Acclimation, constitutive protection and evolutionary adaptation

A plant's response to stress is influenced by both its inherited characteristics and its prior experience. Constitutive protection is present before the particular challenge under investigation. Examples include structural barriers, developmental traits and proteins already present in a tissue. Inducible protection develops or increases following a signal or exposure. A constitutive trait can have a genetic basis and still vary with ordinary developmental or environmental conditions. Inducible traits likewise depend on inherited regulatory machinery. The distinction describes whether protection is already present or becomes established in response to the relevant treatment, not whether genes are involved.

Acclimation is a change in the physiological or developmental state of an individual in response to its environment that modifies its subsequent performance. In plant stress studies, the term often describes an acquired increase in capacity to withstand a later challenge. A moderate, nonlethal exposure may initiate responses that improve performance during a stronger exposure. These changes can persist for variable lengths of time after the first exposure ends. Acclimation may involve altered gene expression, protein abundance, metabolism, membrane properties and developmental organization. It occurs within the lifetime of an individual and does not require a change in population allele frequencies.

The terms acclimation and acclimatization are sometimes distinguished according to whether the environmental change is imposed experimentally or occurs under natural conditions. Other authors use them interchangeably. The essential contrast for this chapter is with evolutionary adaptation, not a rigid distinction between laboratory and field terminology. In an examination, the wording of a definition matters, but a within-individual physiological adjustment is not converted into heritable evolutionary change simply because it occurs outside a laboratory.

Basal tolerance is the capacity to withstand an initial challenge without a specific acclimating pretreatment. Acquired tolerance is the increased capacity measured following an appropriate pretreatment. These definitions require an explicit experimental protocol. A normally grown seedling has a particular developmental and environmental history, and that history can affect its baseline state. Thus basal tolerance is not an absolute condition in which all protective responses are absent. It is the capacity observed in the reference group that did not receive the designated conditioning treatment.

Heat provides a classical experimental demonstration. Seedlings can be assigned to a control group, a group receiving a moderate heat pretreatment alone, a group receiving a severe challenge alone and a group receiving the pretreatment followed by the challenge. After recovery, viability or elongation is measured at a standardized time. If the pretreated group performs better following the severe challenge than the challenge-only group, the data support acquired thermotolerance. The pretreatment-only group is required to reveal any cost or injury caused by the conditioning exposure itself. The untreated control establishes normal development over the corresponding interval.

Hong and Vierling used an Arabidopsis genetic screen based on seedling hypocotyl elongation to identify mutants defective in acquired thermotolerance. A mutation in the HOT1 locus affected the HSP101 protein. This approach connected an observed failure to acquire protection with a specific molecular component. It was more informative than observing that heat-induced proteins accumulate in pretreated plants because an altered gene allowed the investigators to test the functional contribution of a candidate protein. Other loci recovered in the screen also indicated that acquired thermotolerance is not adequately explained by one protein alone.

The inference from such a screen has boundaries. A mutation that prevents growth after heat could affect normal growth, perception, protection or repair. Normal performance in an unstressed control reduces the likelihood that the phenotype merely reflects a general growth defect. Restoration of the relevant function through an appropriate genetic complementation test strengthens the assignment of a causal role to the affected locus. Further comparisons can identify whether the mutant has impaired basal tolerance, acquired tolerance or both under the selected protocol. These controls make a genetic observation useful for physiological explanation.

HSP101 belongs to a class of molecular chaperones associated with the recovery of protein function following thermal damage. Protein aggregation can interfere with normal cellular activities, and chaperone systems can contribute to restoring soluble functional protein populations. Protection need not consist solely of preventing every damaging event: the ability to reverse or repair some damage can also improve survival. The detailed regulation of heat-shock proteins belongs to 6.H.06; here HSP101 is an example of why a measurable acquired phenotype should be connected to a tested molecular function.

Acclimation can be lost when the conditions that maintain it disappear. Cold-acclimated plants may gradually lose acquired freezing tolerance after return to warmer conditions, a process called deacclimation. A previously exposed plant can retain some altered response capacity for a limited interval, and a subsequent challenge may elicit a faster or stronger response. Such persistence is sometimes called stress memory or priming. These terms describe an altered response after prior experience and do not necessarily imply a permanent change in DNA sequence. An apparent memory can also reflect residual metabolites, proteins or unresolved damage; the mechanism must be tested.

Priming differs conceptually from a fully activated defense maintained at high intensity. A primed plant can display relatively modest changes before the next challenge yet respond differently when it arrives. In contrast, a plant that continuously produces a high abundance of defensive compounds may be in an already activated protective state. Measurements taken only after the second challenge cannot reliably distinguish these possibilities. Samples before the challenge, during the early response and after recovery help establish how the first exposure altered subsequent behavior. The detailed systemic defense examples are reserved for the concluding node of this subunit.

Evolutionary adaptation concerns inherited characteristics shaped by evolutionary processes across generations. Natural selection can increase the frequency of heritable variants that contribute to reproductive success in a particular environment. A population inhabiting a seasonally dry environment may contain genotypes whose development is timed to available moisture or whose tissues possess particular protective characteristics. If these traits contribute to greater reproductive success and are heritable, selection can alter the population over generations. The plants do not acquire the variants because they foresee their needs; variation and differential reproductive success are the relevant processes.

A phenotype observed in a natural population does not automatically establish local adaptation. Plants from a dry site may have smaller leaves than plants from a wetter site because their current growth conditions restrict leaf expansion. If both populations are grown in a shared environment, some differences may disappear, suggesting an environmental contribution. Persistent differences may indicate inherited or persistent parental effects. The common-garden approach reduces environmental variation but does not by itself prove that a persistent trait improves fitness in the native habitat. Demonstrating an adaptive advantage requires a relevant measure of performance.

A reciprocal-transplant experiment compares populations in their home and away environments. If plants from a dry habitat achieve greater reproductive success than plants from a wet habitat in the dry habitat, while plants from the wet habitat have an advantage in the wet habitat, the pattern supports local adaptation. Replication across sites or seasons is valuable because the habitats may differ in many factors besides water availability. A controlled environmental experiment can then investigate which factor contributes to the fitness differences. An observed difference in seed number must also be interpreted in light of survival, germination and other components of lifetime fitness.

Consider a teaching example in which seeds from two populations are raised for one generation in a common environment before their offspring are compared. In the dry treatment, dry-origin plants produce seventy seeds on average and wet-origin plants produce forty. In the moist treatment, dry-origin plants produce ninety seeds and wet-origin plants produce one hundred and thirty. These hypothetical results suggest that the relative success of the populations depends on environment. They do not identify the underlying genes, demonstrate that one particular trait caused the pattern, or rule out all persistent parental effects. Additional evidence would be required for those conclusions.

It is possible for evolutionary adaptation to modify the capacity for acclimation. A population may evolve differences in how strongly it responds to a conditioning stimulus, in the threshold for inducing protection, or in how long an induced state persists. The ability to acclimate can itself be a heritable trait, even though a particular acclimated state is acquired within an individual's life. This distinction is central to understanding phenotypic plasticity. Inherited machinery and environmentally induced outcomes are different levels of explanation rather than competing alternatives.

A parental exposure may influence offspring through seed provisioning, hormones or other persistent effects. Some such effects can extend beyond a single generation, but persistence in offspring is not sufficient proof of a DNA sequence change or an evolutionarily selected adaptation. Experiments that grow subsequent generations in common conditions and distinguish parental environmental effects from genetic variation are needed. Claims of stable transgenerational stress memory require particularly careful controls. For foundational examination purposes, do not equate an environmentally induced response, an inherited capacity to respond and an evolutionary change in population composition.

9. Measuring internal stress, injury and physiological performance

A successful experiment begins by distinguishing the condition imposed by the investigator from the response that is actually measured. An irrigation treatment describes the water delivered to a pot, not the water status of a leaf. A heating treatment describes the temperature of the chamber, not necessarily that of the tissue. Inoculation specifies the attempted introduction of an attacker, not the final burden established in the host. Measuring variables at several levels allows us to test the intervening steps. The following methods are introduced as tools of interpretation rather than as detailed laboratory procedures.

Relative water content, commonly abbreviated RWC, estimates how much water a tissue contains relative to the amount it can hold at a defined fully hydrated reference state. The original fresh mass is designated F, the turgid mass T and the dry mass D. Fresh mass contains both tissue solids and water. Subtracting dry mass estimates the mass of water in the sampled tissue. Subtracting dry mass from turgid mass estimates the water content at the chosen hydrated reference. The ratio of these two estimates is expressed as a percentage: RWC = 100 × (F − D)/(T − D). All masses must use the same units, and T must exceed D. The ratio itself is dimensionless.

For example, a leaf sample has a fresh mass of 0.72 grams, a turgid mass of 1.00 gram and a dry mass of 0.20 grams. Its estimated water mass at sampling is 0.52 grams and its estimated water mass at full hydration is 0.80 grams. Dividing the former by the latter and multiplying by one hundred gives an RWC of sixty-five percent. This does not mean sixty-five percent of the fresh mass is water. The ordinary water fraction on a fresh-mass basis would be 0.52/0.72, approximately seventy-two percent. The denominators describe different reference quantities, which is why the answers differ.

The hydrated reference is an operational state, not a theoretical maximum that can always be attained without changing the sample. If damaged tissue cannot rehydrate normally, if solutes leak during hydration, or if tissue changes dry mass during the procedure, the interpretation of RWC becomes less straightforward. Different leaves may also vary in anatomy and water storage. RWC compares water content relative to a sample-specific reference, whereas water potential relates to the energetic status of water. Two tissues with the same RWC need not have identical water potentials or turgor pressures because their solute composition and cell-wall properties can differ.

For exam reasoning, water potential is usually expressed in pressure units such as megapascals. A simple cellular treatment neglecting gravitational and matric contributions uses the relationship Ψw = Ψs + Ψp, where Ψw is water potential, Ψs is solute potential and Ψp is pressure potential. Solutes make Ψs negative relative to pure water under the reference conditions. Turgor in an ordinary hydrated living cell contributes positive pressure potential. In bulk soil, matric effects can be substantial, and in tall plants gravitational potential may be relevant. The simplified cell equation should not be applied indiscriminately to every component of the soil–plant system.

Suppose an illustrative living leaf cell has Ψs equal to −1.2 megapascals and Ψp equal to +0.5 megapascals. Its simplified Ψw is −0.7 megapascals. A second cell has Ψs equal to −0.8 megapascals and Ψp equal to +0.1 megapascals, giving the same water potential of −0.7 megapascals. Despite equal calculated water potentials, the cells have different turgor and solute potentials. The first cell has a more negative solute potential compensated by higher positive pressure. Measurements of water potential alone therefore do not establish whether osmotic adjustment has occurred or whether growth pressure has been maintained. To infer active adjustment, changes in solute content must be distinguished from the passive concentration of solutes caused by water loss.

Electrolyte leakage provides a different type of information. Ions conduct electricity in solution. When cell membranes are damaged or become more permeable, ions can move into a surrounding solution and increase its conductivity. An initial conductivity measurement C1 after a standardized incubation can be compared with a total conductivity measurement Ctotal after the tissue's accessible electrolytes are released. If the blank solution has conductivity Cb, an illustrative normalized leakage calculation is EL = 100 × (C1 − Cb)/(Ctotal − Cb). The three conductivities must have the same units, commonly microsiemens per centimeter, and the total must exceed the blank. The calculation assumes measurements are comparable in volume and temperature and that the final value represents the relevant total pool.

If C1 is 42, Ctotal is 162 and the blank is 2 microsiemens per centimeter, the corrected initial value is 40 and the corrected total is 160. The normalized leakage equals twenty-five percent. Without blank correction, the ratio would be 42/162 or approximately twenty-six percent. The difference is modest here but can matter when leakage is small or background conductivity is high. Some published protocols use a different normalization or measure conductivity increments separately; students should follow the explicitly defined formula rather than combine quantities from distinct methods.

Leakage is particularly useful when comparing tissue injury at matched internal stress. If two equally hydrated tissues are heated to the same measured temperature and one later releases a greater fraction of its electrolytes, the difference indicates greater loss of membrane integrity under the conditions of the assay. However, it does not establish that membrane damage caused every observed change in growth. Membrane leakage might follow another injury process, and differences in tissue anatomy or cutting damage can influence results. Unstressed controls, equivalent samples and independent viability or functional measurements strengthen the inference.

Chlorophyll fluorescence provides information about the behavior of photosynthetic apparatus. When chlorophyll absorbs light, excitation energy may contribute to photochemistry, be dissipated as heat or be emitted as fluorescence. The relative contributions change with the state of the photosynthetic system. In a standard dark-adapted measurement, F0 denotes minimal fluorescence when photosystem II reaction centers are predominantly open, and Fm denotes maximal fluorescence elicited by a saturating pulse. Variable fluorescence is Fv = Fm − F0. The ratio Fv/Fm = (Fm − F0)/Fm is used as an estimate of the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II photochemistry under the measurement assumptions.

For an illustrative leaf with F0 equal to 200 and Fm equal to 1000 instrument units, Fv equals 800 and Fv/Fm equals 0.80. For a second leaf with F0 equal to 300 and Fm equal to 750, Fv equals 450 and the ratio equals 0.60. The second value is lower, consistent with altered photosystem II performance or persistent energy dissipation. It is not a measurement of carbon dioxide fixation, and the ratio alone cannot identify the initiating cause of the change. An adequately dark-adapted leaf and a suitable saturating pulse are important assumptions. Some forms of photoprotection and injury can produce overlapping fluorescence effects, so recovery observations and complementary measurements are necessary.

Gas exchange can measure net carbon dioxide assimilation and stomatal conductance under specified light, temperature, humidity and carbon dioxide conditions. A decline in net assimilation accompanying reduced stomatal conductance is compatible with a limitation of carbon dioxide diffusion, but it is not proof that diffusion is the only limitation. Metabolic capacity, mesophyll conductance and other factors may also change. If the decline in assimilation persists after water status and stomatal behavior recover, additional limitations are worth investigating. Comparing several variables across time helps distinguish a rapidly reversible response from persistent impairment.

Water use efficiency must also be defined before comparing plants. At the leaf scale, one measure is the ratio of net assimilation A to transpiration E. Its units depend on the units chosen for the fluxes. Intrinsic water use efficiency uses A divided by stomatal conductance to water vapor, which is a different quantity and excludes some of the direct effects of atmospheric demand. Whole-plant water use efficiency may instead relate dry biomass or yield to the amount of water used. A higher ratio is not necessarily a higher carbon gain or greater yield because both numerator and denominator can decline.

For instance, suppose net assimilation decreases from 12 to 8 micromoles of carbon dioxide per square meter per second while transpiration decreases from 4 to 2 millimoles of water per square meter per second. The illustrative instantaneous assimilation-to-transpiration ratio rises from 3 to 4 micromoles of carbon dioxide per millimole of water. The plant is assimilating less carbon per unit time but using less water relative to the carbon acquired. The comparison cannot establish whole-season productivity because leaf area, nighttime water use, duration of the treatment and allocation of assimilates also contribute.

Pathogen abundance can be assessed using methods appropriate to the organism. Bacterial colony counts estimate viable culturable organisms under specified conditions, whereas nucleic acid measurements may include material from organisms that are no longer viable unless the method accounts for that distinction. Visual disease severity describes a host or tissue phenotype. It can be useful for estimating damage but does not directly replace a measure of pathogen burden. A strong experiment comparing biotic stress resistance and tolerance therefore records both the biological challenge and the host outcome, using a measurement suited to the pathogen or herbivore in question.

10. Growth, yield, survival and the dynamics of recovery

The outcome of a stressful episode can be described at different scales of time and biological organization. Growth refers to an increase in size, mass or another selected quantitative characteristic. Development refers to changes in organization, identity and stage, such as the transition from vegetative growth to flowering. These processes are connected but can respond differently to the environment. A plant may begin flowering earlier while producing less vegetative biomass, and a treatment that maintains leaf area may still delay reproduction. An evaluation of resistance should therefore specify whether its endpoint is growth, development, reproduction or persistence.

Dry mass is a useful growth measurement because it reduces the contribution of variable water content to fresh mass. A leaf that wilts during the day may lose fresh mass and regain it after rehydration without producing substantial new dry material. Conversely, accumulation of dry mass requires net retention of organic material and minerals. The dry mass of one organ can decline when stored compounds are mobilized to another organ. Whole-plant mass and organ-specific mass consequently answer different questions about resource use. Measuring both can reveal changes in allocation that are hidden in a single total value.

Absolute growth rate over an interval can be estimated as AGR = (W2 − W1)/(t2 − t1), where W1 and W2 are positive biomass measurements at times t1 and t2 and t2 exceeds t1. If masses are in grams and time in days, the rate is in grams per day. An illustrative plant growing from 4 to 10 grams over six days has an average AGR of 1 gram per day. A second plant growing from 8 to 14 grams over the same interval has the same absolute rate. However, the first plant added more mass relative to its initial size.

Relative growth rate can be estimated over an interval as RGR = [ln(W2) − ln(W1)]/(t2 − t1), equivalently ln(W2/W1)/(t2 − t1). The logarithm is natural and the biomass ratio is dimensionless; the rate has inverse-time units. For the first plant, ln(10/4)/6 is approximately 0.153 per day. For the second, ln(14/8)/6 is approximately 0.093 per day. The distinction between relative and absolute growth is important when genotypes differ in initial size. This finite-interval estimate is the average logarithmic growth rate; it does not require growth to be exactly exponential over the interval, although interpreting it as a constant rate throughout would require an additional assumption.

Under stress, relative growth rate can be negative if measured biomass declines. This may occur in particular organs when reserves are mobilized or tissue is lost, and in a whole plant under a sufficiently unfavorable carbon balance. A negative mass-based growth rate does not necessarily establish cell death, since respiration can consume stored resources without killing all tissues. Similarly, an increase in fresh mass following rewatering may primarily indicate rehydration rather than new biomass production. Examination questions that switch between fresh and dry mass should be interpreted with these differences in mind.

Yield describes the quantity of the harvested product, which may be grain, fruit, roots, leaves or another plant component. For grain crops, total reproductive output depends on the number of reproductive structures, the proportion that form viable grains and the mean mass of those grains. A stress during flowering may reduce grain number, whereas a later stress may reduce individual grain mass. A treatment that preserves vegetative growth is not guaranteed to preserve either component. A useful yield analysis records the developmental timing of the challenge and decomposes the final endpoint into biologically interpretable contributions.

Harvest index provides a simple measure of allocation to a harvested product. For a conventional grain-crop comparison, HI can be defined as grain dry mass divided by total aboveground dry mass at harvest, often expressed as a percentage. If grain dry mass is 24 grams and total aboveground dry mass is 60 grams, HI equals forty percent. A stressed plant might produce 15 grams of grain from 30 grams of aboveground dry mass, giving a higher HI of fifty percent but a lower grain yield. An increase in the ratio does not necessarily mean increased productivity. The denominator and the basis of mass measurement must be consistent across groups, and a different crop may require a different definition of the harvested component.

Survival is an endpoint of persistence rather than a measure of productivity. A common survival percentage is 100 multiplied by the number of individuals meeting a specified viability criterion divided by the original number subjected to treatment. If sixteen of twenty plants resume sustained growth following recovery, survival by that criterion is eighty percent. The result differs from a report that sixteen plants retained some green tissue immediately after treatment. A meaningful survival assay specifies both its recovery interval and criterion. Small sample sizes can produce large uncertainty even when a percentage is easy to calculate.

The distinction between survival and growth is not merely theoretical. Skirycz and colleagues compared Arabidopsis performance in different water-limitation scenarios, emphasizing that survival under severe drought does not predict growth maintenance under mild limitation. The result cautions against using a dramatic short-term survival assay as a universal screening method for crop performance. A plant that conserves resources through strong growth restriction may survive a severe episode but perform poorly in a season of moderate persistent water deficit. Conversely, a genotype that continues growing under moderate limitation need not survive the most severe drying treatment. The appropriate assay depends on the intended environment and objective.

A time course provides information that a single final observation cannot. Consider an illustrative measurement of net carbon assimilation normalized to a pretreatment value of one hundred. At the end of stress it falls to forty. One day after removal of the stressor it reaches seventy, and four days later it reaches ninety. A recovery fraction relative to the lost performance can be defined as Q = (Xr − Xs)/(X0 − Xs), where X0 is the chosen reference value, Xs is the value at the end of stress and Xr is the recovery value. The denominator must be positive for this particular convention. At one day, Q equals 30/60 or 0.50; at four days it equals 50/60 or approximately 0.83. Expressed as percentages, half and about eighty-three percent of the initial deficit have been regained.

Recovery fraction is not identical to performance relative to the reference. The value of ninety represents ninety percent of the initial assimilation measurement, but only about eighty-three percent of the deficit from one hundred to forty has been regained. If recovery exceeds the initial reference, Q can exceed one, and if deterioration continues it can become negative. These values can be meaningful rather than mathematically impossible. However, if an unstressed control changes during the experiment because of normal development, the pretreatment value may not be the most suitable reference for evaluating full recovery. A time-matched control can help separate the effect of stress from background changes.

Recovery rate also differs from the final extent of recovery. Two plants might both attain ninety percent of the control after one week while one attains that level after two days and the other approaches it slowly over seven days. The first spends less time with impaired performance. In a fluctuating environment, this may matter because a subsequent challenge can arrive before the slower plant has recovered. Measuring several time points permits comparison of the trajectory rather than relying on a single recovery percentage.

A delay following removal of the stressor can have several causes. Water must be transported to tissues, ion gradients may need restoration, protective states may persist, and damaged structures may require repair or replacement. A rapidly restored hydration measurement with slow recovery of assimilation suggests that water content alone does not explain the persistence of functional limitation. It does not uniquely identify the additional limitation. A measurement of stomatal conductance, photosynthetic fluorescence or tissue integrity can help distinguish alternative explanations. The logic is to test sequential links rather than assign a mechanism on the basis of a lag alone.

The cumulative consequences of delayed recovery can be represented by a performance deficit over time. In an illustrative comparison with a constant control value of one hundred, suppose treated plants perform at sixty for two days and eighty for another two days. Approximating the response as constant within each interval, the cumulative deficit is forty times two plus twenty times two, or one hundred and twenty percentage-point-days. If the measured variable were a rate of carbon acquisition in suitable units, integrating the rate difference could estimate the corresponding cumulative carbon deficit. Integrating percentages without a known control rate produces an index rather than a mass or amount of carbon. The model also assumes the selected reference is meaningful across the interval.

These calculations demonstrate a general principle: a stress phenotype has a history. Final survival, instantaneous function and integrated production measure distinct outcomes. The strongest experimental conclusions are obtained when a well-defined external treatment is linked to internal disturbance, injury, changes in growth and the capacity to recover. Not every study needs every measurement, but it must include the observations required to support its particular claim.

11. Designing experiments and integrating the plant stress response

A stress experiment is most informative when its question determines its design. If the aim is to discover whether a trait improves dehydration avoidance, the central measurements are external water availability and tissue hydration. If the aim is to test dehydration tolerance, injury or function at comparable internal hydration becomes essential. If the aim is to improve crop yield, the treatment regime and final harvested product must resemble the relevant agricultural problem. A single experiment may address several questions, but each inference must be supported by the variables measured.

A control group should differ from the experimental group in the intended treatment rather than in incidental conditions. Light, growth medium, nutrient supply, temperature, plant age and measurement time may all influence physiological variables. Random allocation of experimental units reduces the risk that pre-existing differences become systematically associated with the treatment. Blocking can account for predictable spatial variation, such as positions in a greenhouse. The investigator should distinguish independent biological replicates from repeated technical measurements on the same biological sample. Measuring five leaves from one plant does not automatically create five independent plant-level replicates.

Pseudoreplication is a particularly important problem in controlled environment experiments. Suppose all control plants occupy one growth chamber and all heat-treated plants occupy a second chamber. If the chambers differ in humidity or illumination, the treatment is confounded with chamber identity. Many plants within each chamber improve precision for variation among those plants but cannot by themselves separate a chamber effect from the heat effect. Replicated chambers, appropriate rotation or independent repeat experiments can improve the design, depending on the physical constraints and the hypothesis. The experimental unit is the smallest unit independently assigned to the treatment.

Sampling before, during and after exposure enables the distinction between basal state, response and recovery. A destructive measurement, such as determining dry mass, cannot be repeated on exactly the same intact plant at multiple times. Instead, independently assigned plants may be harvested at each time point. Non-destructive measurements can be repeated on the same individual, which permits analysis of its trajectory but introduces dependence among measurements from that individual. The distinction matters when assessing variation and statistical evidence. The examination principle is to match the design and interpretation to the structure of the data.

A combined-stress experiment can be organized as a factorial design. With drought and heat as two factors, four groups receive neither challenge, drought alone, heat alone or both. This makes it possible to estimate the effect of each treatment at each level of the other. On an absolute response scale, an interaction contrast is Ydh − Yd − Yh + Y0, where Y0 is the control response, Yd and Yh are responses to each stress alone, and Ydh is the combined response. All quantities must have the same units. A nonzero contrast describes departure from additivity on that chosen scale; statistical uncertainty must be assessed before claiming a reliable interaction.

Suppose illustrative final dry masses are 20 grams in the control, 15 under drought alone, 16 under heat alone and 8 under both. The individual losses are 5 and 4 grams, so an additive-loss expectation gives 20 − 5 − 4 = 11 grams for the combined group. The actual illustrative combined value is 8 grams. The interaction contrast is 8 − 15 − 16 + 20 = −3 grams. The combined response is lower than the additive expectation by 3 grams. This does not establish the molecular cause of interaction, and a multiplicative model for fractional retention would give a different expectation. The reference model and response scale must therefore be stated.

A multiplicative expectation for the same illustrative data uses the retention fractions 15/20 and 16/20, which are 0.75 and 0.80. If these fractions multiply independently, the expected combined mass is 20 × 0.75 × 0.80 = 12 grams. The observed mass of 8 grams is also below this expectation, but by 4 rather than 3 grams. The two calculations answer related but distinct questions. Researchers must choose a model based on the scientific context rather than select a model after observing the data solely to obtain a desired conclusion.

Combined stresses connect several topics in Unit 6. During hot dry weather, stomatal regulation affects both water loss and carbon assimilation. A decrease in transpiration can conserve water yet limit evaporative cooling. Carbon limitation alters the demand for products of photosynthetic electron transport, and respiration supplies energy for maintenance and repair. Nitrogen assimilation and protein synthesis influence the resources available for protective enzymes and chaperones. These connections show why stress physiology cannot be understood as an isolated biochemical pathway. Its purpose is to explain the consequences of a disturbance across coordinated physiological systems.

Plant hormones provide another layer of integration. Abscisic acid participates in many water-deficit responses, while other hormones influence growth and defense in context-dependent ways. Jasmonate and salicylic acid contribute to important forms of defense, but a hormone cannot be assigned one universal response independent of tissue, attacker and other signals. A physiological observation such as stomatal closure may arise in response to more than one challenge. Establishing the contribution of a signal requires evidence concerning its production, perception and downstream effect, not merely detection of the final phenotype. The detailed early signaling mechanisms are the subject of the next node.

Specialized metabolites can contribute to protection by influencing interactions with other organisms or modifying the chemical environment of a tissue. Their synthesis and storage draw on primary metabolism, and their distribution determines which organisms or compartments encounter them. A plant can constitutively possess a chemical barrier or increase defensive compound production following recognition of a threat. The study of stress physiology builds on the earlier account of specialized metabolism by asking whether the presence or induction of a compound changes exposure, injury, pathogen burden or plant performance.

An integrated experimental analysis may proceed by constructing alternative causal models. One model proposes that a genotype survives drought because its stomata close early and prevent substantial dehydration. Another proposes that its tissues tolerate low hydration with limited injury. A third proposes that both mechanisms contribute. Observing better survival after withholding water is compatible with all three. Measuring internal water status under the same external treatment tests the first contribution; comparing injury at matched hydration tests the second. An intervention affecting stomatal control or a protective cellular mechanism, with appropriate controls, can provide further evidence. Models become distinguishable when they generate different predictions for measurements or manipulations.

The same reasoning applies to biological defense. After equal inoculation, a genotype with fewer symptoms might restrict pathogen proliferation, tolerate a given burden, or differ in its baseline development. Quantifying pathogen abundance and measuring host performance relative to an uninoculated control help separate these explanations. If host performance is plotted against pathogen burden across several inoculation levels, a less negative slope can indicate greater tolerance on the chosen scale, provided burden is measured appropriately and the comparison accounts for baseline differences. Merely comparing symptom scores at one inoculum does not reveal that relationship.

The broad concepts in this chapter prepare the student for the subsequent molecular and physiological chapters. Stress perception asks how environmental perturbation produces a cellular signal. Oxidative stress examines how reactive molecules contribute to regulation and injury. Drought, salinity, temperature and flooding illustrate different initial disturbances and specific protective mechanisms. Plant immunity and herbivory extend the same causal framework to interactions with living organisms. Across all of these examples, the guiding questions remain the same: what is the challenge, what changes inside the plant, how is the change sensed and regulated, what happens to function, and what evidence separates the competing explanations?

Common misconceptions and examination traps

  1. A stressor is the same thing as tissue injury.

    Correction: The stressor is the challenge; internal strain, regulation and injury are different outcomes.

    Why it is tempting: The word stress is used loosely for both cause and consequence.

    Examination reasoning: When identical external exposure produces different hydration, compare internal state before comparing damage.

  2. Every reduction in growth is irreversible injury.

    Correction: Growth can be reversibly restricted while tissue remains viable.

    Why it is tempting: A low growth rate looks like deterioration even if regulation is intact.

    Examination reasoning: Use recovery, viability and leakage measurements to discriminate restriction from injury.

  3. The same salt concentration means the same intracellular sodium exposure.

    Correction: Uptake, transport and compartmentation influence actual cytoplasmic exposure.

    Why it is tempting: External concentration is easier to measure than internal ion distribution.

    Examination reasoning: Compare tissue and, where relevant, compartment-level ion status before inferring tolerance.

  4. All water-related stresses are dehydration.

    Correction: Flooding can primarily restrict oxygen supply, despite abundant water.

    Why it is tempting: The name water stress is mistaken for one mechanism.

    Examination reasoning: Identify the affected resource or flux: water availability in drought, oxygen delivery in waterlogging.

  5. Drought escape proves mature-leaf dehydration tolerance.

    Correction: Escape reduces temporal overlap; tolerance preserves function at matched internal strain.

    Why it is tempting: Early flowering and survival are both called drought resistance.

    Examination reasoning: Match developmental stage and hydration before comparing leaf injury.

  6. Higher RWC under the same drought proves greater cellular tolerance.

    Correction: Higher RWC supports reduced dehydration; tolerance requires an injury comparison at matched hydration.

    Why it is tempting: A healthy-looking hydrated plant is often labeled tolerant.

    Examination reasoning: Measure external challenge, internal hydration and injury separately.

  7. Lower disease symptoms always mean lower pathogen abundance.

    Correction: Symptoms reflect host damage and responses, not necessarily pathogen multiplication.

    Why it is tempting: Visual disease scoring is often used as a convenient screening measurement.

    Examination reasoning: Quantify pathogen burden as well as host performance to separate resistance from tolerance.

  8. Acclimation is genetic adaptation acquired by an individual.

    Correction: Acclimation is a within-lifetime change; evolutionary adaptation involves heritable variation and selection.

    Why it is tempting: Both can increase performance in a challenging environment.

    Examination reasoning: Identify the time scale and the unit of change: individual state versus population over generations.

  9. A constitutive trait is unrelated to gene regulation.

    Correction: Constitutive means already present relative to a chosen challenge; its abundance can depend on genotype and development.

    Why it is tempting: Constitutive and induced are confused with genetic and nongenetic.

    Examination reasoning: Compare baseline and post-treatment measurements under matched conditions.

  10. Stress-induced gene expression proves causal protection.

    Correction: Expression may be a response marker, compensation or consequence of injury.

    Why it is tempting: Induction is often presented alongside a protective phenotype.

    Examination reasoning: Use functional perturbation, controls and physiological outcomes to test necessity or contribution.

  11. Reactive oxygen species are always harmful.

    Correction: Controlled ROS can participate in signaling; excess or mislocalized ROS can cause injury.

    Why it is tempting: Oxidative damage is a prominent stress phenotype.

    Examination reasoning: Consider timing, location, regulation and injury measurements before assigning a role.

  12. A recovered growth rate erases the past growth deficit.

    Correction: Current rate may recover while cumulative biomass remains lower.

    Why it is tempting: Rate and final amount are confused.

    Examination reasoning: Compare trajectories and integrate the performance deficit; distinguish rate from total production.

  13. Equal RWC establishes equal water potential.

    Correction: RWC measures relative water amount; water potential depends on energetic state and its components.

    Why it is tempting: Both variables describe tissue water relations.

    Examination reasoning: Use the relevant formula and measure solute and pressure contributions where needed.

  14. A higher proportional retention always means greater stressed yield.

    Correction: Absolute and normalized performance may rank genotypes differently.

    Why it is tempting: Percentages obscure baseline differences.

    Examination reasoning: Report both untreated and stressed values, and state the selection objective.

  15. A single high-stress survival assay identifies the best crop under mild chronic stress.

    Correction: Survival and productive growth are distinct endpoints and depend on stress regime.

    Why it is tempting: Dramatic survival differences are easy to observe.

    Examination reasoning: Use the intended agricultural exposure and measure yield or growth directly.

  16. Equal intensity-times-duration indices imply equal biological injury.

    Correction: A simple exposure index omits nonlinear damage, repair and acclimation.

    Why it is tempting: A convenient numerical dose is treated as a universal biological model.

    Examination reasoning: Compare actual physiological trajectories rather than assuming equivalent injury.

  17. A higher harvest index proves higher grain production.

    Correction: Harvest index is a ratio; grain mass may decline while the fraction rises.

    Why it is tempting: A larger proportion is mistaken for a larger absolute amount.

    Examination reasoning: Calculate numerator and denominator separately before interpreting allocation.

  18. The two single-stress responses determine the combined-stress response by simple addition.

    Correction: Combined effects can depart from a defined additive or multiplicative expectation.

    Why it is tempting: Individual treatment effects are often extrapolated without testing interactions.

    Examination reasoning: Use a factorial comparison with neither, each alone and both; define the response scale.

B. Reduced stomatal water loss that maintains hydration. Avoidance reduces internal dehydration during external stress. The first option describes tolerance; the last two concern recovery or replacement.

A. Drought escape. Temporal separation of sensitive activity from the dry period is escape. The remaining traits are mechanisms that might act during exposure, which was not demonstrated.

Acquired thermotolerance following a moderate heat pretreatment in the same seedlings exemplifies:

C. Acclimation. The improved ability follows a prior exposure within individual plants. No population genetic change is required. Constitutive protection alone does not explain the pretreatment dependence.

  4. The dry-origin population is superior everywhere
Answer and explanation

C. The ranking depends on environment, consistent with local adaptation if persistent origin effects and fitness are appropriately tested. The dry-origin plants outperform in dry conditions and wet-origin plants in moist conditions. Further replication and controls are required to establish adaptation and its genetic mechanism.

36. Part C

All heat-treated plants occupy one chamber, all controls another. There are 40 plants per chamber. What is the principal design problem?

  1. Heat is not a real stress
  2. Chamber identity is confounded with treatment
  3. The leaf samples are too green
  4. Forty plants guarantee complete independence
Answer and explanation

B. Chamber identity is confounded with treatment. Plants within a single treatment chamber share chamber conditions. More plants cannot separate a chamber effect from the assigned treatment. Independent treatment units are needed.

37. Part C

Two treatments have equal temperature-excess exposure indices: 5 °C for 2 h and 2 °C for 5 h. Which conclusion is valid?

  1. Both give 10 °C·h, but nonlinear damage and acclimation may produce different outcomes
  2. Their biological injuries must be equal
  3. The second must always be lethal
  4. Duration can be ignored
Answer and explanation

A. Both give 10 °C·h, but nonlinear damage and acclimation may produce different outcomes. The simple integral of constant intensity is equal. It does not capture nonlinear temperature effects, changing repair capacity or pretreatment history.

38. Part C

In a salt experiment, early growth declines similarly under NaCl and a nonionic osmotic treatment with matched external water potential, but late injury is greater under NaCl. What is the best next inference?

  1. Late injury is unrelated to salt
  2. The nonionic treatment is guaranteed biologically inert
  3. Sodium is the only possible cause of all early responses
  4. Early osmotic effects are plausible; measure tissue ions and hydration to test the later salt-specific effect
Answer and explanation

D. Early osmotic effects are plausible; measure tissue ions and hydration to test the later salt-specific effect. The early shared response is compatible with osmotic disturbance. Later divergence suggests a salt-specific contribution but does not prove its identity; internal exposure and treatment-specific effects need measurement.

39. Part C

Two infected genotypes support similar bacterial burdens, but A retains 90% and B 50% of control biomass. What is suggested?

  1. B must have greater resistance because it grows less
  2. Both are uninfected
  3. A necessarily restricts bacterial multiplication more strongly
  4. A shows greater disease tolerance for this endpoint
Answer and explanation

D. A shows greater disease tolerance for this endpoint. The comparable burden allows comparison of host performance at similar challenge. The data do not establish a difference in resistance to multiplication.

40. Part C

After rewatering, a leaf rapidly regains RWC but assimilation stays low. Which next step best tests alternative causes?

  1. Measure only soil volume
  2. Declare all damage irreversible
  3. Compare stomatal conductance, fluorescence and other relevant functional measurements during recovery
  4. Conclude that water status never mattered
Answer and explanation

C. Compare stomatal conductance, fluorescence and other relevant functional measurements during recovery. Hydration recovery does not identify why assimilation remains low. Measurements of diffusion and photosynthetic performance, alongside injury and recovery evidence, can distinguish candidate mechanisms.

41. Part C

A genotype has control biomass 10 g and stressed biomass 6 g; another has 8 g and 7 g. On the absolute mass scale, how large is the difference between their stress-induced losses?

  1. 3 g
  2. 1 g
  3. 5 g
  4. 0 g
Answer and explanation

A. 3 g. The losses are 4 and 1 g, differing by 3 g. The crossing reaction norms suggest genotype-by-environment dependence; variability is needed for statistical inference.

42. Part C

A control performs at an index of 100. A treated group performs at 60 for two days and 80 for two days. Using constant-within-interval approximation, what is the integrated deficit?

  1. 40 percentage-point-days
  2. 120 percentage-point-days
  3. 80 percentage-point-days
  4. 280 percentage-point-days
Answer and explanation

B. 120 percentage-point-days. The first interval loses 40×2 = 80, the second 20×2 = 40, totaling 120. The quantity is an index, not a mass of carbon unless the underlying rate and units are known.

43. Part C

An annual flowers before terminal drought, while a late-flowering line remains vegetative. Which additional test best compares their mature-leaf dehydration tolerance?

  1. Measure flowering date again
  2. Observe survival without determining internal hydration
  3. Compare injury at matched leaf hydration and comparable developmental stage
  4. Count only seeds produced before drought
Answer and explanation

C. Compare injury at matched leaf hydration and comparable developmental stage. Flowering timing tests escape. Matched internal hydration and comparable tissue state are required to isolate tolerance, with treatment history controlled.

44. Part C

After a mild conditioning exposure, plant group P responds faster to a later challenge than naïve group N. Before challenge, P has elevated protective protein abundance. What can be concluded?

  1. A permanent DNA mutation has been proven
  2. Prior exposure altered response capacity; residual protection and priming need further distinction
  3. The effect cannot involve previous exposure
  4. All offspring must inherit the state
Answer and explanation

B. Prior exposure altered response capacity; residual protection and priming need further distinction. The response depends on history. Persistent protein abundance can explain at least part of the altered state, so faster response does not establish permanent genetic change or a specific memory mechanism.

45. Part C

During heat alone, open stomata keep leaves cooler. During heat plus drought, the same stomatal behavior lowers tissue hydration. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. Avoidance and tolerance are identical
  2. All stress combinations are additive
  3. A mechanism can reduce thermal exposure while increasing dehydration risk
  4. Stomatal opening has no physiological effect
Answer and explanation

C. A mechanism can reduce thermal exposure while increasing dehydration risk. Transpiration contributes to evaporative cooling but consumes water. The benefit of the trait depends on water supply and the combined environmental challenge.

46. Part C

Twenty plants are treated, and sixteen resume sustained growth after a defined recovery period. Eight of those survivors have poor seed set. Which conclusion is correct?

  1. Survival by the defined criterion is 80%; reproductive recovery is a separate endpoint
  2. The treatment killed all plants
  3. All survivors have full reproductive recovery
  4. Survival is 40% because eight have poor seed set
Answer and explanation

A. Survival by the defined criterion is 80%; reproductive recovery is a separate endpoint. Sixteen of twenty meet the stated survival criterion. The seed-set result concerns reproduction, not the definition of survival.

47. SET / integrated

Assertion: A plant can exhibit reduced growth without irreversible tissue injury. Reason: Regulated restriction of expansion can occur while cells remain viable.

  1. Both true, but unrelated
  2. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

B. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. A viable plant may restrict growth as part of regulation or reversible inhibition. The reason directly supports the assertion.

48. SET / integrated

Assertion: Equal leaf RWC guarantees equal leaf water potential. Reason: Solute composition and tissue properties can differ at a given hydration.

  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Both true
  3. Both false
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

A. Assertion false, reason true. RWC and water potential are different physical quantities. Solute and pressure components can differ at the same relative water content.

49. SET / integrated

Assertion: An adapted population can possess a heritable capacity for acclimation. Reason: Natural selection can act on genetic variation in the response to an environment.

  1. Both false
  2. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

B. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion. Acclimation is acquired within individuals, but the machinery and capacity for acclimation can vary genetically and be subject to selection.

50. SET / integrated

An illustrative plant has pretreatment index 80, end-stress index 20 and recovery index 65. Calculate its recovery fraction.

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.75
  3. 0.81
  4. 0.56
Answer and explanation

B. 0.75. The regained deficit is 65−20 = 45, and the initial deficit is 80−20 = 60. Thus 45/60 = 0.75. The 0.81 distractor uses the reference value rather than the deficit.

51. SET / integrated

Which pair of statements is correct? I. A high stress-induced transcript alone proves tolerance. II. A matched-burden comparison can reveal disease tolerance. III. A time-matched control aids recovery interpretation. IV. All stress-induced cell death is beneficial.

  1. I, II and III
  2. I and IV only
  3. II and III only
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

C. II and III only. II and III identify useful experimental comparisons. A transcript does not establish benefit, and cell death can be detrimental or even favor particular attackers.

52. SET / integrated

A treatment causes fresh mass to rise immediately after rewatering while dry mass stays unchanged. What does this most directly suggest?

  1. Rapid production of new dry biomass
  2. Increased pathogen resistance
  3. Rehydration rather than demonstrated new dry biomass
  4. Population-level adaptation
Answer and explanation

C. Rehydration rather than demonstrated new dry biomass. Fresh mass includes tissue water. A rapid increase without a dry-mass change is consistent with water uptake and does not establish net biomass synthesis.

Revision and mastery

Chapter summary and high-yield concepts

  • Disturbance: An external challenge can produce different internal strain in different plants. Define both before comparing performance.
  • Homeostasis: Regulatory changes can protect internal function but impose costs on growth and resource acquisition.
  • Injury: Reversible inhibition, repairable damage and irreversible cell death have distinct recovery implications.
  • Strategies: Escape reduces temporal overlap, avoidance reduces internal strain, tolerance preserves function at comparable strain, and resistance in biotic studies can mean reduced attacker burden.
  • Acclimation and adaptation: An individual can acquire protection without a genetic change; inherited capacity for acclimation can evolve across generations.
  • Quantitative analysis: RWC, leakage, fluorescence, growth rate, yield and recovery measure different properties. Use the correct reference, units and assumptions.
  • Experimental interpretation: Use matched comparisons, independent replication, time-matched controls and factorial designs to test causal alternatives.
