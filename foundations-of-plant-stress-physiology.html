1. The biological problem: environmental change and plant performance

A young wheat plant growing in a field cannot move into the shade when the afternoon becomes hot. Nor can it leave a patch of soil when the concentration of dissolved salts rises around its roots. Yet it is not merely a passive victim of its surroundings. Its leaves can alter their orientation and gas exchange, its roots can change the regions in which they grow, and its cells can adjust metabolism. The scientific problem is to explain how these responses protect living functions, when protection is sufficient, and why it sometimes fails. Stress physiology begins with that problem rather than with a catalogue of stress-induced genes.

The ordinary operation of a plant already involves continual adjustment. During the morning, light intensity increases and photosynthesis becomes more active. Water evaporates from moist cell walls inside the leaf and passes to the atmosphere through stomata. Roots absorb water from soil and transport it toward the shoot. At night the balance between carbon gain and respiratory carbon expenditure changes. These repeated transitions are not necessarily harmful. Plants have regulatory systems that accommodate the ordinary variation characteristic of their habitats. An environmental change becomes important as a stressor when it challenges those regulatory systems or impairs functions under the conditions being considered.

The word stressor denotes the causative environmental agent or condition. A reduction in soil water availability, an unusually high temperature, excessive salt, and invasion by a pathogenic fungus are examples. The physiological state produced by exposure may be called stress, while the measurable departure of a biological variable from its reference condition can be called strain. Terminology differs among textbooks and researchers, particularly concerning whether stress names the external factor or the internal condition. We shall keep cause, internal disturbance, response and injury separate even when the literature uses the same word for more than one of them. The distinction is useful because the same external challenge can induce different internal disturbances in different organisms.

Imagine two plants exposed to the same dry air. One closes its stomata promptly and retains much of its leaf water. The other loses water more rapidly, and its leaf cells lose turgor. The external vapor pressure deficit may be identical, but the internal water deficit is not. Now consider a third plant whose leaves reach the same low water content as the second plant but continue to retain membrane integrity. Its tissues may withstand dehydration more successfully. Without measuring both exposure and internal state, an experiment cannot reliably distinguish protection from reduced exposure and protection in the presence of exposure.

Plant performance is not represented by one universal number. A leaf may remain green after a period of water shortage while its expansion rate is very small. A plant may continue growing during moderate heat yet produce sterile pollen when the temperature rises at flowering. A seed may cease visible development during unfavorable conditions and germinate successfully when conditions improve. Growth rate, carbon assimilation, reproduction, tissue integrity and survival are related but not interchangeable. A sound stress diagnosis identifies a relevant function and a meaningful reference condition.

The reference condition itself requires thought. A temperature of 15 degrees Celsius can be suitable for a species from a cool habitat and detrimental to a tropical species. The significance of darkness differs between a buried seed and an expanded photosynthetic leaf. Even within one plant, the temperature and oxygen conditions tolerated by a mature leaf can differ from those tolerated by an actively dividing meristem. Stress is therefore relational rather than an intrinsic property of a single numerical value. Statements such as a temperature is stressful must specify the organism, tissue, developmental context, duration and measured consequence.

A useful framework treats the plant as an open system exchanging energy and matter with its surroundings. It absorbs photons, water and nutrients, exchanges gases, produces organic molecules and releases heat. The maintenance of living organization requires energy expenditure. A disturbance can alter an incoming resource, change the rate of an outgoing flux, interfere with a metabolic reaction or destroy a physical barrier. An excess is sometimes as consequential as a shortage: too much light can overload photochemistry, too much water can restrict oxygen supply to roots, and too much of an essential ion can be toxic.

Consider how this connects to the earlier chapters on photosynthesis and transport. The rate at which a chloroplast uses absorbed energy depends on the availability of carbon dioxide and the ability of metabolism to consume ATP and reducing equivalents. A dry atmosphere can lead to stomatal closure, which limits carbon dioxide entry. A leaf may then absorb more light than its restricted carbon metabolism can use. An initial disturbance of water balance consequently becomes a challenge to chloroplast energy balance. This is one reason why seemingly different stresses converge on several common protective processes, although their initiating events are not identical.

The history of the subject explains why definitions matter. Earlier physiological work often compared injured plants with healthy controls and focused on the environmental conditions under which functions declined. Jacob Levitt's influential treatment of plant environmental stress organized the subject around external challenges, internal strain and mechanisms of resistance. Later experimental work expanded the explanation to include rapid regulation, gene expression and the costs of protection. This progression did not make the older physiological measurements obsolete. Molecular information becomes biologically interpretable only when it is related to the performance and physical condition of the organism.

An exam question that reports a stress-responsive transcript but no physiological measurements illustrates the distinction. The transcript may identify an early response, a compensatory reaction, an indicator of injury or a consequence of slowed development. Its presence establishes neither that the corresponding protein is active nor that the response improves survival. To investigate function, compare a perturbation of the relevant gene with appropriate controls and examine the internal stress state and biological outcome. The resulting explanation should connect the molecular event with the actual problem the plant experiences.

2. Abiotic and biotic disturbances

An abiotic stress originates principally from a nonliving environmental factor. Water deficit, excessive salinity, high and low temperature, oxygen shortage, excessive irradiance and unfavorable mineral conditions belong to this category. A biotic stress arises from interactions with living organisms, such as pathogenic bacteria, fungi, viruses, nematodes and herbivorous insects. Competition with neighboring plants can also create resource limitation, although the immediately experienced limitation may be a shortage of light, water or nutrients. The distinction classifies the origin of the disturbance; it does not imply that every abiotic response differs from every biotic response.

Water availability is governed by more than the amount of liquid present. Water moves according to differences in water potential, a measure of the thermodynamic tendency of water to move. Dry soil can make uptake difficult because water becomes held more strongly by the soil matrix. Soil containing abundant dissolved salts can also have a low water potential even while it appears wet. On the atmospheric side, dry air can promote rapid evaporation. The plant must therefore balance the supply of water through roots and xylem with loss from exposed surfaces. The detailed components of water potential and the transport pathways belong to 6.F; the central stress concept here is that exposure is not synonymous with the water content of the soil.

Salinity illustrates an additional distinction. Dissolved salts reduce the water potential of the external solution and may therefore limit the initial uptake of water. Over longer periods, particular ions can accumulate in tissues and disrupt metabolic and ionic balance. The two components need not arise with the same timing or severity. If a plant experiences sodium chloride and a nonionic osmotic treatment adjusted to a comparable external water potential, a shared early response is consistent with a common osmotic component. Later injury specific to the salt treatment may involve ions. Such a comparison is informative but not perfect: nonionic osmotic agents can have other effects, and equal external water potential does not ensure equal internal hydration.

Temperature acts directly on reaction rates, molecular motion and the physical state of membranes. Warming within a suitable range can accelerate processes, but beyond that range destabilization of proteins, changes in membrane behavior and imbalances among metabolic pathways become important. Low temperature slows many reactions, and freezing adds the possibility of ice formation and water redistribution. Chilling injury can occur without ice, particularly in chilling-sensitive species. Thus heat, chilling and freezing should not be treated as opposite points on a single linear damage scale. Their mechanisms overlap in some respects and differ in others.

An oxygen deficit can occur when soil pores fill with water. Oxygen diffusion through water is much slower than through air, so the rate of oxygen delivery to respiring roots can become insufficient. Mitochondrial respiration is constrained, and cells may increase their dependence on glycolysis and fermentation to maintain a limited supply of ATP. A flooded root experiences an energy problem even though water is abundant. This example prevents an overly simple classification in which all unfavorable water conditions are assumed to be forms of dehydration. The fate of the shoot depends partly on how well roots retain transport functions and how the plant redistributes resources.

A pathogen creates disturbances by establishing itself in or on plant tissue, obtaining resources, altering cell functions or destroying living structures. A biotrophic pathogen depends on living host cells for much of its nutrition, whereas a necrotrophic pathogen can exploit killed tissue. These distinctions help explain why death of a host cell is not invariably an effective defense. A virus differs from a fungus in its mode of reproduction and dependence on host-cell machinery. Herbivorous insects remove tissue, withdraw cell contents or phloem sap, and can deliver signals or introduce pathogens. An examination question should identify the particular interaction rather than assume every biological attacker produces the same type of injury.

The plant response can begin before any visible symptom appears. Molecules from a microorganism may be recognized by receptors on the cell surface, and damaged host tissue may release signals that activate protective processes. These processes form part of innate immunity, a pre-existing capacity to recognize and respond to biological threats. Plants do not possess the vertebrate system of antibody-producing lymphocytes. Their defenses combine structural barriers, chemical protection and regulated cellular responses. Later chapters explain the molecular organization of pattern-triggered and effector-triggered immunity; this introduction establishes why the response must be interpreted relative to both the attacker and the host.

The distinction between a pathogen and an organism that is simply present is also essential. Roots normally interact with diverse microorganisms, some of which provide benefits or modify plant growth. A microorganism detected near a root is not automatically a pathogen. Evidence of a detrimental interaction requires appropriate observations, such as altered function, tissue invasion or reproducible disease symptoms under defined conditions. Similarly, a defense-associated signal can be induced during interactions that do not ultimately cause disease. Recognition and successful infection are different events.

Abiotic and biotic stressors frequently coexist. High temperature can influence pathogen multiplication and plant defense, while herbivore damage may alter water loss and carbon availability. A droughted plant may have fewer resources for growth or defense, but this does not establish that drought always increases disease. Changes in tissue chemistry, pathogen life cycle, microclimate and plant signaling can lead to different outcomes. Combined-stress experiments must measure the response to each challenge alone and to both together. This permits the investigator to ask whether the combined effect differs from a defined expectation rather than merely noting that two adverse conditions occurred.

A classification is useful when it directs measurement. For low soil water availability one may examine leaf water status, conductance and growth; for heat one may examine tissue temperature, viability and reproductive function; for infection one may examine pathogen abundance, damage and host performance. Some measurements are shared, such as membrane integrity, biomass or photosynthesis. But a shared endpoint does not identify the original disturbance. When a salt-treated plant shows reduced photosynthesis, the result alone does not reveal whether carbon dioxide diffusion, photochemical damage, metabolic inhibition or a combination caused the reduction.

3. Homeostasis, regulatory responses and the cost of protection

Homeostasis means the maintenance of internal conditions within ranges compatible with living function despite variation in the surroundings. A plant does not maintain every variable at a perfectly constant value. Guard-cell turgor, soluble carbohydrate concentration and hormone abundance can change substantially over a normal day. A regulated response often involves changing one variable to protect another. Closure of stomata, for example, lowers the conductance of the pore and can reduce water loss, while simultaneously restricting diffusion of carbon dioxide. The change in conductance may be a means of preserving tissue hydration rather than an indication that regulation has failed.

A feedback system contains a regulated variable, a way of detecting relevant changes and an effector capable of modifying a process that influences that variable. Negative feedback describes a relationship in which the response opposes the original deviation. If water loss lowers leaf water status and the resulting response reduces further water loss, this has a negative-feedback character. It does not require a single central control organ. Local sensing and communication among tissues can jointly produce regulation. The existence of feedback also does not mean that the regulated variable returns immediately to its original value; a persistent external disturbance may produce a new operating state.

Some responses have a feedforward component. A plant can alter stomatal behavior in response to environmental information before leaf water content has fallen appreciably. This may reduce the magnitude of a later disturbance. Circadian regulation of metabolism can prepare tissues for predictable transitions between day and night. Anticipatory regulation is not conscious prediction; it is the operation of inherited and environmentally responsive biochemical systems. Distinguishing feedback from feedforward reasoning requires observing the order of events and testing whether an internal change is necessary to elicit the response.

At the cellular level, membrane transport is a major contributor to homeostasis. The plasma membrane separates the cytoplasm from the external medium, while the tonoplast separates the cytoplasm from the vacuole. Selective transport allows cells to maintain suitable concentrations of ions and metabolites in distinct compartments. Proton pumps use energy to build gradients that can drive coupled transport. If a disturbance reduces ATP supply or increases ion leakage, the gradients may become difficult to maintain. Protection can involve reducing leakage, altering transport activity or changing the demand on energy metabolism. These outcomes are meaningful only when related to the affected compartment.

Metabolic networks also depend on a balance between production and consumption. During photosynthetic carbon limitation, the supply of reducing power may exceed the capacity of downstream reactions to consume it. Photoprotective processes can dissipate excess excitation energy and reduce the probability of damaging reactions. Reactive oxygen species are not simply waste products; at controlled levels and locations they participate in signaling. When formation exceeds the capacity for regulation and detoxification, oxidative injury may develop. The subsequent node on oxidative stress explains individual species and enzymatic protection, while the present chapter emphasizes the difference between a regulated signal and a damaging imbalance.

A physiological response can be protective, harmful or neutral with respect to a specified outcome. Reduced leaf expansion during moderate water shortage may preserve resources and limit the development of additional transpiring surface. It also reduces the area available to capture light later. If the shortage is transient, conserving water may be beneficial; if the shortage is mild and persists for an entire growing season, the accumulated cost of reduced leaf area can be substantial. It is therefore misleading to label every stress-induced growth reduction as injury or every growth-promoting response as protection.

The costs of protection include energy, material and opportunity. Synthesizing protective proteins consumes amino acids and requires energy for transcription and translation. Producing specialized metabolites diverts precursors that could participate in other functions. Repairing damaged membranes requires components and metabolic activity. Some changes, such as stomatal closure, have an opportunity cost even without large construction expenditure because they reduce carbon acquisition. Whether the expenditure improves lifetime performance depends on the threat and on the capacity of the plant to recover. The resources committed to defense should not be imagined as a fixed budget independent of metabolism: successful protection can preserve future resource acquisition, thereby increasing the budget available later.

This distinction clarifies a familiar experimental result. A genotype that accumulates more protective solute during drought may maintain better growth, but accumulation alone is not proof that the solute caused protection. Higher solute abundance could arise because the genotype has greater initial metabolic reserves, because water loss concentrates existing solutes, or because it experiences more severe internal stress. A causal test needs a relevant genetic or physiological perturbation, direct measurement of water status, and a comparison of function at comparable conditions. A correlation can guide investigation without resolving the mechanism.

Regulation has limits. Pumps cannot preserve a membrane gradient indefinitely if the membrane becomes highly permeable, and repair cannot restore tissue that has lost viability. A plant may sustain essential functions at the expense of nonessential growth for some time, yet ultimately exhaust its reserves or suffer accumulating damage. The existence of stress-response pathways is therefore not a guarantee of resistance. One goal of stress physiology is to determine the range of environmental conditions in which regulation succeeds and to identify the transitions at which maintenance, repair and recovery become inadequate.

4. Primary disturbance, secondary injury and reversibility

When a plant is exposed to an unfavorable condition, not all of the resulting changes occupy the same position in the causal sequence. Primary injury refers to injury directly associated with the initial action of the stressor, whereas secondary injury develops because the initial disturbance disrupts other processes. The distinction is useful for tracing mechanisms but depends on the causal level of the explanation. In heat-stressed tissue, thermal destabilization of a protein can be direct, while metabolic failure arising from loss of that protein's activity can be indirect. The latter may eventually produce membrane injury through changes in energy supply or redox balance. A membrane protein can itself be a primary target of heat, so the word membrane does not automatically designate secondary injury.

Dehydration provides a particularly clear sequence. A decline in the availability of water alters the tendency for water to move into roots. If supply becomes smaller than loss from the shoot, leaf water status falls and turgor can decline. Leaf cell expansion depends in part on pressure exerted against a yielding cell wall, so loss of turgor can reduce expansion without killing cells. As dehydration becomes severe, the physical and chemical environment within cells changes. The increased concentration of solutes, altered membrane organization and disturbance of metabolism may contribute to actual tissue injury. The earliest reduction in expansion and the later destruction of cellular structures should not be regarded as the same event.

A similar distinction applies to salinity. In the short term a lower external water potential can reduce the water status and expansion of a young leaf. Sodium and chloride delivered to the shoot over time may accumulate in particular tissues and compartments. If the plant cannot sufficiently limit the concentration of harmful ions in sensitive regions, metabolic and ionic disturbances develop. Damage to older transpiring leaves can reduce total photosynthetic area, and reduced carbon delivery to roots can in turn compromise uptake and growth. Thus an initially local disturbance can have a whole-plant consequence that returns to aggravate the original problem.

Physiological inhibition can be reversible without requiring replacement of the affected cells. A stomatal pore that closes in response to a short dry period can reopen when conditions become favorable. An enzyme reaction that slows at a low but noninjurious temperature can accelerate after warming. A viable seed held in unsuitable conditions can resume germination when its requirements are met. In all three examples the measured rate may be close to zero at one time while the capacity for later function remains. A rate measurement is therefore not identical to a measurement of functional capacity.

Injury can also be repairable even when it is not immediately reversible. A plant exposed to excessive light may temporarily lose a fraction of its photosystem II reaction-center activity and subsequently replace damaged components. The observed recovery requires metabolism and time; it is not merely the instantaneous removal of the original disturbance. Repair implies that some structure or function was impaired, whereas reversible regulation can occur without such impairment. Both may contribute to the same observed recovery curve, and separating them experimentally requires measurements that distinguish functional state from structural damage.

Irreversible injury is injury that cannot be restored in the affected biological entity under the recovery conditions considered. Dead cells cannot regain viability after water is supplied. A killed leaf cannot resume photosynthesis, although the plant bearing that leaf may survive by producing new foliage. A sterile flower cannot regain its lost opportunity to set seeds merely because the next day becomes cooler, even if the plant as a whole remains healthy. It is therefore essential to specify whether irreversibility concerns a molecule, cell, organ, individual or a particular reproductive event.

The distinction between repair and replacement is especially important for modular organisms such as plants. A shoot apex can die while an axillary bud remains viable and later forms a new shoot. The reappearance of green leaves establishes recovery of whole-plant growth, not recovery of the dead apex. Similarly, a tree may lose distal branches after drought yet survive through the tissues that retained sufficient hydraulic and metabolic function. A survival assessment must define its criterion, and an account of physiological recovery should identify which tissue is being measured. Otherwise a newly formed organ can be mistaken for repair of the original organ.

Some cellular measurements help establish whether structural integrity has been impaired. If membrane permeability increases, ions can escape from tissue into a surrounding solution, raising its electrical conductivity. Electrolyte leakage therefore provides an operational indicator of membrane injury. However, the experiment must standardize the amount and handling of tissue, solution volume, incubation duration and temperature. Cutting a leaf disc can itself release solutes, and total electrolyte content differs among tissues. Comparing initial leakage with total leakage after membrane disruption helps normalize the result, but does not identify which membrane or molecular process was initially damaged.

Vital stains and observations of subsequent growth provide complementary evidence. A tissue may show reduced metabolism yet remain viable, and some viability assays depend on the metabolic activity of living cells. A weak metabolic stain can therefore be ambiguous when cells are temporarily inactive. An assessment combining membrane integrity, metabolic capacity and recovery is more informative than relying on one test. For plants undergoing programmed loss of a tissue, it is also necessary to distinguish ordinary developmental senescence from premature deterioration caused by the imposed treatment.

Temporal ordering helps identify primary effects. If a reduction in leaf water potential consistently precedes an increase in membrane leakage, the data are compatible with dehydration contributing to later injury. They do not by themselves prove that dehydration is the sole cause, since a third process could influence both measurements. If maintaining leaf water potential through an independent manipulation reduces later leakage under the same external treatment, the case for causal involvement becomes stronger. If leakage persists despite maintained hydration, additional processes need investigation. A careful answer moves from a pattern in time to a test of alternative mechanisms.

Recovery must be evaluated against an appropriate control. A plant treated for five days may resume its original rate of growth after the treatment, yet its untreated counterpart continued growing during those five days. Even complete recovery of current growth rate does not imply elimination of the accumulated biomass deficit. An experiment can measure the instantaneous rate, cumulative growth and final yield separately. These endpoints answer different biological questions. Restoration of immediate function concerns physiological resilience; the remaining cumulative loss concerns the history of exposure and its consequences.

A practical classification of observations is consequently more informative than a rigid division of all stress into two phases. A decrease in turgor that reverses promptly after rewatering suggests a reversible physical disturbance. Reduced stomatal conductance may represent active regulation, whereas persistent low conductance after hydration has recovered may involve altered signaling or injury. A marked rise in electrolyte leakage suggests impaired membrane function. A failure to regrow despite suitable recovery conditions raises concern about loss of viability. Each interpretation should be qualified by the measurement method and the specific tissue being examined.

5. Intensity, duration, rate of onset and dose–response relationships

A stress response depends on how far conditions depart from a relevant operating range, how long the departure lasts, and how rapidly it develops. Intensity describes the magnitude of a specified challenge, such as the external salt concentration or temperature above a selected reference. Duration describes the time over which the challenge acts. A sudden transition can produce a different response from a gradual transition to the same final intensity because the plant has different opportunities to adjust transport, metabolism and gene expression. A duration without an intensity and an intensity without a duration are incomplete descriptions of most treatments.

The classic graphical approach begins with a response variable plotted against environmental intensity. Suppose a student grows a species at several constant temperatures and measures the production of dry biomass over a fixed interval. Biomass production may rise from a low-temperature region toward a broad optimum and fall at higher temperatures. The optimum is the region that maximizes the chosen response under the specified conditions, not necessarily the optimum for every physiological process. Germination, pollen development, photosynthetic carbon fixation and root growth may have different thermal optima. A curve for net growth should not be interpreted as a curve for a single enzyme reaction.

When a stress increases in one direction from a suitable reference condition, the response may initially change little because protective regulation compensates. As intensity increases, performance can decline, and at high exposure viability may be lost. This familiar pattern is a useful conceptual model but not a universal curve. Different endpoints can have different shapes. A protective transcript may rise while growth declines, membrane leakage may remain low until injury develops, and survival may remain high over a range in which yield is reduced. A low-intensity stimulus may even improve a selected performance measurement in certain circumstances. Such stimulation does not imply that mild exposure is universally beneficial or that its consequences are beneficial for every organ.

It is tempting to speak of a single threshold at which stress begins. In practice a threshold is an operational boundary defined using a particular measurement. If a researcher defines a growth threshold as the intensity at which growth falls to ninety percent of the control, that threshold can differ from the intensity at which membrane leakage becomes detectable. It also depends on experimental precision and treatment duration. A biological response that is gradual may appear abrupt when the assay cannot resolve small changes. A threshold should therefore be reported with the variable, exposure protocol and decision rule that define it.

Dose–response studies can compare genotypes by examining the entire response curve rather than a single treatment. A genotype may perform better near the optimum but lose performance more rapidly when exposure increases. Another may have a lower maximum but retain function over a wider range. The ranking depends on the environment and on whether absolute or relative performance is of interest. A single concentration can conceal differences at lower or higher intensities. It can also lead to an erroneous claim of universal resistance when the measured advantage is limited to a narrow interval.

A relative performance index can be calculated as R = 100 × Xs/Xc, where Xs is the measured value under stress and Xc is the corresponding control value for the same genotype and duration. Xs and Xc must have the same units and Xc must be positive. R is expressed as a percentage of the control; the corresponding percentage reduction is 100 − R. If untreated plants produce 12 grams of dry mass and stressed plants produce 9 grams, R equals 75 percent and the reduction equals 25 percent. This normalization describes proportional retention of performance, not necessarily the amount of material produced.

Consider two genotypes. Genotype A produces 20 grams under control conditions and 12 grams under a moderate challenge; genotype B produces 10 grams under control conditions and 8 grams during the same treatment. The relative performance indices are 60 and 80 percent, respectively. B retains a greater fraction of its own control performance, while A produces more biomass in the stressed environment. Selection for crop productivity in that environment may favor A, whereas investigation of proportional sensitivity may focus on B. The comparison shows why a numerical index cannot substitute for a defined biological objective. It also shows why both control and stress values should be reported.

A half-response intensity, sometimes expressed as an EC50 in a dose–response study, is meaningful only when the effect is defined. In a simple inhibition experiment it may denote the treatment intensity producing a specified fifty percent effect relative to the chosen reference. In a fitted curve with nonzero upper and lower asymptotes, it often refers to the midpoint between those asymptotes rather than fifty percent of untreated performance. A lethal exposure estimated for half of a population is a different endpoint, commonly termed an LD50 if dose is appropriate or an LT50 if a temperature or time is being specified with an explicit convention. A student should identify the meaning from the experimental definition rather than infer it solely from the subscript.

To see why duration matters, suppose seedlings receive a moderate heat treatment for one hour or eight hours. At first they may induce protective responses while retaining membrane integrity. Continued exposure can consume reserves, impair proteins and accumulate damage even if the temperature remains unchanged. Conversely, the additional time might allow a plant to establish effective protection in some moderate conditions. Duration is not always equivalent to accumulating a fixed amount of injury per hour. The damage rate can change as the physiological state changes.

In simple teaching models, an accumulated exposure index may be written D = integral of S(t) dt, where S(t) is the nonnegative intensity above a defined reference and t denotes time. If the intensity is constant, D equals S multiplied by duration. For a temperature excess measured in degrees Celsius over hours, D has units of degree-hours. Two hours at five degrees above a reference and five hours at two degrees above it both give ten degree-hours. This is an exposure index, not a universal prediction of injury. If damaging reactions accelerate nonlinearly with temperature, if the reference is not biologically appropriate, or if acclimation and recovery occur, equal degree-hours can produce different effects.

A better conceptual model recognizes that the state of the plant changes over time. The current injury burden is influenced by the rate of damage formation and the rate of repair. If damage accumulates more rapidly than repair, injury increases. If a stressor is removed and viable tissues retain repair capacity, the injury burden may decrease. If the intensity is so high that it compromises repair machinery, the decline in repair capacity can accelerate injury. This reasoning explains why the duration needed to reach a particular degree of damage may vary greatly with intensity and prior history. It also explains why an intermittent stress can be different from one continuous exposure with the same total time.

A gradual onset provides an opportunity to modify physiological state before peak intensity is reached. Slow soil drying may permit adjustments in stomatal behavior, root development and cellular solute composition. Rapid transfer to a highly concentrated solution can impose an abrupt osmotic disturbance that does not allow the same sequence of responses. To distinguish onset rate from total exposure, a researcher must specify both the treatment history and the endpoint conditions. A gradual treatment may take longer and thus have a different total duration; the history needs to be included in interpretation rather than assuming the two treatments differ in only one respect.

The recovery interval between episodes can change the outcome of a repeated stress. During an interval without the stressor, repair and replenishment of reserves may occur. Certain induced proteins and regulatory states may also persist into the next episode, changing the response. However, incomplete repair may make a later episode more damaging. Repeated exposure therefore need not have a single directional effect. A comparison between one exposure and several exposures should match total treatment and elapsed time as far as the research question permits, include an appropriately scheduled control and measure the state of the plant before each episode.

6. Why developmental stage, genotype and environment alter sensitivity

Developmental stage is a major determinant of the biological consequences of a stress. A seed, a recently germinated seedling, a vegetative plant and a flowering plant have different requirements and different capacities for protection. Mature dry seeds of many species can tolerate substantial water loss as part of normal development. Germinating seedlings generally become more dependent on continuous water availability as cells expand and metabolism increases. This does not mean that every seed is desiccation tolerant: recalcitrant seeds of some species are damaged by drying, and even an orthodox seed can lose its tolerance during germination. The appropriate comparison therefore specifies both species and developmental state.

An actively expanding leaf contains cells that must maintain sufficient turgor and wall extensibility for expansion. A fully expanded leaf no longer depends on those processes to the same degree for increasing surface area, although its photosynthetic and transport functions still depend on viable hydrated cells. A stress imposed during expansion may therefore reduce final leaf area without producing the same immediate change in an already mature leaf. Measuring only final area cannot distinguish fewer cells from smaller cells or changes in developmental timing. A combination of growth trajectories and cellular observations is more informative.

The reproductive stage introduces endpoints that cannot be inferred from vegetative appearance. High temperature or water shortage during sensitive periods of pollen formation, anthesis or fertilization can reduce seed set even when leaves remain green. Later, restrictions on photosynthetic carbon supply or assimilate transport can alter seed filling. The yield of a crop can therefore respond to the timing of stress as much as to its average intensity over a season. An early stress that reduces leaf number may have a different effect from a late stress that reduces seed mass. Recording phenological events alongside environmental measurements helps identify the affected developmental process.

Tissue-specific vulnerability also follows from spatial organization. Roots encounter soil salinity, mineral conditions and oxygen availability directly; shoots experience radiation, atmospheric temperature and evaporative demand. Young growing tissues depend on the import of resources, whereas mature photosynthetic leaves often act as sources of assimilates. A loss of root function can reduce water and nutrient supply to the shoot even when the shoot is not directly exposed to the causal agent. Conversely, severe leaf damage can reduce carbon delivery to roots. Whole-plant stress physiology must therefore connect organ-specific measurements with the exchange of water, nutrients and assimilates among organs.

Genotype refers to inherited genetic constitution. Genetic differences can influence structural features, the abundance and regulation of transport proteins, developmental timing, metabolic capacities and the response to environmental signals. A genotype with a deeper root system may encounter a different effective water supply than a shallow-rooted genotype growing in the same field. A genotype with different membrane composition may respond differently at the same tissue temperature. These examples distinguish a genetically determined difference in exposure from a genetically determined difference in the ability to withstand a given internal disturbance.

Phenotype is the observable state produced through the interaction of genotype, environment and developmental history. Phenotypic plasticity is the ability of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. It can involve changes in morphology, developmental timing, physiology or metabolism. Not every plastic change is protective. A smaller leaf in a stressful environment may represent adaptive limitation of water loss, unavoidable restriction of expansion, or both. To determine whether a plastic response is advantageous, compare relevant outcomes in the environments in which the response occurs and, where possible, alter the response without altering unrelated traits.

A reaction norm plots the phenotype of a genotype across environmental conditions. If two genotypes have parallel reaction norms, their difference in the chosen trait remains constant on the plotted scale as the environment changes. If the curves differ in slope or cross, the environmental effect differs between genotypes on that scale. This is a genotype-by-environment interaction. Crossing curves are especially important because the ranking of genotypes changes. The absence of a crossing does not exclude interaction; different slopes can produce interaction even when one genotype has a higher value everywhere.

Suppose plants of genotype A produce 10 grams of dry biomass in favorable conditions and 6 grams in a stressful environment. Genotype B produces 8 grams and 7 grams, respectively. A is larger under favorable conditions, but B is larger under stress. On the absolute scale the treatment effects are a loss of 4 grams for A and 1 gram for B, giving a difference of 3 grams between the treatment effects. The crossing demonstrates that a ranking derived in a favorable growth chamber need not predict the ranking in a stressful field. Statistical support for an interaction requires replication and an estimate of variability; the numerical example illustrates the concept rather than proving significance.

Even individuals with identical genotypes can differ in sensitivity because of their growth history. A plant previously exposed to a moderate challenge may possess protective proteins or altered physiological properties when a later challenge arrives. Another individual of the same age may lack these changes. Differences in seed size, maternal environment or initial plant size can also influence subsequent performance. Controlled studies often use genetically similar material and standardize age, size and pre-treatment conditions, not because variation is unimportant but because the purpose is to identify its causes.

External conditions interact with each other in determining internal exposure. Air temperature alone does not determine leaf temperature; absorbed radiation, air movement and evaporative cooling affect the leaf energy balance. Soil water content alone does not determine plant hydration; the water retention properties of soil and the atmospheric demand for water matter. A salinity measurement in an irrigation solution does not directly specify the concentration inside a leaf cell. A well-described experiment measures biologically relevant variables rather than assuming a nominal treatment exactly represents the condition experienced by each tissue.

These considerations have practical significance for experimental design. A randomized comparison of genotypes under the same nominal treatment is valuable, but interpretation still requires baseline measurements and information about actual internal state. Genotypes of very different sizes can dry their pots at different rates if watering is stopped simultaneously. The larger plant might then experience a more severe decline in soil water availability. A comparison at matched soil water status or matched tissue hydration answers a different question from a comparison of time until wilting. Both can be useful provided the investigator states which aspect of resistance is being assessed.
