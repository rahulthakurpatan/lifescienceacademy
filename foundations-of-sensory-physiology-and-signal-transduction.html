1. The biological problem: obtaining useful information about the environment

A small animal moving through its surroundings must distinguish the arrival of food from the approach of a predator. A flying insect has to maintain its direction while the pattern of light reaching its eyes changes continuously. A mammal holding a delicate object must increase its grip when the object starts to slip but avoid squeezing hard enough to damage it. Each situation requires information about events occurring outside the central nervous system. The animal cannot directly examine the physical world from within the brain. Instead, specialised peripheral cells and structures respond to selected changes in the environment. The nervous system receives electrical and chemical signals generated by these structures and uses them to guide behaviour. Sensory physiology studies the chain of events that connects a physical or chemical disturbance to this usable information.

The starting disturbance is called a stimulus. A stimulus is not necessarily an object. It can be a change in the energy reaching a tissue, a change in the concentration of a substance, displacement of a membrane, or a change in the conditions within the animal. A moving object may alter the distribution of light across the retina and produce vibrations in the air at the same time. The same event can consequently provide visual, auditory and tactile information. Each form of information begins with a different physical variable. The visual system responds to light; the auditory system samples pressure fluctuations through an apparatus that converts them into mechanical displacement; the tactile system responds to deformation of skin and related structures. What these systems have in common is their dependence on a cellular mechanism that converts changes in a relevant physical variable into a change in electrical activity.

This conversion is termed sensory transduction. It should be distinguished from transmission, which is the passage of an already generated signal along a neuron or across a synapse, and from perception, which is the interpretation of sensory activity by neural circuits. If a hair cell responds to displacement of its bundle, transduction has occurred even if the animal subsequently fails to detect the sound because a downstream connection is interrupted. Conversely, stimulation of a sensory pathway with an electrode can produce a sensation without the usual peripheral transduction event. These observations separate stages that are often compressed into the single phrase sensing a stimulus. A sound is not literally carried as sound through an auditory axon, and a light ray does not travel through the optic nerve.

A useful sensory signal must preserve several properties of the stimulus. The nervous system must establish its modality, or kind, such as light or vibration; its location, if location is biologically relevant; its intensity; and its temporal characteristics. A sound may be brief or sustained, and two sounds of the same average intensity can differ in frequency and timing. A point of pressure can move across the skin even if its strength remains constant. Thus an account of sensory coding cannot end by establishing that a neuron fires in response to stimulation. It must also explain what aspects of the discharge change when the stimulus changes and how other neurons could distinguish one pattern from another.

Information has a cost. A receptor cannot produce an unlimited number of impulses, and neural tissue consumes energy maintaining the ion gradients required for electrical signalling. If every sensory fibre discharged maximally at all times, an important new event could not be distinguished from background activity. Sensory systems therefore exploit selective responsiveness, restricted dynamic ranges, adaptation, and cooperation between populations of neurons. A receptor that rapidly reduces its response to a constant stimulus may be unusually useful for detecting motion or vibration, while a different receptor may maintain a discharge to report that the stimulus persists. Neither strategy is universally superior. Their suitability depends on the biological variable that the receiving circuit needs to estimate.

Consider the simple act of holding a cup. The cup initially contacts the skin, creating rapid changes in local deformation. Once it is held steadily, some of these changes disappear, although its weight and the pressure on the fingers remain. If it begins to slip, small transient deformations reappear before the cup has moved very far. A set of receptors sensitive to rapid changes can notify the motor system of the onset of contact and the beginning of slip. Receptors with sustained responses can provide complementary evidence about the ongoing load. Motor commands then alter the force exerted by muscles, producing further sensory consequences. This closed loop links the sensory principles developed here to the reflex and postural mechanisms of Subunit 7.D.

A second distinction is between detection and discrimination. Detecting a spot of light means establishing that a visual event has occurred. Discriminating two nearby spots requires a spatially informative representation that differs from the representation of one spot. Similarly, an animal can detect contact with its skin while failing to distinguish two closely separated points of contact. The amount of activity may be sufficient for detection but its distribution may be insufficient for spatial discrimination. These functions are influenced by receptor density, convergence between neurons, receptive-field organisation and the noise present at each stage. It follows that high sensitivity to a weak stimulus does not automatically guarantee high spatial acuity.

Sensory organs are assembled structures, not simply collections of electrically excitable cells. Transparent media and the lens of an eye modify the light distribution before it reaches the photoreceptors. The middle ear transfers mechanical energy from the air to the fluid-filled inner ear. The mechanical properties of the skin and of encapsulated endings determine how pressure at the surface is conveyed to a receptor membrane. Accessory structures can therefore alter sensitivity, time course and selectivity without any mutation in a transduction protein. This relationship will recur throughout the following nodes. It also supplies an important experimental principle: a reduced neural response can originate either in the delivery of the stimulus or in the receptor's ability to respond to the stimulus after delivery.

Sensory experience is not an exact copy of the physical surroundings. The range of light wavelengths that activates a given visual system is limited, the audible frequencies depend on mechanical and neural specialisation, and regions of the skin differ in spatial resolution. Two animals occupying the same habitat may consequently obtain different information from the same event. Such differences do not imply that one nervous system is generally better than another. They reflect different sampling mechanisms and ecological needs. The principles of transduction and coding can be studied without describing every specialised sensory organ, because different organs implement these principles in recognisably related ways.

2. Sensory receptor classification and adequate stimuli

The term receptor has two related meanings in biology. A receptor protein is a molecular structure capable of responding to a signal, such as a mechanically sensitive ion channel or a light-sensitive pigment. A sensory receptor is a functional cellular structure that responds to a stimulus and initiates sensory signalling. It may be the terminal of a sensory neuron, a specialised cell connected to a neuron, or an arrangement involving both neuronal and non-neuronal components. A receptor organ may additionally include mechanical or optical structures that shape the stimulus. Keeping these levels of organisation distinct prevents confusion when a question describes the loss of a membrane protein but asks about the behaviour of the whole sensory ending.

Classification by adequate stimulus is especially useful for comparing modalities. The adequate stimulus is the form of energy or chemical change for which a receptor is specialised and generally most sensitive. Photoreceptors respond to light because they contain pigments that can undergo a molecular change after photon absorption. Mechanoreceptors respond to mechanical effects such as deformation, displacement or tension. Thermoreceptors report changes in temperature within their relevant operating ranges. Chemoreceptors respond to particular chemical conditions or molecules. Nociceptors respond to potentially damaging stimuli and are often polymodal, responding to more than one kind of stimulus at noxious levels. These categories describe functional specialisations rather than an absolute rule that every receptor can be influenced by only one variable.

The adequate stimulus must also be specified at the receptor rather than merely at the source. Airborne sound has pressure fluctuations, but the transduction event in a cochlear hair cell depends on mechanical displacement of its sensory bundle. Such displacement can also arise from experimental manipulation that does not use a sound wave. The adequate stimulus for the cellular machinery is therefore a particular mechanical input, while the organ as a whole is specialised to receive and analyse sound. The same reasoning applies to a skin receptor, where a probe exerts force on a patch of skin but the actual mechanically sensitive membrane experiences local deformation determined by the tissue between the probe and the ending.

Selectivity is relative, not absolute. An electrical pulse applied to a sensory nerve may elicit activity even though it is not the normal adequate stimulus. Mechanical disturbance of the eye can sometimes cause a visual sensation because it activates parts of a visual pathway through an unusual route. This does not mean the eye is a normal pressure-sensing organ. It demonstrates that the meaning of neural activity depends in part on which sensory pathway is activated. The adequate stimulus is a property of the receptor's sensitivity and normal physiological role, not a restriction on every possible means of depolarising a neuron.

Classification by location answers a different question: where does the biologically relevant stimulus arise? Exteroceptors report variables associated with the external environment, including light, sound and contact at the body surface. Interoceptors monitor conditions within the body, such as mechanical distension or the chemical composition of internal fluids. Proprioceptors provide information about the state of muscles, tendons and joints, contributing to knowledge of body position and movement. A mechanoreceptor in skin and a stretch-sensitive sensory ending in a muscle can therefore belong to the same broad stimulus class while belonging to different location classes. Location and stimulus type are independent axes of classification.

The location categories sometimes overlap in a functional sense. Vestibular receptors monitor movement of the head and contribute to orientation relative to gravity. Their role relates to proprioceptive estimates of body configuration, but their specialisation in the inner ear and their mechanical stimulus distinguish them anatomically from muscle receptors. Internal pressure-sensitive receptors also transduce force or stretch, illustrating that the physical principle of mechanoreception is useful in both external sensation and homeostatic control. This chapter uses such examples to clarify classification, while reserving detailed proprioceptive control for the nervous-system nodes and vestibular mechanisms for the later auditory chapter.

A third classification concerns the arrangement of cells. Some receptors are specialised endings of primary sensory neurons. The receptor membrane belongs to the same cell whose axon conveys information into the central nervous system. Other receptors are separate specialised cells that communicate with an afferent neuron at a synapse. These are often called secondary sensory receptor cells in introductory physiology, although terminology varies across textbooks. Mammalian auditory hair cells illustrate the latter arrangement particularly clearly. The hair cell produces a graded electrical response and changes transmitter release, while the sensory neuron generates the propagated impulses that travel along the auditory nerve. Photoreceptors are neurons themselves, but rods and cones generally communicate through graded changes in transmitter release to retinal circuits rather than directly generating the optic nerve spikes. Thus it is necessary to specify what is meant by primary and secondary instead of assuming that a receptor's cellular identity predicts every aspect of its output.

Receptors can also be grouped according to the accessory structures associated with the sensory membrane. A free nerve ending has relatively little specialised surrounding structure compared with an encapsulated ending. An encapsulated receptor can possess layers of tissue with distinctive mechanical properties. Those layers are not merely protective packaging; they can filter changes in applied force before deformation reaches the excitable terminal. Separate receptor cells can occur in organised epithelia whose arrangement maintains an appropriate chemical and mechanical environment. Differences between these structures predict differences in performance even when the underlying principle of voltage-dependent signalling is shared.

Classification is a tool for reasoning rather than a requirement to memorise mutually exclusive lists. A Pacinian corpuscle can be identified simultaneously as a cutaneous or subcutaneous mechanoreceptor, an encapsulated ending of a sensory neuron, and a rapidly adapting detector of dynamic deformation. The terms answer distinct questions. In contrast, a hair cell is a mechanically sensitive specialised epithelial cell connected to afferent fibres; the label mechanoreceptor does not imply that it has the same anatomy or time course as the Pacinian ending. A student asked to identify the common feature of these two receptors should look for mechanical transduction, not identical neural connectivity or identical ion composition.

Sensitivity to a stimulus is strongly affected by its spatial and temporal pattern. A broad deformation of the skin may recruit multiple endings, while a narrow probe of the same total force may generate a larger local pressure and activate a smaller group more strongly. A periodic movement at a receptor's preferred frequency can elicit a clear response even when a static displacement of similar size does not. Consequently, the adequate stimulus often has to be described in terms of multiple variables, such as frequency, direction, wavelength or location, rather than as an isolated word like light or pressure. A meaningful comparison between receptors requires that these variables be controlled or reported.

3. From stimulus energy to an electrical signal

The membrane of a sensory cell separates electrically charged solutions. Its resting voltage depends on ion concentration gradients and the membrane's selective permeability. This principle, established in the earlier nervous-system chapters, is the foundation for transduction. If an appropriate stimulus changes the probability that a transduction channel is open, it changes the ionic conductance of the membrane. Ions then move according to their electrochemical driving forces. The net current changes the charge distribution across the membrane, altering its voltage. A receptor does not need to create ions or invent a new form of electrical energy for every stimulus. It regulates the flow of ions using gradients already established by cellular transport mechanisms.

There are several ways to connect the stimulus to the conductance change. In a mechanically sensitive ending, tension or deformation can alter the conformation of an ion channel directly or through coupling to surrounding structures. In a photoreceptor, absorption of a photon changes a pigment molecule, initiating a biochemical cascade that changes the concentration of a second messenger and thus the opening of channels. In chemical detection, binding of a substance can directly regulate an ion channel or act through a signalling pathway. These are alternative implementations of the same general transformation: a particular external or internal event changes membrane conductance in a way that alters the electrical state of the sensory cell.

The mechanically sensitive channel provides a useful example because the causal sequence can be represented with relatively few steps. Mechanical deformation is transmitted to a membrane or protein complex. The probability of the conducting state rises for a channel that opens in response to this deformation. If the channel admits cations and the membrane voltage is more negative than its reversal potential, there is a net inward positive current. This current depolarises the ending. Voltage-dependent channels at a nearby excitable region can then generate propagated impulses if the depolarisation reaches the appropriate level. Release of the deformation reduces the probability of channel opening and can return the terminal toward its prior state. Real endings can have more complex dynamics, but the simplified sequence identifies which stage an experiment must examine.

The direction of voltage change cannot be inferred simply from the word activation. If a stimulus closes a channel that previously carried inward current, the membrane may become more negative because other outward currents remain. Vertebrate rods and cones exemplify this important exception. Light reduces the conductance of channels maintained open in darkness by cyclic GMP, and the resulting decrease in inward current hyperpolarises the photoreceptor. The release of glutamate from the photoreceptor consequently decreases. The visual circuitry transforms this reduction into different responses in downstream pathways. Thus a negative voltage change may be an informative sensory response even though many textbook examples begin with depolarisation.

The electrochemical driving force makes the distinction between conductance and current precise. In a simple ohmic approximation the current through a class of channels is expressed as I = g(V − E), where I is current in amperes, g is conductance in siemens, V is the intracellular membrane voltage relative to the exterior in volts, and E is the reversal potential in volts. With the conventional sign used here, positive current is outward. A channel can have high conductance yet carry little net current when V is near E. Opening a channel with E more positive than V produces inward current under this convention. Increasing the conductance tends to drive V toward E, but other conductances oppose or modify that change.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose a nonselective cation conductance increases by 2 nS in a cell clamped at −60 mV, with a reversal potential of 0 mV. Multiplying 2 × 10⁻⁹ S by −60 × 10⁻³ V gives −120 × 10⁻¹² A, or −120 pA. The minus sign indicates an inward net current. Repeating the calculation at a holding potential of +20 mV gives +40 pA, an outward current. The applied stimulus and channel conductance may be unchanged in the two conditions, yet the direction of current differs because the electrochemical driving force has reversed. At 0 mV this ideal conductance carries zero net current. The example is a teaching model rather than a reported measurement of a specific receptor.

Voltage clamp and current clamp provide complementary experimental information. In voltage clamp, the recording system holds the membrane near a selected potential by supplying compensating current. The current required to maintain that voltage gives information about stimulus-dependent membrane current. Repeating the stimulus at different holding potentials can help identify the reversal potential and distinguish changes in conductance from changes in driving force. In current clamp, the membrane potential is allowed to vary; the investigator observes the voltage response and, if the recorded region supports action potentials, the resulting spikes. A current trace is not the same observable as a voltage trace, and interpreting one as the other leads to incorrect predictions about the direction or magnitude of transduction.

The receptor membrane is also a capacitor because the lipid bilayer separates charge. A small current need not cause an instantaneous change in membrane voltage. Part of the current changes the stored charge, while conductive pathways allow charge to flow back across the membrane. In a passive approximation, the product of membrane resistance and capacitance gives a time constant, τ = RC. If R is measured in ohms and C in farads, τ is in seconds. For example, 100 MΩ multiplied by 100 pF gives 0.01 s, or 10 ms. A sustained current step through an ideal RC membrane reaches about 63% of its eventual voltage change after one time constant. Actual sensory membranes contain voltage-dependent and stimulus-dependent channels, so a single time constant is an approximation. Nevertheless, it explains why very rapid fluctuations can be filtered and why a short stimulus may generate less voltage change than a longer stimulus with the same instantaneous current.

Transduction can involve biochemical amplification. A single activated receptor molecule can influence more than one downstream molecule, and an enzyme can turn over multiple substrate molecules. This increases the electrical effect of an initial event. Amplification improves the ability to distinguish a weak signal from some forms of noise, but it also introduces potential delays, saturation and additional points of regulation. Directly regulated channels can offer short delays, while cascades may provide increased sensitivity or flexible control of gain. These are tendencies rather than absolute categories. The time course and sensitivity of any particular organ depend on its molecular machinery, accessory structures and the properties of the receiving cells.

An energetic distinction is essential. The stimulus supplies information and may supply the energy needed to change the conformation of a molecular sensor. The electrical response depends on pre-existing ionic gradients that are continually maintained by metabolism. A very small mechanical event can initiate an impulse that propagates along an axon because voltage-dependent channels regenerate the signal using local ion gradients at successive membrane regions. The mechanical energy is not carried intact from the skin to the brain. The biological system uses its own stored electrochemical energy to represent the event. This is why loss of ion gradients or severe metabolic failure can impair transduction even when the external stimulus is unchanged.

A decrease in response can arise at any step of the chain. An accessory organ may transmit less mechanical energy. A channel may fail to open. The electrochemical driving force may decrease. The terminal's input resistance may be reduced, allowing more current to escape without producing a large voltage change. An excitable region may fail to generate impulses, or a synapse may fail to relay information to the next cell. These possibilities can be separated by choosing complementary measurements. Recording the stimulus-evoked current while clamping voltage examines transduction more directly than counting spikes alone. Recording spikes in response to an injected depolarising current bypasses the normal transduction machinery and can test whether the spike generator remains functional.

4. Receptor and generator potentials: analogue signals and all-or-none impulses

A receptor potential is a stimulus-induced change in membrane voltage at the sensory receptor. The term generator potential is often applied to the local graded voltage change in a primary sensory nerve ending that contributes to initiation of action potentials. Usage differs among physiology texts, and some use the terms interchangeably for primary endings. It is more informative to specify whether the measurement is from a transducing membrane, a spike initiation site or a separate receptor cell. In this chapter, receptor potential is the broad term and generator potential denotes the graded response of the primary afferent ending where the distinction is helpful.

The defining feature of a graded potential is that its amplitude and duration vary with the input and with membrane properties. A weak mechanical deformation might open a small fraction of the available channels, while a stronger deformation opens more channels and produces a larger change in voltage. This relationship is valid only over a range. Once the transduction machinery approaches saturation, additional stimulus intensity may cause little further increase in the response. The size of a graded potential is also constrained by the reversal potentials of the relevant conductances. Increasing the number of open channels cannot indefinitely move voltage beyond the reversal potential of the conductance that produces the change.

A propagated action potential behaves differently. When sufficient local depolarisation activates voltage-dependent sodium channels in a conventional sensory axon, the ensuing regenerative process produces a rapid impulse. At a fixed recording location under stable conditions, individual action potentials have broadly similar amplitudes over a wide range of stimulus strengths. A stronger stimulus usually changes the number, rate or timing of impulses rather than making every impulse proportionately taller. Refractory processes limit how soon another impulse can occur. This distinction supplies the basis for frequency coding and explains why the height of the spike in an extracellular recording is generally not used as a simple measure of stimulus strength.

Between the transducing membrane and the first spike initiation region, a graded voltage change spreads electrotonically. Unlike a regenerative action potential, its amplitude can decline with distance. The extent of the decline depends on the electrical properties and geometry of the membrane. A larger local receptor potential is therefore not necessarily a larger depolarisation at the trigger region if the distance or leakage differs between the two conditions. A physiological sensory ending has structural arrangements that permit relevant graded responses to influence the spike generator. An experiment that changes membrane resistance can alter this coupling without changing the opening of transduction channels.

Consider a simple model in which a resting excitable region is at −65 mV and reaches spike threshold at approximately −50 mV. A stimulus produces a local generator potential that gives only a 10 mV depolarisation at the spike initiation site. The resulting voltage, −55 mV, remains below the specified threshold and no regenerative action potential is expected. If a stronger stimulus produces a 20 mV depolarisation, the region crosses the nominal threshold and can fire. This does not mean that the weaker stimulus failed to be transduced; a graded voltage change still occurred. The exact threshold in a real neuron depends on preceding activity and the availability of voltage-dependent channels, so the numerical values are illustrative and not universal constants.

Temporal summation can alter the relation between single stimulus events and spike output. Suppose two brief stimuli individually produce small graded responses that decay slowly enough to overlap. The second response arrives while the membrane is still depolarised by the first, potentially allowing the combined effect to reach the firing region's threshold. If the stimuli are separated by a long interval, the first response may decay almost completely before the second arrives. The same total number of stimulus events can therefore produce different impulse counts depending on temporal organisation. The relationship is not necessarily simple addition because transduction channels and voltage-dependent conductances may themselves change their responsiveness following an earlier event.

In specialised receptor cells, the graded response may change transmitter release without first generating a conventional propagated spike in the receptor cell. In a mature cochlear inner hair cell, displacement in the excitatory direction depolarises the receptor and promotes opening of voltage-gated calcium channels at its synaptic region. Calcium entry promotes vesicular transmitter release onto the afferent neuron. The postsynaptic response influences the initiation of action potentials in that afferent. In contrast, rods and cones reduce their transmitter release in response to light-induced hyperpolarisation. Both examples illustrate how a graded voltage response can alter a chemical signal and then influence spikes downstream. The sign and details of the synaptic effect must be established for the actual circuit.

A tonic release of transmitter or a maintained baseline discharge makes decreases in activity informative. If an afferent neuron normally fires at 25 impulses per second, a stimulus that reduces its rate to 10 impulses per second produces a reliable change even though the stimulus did not increase excitation. An afferent that is completely silent at baseline cannot represent further reductions by decreasing its spike rate below zero. Sensory systems can overcome this limitation through spontaneous activity, complementary populations or pathways with opposite response polarities. The important principle is that information can be represented by a change from baseline in either direction, not merely by the presence or absence of spikes.

Electrophysiological recordings have to be interpreted in relation to the type of electrode and its position. An intracellular electrode at a sensory terminal can reveal a graded potential and sometimes superimposed action potentials. An extracellular electrode near an axon detects voltage differences associated with transmembrane currents during a passing impulse. The amplitude of that extracellular event depends on distance from the axon, orientation and recording conditions. A large extracellular spike may come from a nearby fibre rather than from a receptor responding more strongly. Counting distinguishable spikes from an identified fibre, measuring intervals between spikes and comparing responses under controlled stimulus conditions are therefore more informative for coding than comparing raw amplitudes from uncontrolled recordings.

Early single-fibre experiments by Edgar Adrian and Yngve Zotterman helped establish the physiological distinction between impulse size and impulse frequency. Their 1926 work on sensory endings, including single-end-organ responses and responses to touch and pressure, provided a way to ask how changes in a controlled stimulus were reflected in individual nerve impulses rather than in a summed nerve response. The significance for modern students is methodological as well as historical: identifying a single unit makes it possible to distinguish a change in the activity of one fibre from recruitment of additional fibres. An increase in the size of a compound nerve recording cannot, by itself, establish which process occurred.

5. Sensitivity, thresholds and the dynamic range of sensory responses

A stimulus may be physically present without producing a measurable response. Whether a receptor responds depends on the amount of relevant stimulus reaching its transducing structures, the sensitivity of its molecular machinery, the state of its membrane and the sensitivity of the recording method. A threshold is the smallest stimulus level that satisfies a stated response criterion under specified conditions. The last part of this definition matters because a threshold measured with a voltage recording need not coincide with a threshold measured with a behavioural task. Threshold is not an independent substance or fixed boundary inside the sensory organ. It is a way of characterising the relationship between stimulation and response.

At the earliest stage, stimulus-dependent channel activity may change by a small amount. An investigator with a sufficiently sensitive recording can detect a graded current even when the resulting voltage is too small to initiate action potentials. The threshold for a measurable transduction current can consequently lie below the threshold for a propagated spike. Moreover, an afferent spike need not produce a detectable conscious sensation if the receiving circuits do not obtain sufficient evidence that the stimulus differs from background activity. Conversely, a weak stimulus distributed across several receptors can produce detectable population activity even if no individual receptor gives a large response. The relationship between thresholds at different levels is therefore determined by the actual circuitry, not by a universal ordering of numerical values from incomparable experiments.

A simple stimulus-response experiment illustrates how sensitivity can be measured. A probe applies repeated mechanical indentations of known depth at one position on the skin while an investigator records from a sensory afferent. Each indentation lasts for the same duration and begins from the same starting condition. If the depths are presented in random order with intervals sufficient for recovery, the measurements are less likely to be confounded by progressive adaptation. At each depth the investigator compares the number of impulses within a defined observation window with the number recorded during trials without indentation. A reproducible increase above the chosen criterion indicates that the afferent has responded. Changing the duration of the counting window or requiring a larger difference from baseline can change the estimated threshold even with identical raw recordings.

Sensitivity can refer to the smallest detectable stimulus or to the change in response associated with a specified change in stimulus. These definitions are related but not equivalent. A receptor with a low detection threshold may show only a small increase in firing rate when the stimulus becomes stronger. Another receptor may have a higher threshold but a steep increase in rate once that threshold is exceeded. When discussing the slope of a stimulus-response curve, sensitivity is often expressed as a derivative or as a change in response divided by a change in stimulus over a small interval. An afferent whose rate rises by 20 impulses per second when indentation increases by 0.5 mm has an average slope of 40 impulses per second per millimetre over that interval. This calculation does not determine the threshold unless there is additional information about baseline activity and the response at weaker indentations.

The range of intensities over which a sensory cell can represent meaningful differences is its dynamic range, with the precise boundaries depending on the response measure and criterion. At very low intensities the response can be difficult to distinguish from spontaneous variations. At intermediate levels the output may change substantially with intensity. At high intensities the output can approach a plateau because transduction channels, synaptic release or the spike generator have reached a functional limit. A plateau does not imply that the physical stimulus has stopped changing. It indicates that the measured response is no longer an effective way of distinguishing those particular intensities.

The limitation becomes apparent when a receptor has a firing rate of 5 impulses per second at baseline, 25 at a moderate stimulus, and 95 at a high stimulus, while an even stronger stimulus yields 98. The increase from 25 to 95 provides a large change in output, but the increase from 95 to 98 is small and may be comparable with fluctuations between trials. Another receptor with a higher operating range may distinguish the two strong stimuli more reliably. Differences in operating range between receptor populations allow the nervous system to represent a much broader set of intensities than would be possible if every cell had the same threshold and saturation point. The numerical rates here illustrate the principle and are not measurements of a specific biological receptor.

A saturating transduction response can be represented by a simple mathematical function without implying that every receptor obeys it. Suppose the stimulus intensity is S, the maximum stimulus-dependent response is Rmax and the half-maximal stimulus is K. A convenient model is R = Rmax S/(K + S), with S and K expressed in the same stimulus units. If S equals K, the response is half of Rmax. If S is much smaller than K, the denominator is approximately K and the response increases almost linearly with S. If S becomes much larger than K, the response approaches Rmax. The quantity K is a characteristic scale of this model, not automatically a detection threshold. A detection criterion of 10% of the maximal response, for example, corresponds to a stimulus well below K.

In a worked example, let the maximum stimulus-dependent change in firing rate be 80 spikes per second and let K be 2 mm of indentation in a hypothetical model. At 2 mm, the model gives 40 spikes per second above baseline. At 6 mm it gives 80 × 6/8 = 60, and at 18 mm it gives 80 × 18/20 = 72. An increase of 4 mm from 2 to 6 produces a response change of 20 spikes per second, whereas an increase of 12 mm from 6 to 18 produces a change of only 12. The derivative of the response decreases as the stimulus grows, demonstrating how saturation compresses large intensities. The model assumes an unchanged state of adaptation, fixed location and stimulus duration, and a monotonic response. It should not be used to extrapolate to damaging indentation or to receptors whose response decreases with greater stimulation.

Sensory gain is a useful concept when discussing changes in the stimulus-response relationship. Gain refers to how strongly output changes in relation to input. If a receptor adapts to a sustained background, its response to a small additional stimulus may differ from the response it would produce in darkness, at rest or under another background condition. A gain adjustment can preserve useful sensitivity to fluctuations around the new background even though the absolute response to the background itself is smaller. Distinguish a reduction in baseline output from a reduction in sensitivity to increments; the two can occur together, but one does not mathematically imply the other. To measure gain experimentally, the investigator must compare responses to controlled variations around a defined operating state rather than only compare responses to one stimulus.

The lower limit of sensory detection also depends on noise. Noise is variability not attributable to the controlled feature of the stimulus in the experiment. It can arise from random molecular events, spontaneous receptor activity, unrelated background stimulation, changes in attention or other physiological processes. An appropriate analysis compares distributions of responses rather than assuming that each stimulus intensity produces a single exact output. A weak stimulus might evoke an average count of four spikes in an interval while blank trials average three, but the two distributions can overlap strongly. A much larger difference relative to their variability would allow more reliable detection. Thus both response magnitude and reliability are necessary for understanding threshold.

6. Coding stimulus intensity, identity and temporal structure

A propagated sensory impulse is a broadly standardised electrical event. The nervous system nevertheless represents different stimulus intensities because information is carried by the pattern of events. One elementary pattern is the frequency of impulses within a defined interval, called a firing rate. If an afferent discharges 12 times in a 0.4-second observation window, its average rate in that window is 12/0.4 = 30 Hz. The unit hertz denotes events per second here, not the physical frequency of a vibration or a sound. A receptor exposed to a 200 Hz vibration need not discharge at 200 spikes per second. Distinguishing these quantities is particularly important for the later auditory and tactile nodes.

Why does a stronger generator potential often increase the firing rate? A sustained depolarising current charges the excitable membrane toward the voltage range in which regenerative activity occurs. After each impulse, the membrane repolarises and channel availability recovers. If the depolarising drive persists, the membrane may reach the firing range again. A stronger drive can shorten the time required to reach that range, yielding more impulses within a fixed interval. This explanation is a simplification because the threshold and the conductances responsible for repolarisation and recovery are dynamic. The essential consequence is that, under suitable operating conditions, the intensity of a local graded response is transformed into a measure of repeated neural activity.

The time between successive action potentials is the interspike interval. For a perfectly regular train with intervals of 25 ms, the reciprocal gives a rate of 1/0.025 = 40 impulses per second. If intervals shorten to 10 ms, the corresponding regular rate is 100 impulses per second. For irregular trains, the number of spikes divided by the total duration is usually a clearer average rate than taking the average of the reciprocals of individual intervals. Rate estimates also depend on observation time. In a very short window, adding one spike can change the estimated rate considerably; longer windows give more stable averages but can conceal rapid variations in activity. The choice of window is therefore a compromise between temporal resolution and reliability.

Refractory periods constrain the maximum sustained firing rate. If an idealised neuron has an absolute refractory period of 2 ms, no two spikes can begin less than 2 ms apart and the reciprocal, 500 impulses per second, gives a mathematical upper bound in this simplified calculation. Real sustainable rates are generally lower because relative refractory effects, membrane charging, synaptic input and other cellular processes require additional time. It is incorrect to interpret the reciprocal of the absolute refractory period as a guaranteed physiological rate. The calculation identifies a constraint on possible responses. Stronger stimuli cannot overcome the complete unavailability of a regenerative mechanism during its absolute refractory phase merely by increasing their intensity.

Stimulus intensity is also represented by recruitment. A weak indentation may excite only the most sensitive endings close to the contact. With greater deformation, the same endings can increase their firing rates and additional endings may be activated because their thresholds are exceeded or because deformation extends into their receptive fields. If a recording measures the total activity of a nerve bundle, an increase can thus reflect both rate changes within active fibres and an increase in the number of fibres contributing. A single-unit recording is needed to isolate the response of a particular afferent. A population measurement may be more relevant to the information available to the central nervous system, but it must be interpreted with the possibility of recruitment in mind.

Imagine four receptors with nominal activation thresholds of 1, 3, 5 and 7 arbitrary stimulus units. In a model with no spontaneous activity, a stimulus of 4 units can activate the first two but not the last two. If intensity rises to 6 units, a third becomes active. Even if the most sensitive receptor has already saturated, the population changes its pattern of activity. A downstream neuron can combine the weighted contributions of active inputs, but the pattern of which fibres respond may also contain information that is lost in a simple sum. The thresholds in this example are illustrative; biological receptors show gradual response functions and variability rather than ideal switches. The model isolates the reason that heterogeneous populations broaden the usable range of stimulus intensities.

Population coding does not mean that all neurons represent a variable in the same way. Consider two positions on the skin that generate equal total counts of 60 impulses in two afferents. Position A evokes 50 impulses in one afferent and 10 in the other; position B evokes 10 in the first and 50 in the second. Summing their outputs discards the distinction, but preserving the identity of the active fibres makes the two positions readily separable. A neural circuit can maintain separate pathways, compare relative activity, or combine them with unequal weights. Thus a population code may represent intensity, location or another feature through the distribution of activity, not merely through the number of participating neurons.

Time is another dimension of sensory information. Two spike trains may each contain ten impulses during one second but differ in their arrangement. One can contain an intense burst at the beginning followed by silence, while another is spread evenly through the entire interval. The overall mean rate is identical, yet the first train suggests a transient event and the second suggests persistent activation. A receiving neuron with a short integration time responds to closely spaced impulses differently from a neuron that integrates over a longer time. The meaning of a temporal pattern therefore depends on the temporal sensitivity of the circuit that reads it. Rate coding and temporal coding are not opposing theories that require choosing only one mechanism; real systems can exploit both aspects of the same impulse sequence.

Periodic stimuli reveal a further possibility. When a vibration causes the probability of firing to increase near a particular phase of each cycle, spikes may become aligned with that phase. This is called phase locking. The neuron need not fire during every cycle. If it fires during selected cycles but consistently at a similar phase, the distribution of spike times still carries information about periodicity. The phenomenon has limits that depend on the receptor, afferent and frequency range. In auditory physiology, frequency can also be represented by the location of maximal mechanical excitation in the cochlea, a principle that will be treated in later nodes. For general sensory coding, the important distinction is between the rate of impulses, the temporal alignment of impulses, and the location or identity of neurons most responsive to a stimulus.

A useful experiment to distinguish temporal and rate information compares responses to periodic inputs while keeping their overall duration and mean strength controlled. Suppose a recording reveals equal mean firing rates during two vibrations but a different distribution of interspike intervals or a different relation between spike times and the stimulus cycle. The afferent may still distinguish the vibrations temporally. Conversely, if the only available record is a total spike count, no conclusion about phase locking can be drawn because the necessary timing data have been discarded. The form of the recorded data constrains the biological conclusions that can be supported by the experiment.

Latency can supply information as well. A strong stimulus may bring a receptor to a firing condition sooner than a weak stimulus, so the time from onset to the first spike can change. However, latency also includes delays in stimulus delivery, receptor dynamics and axonal conduction. A difference in first-spike latency between two fibres with different conduction distances cannot be attributed solely to receptor sensitivity without controlling or accounting for those other factors. Distinguishing receptor latency from conduction latency will help when interpreting sensory pathways and their functional delays. A common examination error is to infer a specific molecular change from the timing of a downstream response when several upstream processes could contribute.

The classical single-unit strategy provides a framework for testing these distinctions. A stimulus is delivered at several intensities while an identifiable afferent is recorded repeatedly. One can plot its spike count against stimulus intensity, examine raster plots in which individual spike times are marked on separate trial rows, and construct a histogram of activity relative to stimulus onset. A raster preserves much of the trial-by-trial timing information, whereas an averaged histogram can reveal reproducible response phases while concealing some details of individual trials. Combining both displays helps determine whether greater intensity changes onset latency, sustained firing or the timing of a transient burst. Such analyses are directly relevant to the interpretation of Part C questions involving sensory recordings.

7. Sensory adaptation: why responses change during constant stimulation

When a stimulus begins, a sensory neuron often produces a response that differs from the response observed after the same stimulus has been present for some time. The general decrease or adjustment in responsiveness during sustained stimulation is called sensory adaptation. The phenomenon has to be demonstrated while the relevant stimulus is maintained or carefully controlled. A decline in neural activity during an uncontrolled change in pressure could simply indicate that the pressure is decreasing. The experimental challenge is to establish that the external input remains constant while the receptor, afferent or central pathway changes its response.

A step stimulus provides a particularly informative test. Before the step, the receptor is at a defined baseline. At onset, the stimulus rapidly changes to a new level. That level is maintained for a chosen interval and then returned to baseline. A recording during the entire sequence reveals the onset response, the activity during the maintained period and the response to stimulus removal. Receptors described as rapidly adapting or phasic commonly respond strongly to the change at onset and may respond to offset, while exhibiting relatively little sustained activity. Slowly adapting or tonic receptors maintain a response over a longer portion of the step, although their firing may also decline from its initial peak. The terms refer to response dynamics, not to the speed at which an action potential travels along the axon.

The two types of response can be complementary even when both receptors experience identical external events. A sustained indentation provides information about the presence and approximate magnitude of contact. A receptor that continues to fire during the indentation can report this aspect of the event. A rapidly adapting ending can respond strongly to the onset, offset or small vibrations superimposed on the indentation. It gives disproportionate emphasis to changes. If only the rapidly adapting response were available, a steady stimulus could become difficult to distinguish from the absence of that stimulus after the transient had decayed. If only a sustained response were available, the onset and small fast changes might be less conspicuous. The combined activity of receptor populations supports a richer representation.

Mechanical filtering provides a concrete mechanism of adaptation. In an encapsulated receptor such as a Pacinian corpuscle, a sudden deformation can transmit a strong mechanical disturbance through the surrounding lamellae to the central nerve ending. During sustained external deformation, redistribution of mechanical stress through the accessory structures can reduce the deformation acting directly on the terminal. The applied stimulus at the outer surface remains unchanged while the stimulus experienced by the transduction membrane declines. Removing or altering the capsule can consequently change the time course of the generator potential. The capsule does not create the action potential itself; it modifies the mechanical input that reaches the electrically excitable ending.

Loewenstein and Mendelson investigated components of adaptation in the Pacinian corpuscle in work published in the 1960s. Their studies are valuable because they address the question of whether the rapid decline of response is explained solely by the electrical properties of the nerve ending. By investigating the effects of the laminar capsule and intrinsic properties of the receptor, they showed that more than one determinant contributes to the observed adaptation. A simple educational reconstruction compares responses of an intact corpuscle and an exposed ending under controlled deformation. A more sustained generator response after changing the capsule supports a mechanical contribution. Residual adaptation in the exposed preparation would indicate that accessory filtering is not the entire explanation. This reconstruction illustrates the logic of the published work rather than reproducing its exact protocol or data.

Intrinsic molecular adaptation can occur even when the stimulus delivered to the transduction membrane remains constant. A stimulus may initially increase channel opening, but channel activity can subsequently decline because of changes in channel state or regulatory feedback. In a biochemical signalling pathway, an accumulating intracellular signal can reduce the gain of an earlier transduction step. Calcium provides an important form of feedback in several sensory systems, but its effects depend on the channels and proteins involved. In vertebrate photoreceptors, changes in intracellular calcium during illumination influence processes that help adjust the response and restore sensitivity. The detailed phototransduction cascade is reserved for 7.E.03; the shared principle is that feedback can change the relation between a sustained stimulus and the receptor's electrical output.

The spike generator can introduce a separate decline in discharge. Even if a receptor potential remains elevated, repeated firing may activate outward currents, alter channel availability or otherwise increase the interval between impulses. This process is often called spike-frequency adaptation. It can be examined by comparing spike output during a constant depolarising current injection with spike output during a natural stimulus. If a neuron shows a decline in firing during both conditions, an intrinsic excitability mechanism may contribute. Such an observation does not rule out an additional contribution from transduction or mechanical filtering during natural stimulation. The most convincing conclusion requires recordings that separate the relevant stages.

Synapses and central pathways can also change their response during a maintained or repeated input. Transmitter release and postsynaptic responsiveness are affected by preceding activity. In a central circuit, inhibitory feedback or a change in the balance of excitation and inhibition can modify the output to a persistent stimulus. A subject may stop noticing a familiar background even when peripheral receptors continue to provide information. This observation should not automatically be labelled complete peripheral receptor adaptation. Habituation, a reduction in behavioural response to repeated harmless stimulation, and changes in attention occur at different explanatory levels and can involve central processing. Demonstrating a decline in behavioural response alone does not specify which peripheral or central mechanism caused it.

An idealised exponential response describes one possible time course of adaptation after stimulus onset. Let r(t) = r∞ + (r₀ − r∞)e^(−t/τ), where r₀ is the initial rate after onset, r∞ is the long-time rate, τ is the adaptation time constant and t is elapsed time. Rates are measured in spikes per second; t and τ must use the same time units so that their ratio is dimensionless. At t = τ the excess activity above the long-time rate is about 37% of its original value. After three time constants it is about 5%. The model does not describe the rise time at stimulus onset, the offset response or necessarily the recovery after adaptation. Different receptor processes may produce several time constants rather than one.

For a numerical example, suppose a slowly adapting afferent has an initial firing rate of 80 Hz and an eventual rate of 20 Hz during sustained indentation, with τ = 2 s. After 2 s, its estimated rate is 20 + 60/e ≈ 42.1 Hz. After 6 s, the estimate is 20 + 60e^(−3) ≈ 23.0 Hz. The remaining activity is not zero because the model includes a sustained component. A rapidly adapting afferent might instead have a long-time rate close to baseline and a much smaller time constant. The term rapid refers to how quickly the response approaches its maintained level, while the maintained level determines whether the receptor continues to report the presence of the stimulus. These are two distinguishable characteristics of the response curve.

A comparison of adaptation can be distorted if receptors are stimulated at different positions or intensities. A stronger step may generate a different initial firing rate and may also alter the relative importance of the mechanisms responsible for adaptation. A receptor's state before the step matters because preceding stimulation may have changed its gain. Thus an experiment comparing two endings should report stimulus magnitude, duration, location, baseline state and the time at which the maintained response is measured. Comparing a rate measured immediately after one stimulus with a rate measured much later after another provides little evidence about relative sensitivity unless the time dependence has been considered.

Recovery is the complementary process by which responsiveness returns after the adapting stimulus is removed or reduced. Its time course need not be identical to that of the decline during stimulation. A two-pulse experiment can examine recovery by applying a conditioning step, waiting a variable interval and then testing the response to a standard probe stimulus. If the probe produces a smaller response at short intervals but a larger response after longer intervals, recovery is occurring. An unchanged response to directly injected current together with a reduced response to the natural probe would suggest that the reduction lies upstream of spike generation. The experiment should include trials without the conditioning step, because responses can drift for reasons unrelated to adaptation.

Adaptation can help maintain useful responses over wide ranges of background conditions. The visual system must operate in dim and bright environments, and tactile receptors encounter different levels of ongoing mechanical load. If the transduction machinery used an identical gain at all backgrounds, an intense background could drive many receptors toward saturation, leaving little capacity to distinguish a small additional change. Adjusting sensitivity can shift the operating range so that variations remain distinguishable. Yet excessive adaptation can compromise the representation of persistent information. Different sensory channels balance these competing needs according to their physiological role. The examination-relevant conclusion is that adaptation is functional adjustment, not a universal loss of receptor viability or an obligatory disappearance of every sensation.

8. Receptive fields and spatial representation

A sensory neuron is influenced by stimuli in particular parts of the environment or body. Its receptive field is the region within which stimulation under specified conditions changes its activity. For a cutaneous afferent, this can be mapped as a region of the skin. For a retinal neuron, it corresponds to a region of visual space or of the retinal surface, depending on the coordinate system used in the experiment. A receptive field is defined by response, not simply by tracing the visible outline of a neuron or counting its anatomical branches. The field of a central neuron can include inputs from several peripheral receptors and may contain regions whose stimulation has opposite effects on firing.

A practical method for mapping a tactile receptive field is to move a controlled probe between locations while recording from an identified afferent. At each location, the same magnitude and duration of indentation are delivered and the change from baseline activity is measured. Positions at which stimulation produces a reproducible response define a map of sensitivity. A more detailed representation shows the response magnitude at each position rather than merely drawing a binary boundary. The chosen stimulus strength and response criterion can affect the apparent size of the field. A stronger probe can reveal weakly sensitive peripheral regions or spread deformation beyond the point of direct contact. To compare field sizes, the mapping conditions must be standardised.

Receptive fields can overlap. A given skin location may influence several neighbouring afferents, and each afferent may be influenced by a region containing multiple possible points of contact. Overlap does not make spatial localisation impossible. If the relative responses of nearby afferents vary systematically with stimulus position, a population of overlapping fields can carry information about location. A stimulus near the centre of one field and near the edge of another may activate the first more strongly. As the stimulus moves, the relative activity shifts. The central nervous system can use the identity and pattern of the responding neurons to distinguish locations even when no single receptor provides a unique address for every point.

Spatial resolution depends on the size and density of receptive fields. In a region with many densely distributed small fields, two nearby stimuli are more likely to produce distinct patterns of neural activity than in a region sampled by relatively few large fields. This explains part of the superior tactile acuity of the fingertips relative to broad areas of the trunk. But field size is not the sole determinant. The amount of overlap, sensitivity gradients within each field, central processing and the physical spread of stimulus energy also influence discrimination. A conclusion about acuity should therefore consider the entire pathway from the external stimulus to the neural representation.

Convergence occurs when multiple input neurons contribute to the response of a downstream neuron. A central neuron receiving excitation from five neighbouring receptors can respond to stimulation at any of their corresponding positions, giving it an effective field that is broader than that of each input. This arrangement can improve detection if weak inputs sum to produce a measurable response. It can also reduce the spatial specificity of that particular downstream response because distinct input locations now converge on a common output. The outcome depends on whether the input identities remain available elsewhere in the network. Convergence is therefore not intrinsically disadvantageous; it trades or combines different kinds of information depending on how the network is organised.

Divergence is the converse anatomical arrangement: one input influences more than one target. A tactile afferent can contribute to local motor responses as well as to ascending pathways that support perception, through different branches and circuits. This means that a single receptor event can have different functional consequences depending on the receiving pathway. Divergence also permits the same stimulus information to be transformed in different ways, for example into representations emphasising timing or spatial relationships. It does not mean that an impulse splits into smaller-amplitude copies at each branch; under normal conditions the regenerative mechanism supports propagation into the appropriate axonal branches.

Topographic organisation preserves ordered relationships among sensory inputs. Neighbouring locations on a sensory surface can be represented by neighbouring groups of central neurons. A somatotopic representation preserves aspects of the spatial organisation of the body, and a retinotopic representation preserves aspects of the spatial organisation of visual input. A sensory map need not assign equal neural territory to equal physical distances. Regions with greater receptor density, more detailed input and important behavioural functions can have disproportionately large representations. Central map size reflects the organisation and processing requirements of the pathway rather than simply the physical area of the body or sensory organ.

The concept of a receptive field also applies to central cells whose activity represents more complex features. A neuron's response may depend on the pattern of stimulation across its field, the direction of movement, or the relationship between a central region and its surroundings. In such cases, identifying the location alone is insufficient to predict the response. The mapping stimulus must test the relevant spatial and temporal arrangements. A central neuron that responds strongly when a local feature differs from its surroundings may respond weakly when a uniform stimulus covers the entire field. This distinction is the foundation for understanding lateral inhibition and centre-surround organisation.

9. Labelled lines, modality and central interpretation

The electrical properties of an impulse do not by themselves establish whether it represents a visual event or a mechanical disturbance of the skin. A propagated impulse from a cutaneous afferent can resemble one recorded from an auditory afferent. The information about modality arises partly from the identity of the activated neuron and the connections through which its signals reach other parts of the nervous system. The labelled-line principle describes the idea that activity in a particular sensory pathway has meaning because of that pathway's origin, selectivity and connections. The label is not a chemical tag attached to each impulse. It is a functional property of the organised neural circuit.

Imagine two afferents delivering impulse trains with the same average firing rate. One originates in a mechanically sensitive ending in the finger, and the other is connected to a cochlear receptor. Even if the rates are identical, their activity arrives in different anatomical pathways and contributes to different patterns of central processing. A sensory circuit does not need to infer the modality from the rate alone because it receives the events through connections with different input identities. This is the reason that a stimulus applied artificially to a sensory pathway can evoke a sensation associated with that pathway even if the normal receptor has not been stimulated. The interpretation of the activity depends on the biological organisation of the circuit.

The doctrine commonly associated with the specific energies of nerves emerged historically from observations that different sensory nerves produced characteristic sensations and that unusual forms of stimulation could activate a modality-specific experience. Modern sensory physiology retains the insight that pathway identity is important while adding a mechanistic account based on receptor specialisation, synaptic organisation and central population activity. It would be misleading to interpret the historical doctrine as stating that visual impulses are composed of a fundamentally different electrical energy from tactile impulses. The basic regenerative electrical mechanism is shared; selective peripheral transduction and central organisation establish the relationship between an impulse pattern and a stimulus category.

Labelled lines are compatible with population coding. A visual pathway may carry light-related information as a broad modality, while multiple visual neurons with different receptive fields and response properties together encode location, contrast and wavelength-related features. Likewise, two tactile afferents may differ in the type of mechanical event to which they are most responsive even though both contribute to somatosensation. The nervous system can compare these labelled inputs to estimate an event's properties. A model in which every possible stimulus must have a dedicated neuron would be unrealistic, because each sensory variable can take many values and stimuli often combine several properties simultaneously. Population responses allow a finite network to represent a large range of such combinations.

The principle also has limitations. A cell can be responsive to more than one type of stimulus, and central neurons can receive inputs related to different sensory modalities. A nociceptor may respond to strongly mechanical, thermal or chemical events associated with potential tissue damage. Its discharge does not always identify the exact physical source without contributions from other receptors. In a multisensory task, visual and auditory information may both influence the interpretation of an event. These examples show that pathway identity constrains meaning but need not uniquely determine every aspect of perception. The appropriate conclusion is not that labelled lines are false, but that the nervous system can use selective channels together with distributed and convergent representations.

Central processing is required for the recognition and use of many sensory patterns. An animal may generate a rapid motor response to touch without first producing a conscious report of the event. A sensory signal can reach local circuitry and also contribute to an ascending pathway. The fact that peripheral transduction is necessary for a normal stimulus-evoked response does not imply that every physiological response requires awareness. Conversely, a normal peripheral recording does not establish that the complete sensory function is intact. A defect in the subsequent pathway could alter the perception or use of information despite preservation of receptor currents and afferent impulses. This separation of stages will be especially valuable when analysing the visual and auditory systems.

The problem of localisation illustrates why pathway identity is only part of a complete explanation. Knowing that a train of impulses comes from a tactile afferent establishes that it is related to contact or another appropriate mechanical stimulus, but an observer also needs information about the field of that afferent and its response relative to nearby afferents. Knowing that activity comes from the left eye does not identify a particular object within the visual field. The central organisation of projections, together with the distribution and timing of impulses, allows circuits to recover more specific spatial or temporal information. Thus modality, location, intensity and duration are different decoding problems, although their neural solutions can interact.

A useful reasoning exercise considers two manipulations. The first selectively disrupts a sensory transduction protein in an otherwise intact receptor. The second interrupts an ascending pathway after normal peripheral activity has been generated. A natural stimulus may fail to produce a normal percept in both cases. However, in the first case a peripheral recording can reveal reduced transduction, and direct electrical stimulation of an intact downstream segment may bypass the defect. In the second case the peripheral response may remain normal, but stimulation below the interruption may still fail to reach the relevant central circuits. An experiment must therefore specify where the signal is introduced and where it is measured. Identical behavioural outcomes can arise from very different defects in the chain of sensory processing.

10. Lateral inhibition, contrast enhancement and spatial discrimination

A sensory surface does not merely report the absolute intensity at each location. Many important features are defined by relationships between neighbouring locations. The boundary between a bright object and a dark background can be more informative about the object's outline than the average amount of light in the entire scene. A local point of pressure is more easily identified when the strongest response is distinguishable from activity generated by the surrounding tissue. Lateral inhibition is a circuit mechanism in which activity associated with one sensory location suppresses activity associated with neighbouring locations or channels. By changing the relative response of adjacent units, it can make spatial differences more prominent.

An elementary model begins with three neighbouring sensory channels. Each channel receives excitation related to its own stimulus, and each can also be influenced by inhibitory activity associated with nearby channels. In a spatially uniform stimulus, all three channels receive similar direct excitation and similar inhibitory influences. Their outputs may be reduced but remain relatively similar. When one channel receives greater excitation than its neighbours, its inhibitory influence on those neighbours is larger than the reverse influence they exert on it, under suitable connectivity. The local response maximum becomes more distinct relative to its surroundings. This is contrast enhancement; it does not require an increase in the absolute firing rate of the most strongly stimulated unit.

Lateral inhibition is usually implemented through organised synaptic connections, often involving interneurons or specialised retinal circuitry. It should not be imagined as one propagated impulse physically colliding with another in a neighbouring axon. In a typical inhibitory pathway, transmitter release alters the postsynaptic conductance or the state of another cell, thereby reducing the probability or magnitude of that cell's response to excitation. In some circuits, inhibitory effects can also act on transmitter release at an earlier stage. The anatomical sign of a synapse and the net effect of a multi-neuron pathway are distinct concepts. A circuit can include several connections whose combined effect is suppression of neighbouring responses even when the detailed implementation differs among species and modalities.

The classical experiments of H. K. Hartline, H. G. Wagner and Floyd Ratliff on the lateral eye of the horseshoe crab Limulus in 1956 supplied direct evidence for inhibitory interactions between receptor units. Each ommatidial unit contributes an identifiable impulse discharge in an optic nerve fibre. An investigator can illuminate a selected test unit while recording its impulses, and then illuminate other units while keeping the direct illumination of the test unit unchanged. A reduction in the test discharge when neighbouring units are stimulated demonstrates that the test response is not determined solely by the light falling on that unit. Subsequent work explored the relationships among inhibitory influences from different units. This is a particularly clear historical example of the value of independent control of a local stimulus and its surround.

The interpretation requires appropriate controls. If the test unit is illuminated alone and its rate is already falling because of adaptation, a later reduction in rate when another unit is illuminated could be wrongly attributed to inhibition. Repeated test-alone trials at comparable times and alternating stimulus conditions help distinguish the two effects. It is also necessary to control unwanted changes in light reaching the test unit. Light scattered from a neighbouring stimulus could alter direct excitation, potentially obscuring the effect being measured. Holding direct excitation fixed while selectively changing the neighbour is the logical basis for identifying an interaction. The exact numerical examples used below are simplified teaching models, not the published recordings of Hartline and colleagues.

Consider an illustrative feed-forward subtraction model with three channels A, B and C. Let their direct excitatory drives be xA, xB and xC, expressed as equivalent spike-rate units. Suppose the output of channel B is yB = max(0, xB − k(xA + xC)), where k is a dimensionless inhibitory weight, and the max function prevents negative firing rates. For an interior channel, the model assumes that inhibition from the two immediate neighbours is additive and that the excitatory drives are independent of the output rates. If xA = 20, xB = 60 and xC = 20 with k = 0.25, the central output is 60 − 0.25(40) = 50. If the centre's direct drive remains at 60 but each neighbouring drive increases to 60, the central output falls to 30. In the model, the centre responds differently to an identical local stimulus because the surrounding stimulus changed.

The same model clarifies edge enhancement. Imagine a long row of channels, where those on the dark side of an edge have a direct drive of 20 and those on the bright side have a drive of 60. Away from the edge, a dark-side interior channel has an output of 20 − 0.25(20 + 20) = 10, and a bright-side interior channel has an output of 60 − 0.25(60 + 60) = 30. At the border, the last dark channel has neighbours of 20 and 60, so its output becomes 20 − 0.25(80) = 0. The first bright channel also has neighbours of 20 and 60, so its output becomes 60 − 20 = 40. The output difference across the border is therefore 40, compared with the difference of 20 between the two uniform interior regions after inhibition. The border contrast is accentuated relative to the uniform-region contrast even though the bright border channel's output remains below its uninhibited direct drive of 60.

This model makes useful predictions but does not reproduce every detail of a biological inhibitory network. Real units can inhibit one another reciprocally; the inhibitory effect may depend on the actual discharge of the neighbour rather than on its direct stimulus drive. Inhibition may have a threshold, a delay or a saturating dependence on input, and the receiving cell can also have nonlinear excitatory responses. Some sensory fields include several spatial scales of excitatory and inhibitory influence. A model based on subtracting two fixed neighbour weights is useful for learning the sign and spatial consequences of inhibition, but its quantitative predictions must not be transferred automatically to an actual organ.

Centre-surround organisation describes an effective receptive field with opposing response regions. For an ON-centre, OFF-surround retinal ganglion cell, light in the centre increases its discharge relative to baseline under appropriate adaptation conditions, while light in the surround tends to decrease it. An OFF-centre, ON-surround cell shows the complementary relationship. If a spot illuminates only the centre of an ON-centre field, the excitatory effect is relatively unopposed. If a larger uniform spot illuminates both centre and surround, the opposing influences can partially cancel, producing a smaller net response. Such cells are particularly informative about local differences in illumination rather than simply the average brightness across the entire field. The detailed retinal circuit, including the signs of photoreceptor and bipolar-cell responses, belongs to 7.E.03.

An important distinction is that centre-surround antagonism is a measured functional organisation of a receptive field, whereas lateral inhibition refers to a mechanism involving interactions between spatially or functionally related channels. The two are related but not identical. One cannot determine the complete synaptic architecture merely from a centre-surround response map. Different networks can produce similar maps, so additional measurements or manipulations are required to identify the mechanism. This is a general principle of scientific inference: a useful computational description of a circuit's output does not necessarily specify the molecular or anatomical implementation that produces it.

In tactile processing, inhibitory interactions can help distinguish nearby points by suppressing activity between strongly excited regions. Suppose two points of contact initially produce broadly overlapping excitation. If spatial interactions reduce the activity of less strongly excited channels between or around the maxima, the resulting representation may contain two more distinguishable peaks. However, if the two points excite virtually the same receptor population and produce no reliably distinguishable differences in input, downstream inhibition cannot recreate spatial information that was never sampled. Lateral inhibition enhances usable contrast within the constraints of peripheral sampling. It does not give an organ unlimited spatial resolution.

Excessive inhibition can also impair detection. If a weak isolated signal occurs in a region receiving substantial inhibition from a bright or strongly activated surround, the net output may fall below a criterion even though the peripheral receptor itself remains responsive. A circuit optimised for contrast may give relatively little weight to the absolute intensity of a broad uniform stimulus. Other channels with different spatial organisation can supply complementary information. These trade-offs explain why sensory systems often include parallel pathways with different receptive-field properties. They also explain why the statement that inhibition always increases sensitivity is incorrect: improvement of discrimination between spatial features is not the same outcome as improved detection of every weak stimulus.

11. Sensory acuity and psychophysics: relating physical stimuli to detection and perception

Physiological recordings establish how receptors and neurons respond, but the function of a sensory system is ultimately evaluated in relation to what the animal can detect, discriminate or use. Psychophysics studies relationships between controlled physical stimulation and behavioural or perceptual responses. It differs from a direct recording of receptor current because the measured outcome may depend on many stages of processing, including decision and motor response. In a simple human detection task, the experimenter presents a stimulus or a blank and asks whether a stimulus was perceived. In an animal experiment, a trained subject may perform one of two responses depending on the presence or location of a stimulus. In both cases the response is a behavioural measure, not a direct measurement of the membrane potential of a receptor.

An absolute threshold is a stimulus intensity corresponding to a specified probability of detection in a defined task. If intensities are presented repeatedly, the proportion of correct detections or responses generally changes gradually rather than jumping sharply at one physical value. A psychometric curve plots the response probability against the relevant stimulus variable. In a yes-or-no task, a threshold might be described by a chosen detection percentage, provided false reports on blank trials are considered. In a two-alternative forced-choice task, chance performance is 50%, and a criterion such as 75% correct is often used to define a threshold above chance. These criteria are conventions tied to the task and cannot be compared indiscriminately across different procedures.

For example, suppose a subject must indicate in which of two time intervals a brief vibration occurred. At a very weak amplitude, the subject guesses and obtains approximately 50% correct. At increasing amplitudes, performance rises to 60%, 75%, 90% and eventually approaches 100% under favourable conditions. If the protocol defines threshold as the amplitude corresponding to 75% correct, that value estimates detection sensitivity for the stated conditions. It does not mean that a smaller vibration can never be detected or that every larger vibration will always be detected. The curve reflects variability across trials, and the threshold is a summary of this probabilistic relationship. Presenting the actual curve gives more information than reporting the threshold alone.

In a yes-or-no task, the participant may adopt a liberal or conservative decision criterion. A liberal criterion increases the tendency to report that a stimulus was present, which can increase both correct reports on stimulus trials and incorrect reports on blank trials. A conservative criterion reduces false alarms but can also increase missed detections. A measured increase in the proportion of yes responses therefore does not necessarily indicate improved peripheral sensitivity. The experiment should distinguish hits, misses, false alarms and correct rejections, and control task instructions and stimulus probabilities when comparing conditions. This elementary distinction between sensitivity and response criterion is sufficient for interpreting many examination questions without invoking more specialised mathematical models of signal detection.

A difference threshold, often called a just-noticeable difference, concerns discrimination between stimuli rather than detection of a stimulus relative to its absence. A subject may reliably detect a weight placed on the hand but require a particular additional weight to distinguish it from a comparison weight. The minimum reliably discriminable increment depends on the starting intensity and on the method used to test discrimination. At many intermediate stimulus levels, an approximate empirical relationship called Weber's law states that the ratio of the just-noticeable increment ΔS to the reference intensity S is approximately constant for a specified modality and condition. This dimensionless ratio, k = ΔS/S, is the Weber fraction.

Suppose a hypothetical weight-discrimination experiment gives a just-noticeable difference of 5 g at a reference mass of 100 g. The Weber fraction is 5/100 = 0.05. If the same fraction remains valid at a reference mass of 300 g under the same experimental conditions, the predicted increment is 0.05 × 300 = 15 g. Notice that the absolute increment becomes larger at the greater reference mass while the relative increment remains constant. Mass is used as a convenient stimulus variable in this example; the gravitational force is proportional to mass at the same location. The prediction is conditional on the tested operating range and cannot be applied automatically to extremely small or large masses, to different tasks or to a different modality.

Weber's law is distinct from Fechner's historical proposal about perceived magnitude. If one assumes that each just-noticeable increment corresponds to an equal small increment in perceived sensation, and that Weber's proportionality holds over a range, then a mathematical integration gives a logarithmic relationship between an idealised sensation magnitude and physical intensity. In symbolic form, let dP = a dS/S, where P is a hypothetical perception scale and a is a proportionality constant. Integrating from a positive reference intensity S₀ to S gives P(S) − P(S₀) = a ln(S/S₀). The logarithm operates on a dimensionless ratio, and the reference cannot be zero. This derivation shows exactly which assumption connects the discrimination result to the sensation scale. Weber's law itself does not establish that subjective magnitude must follow a logarithmic function, and real perceptual relationships can deviate from both simplifications.

The distinction becomes clear when a hypothetical question compares equal increments in absolute intensity and equal ratios of intensity. Under Weber's law, a change from 10 to 11 units and a change from 100 to 110 units both have a relative increment of 0.1, so both have the same increment expressed in Weber fractions if the constant remains unchanged. Under the idealised logarithmic magnitude model, the change in P also depends on the ratio and is the same for the two cases. However, the models address different dependent variables. The former predicts discriminability under stated conditions, and the latter proposes a relation for perceived magnitude under additional assumptions. A receptor's firing rate is not necessarily equal to either of these quantities; psychophysics concerns the relation between physical stimulation and perceptual or behavioural outcomes.

Spatial acuity is the ability to discriminate fine differences in stimulus position or spatial pattern. In tactile physiology, a classical task asks whether simultaneous contact at two points is experienced as one or two. The minimum separation at which the subject reliably distinguishes the points gives an operational two-point discrimination threshold. The result depends on the physical arrangement of the probes, their pressure, timing, orientation, the region of skin, attention and the response criterion. It is not a direct measurement of the diameter of a single receptor field. A low discrimination threshold is consistent with high spatial acuity, but several mechanisms can contribute to the result.

Consider two skin regions with comparable detection thresholds for an isolated probe but different receptor densities and convergence. One region has many small overlapping fields and maintains much of the spatial detail in its central representation. The other has broad fields and extensive convergence. Both may respond when a weak probe contacts the skin, yet the second region may require wider separation before two probes generate distinguishable patterns. This dissociation between detection and discrimination is not a contradiction. It demonstrates that the total evidence for stimulation and the spatial information contained within that evidence are different properties of a sensory code.

There are limits to inferring sensory architecture from psychophysical performance alone. If training improves the ability to discriminate two nearby stimuli, the improvement might result from better use of existing neural information, altered attention or changes in central processing rather than an increase in the number of peripheral receptors. Conversely, a reduction in performance can reflect an inappropriate response criterion, fatigue or impaired motor reporting, as well as a change in peripheral sensitivity. Combining behavioural measurements with physiological recordings makes explanations more specific. The key question in an experimental interpretation is which alternatives have been excluded by the available observations and which remain possible.

12. Experimental integration: locating the origin of a change in sensory output

The previous sections describe a sequence of physical, electrical, synaptic and central transformations. A change at the end of that sequence does not by itself identify the step responsible. A useful experimental strategy is to measure signals at successive stages and then bypass particular steps with controlled stimulation. Consider a tactile ending whose spike response to a standard indentation has decreased. Possible explanations include a change in the mechanical coupling between probe and ending, a reduction in transduction current, a smaller voltage response to the same current, a failure of the spike initiation mechanism or a defect in propagation. If the behavioural response rather than peripheral firing is the measured outcome, defects in central processing and motor reporting must also be considered.

An initial experiment can compare the current evoked by the standard indentation at the same holding voltage before and after a manipulation. If the stimulus-dependent current is reduced, the difference lies at or upstream of the measured current-generating process, assuming comparable recording conditions. Further experiments can determine whether the physical deformation actually reaching the transducing membrane is reduced. If the deformation is unchanged and the current is smaller, a change in channel availability, channel regulation or the relevant ionic driving force becomes more plausible. Measuring current-voltage relations can help distinguish a decrease in conductance from a shift in reversal potential. Neither observation by itself identifies a particular gene or protein without additional evidence.

A complementary experiment injects a controlled depolarising current while recording the voltage and spikes of the sensory neuron. This stimulation bypasses the ordinary mechanism that couples the external stimulus to a change in conductance. If the neuron produces normal responses to injected current but responds poorly to natural stimulation, its spike generator can still operate under the test conditions. If both responses are reduced, altered excitability or another downstream problem becomes a possibility. The interpretation depends on where current is injected; stimulation of an axon may bypass a defect in the normal spike initiation region. The most precise conclusion is therefore about the pathway segments included in each test, not the entire neuron indiscriminately.

The same reasoning can distinguish peripheral and synaptic defects in a receptor-cell system. If displacement of an auditory hair bundle still produces a normal graded voltage response but the connected afferent no longer fires normally, transduction is preserved by that particular measure. Altered calcium entry, transmitter release, postsynaptic sensitivity or afferent excitability could account for the deficit. Direct recording of synaptic responses and stimulation of the afferent can narrow these alternatives. A normal voltage response alone cannot establish normal transmitter release because the coupling from voltage to vesicle fusion is itself a distinct physiological process. Such a result is an excellent illustration of the importance of separating a receptor potential from propagated neural output.

Experimental controls are indispensable when comparing response dynamics. Suppose an afferent discharges at 70 Hz after the onset of a step and at 20 Hz near the end. The investigator wants to conclude that adaptation has occurred. The stimulus must be verified as constant at the relevant external measurement point, and baseline and recording stability must be monitored. If the mechanical force decays during the step, the fall in response may simply follow that decrease. If the force remains constant but the deformation of the nerve terminal changes because of the capsule, this supports an accessory mechanical contribution to adaptation. If terminal deformation and transduction current remain constant while firing declines, a contribution from downstream excitability becomes more plausible. Each additional measurement excludes particular alternatives rather than automatically proving a single mechanism.

For examining receptive fields, the equivalent strategy is to hold stimulus strength constant and change position, rather than varying strength and position simultaneously. For examining lateral inhibition, one holds the direct stimulus to a recorded unit fixed and changes the surround. For examining temporal coding, one retains the spike timestamps and relates them to the stimulus time course rather than relying only on the total count. For examining behavioural sensitivity, repeated controlled trials and appropriate blank or comparison conditions are necessary. Although these experiments investigate different properties, they share a common principle: vary the independent variable relevant to the proposed mechanism while controlling other factors that could account for the outcome.

The distinction between a hypothetical dataset and published evidence must remain clear. Numerical models are valuable because their assumptions and predictions can be examined without the additional variability of a complex preparation. Their parameters, however, do not become physiological constants simply because they appear in a scientific calculation. A fitted time constant in one sensory ending need not describe another ending or even the same ending under different conditions. A subtraction model of lateral inhibition does not prove that a particular circuit implements exactly those weights. To apply a model to experimental data, compare its predictions with observations, examine discrepancies and consider alternative mechanisms. This reasoning is central to the analytical expectations of CSIR-NET Part C.

Vision, hearing and tactile sensation now provide three opportunities to apply the common framework. In vision, the optical apparatus establishes the distribution of light at the sensory surface, and retinal cells convert that distribution into a structured neural output. In hearing, mechanical processing separates and transfers components of sound to receptor cells, which communicate with afferent fibres. In touch, skin mechanics and receptor specialisations convert local deformation into patterns of afferent activity with different spatial and temporal sensitivities. In each case the next questions are the same: which physical stimulus reaches the receptor, which molecular events change the membrane signal, how is the output encoded, and what transformations allow the nervous system to discriminate the features that matter? The following chapters develop the specific organs using this shared conceptual foundation.

14. Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Every sensory receptor is a separate epithelial cell.

Correct concept: Many receptors are endings of primary sensory neurons; others are separate cells connected to afferents. Reasoning: Diagrams of special sense organs often show two cells. Follow the transducing membrane and identify the axon carrying spikes.

2. Adequate stimulus means the only stimulus that can activate a pathway.

Correct concept: Adequacy denotes physiological specialisation and relative sensitivity; artificial activation may bypass the receptor. Reasoning: A question about electrical nerve stimulation tests pathway identity rather than normal stimulus selectivity.

3. All stimuli depolarise their receptor cells.

Correct concept: Vertebrate rods and cones hyperpolarise in light as inward conductance declines. Reasoning: Activation is confused with excitation. Determine which conductance changes and compare membrane and reversal potentials.

4. A receptor potential must reach spike threshold to exist.

Correct concept: A graded response can occur below the firing threshold. Reasoning: Distinguish recording a current or voltage from recording propagated impulses.

5. A strong stimulus produces proportionately larger propagated spikes.

Correct concept: Intensity commonly changes rate, timing and recruitment, not the height of individual regenerative impulses. Reasoning: A larger compound recording can reflect more fibres; check whether the data describe a single axon.

6. A stimulus frequency and an afferent's spike frequency are identical.

Correct concept: Physical cycles and neural events per second are distinct; phase locking can skip cycles. Reasoning: Both are measured in hertz. Identify the variable and the method of measurement.

7. Rapid adaptation means fast axonal conduction.

Correct concept: Adaptation describes response dynamics during sustained stimulation; conduction describes propagation. Reasoning: Do not use a brief onset response to infer axon diameter or myelination.

8. A slowly adapting receptor never changes its firing rate.

Correct concept: It maintains some response but may decline from an onset peak. Reasoning: Inspect the long-time response and the time constant separately.

9. Peripheral adaptation is the same as behavioural habituation.

Correct concept: A behavioural decline may result from central processes even when the receptor responds normally. Reasoning: Require stage-specific recordings to localise the cause.

10. A constant force means constant deformation at the nerve ending.

Correct concept: Accessory tissues may redistribute mechanical stress despite unchanged external force. Reasoning: Compare the externally controlled variable with the actual input to the receptor membrane.

11. A receptive field has a fixed, sharply defined anatomical boundary.

Correct concept: It is an operational response map that can depend on stimulus and criterion. Reasoning: Standardise probe strength and response criterion when comparing field sizes.

12. Convergence always improves acuity.

Correct concept: Convergence may aid detection but reduce spatial specificity in a shared downstream output. Reasoning: Distinguish summation from preservation of spatial identity.

13. Labelled lines exclude population coding.

Correct concept: Pathway identity and patterns across selective channels can jointly convey information. Reasoning: Equal total counts need not mean equal perceptual or spatial information.

14. Lateral inhibition necessarily raises the peak firing rate.

Correct concept: Contrast may increase even while all absolute rates fall. Reasoning: Compare differences between neighbouring outputs rather than the maximum alone.

15. Lateral inhibition can reconstruct information never sampled.

Correct concept: It enhances available differences but cannot uniquely decode identical peripheral patterns. Reasoning: Compare input patterns first; do not assume central processing creates missing spatial data.

16. A detection threshold is an invariant stimulus below which responses are impossible.

Correct concept: Threshold is estimated at a defined detection probability or response criterion. Reasoning: Identify the task, baseline and variability before interpreting threshold.

17. Weber's law says every receptor fires logarithmically.

Correct concept: Weber's law concerns relative just-noticeable differences; Fechner's magnitude model adds an assumption. Reasoning: Keep psychophysical discrimination, perceived magnitude and neuronal firing as separate dependent variables.

18. A normal receptor voltage proves normal sensation.

Correct concept: Synaptic transmission, afferent excitability and central interpretation can fail after transduction. Reasoning: Follow the complete signal chain and determine which stages were actually tested.

1. Which property defines an adequate stimulus?

  1. The stimulus to which the receptor is specialised and relatively sensitive
  2. The only event capable of activating the axon
  3. The smallest action potential amplitude
  4. The stimulus producing consciousness in every trial
Answer and scientific explanation

A. The stimulus to which the receptor is specialised and relatively sensitive

Adequacy describes receptor specialisation. Electrical stimulation can bypass transduction, and consciousness depends on subsequent circuits. Spike amplitude is not a criterion of adequacy.

2. A cutaneous mechanoreceptor and an arterial stretch receptor share which classification?

  1. Exteroceptors
  2. Photoreceptors
  3. Separate epithelial receptor cells
  4. Mechanoreceptors
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Mechanoreceptors

Both detect mechanical deformation. Their locations and cellular organisations need not be the same; the arterial receptor is an interoceptor.

3. Which event is sensory transduction?

  1. Conduction of a spike along an axon
  2. Interpretation of an object in cortex
  3. A mechanical displacement changing a receptor's membrane conductance
  4. Contraction of a muscle
Answer and scientific explanation

C. A mechanical displacement changing a receptor's membrane conductance

Transduction converts stimulus information into a cellular electrical signal. Conduction, central interpretation and motor output are subsequent processes.

4. The graded voltage change produced by a sensory stimulus is generally termed:

  1. An obligatory propagated spike
  2. Receptor potential
  3. Refractory period
  4. Conduction velocity
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Receptor potential

The receptor potential can vary with stimulus strength and need not produce a spike. The other terms describe properties of propagated excitation.

5. In a mature mammalian cochlear inner hair cell, the graded response directly regulates:

  1. Myelin thickness
  2. Glutamate release onto afferent neurons
  3. Action-potential amplitude in the optic nerve
  4. Skeletal muscle contraction
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Glutamate release onto afferent neurons

Hair-cell voltage regulates calcium-dependent transmitter release at the afferent synapse. The propagated auditory impulses occur in the sensory neuron.

6. Which receptor response illustrates that transduction need not depolarise the receptor?

  1. Light-induced hyperpolarisation of a vertebrate rod
  2. Ordinary opening of a cation conductance at a negative voltage
  3. A sodium-dependent axonal spike
  4. Opening of a nonselective cation channel at −60 mV with E = 0
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Light-induced hyperpolarisation of a vertebrate rod

Light closes cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels in rods, reducing inward current. The other listed events tend to depolarise a negative membrane or describe a spike.

7. For the convention I = g(V − E), a channel with E = 0 mV at V = −50 mV carries:

  1. Net outward positive current
  2. No current at any conductance
  3. Only potassium efflux regardless of selectivity
  4. Net inward positive current if g is nonzero
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Net inward positive current if g is nonzero

V − E is negative, so the conventional outward current is negative. The channel's ionic selectivity cannot be inferred solely from the sign.

8. The intensity of a suprathreshold stimulus is commonly represented in one sensory afferent by changes in:

  1. Spike height in direct proportion to intensity
  2. DNA content
  3. Impulse rate and timing
  4. Axon length within milliseconds
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Impulse rate and timing

Regenerative impulses are broadly all-or-none at a stable recording location. Rate and timing can vary with stimulus strength; recruitment operates across afferents.

9. Which response is most characteristic of a rapidly adapting receptor?

  1. No response to a changing stimulus
  2. Necessarily slow conduction
  3. Strong response at onset, little maintained response during an unchanged step
  4. A constant maximal response to all intensities
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Strong response at onset, little maintained response during an unchanged step

Phasic receptors emphasise changes. Adaptation describes the time course of activity under stimulation, not propagation speed.

10. A slowly adapting receptor is expected to:

  1. Generate no graded response
  2. Maintain some activity during a sustained adequate stimulus
  3. Respond only after stimulus removal
  4. Always lack accessory structures
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Maintain some activity during a sustained adequate stimulus

Tonic responses preserve information about a persistent stimulus. They can still decline from an onset peak and can have specialised associated tissue.

11. The receptive field of a cutaneous afferent is best defined as:

  1. The skin region in which stimulation changes its response under stated conditions
  2. The diameter of its axon
  3. The region of its cell body in a ganglion
  4. The distance between spinal cord and cortex
Answer and scientific explanation

A. The skin region in which stimulation changes its response under stated conditions

The field is defined functionally by stimulus-response mapping. It is not a geometric measurement of the axon or ganglion.

12. Convergence of several nearby receptors onto a downstream neuron can:

  1. Make all inputs anatomically identical
  2. Eliminate the need for transduction
  3. Always improve spatial discrimination
  4. Increase detection of weak combined input while broadening that neuron's receptive field
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Increase detection of weak combined input while broadening that neuron's receptive field

Summation can aid detection, but shared output can sacrifice location information. The broader network may preserve other inputs in parallel.

13. The labelled-line principle primarily relates modality to:

  1. The size of individual spikes
  2. The mass of the receptor cell
  3. The colour of the sensory nerve
  4. The anatomical and functional identity of the activated pathway
Answer and scientific explanation

D. The anatomical and functional identity of the activated pathway

Pathway identity and connections give impulses sensory meaning. Spike amplitude and gross nerve appearance are not modality codes.

14. Lateral inhibition often improves:

  1. The amplitude of every action potential
  2. The physical intensity of the incoming light
  3. Contrast between neighbouring sensory representations
  4. The number of photoreceptors formed during each stimulus
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Contrast between neighbouring sensory representations

The circuit changes relative neural output to emphasise spatial differences; it does not modify the physical light or necessarily raise absolute firing.

15. Uniform illumination of an ON-centre, OFF-surround field generally produces less excitation than illumination restricted to the centre because:

  1. The optic nerve conducts only during darkness
  2. The surround opposes centre excitation
  3. The centre no longer contains receptors
  4. All inhibitory synapses disappear
Answer and scientific explanation

B. The surround opposes centre excitation

The antagonistic surround contributes an opposing effect when illuminated. This comparison assumes a suitable operating state and stimulus geometry.

16. Which measurement directly distinguishes an absolute detection threshold from a difference threshold?

  2. Axonal diameter
  3. The number of synapses in a nerve
  4. The peak voltage of a propagated spike
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Whether the task compares a stimulus with absence or compares two stimulus intensities

Detection concerns presence relative to a blank; discrimination concerns reliably distinguishing two stimuli. Neither is determined by spike amplitude alone.

17. The Weber fraction is expressed as:

  1. ΔS/S
  2. S/ΔS²
  3. ΔS in volts for all senses
  4. ln(0)
Answer and scientific explanation

A. ΔS/S

It is a dimensionless ratio of just-noticeable increment to reference intensity. Its approximate constancy is restricted to suitable operating ranges.

18. Which observation alone is insufficient to establish peripheral receptor adaptation?

  2. A reduction in generator potential despite maintained external force

  4. A subject ceasing to report noticing a background stimulus

D. A subject ceasing to report noticing a background stimulus

A behavioural decline can reflect central attention or habituation. The other observations provide physiological evidence at specified stages, though their interpretation still requires controls.

19. The main significance of a sensory receptor's saturation is that:

  1. The neuron always dies
  2. The stimulus is no longer physically present

  4. All other receptors must also saturate

C. Increments in a very strong stimulus may produce little additional output

Saturation compresses the measurable response at high input. Other populations may still distinguish intensities.

20. Which statement best distinguishes detection from perception?

  4. 0.12 ms

A. 12 ms

D. Voltage reaches −55 mV and stays subthreshold

Only 9 mV reaches the trigger region: −64 + 9 = −55 mV. The stimulus was transduced but the model predicts no spike.

  2. Both have the same rate
  3. A = 40 Hz; B = 80 Hz
  4. B must be a 80 Hz physical vibration
Answer and scientific explanation

C. A = 40 Hz; B = 80 Hz

Rate is count divided by time: 24/0.6 = 40, 24/0.3 = 80. These values do not establish the physical frequency of the stimuli.

  2. 250 Hz
  3. 400 Hz
  4. 2500 Hz

C. 400 Hz

  2. The third fibre is recruited and other nonsaturated responses can change
  3. The stimulus becomes a different modality
  4. All four fibres become active

B. The third fibre is recruited and other nonsaturated responses can change

At 5 units the first two are active; at 8 the third also crosses threshold. The fourth remains below threshold; saturation of one fibre does not saturate the population.

  4. Discard the identity of each fibre

  4. Their timing codes differ despite equal mean rate

D. Their timing codes differ despite equal mean rate

The total rate is the same, but timing relative to the stimulus differs. Phase locking is statistical alignment and does not require a spike in every cycle.

D. A more liberal response criterion is a plausible contributor

Both positive reports rose, including false reports on blank trials. The pattern alone does not prove improved sensory sensitivity or a specific peripheral mechanism.

20. A receptor's natural-stimulus current is reduced at a fixed clamp voltage; its injected-current spike response is normal. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. All central processing is proven intact
  2. The exact gene mutation is identified
  3. A defect at or upstream of measured transduction is plausible; the tested spike generator can function
  4. The natural stimulus must be stronger
Answer and scientific explanation

C. A defect at or upstream of measured transduction is plausible; the tested spike generator can function

The two measurements narrow the likely location but do not distinguish mechanical coupling, channel availability or driving-force changes without further tests.

17. Original SET and integrated practice

These original questions are designed for practice; they are not claimed to be previous-year questions.

1. SET: Which pair is correctly matched?

  1. Mechanoreceptor — only chemical ligand binding
  2. Interoceptor — necessarily outside the body
  3. Photoreceptor — photon absorption
  4. Phasic receptor — necessarily slow axonal conduction
Answer and scientific explanation

C. Photoreceptor — photon absorption

Photoreceptors specialise in light detection. Mechanoreception, internal location and phasic adaptation are independent concepts.

2. SET: Which is an example of accessory mechanical filtering?

  1. Voltage regeneration in a myelinated axon
  2. Mechanical stress redistribution through a receptor capsule
  3. Transmitter binding in cerebral cortex
  4. A change in DNA sequence
Answer and scientific explanation

B. Mechanical stress redistribution through a receptor capsule

Capsule mechanics can modify the temporal pattern of deformation reaching a receptor ending, upstream of transduction.

3. Assertion–reason: Assertion: sensory impulse rate can increase without an increase in spike amplitude. Reason: the graded input can shorten the interval between regenerative impulses. Choose the correct relation.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion

Stronger maintained depolarising drive can bring the excitable region to firing conditions more often; individual propagated impulses remain broadly all-or-none.

4. Assertion–reason: Assertion: rapidly adapting receptors are especially useful for detecting changes. Reason: they necessarily conduct impulses more slowly than tonic receptors.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Assertion true; reason false
Answer and scientific explanation

D. Assertion true; reason false

The assertion follows from the time course of the response. Adaptation is not conduction velocity, so the reason is false.

5. Numerical: Weber fraction is 0.04 at an intermediate reference intensity of 200 units. Under the same assumptions, the predicted JND is:

  1. 0.0002 units
  2. 5 units
  3. 50 units
  4. 8 units
Answer and scientific explanation

D. 8 units

ΔS = kS = 0.04 × 200 = 8 units. The prediction is valid only within the assumed Weber range.

6. Numerical: In the Fechner model P₂ − P₁ = a ln(S₂/S₁), what happens when both reference and comparison intensities are multiplied by the same positive factor?

  1. The predicted difference doubles
  2. The difference becomes zero in every case
  3. The difference remains unchanged
  4. The expression becomes undefined
Answer and scientific explanation

C. The difference remains unchanged

The common factor cancels from the ratio, preserving its logarithm. The conclusion concerns the idealised model, not every actual perceptual scale.

7. Numerical: A hypothetical response R = 90S/(3 + S) gives what output at S = 6?

  1. 30
  2. 60
  3. 45
  4. 90
Answer and scientific explanation

B. 60

R = 90 × 6/9 = 60 response units. The maximum 90 is approached asymptotically rather than reached at S = 6.

8. Experimental: A receptor's response to a probe is reduced after a conditioning stimulus but recovers with a longer interval. What control is especially informative?

  1. Repeat the probe without the conditioning stimulus at matched times
  2. Remove all measurement units
  3. Change the electrode on each trial
  4. Use a different receptor for every interval without replication
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Repeat the probe without the conditioning stimulus at matched times

Probe-alone controls help separate recovery from drift or repeated testing effects. Keeping other conditions comparable improves the inference.

9. Integrated: A mutation reduces transmitter release from a hair cell while leaving its graded response intact. What is a likely result?

  1. Afferent excitation may be reduced despite preserved receptor voltage
  2. The hair cell cannot possibly transduce movement
  3. The optic nerve necessarily fires faster
  4. Every somatosensory receptive field enlarges
Answer and scientific explanation

A. Afferent excitation may be reduced despite preserved receptor voltage

Voltage-to-release coupling is a distinct step, so normal voltage does not guarantee normal afferent transmission. The other effects are not implied.

10. Integrated: Two points on skin produce indistinguishable activity in every available peripheral channel. Which statement about lateral inhibition is correct?

  1. It guarantees perfect discrimination
  2. It can recover an arbitrarily exact distance from identical inputs
  3. It changes the physical separation of the probes
  4. It cannot reconstruct spatial differences absent from the available input
Answer and scientific explanation

D. It cannot reconstruct spatial differences absent from the available input

Inhibition can accentuate sampled differences but cannot uniquely distinguish inputs that are genuinely identical at every relevant channel.

18. Revision and mastery

All sensory modalities share a processing chain: stimulus delivery, transduction, graded receptor response, neural encoding and central interpretation. The receptor's adequate stimulus reflects specialisation, while pathway identity contributes to modality. The output may rise or fall; both changes can carry information. A graded receptor potential is not equivalent to a propagated impulse, and not every receptor cell directly generates afferent spikes. Rate, timing and population patterns represent complementary stimulus features. Adaptation changes the response to sustained stimulation through accessory, transduction, intrinsic and central mechanisms. Receptive fields, convergence and spatial interactions organise location information; lateral inhibition enhances contrast without recreating information that was never sampled.

Active recall

1. Define stimulus, transduction, transmission and perception.

Identify the physical variable, the conversion into an electrical response, its subsequent propagation and central interpretation.

2. Classify a Pacinian corpuscle by stimulus, location, cellular arrangement and adaptation.

Mechanical, exteroceptive, encapsulated primary sensory ending, rapidly adapting.

3. Explain why opening a channel does not always produce depolarisation.

Use I = g(V − E) and the influence of other membrane conductances; closing an inward conductance can hyperpolarise.

4. Distinguish receptor potential and action potential.

The former is local and graded; the latter is regenerative and generally all-or-none.

5. Explain a separate receptor cell's connection to an afferent.

Graded receptor voltage changes transmitter release, influencing the afferent spike generator.

6. Predict how recruitment can extend population dynamic range.

Higher-threshold afferents become active even when more sensitive afferents saturate.

7. Explain why equal spike counts need not imply equal stimulus information.

Compare timing and identity of participating neurons rather than counts alone.

8. Draw tonic and phasic responses to a step.

Mark onset, maintained step and offset; show sustained firing in the tonic response and a predominantly transient phasic response.

9. Describe two mechanisms of adaptation in a Pacinian preparation.

Mechanical filtering by the capsule and intrinsic receptor or spike-generating processes.

10. Design a receptive-field mapping experiment.

Use identical controlled probes at different locations and map changes in identified unit activity.

11. Describe the trade-off introduced by convergence.

Summation can enhance detection while reducing location specificity of the common output.

12. Predict what occurs when the surround of an ON-centre field is illuminated together with its centre.

Opposing effects can reduce the net response relative to centre-only stimulation.

13. Explain why Weber's fraction is dimensionless.

Both numerator and denominator express the same physical stimulus variable in matching units.

14. Distinguish a receptor-current deficit from a spike-generation deficit.

Compare natural stimulus currents under controlled clamp conditions and responses to injected current.

15. Distinguish detection threshold, difference threshold and acuity.

Detect presence, discriminate an increment, and distinguish spatial detail, respectively.

Mastery checklist

  • Explain each step from physical stimulus to central interpretation.
  • Use reversal potential and conductance to predict the direction of a sensory current.
  • Compare primary afferent endings with separate receptor cells.
  • Interpret adaptation, stimulus-response, receptive-field and psychometric plots.
  • Calculate firing rates, time constants, illustrative inhibitory outputs and Weber increments with assumptions.
  • Design controlled tests of transduction, adaptation and lateral inhibition.
  • Distinguish physiological responses from behavioural criteria and identify what the data cannot establish.
