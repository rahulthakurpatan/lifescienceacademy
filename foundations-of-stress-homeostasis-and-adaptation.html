1. The problem of maintaining life in a changing environment

An animal is not a closed vessel containing a permanent supply of suitable chemicals. Every cell consumes substrates, produces metabolic wastes and exchanges heat and water with its surroundings. A freshwater fish experiences a constant tendency for water to enter its body; a terrestrial insect risks losing water to dry air; a mammal exercising vigorously experiences a sudden demand for oxygen and fuel. In all three cases the problem is not simply that an external condition has changed. The decisive question is whether the animal can maintain the internal conditions needed for biological function while the environment or its own activity changes. The study of stress and adaptation provides a framework for answering that question.

Imagine a resting animal that begins running. Its contracting muscles consume ATP faster than at rest. ATP must be replenished, and sustained aerobic exercise requires delivery of oxygen and substrates, as well as removal of carbon dioxide and heat. If cardiac output, ventilation and fuel mobilisation remained fixed at resting values, tissue demands could exceed supply. Increasing cardiac output is therefore not automatically a departure from health. It is often precisely the response that helps preserve adequate tissue function. Similarly, water conservation during dehydration may require the kidney to produce urine that differs markedly from urine produced when water is abundant. In both examples, an altered organ activity protects a more important physiological variable.

The distinction between the condition that challenges the organism and the response it produces is foundational. A stressor is an internal or external challenge capable of disturbing, or threatening to disturb, an animal's physiological organisation. The stress response is the set of physiological and behavioural adjustments evoked by that challenge. Stress itself is used somewhat differently by different investigators: some emphasise threatened homeostasis, others emphasise the overall response or the state of physiological challenge. In this chapter the word refers to the physiological challenge and its response context, while the more precise terms stressor and response identify causal events. It is not restricted to emotional distress, and an animal need not display a human-like conscious experience for its physiology to be studied under stress.

Consider exposure to high environmental temperature. Air temperature is the external condition. Its capacity to act as a stressor depends on whether it challenges the organism's ability to balance heat gain and loss. A change in skin blood flow, movement to shade or increased evaporative cooling constitutes a response. Internal temperature measured during the exposure is one possible regulated outcome. Tissue injury after excessive heating is a damaging outcome, not a synonym for the cooling response. A student who conflates these four categories cannot infer whether a treatment has reduced stressor intensity, changed compensatory capacity or merely changed a particular biomarker.

Internal challenges are equally important. Bleeding reduces circulating volume, even if the external environment is unchanged. A fall in plasma glucose during fasting reflects the relationship between fuel intake, storage and utilisation. Infection introduces biologically active material and inflammatory signals within the host. Exercise, reproduction and development also alter physiological demands; these processes may be normal components of life rather than disease. Consequently the distinction between external and internal stressors describes where a challenge arises, not whether it is harmful. A single event often has both components: an external heat challenge can increase internal water loss, which then changes the demands on water and circulatory regulation.

A useful causal model distinguishes input, state, response and outcome. Input is the exposure or challenge. State includes the animal's hydration, nutritional reserves, developmental condition and prior experience. Response describes what the animal does in the face of that input while in that state. Outcome describes the resulting maintenance, loss or restoration of function. Two individuals exposed to identical heat need not produce identical responses because their states differ. Two individuals producing the same response need not have identical outcomes because their physiological reserves or other effector systems differ. This is why a physiological measurement must always be interpreted within a defined exposure, species and time interval.

A response can be protective even when it appears costly. A bird that interrupts feeding to escape a predator loses an opportunity to obtain energy, but the immediate survival benefit can outweigh that cost. A mammal that increases ventilation during exertion spends energy on respiratory muscles while maintaining gas exchange. A fish that moves into cooler water can avoid dangerous heat without changing its biochemical tolerance. Behaviour and internal physiology thus form an integrated defence system. Avoiding an exposure is not the same as tolerating it; this distinction becomes essential when designing experiments to measure animal tolerance.

Conversely, a conspicuous response does not guarantee effective protection. If water loss is extreme, thirst may increase while the animal remains unable to find water. If environmental oxygen is severely reduced, increasing ventilation may be insufficient to support aerobic performance. Strong activation and unsuccessful compensation can occur together. Likewise, a small response is not necessarily evidence of safety. It can indicate that the animal avoids the stressor effectively, that its physiology is already well suited to the exposure, or that its response system is impaired. The meaning of the signal depends on an independently measured functional outcome.

The unit of analysis must also be specified. A cell may experience a changed osmotic gradient while the whole animal maintains a nearly stable plasma composition. A tissue can face a transient shortage of fuel during otherwise well-regulated exercise. A population may contain individuals that differ widely in stress tolerance, and the distribution of those traits can influence survival across seasons. Cellular, organ-system, individual and population responses operate at related but distinct levels. This chapter focuses on animal-level physiological principles; subsequent chapters examine neural and endocrine mediators, cellular protective pathways, comparative environmental challenges and cumulative physiological costs.

For examination purposes, begin every stress question by identifying the imposed variable, the response variable, the functional endpoint and the relevant timescale. For example, if investigators expose two groups of fish to the same reduction in dissolved oxygen and one group maintains swimming speed better, the result establishes a difference in performance under that exposure. It does not by itself reveal whether the difference arises from oxygen uptake, oxygen transport, metabolic demand, behaviour or inherited variation. A valid explanation requires evidence that narrows those possibilities. This habit of separating observation from mechanism is the foundation of comparative stress physiology.

2. Regulated variables, feedback and homeostasis

The internal environment of a multicellular animal includes fluids surrounding and supplying its cells. In vertebrates, extracellular fluid comprises plasma and interstitial fluid, which exchange substances across capillary walls. Although external conditions may fluctuate, many properties of this internal environment must remain compatible with enzyme activity, membrane integrity and cellular transport. The nineteenth-century concept of the internal milieu associated with Claude Bernard emphasised the importance of this stability. Walter Cannon later developed the concept of homeostasis to describe the coordinated physiological processes that maintain suitable internal conditions. Homeostasis does not imply absolute immobility. The organism remains alive by continuously exchanging energy and matter with its environment.

A regulated variable is a measurable physiological property whose variation is constrained by regulatory processes. Examples include extracellular osmolality, arterial carbon dioxide tension and, in endotherms, core temperature within context-dependent ranges. An effector variable is an activity that can be changed to influence the regulated property. Ventilation is an effector activity in respiratory regulation; sweating is an effector activity for heat loss in humans; urine water excretion is an effector activity in fluid balance. A common examination error is to assume every quantity that changes in a feedback loop is itself the regulated quantity. Increased ventilation during exercise can help keep arterial carbon dioxide tension near its appropriate operating range even though breathing frequency is much higher than at rest.

A basic regulatory system requires information about the relevant condition, a means of integrating that information and an effector capable of changing it. A sensor detects a physical or chemical signal. An integrating system links that signal to an effector response. The effector changes a rate or flux that influences the variable being regulated. For example, when effective extracellular solute concentration rises following water loss, osmosensitive systems contribute to thirst and water conservation. Drinking can add water; renal conservation can reduce subsequent water loss. These are not identical effects, but both can contribute to restoring fluid concentration. Feedback closes the causal loop because the resulting internal condition influences subsequent sensor activity.

Negative feedback means that the consequences of a response oppose the deviation that initiated it. The word negative refers to the sign of this causal relationship, not to an undesirable outcome or necessarily a decrease in effector activity. If body temperature rises, an increase in heat loss opposes that rise. If circulating volume falls, an increase in fluid conservation opposes further volume loss. Both examples can involve increased activity. A response may amplify another signal within its pathway while the completed physiological loop is still negative. The loop must be classified by the effect of the response on the original regulated variable, not by one molecular arrow in the pathway.

A simple balance model helps clarify how regulation operates. Let X denote the amount of a substance in a specified body compartment, measured in millimoles or another appropriate unit. Its rate of change equals input rate minus output rate. Thus dX/dt = J_in − J_out, where each J is measured in amount per unit time. At a steady state dX/dt is zero, which means the two rates are equal. It does not mean that either rate is zero. For a hypothetical animal taking in and losing 10 mmol of a solute per hour, the amount remains unchanged while substantial material flows through the system. If intake rises to 13 mmol/h and output initially remains 10 mmol/h, the pool increases by 3 mmol/h until adjustment changes the balance. Feedback can alter the rate of removal or intake. The model assumes the compartment is adequately defined and all significant input and output pathways have been included.

Amount and concentration must not be confused. If X is the amount of a dissolved substance and V the volume of the relevant fluid, concentration C is X/V. Concentration may rise because X increases or because V decreases. In an illustrative compartment containing 300 mmol of solute in 1 L, concentration is 300 mmol/L. Removing 0.1 L of pure water without removing solute gives 300/0.9, approximately 333 mmol/L. The increase is about eleven percent, not ten percent, because the denominator changes. Real extracellular fluid is more complex, and changes in effective osmoles, redistribution and solute excretion complicate the calculation. Nevertheless the example explains why a concentration signal alone does not identify the original cause of a disturbance.

Regulation also has finite speed. If a disturbance begins abruptly, the regulated variable may deviate before effectors have achieved their full response. Delays arise from sensory detection, transmission, biochemical reactions and the time required for flux changes to influence the internal compartment. A transient deviation is therefore compatible with successful homeostatic regulation. The amplitude of the deviation, duration and residual error provide different information. An animal might show a large initial deviation followed by rapid recovery, while another shows a modest deviation that persists for a long time. Without a defined endpoint, neither can universally be labelled better regulated.

For a simplified small-perturbation model, write x = X − X_ref, with x expressed in the same units as the regulated variable. If an external disturbance is represented by u in units of X per minute and proportional restoration by −kx, where k is a positive rate constant in inverse minutes, then dx/dt = u − kx. With u constant, the model approaches a steady deviation u/k. When the disturbance is removed, the deviation decreases exponentially according to x(t) = x(0)e^(−kt). A larger k represents faster restoration in this particular model. It is not a universal physiological gain constant. Real regulatory systems include nonlinear responses, delays, saturation, multiple interacting effectors and changing operating conditions.

Suppose that a hypothetical variable deviates by 8 units after an exposure ends. After 10 minutes, a response system reduces the deviation to 4 units. The model implies a recovery half-time of 10 minutes, so the estimated rate constant is ln(2)/10, about 0.0693 per minute. After another 10 minutes the predicted deviation is 2 units. If the measured value instead remains at 4 units, the simple model is inadequate or a disturbance persists. Such calculations help distinguish a descriptive curve from a causal explanation. They also show why data at a single time point are insufficient for judging recovery.

The idea of an operating range is often more realistic than a rigid set point. Body temperature changes with circadian phase, activity and reproductive state. Arterial pressure varies with posture and behaviour. The regulatory system constrains variables within context-appropriate limits rather than maintaining every measurement at its resting mean. A change in temperature can result from a failure to dissipate heat or from a coordinated shift in thermal regulation; the direction of change alone is insufficient to distinguish them. In a fever, for example, coordinated thermoregulatory responses can temporarily defend an elevated temperature; passive overheating has a different relationship between heat load and regulation. Detailed thermal mechanisms belong to the preceding subunit.

Negative feedback cannot prevent every disturbance. Each effector has a finite capacity, and the effectiveness of one pathway may depend on the integrity of another. Increasing peripheral blood flow can help dissipate heat only if the environmental gradient and other physical conditions permit effective heat exchange. Evaporative cooling depends on water availability and the capacity of the environment to accept water vapour. Regulatory limits are therefore determined by both physiological capacity and environmental constraints. Failure beyond these limits does not invalidate the feedback concept; it indicates that the disturbance has exceeded the conditions under which regulation can achieve the required outcome.

The control of an animal is also distributed. Local tissue responses, endocrine signals, neural reflexes and behavioural decisions can all influence the same physiological quantity. There need not be a single central comparator for every regulated variable. A mammalian reflex can be analysed as a convenient sensor–integrator–effector loop while local vascular responses simultaneously influence blood flow. The simplified feedback diagram is useful because it predicts the direction of compensation, but the actual organism is a network. This distinction prevents a student from mistaking an instructional model for a complete anatomical map.

3. Allostasis, anticipation and physiological coordination

Homeostasis describes the achievement of suitable internal stability through regulation. Allostasis highlights the active adjustments by which an organism maintains function under changing demands, including anticipatory and coordinated adjustments. The terms overlap in actual physiological processes and authors do not use them in entirely identical ways. It is unhelpful to treat them as mutually exclusive categories. An animal can maintain stable blood chemistry by changing cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine and behavioural activities. These changing activities can be considered allostatic means of achieving homeostatic ends.

A familiar example is the transition from rest to exercise. Motor commands recruit skeletal muscles, and circulatory and respiratory adjustments can begin in anticipation of the full rise in metabolic products. Feedback from working muscles and blood chemistry continues to refine the response. If the animal had to wait for substantial oxygen deficiency before changing delivery, its performance would suffer. Anticipation can reduce the magnitude of a disturbance. Feedforward regulation means that a response is initiated by a signal predicting a demand or disturbance, rather than exclusively by measurement of the resulting error. It is not identical to positive feedback. In feedforward control the predictor acts before or alongside the consequences of an event, whereas in positive feedback the consequences amplify the originating change.

Predictive signals are valuable only to the extent that they convey reliable information. A nocturnal animal can prepare for the active phase through biological rhythms. A food-associated cue can evoke anticipatory physiological changes. An animal encountering a familiar predator-related signal may alter behaviour before physical contact occurs. In each case the initiating signal is not necessarily the regulated variable itself. If the prediction is wrong, the organism may spend resources unnecessarily. Feedback can then help correct the resulting mismatch. Both prediction and error correction are important because the environment contains regularities as well as surprises.

Allostatic coordination is especially useful when physiological priorities conflict. During activity, blood flow must support contracting muscle while maintaining adequate arterial pressure and perfusion of other essential tissues. During dehydration, preserving circulating volume may become important even as increased evaporation would help cool the body. A regulatory network cannot maximise every desirable outcome independently when resources are limited. It must adjust the relative allocation of resources. This is why an optimal response for one tissue or variable can create a cost elsewhere.

Consider a simplified oxygen-delivery example. Oxygen delivery to the systemic circulation is approximately cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content, provided units are compatible. If cardiac output is 5 L/min and arterial oxygen content is 200 mL O2/L, delivery is 1000 mL O2/min. During a hypothetical activity, output increases to 8 L/min while arterial content remains 200 mL/L, giving 1600 mL O2/min. This calculation is delivery, not oxygen consumption; tissues extract only part of the delivered oxygen. The example illustrates how the rate of a supporting process changes to meet demand without requiring a corresponding change in arterial oxygen concentration. It also highlights the importance of distinguishing a regulated blood property from a flux that supports tissues.

The resources used in compensation are not free. Increasing ventilation and circulation requires energy. Evaporative heat loss consumes water. Maintaining ionic gradients requires active transport supported by ATP. Behavioural vigilance can reduce time available for feeding or mating. These costs can be biologically worthwhile when they help an animal survive a short challenge. However, the same costs may be substantial if the challenge is repeated or the response remains active when it is no longer useful. Allostatic load describes cumulative burdens associated with adjustment, especially when mediators are repeatedly or inappropriately engaged. It is a conceptual framework, not a universal quantity measured by one hormone concentration. The detailed chronic consequences are reserved for 7.H.05.

A useful hypothetical experiment compares two animals facing identical predictable daily heat exposures. One receives a reliable cue before each exposure, while the other receives a cue unrelated to the exposure. If the first group begins a protective behavioural adjustment before temperature rises and shows a smaller internal temperature deviation, the result is consistent with useful anticipation. To isolate the effect of predictability, the groups must experience matched intensity, duration and number of heat episodes, and the cue itself should not directly alter the environment. The experiment should also measure energy or water expenditure. If anticipation reduces peak temperature but increases resource consumption, the benefit must be interpreted together with its cost.

A physiological response is therefore best understood as an adjustment in an integrated system. A rise in a mediator can be protective in one context, neutral in another and costly if persistent or inappropriate. An unchanged mediator does not prove unchanged physiological demand, because another pathway may compensate. Homeostasis and allostasis give complementary ways to organise these observations: one asks which variables are maintained and by what regulatory relationships; the other asks how the organism changes its physiological activities and priorities as conditions change.

4. Stressors vary in magnitude, duration, predictability and context

Stress physiology is often presented as though an exposure could be described simply as present or absent. A more useful description specifies what changed, how far it changed, how quickly, how long it lasted and what other conditions accompanied it. An animal may compensate effectively for gradual cooling but fail when the same final temperature is reached suddenly. A short reduction in oxygen availability may be manageable, whereas a prolonged reduction at the same oxygen concentration may exhaust resources required for compensation. The dose of a stressor therefore has several dimensions; intensity alone does not capture the physiological burden.

Magnitude refers to the size of a perturbation relative to a relevant reference condition. For osmotic challenges, investigators may specify the difference between the animal's customary medium and the experimental medium. For thermal challenges, they may report actual temperature and the difference from acclimation temperature. A fixed difference in temperature does not imply an identical physiological effect across species or even across individuals of the same species. The effect depends partly on where the exposure lies relative to each animal's performance range. Raising temperature by five degrees from a cool starting condition can enhance locomotor performance in an ectotherm, while raising temperature by five degrees near its upper tolerance limit can severely impair the same endpoint.

Duration is the time for which an exposure persists. A sustained perturbation can have consequences that a brief perturbation does not, even if maximum intensity is identical. Water shortage provides a simple illustration. During a short period with limited water intake, redistribution and reduced excretion may preserve circulation. As losses continue, the total body water pool decreases. Further compensation may become more costly, and limits become increasingly important. Measuring an effector such as urine concentration at the beginning and end of the exposure helps identify changing regulatory activity; measuring body water and circulatory function helps determine whether that activity succeeds.

A simple cumulative exposure index can be useful when intensity has an interpretable scale. If E(t) represents excess temperature above a specified reference, measured in degrees Celsius, an illustrative index is the area under E(t) over time, expressed in degree-minutes. An exposure 3 °C above reference for 20 minutes has an index of 60 degree-minutes; an exposure 6 °C above reference for 10 minutes also has an index of 60 degree-minutes. However, equal indices do not imply equal biological outcomes. A high peak may cross a critical limit that the lower exposure never reaches. Rate of heating, recovery between exposures and nonlinear damage can invalidate comparisons based solely on integrated intensity. The index summarises exposure, not a universally applicable measure of stress or tissue injury.

Frequency distinguishes an isolated event from a repeated sequence. Animals can experience repeated brief events without being exposed continuously. The spacing between events then matters because recovery is a physiological process that takes time. If a variable returns to its baseline between challenges, the next response begins under a different condition than when a residual disturbance remains. Recovery can also require replenishing energy stores or repairing damage that is not apparent from the regulated variable alone. Thus normalisation of a visible measurement need not mean that the animal has recovered completely.

Predictability describes how reliably environmental information forecasts an upcoming event. Predictability can differ from regularity: an event may occur at irregular intervals but have a reliable warning cue. Conversely, a regularly repeated exposure can be poorly predictable for an individual whose sensory access to the schedule is limited. A predictable change can be accommodated by anticipatory behaviour or coordinated changes in physiology; an unexpected change often demands rapid correction after the disturbance begins. These are tendencies, not absolute rules. Predictable events can be severe, and anticipation itself may impose costs. To test whether predictability matters, the experimental design must match total exposure while varying the information available to the animal.

Rate of onset is distinct from peak intensity and duration. In water containing decreasing oxygen, a gradual decline allows time for adjustments in ventilation, behaviour and circulation. An abrupt decline to the same final level may produce a different early trajectory because compensatory effectors have finite response times. Comparisons of tolerance thresholds are consequently sensitive to how an experiment approaches the endpoint. A threshold measured during a rapid laboratory challenge is an operational result, not necessarily the threshold for an exposure lasting hours or days in nature. A good answer should state the exposure protocol when comparing physiological thresholds.

Context includes the animal's nutritional state, hydration, activity, developmental stage, reproductive state and previous exposure history. A fasting animal and a recently fed animal may face the same locomotor demand with different immediate fuel availability. A recently dehydrated animal may face a temperature challenge with limited capacity to sustain evaporative cooling. If an experimental group is tested after fasting while another is tested after feeding, a difference attributed to temperature might instead involve nutrition or an interaction between nutrition and temperature. In a controlled experiment, such variables must be standardised or varied deliberately as independent factors.

Multiple stressors can act together. Suppose an aquatic animal experiences both warming and falling dissolved oxygen. Warming may raise metabolic oxygen demand over part of the physiological range, while reduced oxygen availability limits supply. The combined challenge is therefore different from either exposure alone. It is not sufficient to assume that the effects add arithmetically. One stressor can reduce capacity to respond to another, or a protective response to one may indirectly reduce the other. A factorial experiment with control, warming-only, oxygen-reduction-only and combined-exposure groups can examine whether the joint outcome differs from that expected from the separate effects.

To interpret such a factorial experiment, choose an endpoint and an appropriate reference scale. In an illustrative dataset, control swimming performance is 100 units, warming alone gives 90, oxygen reduction alone gives 80 and the combined treatment gives 60. The separate reductions are 10 and 20 units; a simple additive expectation on this scale would be 70. The combined value is 10 units lower than that expectation. This suggests a non-additive interaction on the stated scale, not proof of a particular molecular mechanism. Biological variation, replication and statistical uncertainty must be evaluated before concluding that the interaction is reliable. A different response scale, such as proportional survival, may require a different null model.

Stressors can be grouped by origin or mechanism. Physical challenges include changes in temperature, pressure and mechanical demands. Chemical challenges include changes in oxygen, solutes or potentially damaging compounds. Biological challenges include infection, predation risk and competition. Internal challenges include loss of body fluids, disruption of nutrient availability and changes in metabolic demand. These classifications overlap because an environmental event can generate several physiological consequences. They are useful as starting points for reasoning, but should not be mistaken for fixed mutually exclusive classes with a unique response pathway for each.

The central principle is that physiological stress is relational. A particular environmental value is not inherently stressful to every organism under all conditions. A species adapted to fluctuating salinity may maintain function across a broader salinity range than a species specialised for stable freshwater conditions. A change within the first species' operating range may challenge the second. Likewise, previous acclimation may change the response of the same individual without altering the external exposure. Examination questions that compare identical stressor doses should therefore ask which features of the animal, the response and the outcome have also been held constant.

5. Acute, repeated and chronic stress: interpreting time courses

A stress response unfolds in time. Acute describes a relatively short challenge or response in relation to the biological process under investigation. Chronic describes a challenge or associated physiological disturbance that persists for an extended period. Repeated stress refers to a sequence of separate challenges and may produce either intermittent recovery or a persistent change in baseline. There is no single duration in minutes or days that defines all these categories across organisms and endpoints. A response involving neural activity can develop quickly, whereas changes in tissue composition may develop over a much longer interval. What is acute for one process can be long enough to alter another.

The early response to an unexpected challenge often recruits mechanisms that can adjust organ activity rapidly. A mammal startled by a threatening signal may change posture, locomotion, heart rate and ventilation. The coordination of these changes depends on the nature of the stimulus and the animal's state. The rapid responses support immediate action or protection. Other responses develop later because changing gene expression, replenishing substrates or modifying tissues takes more time. An acute event can therefore initiate effects that outlast the exposure. Conversely, repeated activation of a fast response does not require the underlying mechanisms to become inherently slow.

A time-course graph should be read by first locating the onset and end of the stressor. A physiological measurement taken before exposure establishes a reference, although it may vary with circadian phase and prior conditions. Measurements during the exposure reveal onset latency, rate of change, peak or minimum value, and possible adaptation while the stressor is still present. Measurements after removal reveal recovery. If the signal is measured only at the beginning and end, an early peak can be missed. If only the peak is measured, prolonged recovery or incomplete restoration can be missed.

Different response components may have different peaks. A behavioural adjustment can begin before a circulating mediator reaches its maximum concentration. A change in blood chemistry can lag behind changes in ventilation or circulation. Repair of tissue damage can continue after circulating signals return to their usual ranges. These observations explain why two biomarkers sampled at the same time can give seemingly contradictory results. The apparent contradiction may arise simply because their trajectories differ. The correct response is to compare their biological functions and timescales, not to assume that one must be erroneous.

Consider two hypothetical groups whose deviation from a baseline value is recorded at the end of a challenge and every ten minutes afterwards. Group A shows deviations of 12, 6, 3 and 1.5 units; group B shows deviations of 8, 6, 4.5 and 3.4 units. A has a larger initial disturbance, but it returns toward baseline faster. B maintains a smaller deviation immediately after exposure but has a longer recovery time. If resistance is defined as the ability to limit initial displacement, B appears better on that endpoint. If resilience is defined as speed of restoration following the challenge, A appears better. The conclusion follows from specifying each metric, not from attaching a universal ranking to the two groups.

Habituation is a reduction in a response to repeated presentation of a stimulus, when the reduction is not adequately explained by sensory failure or effector exhaustion. In stress research, a repeated familiar challenge may evoke a smaller response over trials while the animal remains responsive to a novel challenge. A useful test is to introduce a different stimulus after the repeated sequence. Retained responsiveness can help distinguish stimulus-specific habituation from a generalized inability to respond. This is not definitive evidence on its own, since the new stimulus may be more intense or use different sensory pathways. Matching intensity and checking effector capacity improve the inference.

Sensitisation describes an increased response following previous exposure, often reflecting altered responsiveness to later stimulation. A sensitised animal may respond more strongly to the same or a related challenge. Repeated stress does not necessarily yield habituation or sensitisation in every physiological measurement. One response component may diminish while another remains unchanged or increases. A change in circulating hormone concentration, locomotion or feeding can reflect different parts of the integrated response. These processes are introduced here because they demonstrate that previous experience alters the meaning of a stressor; detailed chronic regulatory consequences belong to 7.H.05.

To determine whether an animal is exposed to a chronic physiological challenge, it is important to distinguish exposure from the measured response. A persistent environmental condition need not produce a continuously elevated response signal. An individual may adjust to the condition, alter its baseline state or become less responsive to the same stimulus. A return of one signal to its original value while the stressor persists can indicate compensation, habituation, altered feedback or failure of the measured response. Independent indicators of internal regulation, performance and recovery are needed to interpret the result. The simple claim that all chronic stress produces permanently elevated levels of a particular hormone is therefore unreliable.

An illustrative recovery model makes the role of repeated exposures more explicit. Suppose each challenge instantly adds a deviation of 6 units, and half of the remaining deviation is removed during the interval before the next challenge. Starting from zero, the first peak is 6 and the residual before the second is 3. The second peak is 9 and its residual is 4.5. The third peak is 10.5 and its residual is 5.25. The sequence approaches a peak of 12 units and a residual of 6 units if the model's assumptions continue to hold. In contrast, a longer recovery interval that allows ninety percent removal would leave much less residual disturbance. The model assumes additive disturbances and first-order recovery, and it ignores acclimation, changing sensitivity and damage. It is useful because it shows how repeated equal events can generate a raised baseline even though no individual exposure becomes stronger.

A real animal may adjust to repeated disturbances in ways that change these simple equations. It may increase the capacity of a protective mechanism, reducing the next deviation. It may conserve resources or change behaviour to avoid part of the next exposure. Alternatively it may lose capacity through incomplete repair or nutritional depletion. The measured curve is therefore a consequence of both the stressor sequence and the animal's changing state. Experimental comparisons that manipulate recovery intervals should also monitor the state of the animals before subsequent challenges, rather than assuming that all start from the same baseline.

The adaptive value of acute stress responses arises partly from their coordination with an immediate problem. A temporary increase in energy mobilisation or vigilance may help an animal respond to a threat. When the challenge has passed, continuing to allocate resources to that response may interfere with other functions. Timely termination and restoration are therefore part of successful regulation, not merely the absence of a response. The transition from useful compensation to damaging disturbance depends on intensity, persistence, recovery opportunities and physiological reserves. This is why classifying stress solely as beneficial or harmful without reference to context is too crude for physiological reasoning.

6. Selye's general adaptation syndrome and its evidence

The idea that diverse disturbances can evoke partly shared physiological reactions has a long history. In 1936 Hans Selye published a short report in Nature describing a syndrome observed in rats exposed to different damaging challenges. The importance of the observation was not that every challenge affected every organ identically. Rather, several different severe exposures were associated with a recognisable pattern of changes. Subsequent work developed a broader conceptual scheme commonly taught as the general adaptation syndrome, or GAS. The historical framework helped shift attention from the characteristics of a particular insult to the responses shared across different types of physiological challenge.

The original report described changes including effects on adrenal tissue, lymphoid organs and the digestive tract. These findings concerned experimental animals subjected to substantial physiological injury and should not be treated as a catalogue of symptoms required for every stressful event. The observations raised a scientific question: might some tissue changes reflect common organism-level responses rather than direct actions unique to each exposure? A sensible experimental strategy compares groups subjected to different challenges and examines whether certain outcomes are shared. If a change appears under several physically different conditions, this supports the possibility of a common response, but the shared outcome alone does not identify its mechanism.

An appropriate reconstruction of the reasoning separates the historical observations from a modern experimental design. A modern study would specify the exposure, sampling time, species, age, sex, nutritional conditions and measurement procedures. It would include a comparison group exposed to comparable handling without the challenge of interest. It would assess whether the shared outcome also occurs after different intensities or durations, and whether independent markers indicate successful compensation or tissue injury. Such controls are methodological recommendations, not a claim that every feature was implemented in the original short report.

The general adaptation syndrome is commonly represented in three stages: alarm, resistance and exhaustion. Alarm refers to the initial reaction when a challenge disrupts or threatens physiological stability. At this stage the animal may show rapid mobilisation of protective responses. Some historical presentations distinguish early shock from countershock. The details depend on the nature and severity of the challenge, and the terms should not be equated automatically with specific concentrations of particular hormones. The main teaching point is that exposure onset and the animal's early reaction can be physiologically distinct from its later adjustment.

Resistance describes a period during which compensatory responses allow the animal to withstand a continuing challenge more effectively than during the initial disruption. The word resistance has a specific meaning in the GAS stage model, and it is also used in modern physiology for the ability to minimise an effect of an exposure. The two usages are related but not identical. During sustained compensation, some functions may be preserved even while energy expenditure or other physiological costs are increased. The animal can appear stable on a measured variable while the mechanisms maintaining that stability operate at altered levels.

Exhaustion refers historically to a loss of effective compensation under severe or prolonged challenge. It should not be interpreted as a literal universal depletion of one fixed store of adaptation energy, nor as an obligatory third stage after every challenge. An animal may recover after a short challenge, acclimate to a persistent but moderate one, or fail rapidly if the initial disturbance exceeds its tolerance. Several distinct mechanisms can lead to loss of function, including resource shortage, damage, disruption of regulatory coordination or external conditions beyond compensatory capacity. The model describes a possible progression, not a precise law governing all stress responses.

The historical contribution is strongest when the model is used as a way to organise observations. It encourages students to examine the early phase, sustained compensation and eventual outcome separately. It also emphasises that response mechanisms can be protective in one context and associated with injury in another. However, classifying all forms of stress using a single sequence obscures differences between physical injury, behavioural threat, environmental challenges and normal physiological demands. Modern physiology examines stimulus-specific responses, feedback, anticipation, developmental influences and differences among species. These distinctions extend the historical concept rather than requiring rejection of the idea that some responses are shared.

A schematic GAS curve is often drawn with time on the horizontal axis and resistance on the vertical axis. Its shape should not be confused with a measured hormone concentration or a universal quantitative measure of health. The axis represents a conceptual capacity to withstand a continuing challenge, and the plotted sequence is illustrative. If the challenge ends during the resistance phase, the trajectory may move toward recovery rather than exhaustion. If the animal encounters a more intense second challenge during the period of compensation, performance may deteriorate sooner. The appropriate conclusion depends on exposure intensity and the variable represented by the graph.

Consider a teaching dataset in which an animal's performance under a sustained environmental challenge falls from 100 units before exposure to 65 shortly afterwards, recovers to 90 while the challenge persists, and then declines to 50 during prolonged exposure. This pattern is compatible with an initial disturbance, a period of improved compensation and a later loss of function. It does not establish that the animal passed through a universal endocrine sequence or that the decline resulted from a single depleted resource. To investigate the mechanism, measure potential limiting variables, compare groups released from the challenge at different times and examine whether rest or resource replenishment improves recovery. The data are illustrative and are not results from Selye's experiments.

A critical examination question might ask whether the appearance of similar adrenal or lymphoid changes under several damaging conditions proves that all stress responses are identical. The answer is no. Shared effects indicate common components, while differences in the causes, timing and other outcomes can remain substantial. A second question might ask whether an animal remaining functional during prolonged exposure necessarily has no physiological burden. Again, no: maintaining performance may require sustained compensation. Distinguishing function from the cost of preserving function is one of the main reasons to integrate the GAS concept with modern homeostatic and allostatic reasoning.

7. Resistance, tolerance and resilience as measurable traits

Resistance, tolerance and resilience are useful terms only when the biological endpoint is stated. Resistance can describe the ability to prevent or minimise a perturbation in an internal variable despite an external challenge. Tolerance can describe the ability to maintain function or survive despite exposure, internal disturbance or damage. Resilience can describe the capacity to restore function after disruption, sometimes characterised by recovery rate or extent. Other disciplines define these terms more specifically, such as pathogen resistance through reduction of pathogen burden and disease tolerance through maintenance of health at a given burden. Here the terms are used in their comparative physiological sense, with explicit endpoints rather than assumptions that they are interchangeable.

Imagine an animal exposed to a dry environment. Its ability to prevent loss of body water is one aspect of resistance. Its ability to continue physiological function at a given reduction in body water is one aspect of tolerance. Its ability to restore fluid balance and performance after water becomes available is one aspect of resilience. An animal may excel in one dimension but not another. A species with effective barriers against water loss may seldom need to tolerate large internal disturbances. Another species may experience greater water loss but preserve performance across a broader range of hydration states. Comparing only final survival cannot distinguish the strategies.

The distinction between exposure and internal burden is crucial. If two animals experience the same external salinity but one maintains extracellular ion concentration nearer its usual range, it resists the internal disturbance more effectively. If both experience the same internal ion disturbance and one continues to perform better, that difference suggests greater tolerance of the internal disturbance. If the experiment instead compares performance only at the same external salinity, both mechanisms could contribute. The investigator needs measurements of internal state to separate protection against the disturbance from maintenance of function despite it.

A tolerance curve usually represents a biological outcome as a function of a specified environmental or internal variable. Performance can refer to swimming speed, feeding rate, growth, reproductive output or another appropriately chosen measure. A thermal performance curve in an ectotherm often has a region where performance is comparatively high and declines at sufficiently low or high temperatures. The optimum for a given measurement is the temperature where that measurement is greatest under the experimental conditions. It is not necessarily the temperature most favourable to every biological process. The temperature that maximises rapid swimming may differ from that maximising long-term growth or reproduction.

Critical limits depend on how the endpoint is defined. An experiment might define an upper critical thermal limit by loss of coordinated locomotion under a specified warming rate. Another might measure survival after a fixed-duration exposure. These values answer different questions. Failure to swim at a particular temperature does not establish that every cell has been irreversibly injured. Likewise, survival does not imply unchanged performance. The distinction matters especially when using laboratory results to predict ecological success: an animal may survive a temperature at which it can no longer obtain enough food or escape predators.

Performance breadth refers to the span of conditions over which performance exceeds a chosen criterion. For example, one may compare the range over which swimming performance remains above half of the measured maximum. The criterion must be stated because breadth measured at fifty percent differs from breadth measured at eighty percent. A population with a lower maximum but wider breadth may perform comparatively well across variable environments while a narrowly specialised population performs best in a stable environment. The curves do not imply an inevitable trade-off; that relationship must be tested rather than assumed.

Resistance and resilience can also be quantified from time-series measurements. Let a variable have baseline X0 and peak value Xp during an upward disturbance. An absolute peak deviation is Xp − X0, expressed in the units of the variable. When the baseline is positive and the ratio is meaningful, a relative deviation may be calculated as (Xp − X0)/X0 × 100 percent. If two animals share a baseline of 100 and show peaks of 110 and 125 during matched challenges, the respective deviations are 10 and 25 percent. The first animal resists this deviation more effectively. It is important that the variable actually represents the internal outcome of interest rather than a protective effector signal.

For resilience, an experiment can report the time needed to reach a prespecified recovery criterion, such as returning within five percent of baseline. Alternatively, a recovery constant may be estimated from an appropriate model. A shorter time to criterion indicates quicker recovery under comparable conditions. If the initial deviations differ, time to criterion reflects both starting displacement and recovery dynamics. Estimating a proportional recovery rate can help distinguish these effects, but only if the model is appropriate. If one group reaches a different stable state, time to the original baseline may be an unsuitable metric.

A numerical example illustrates the difference. Two animals each have a baseline functional score of 100. Immediately after a disturbance their scores are 60 and 80. After 20 minutes their scores are 90 and 85, respectively. The first animal recovers 30 of the 40 points lost, or 75 percent of its initial deficit; the second recovers 5 of 20 points, or 25 percent. Despite a larger initial disruption, the first has recovered further in absolute score and fractional deficit. A second experiment with matched initial impairment could further test differences in recovery mechanisms. Such comparisons avoid treating the highest single measurement as a complete summary of physiological capacity.

The choice of endpoint can change conclusions even within the same experiment. An animal that rapidly recovers locomotor function may still have altered energy reserves. Another may preserve internal ion balance while reducing feeding and growth. Measuring both performance and internal regulation reveals different components of adaptation. Where possible, a meaningful design includes more than one endpoint and reports a trajectory instead of a single measurement. The goal is not to demand an unlimited number of measurements; it is to choose measurements that distinguish plausible mechanisms and relevant biological consequences.

Resistance, tolerance and resilience may have different ecological value depending on the pattern of stress. When challenges occur frequently but are brief, quick recovery between events can be important. When environmental conditions persist beyond a particular threshold, the ability to tolerate internal disturbance or maintain compensatory fluxes can matter more. When an exposure can be avoided through behaviour, the measured field outcome may reflect avoidance rather than intrinsic tolerance. Laboratory and field observations complement one another because laboratory experiments can control exposure while field observations reveal how animals actually encounter and modify their environments.

8. Phenotypic plasticity, acclimation and acclimatization

An animal's phenotype is the observable expression of its inherited biological organisation in a particular developmental and environmental context. The same inherited background can produce different physiological traits when conditions differ. Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity for such environmentally dependent variation. It is a property of the relationship between genotype and phenotype, not a synonym for genetic mutation. The ability to alter a trait in response to conditions may itself have a heritable component. It is therefore possible for natural selection to influence the degree or pattern of plasticity, even though the immediate physiological adjustment occurs within the lifetime of an individual.

Plasticity can be rapid, gradual or developmentally established. A reversible change in performance after several days at a different temperature is a form of physiological plasticity. A change in an adult animal's ventilatory response after prolonged residence under reduced oxygen availability is another. Some environmentally influenced traits develop during sensitive periods and persist long after the initiating condition disappears. These persistent developmental effects should not be assumed to be readily reversible merely because they arose through plasticity. Reversibility is particularly characteristic of many adult acclimatory responses, but not of all environmentally influenced phenotypes.

Acclimation conventionally refers to adjustments produced by controlled exposure to an environmental condition, often in a laboratory. Acclimatization conventionally refers to adjustments associated with conditions in the natural environment, where several variables may change simultaneously. Some textbooks emphasise a single controlled variable in acclimation and multiple natural variables in acclimatization; usage varies, and the operational exposure history is more informative than a rigid verbal rule. Neither term requires a change in allele frequency. Both can involve alterations in gene expression, cellular composition, organ function and behaviour.

A researcher wishing to study temperature acclimation in fish might maintain randomly assigned animals at two temperatures for a defined period, while matching other conditions such as food supply, photoperiod and dissolved oxygen as closely as possible. After this conditioning period, both groups could be tested at the same series of temperatures. If performance differs when the test temperature is identical, the prior environmental history has affected the measured trait. Measuring each group only at its own conditioning temperature would be less informative, because the immediate effect of test temperature would be confounded with the effect of prior acclimation.

The distinction between conditioning and test environment is essential. Temperature can immediately alter biochemical reaction rates in an ectotherm without requiring any lasting physiological adjustment. If a fish swims faster at a warmer test temperature than at a cooler test temperature, the difference may reflect an acute thermal effect. If two groups with different prior temperature histories swim differently at the same test temperature, the difference is consistent with acclimation. To identify acclimation, the investigators must separate the environmental history from the immediate measurement conditions. This logic applies to salinity, oxygen availability and other environmental variables as well.

Consider a hypothetical study in which animals from a single randomly assigned population are kept at 15 °C or 25 °C for two weeks. Their locomotor speeds are then measured at 20 °C. If the cold-conditioned group performs at 12 cm/s and the warm-conditioned group at 9 cm/s, the difference cannot be explained simply by the instantaneous effect of different test temperatures, because both were tested at 20 °C. However, the result does not show that cold conditioning improves performance at all temperatures. To evaluate the overall response, measure both groups across a suitable range of test temperatures. The optimum, maximum, and performance breadth may differ independently.

A second hypothetical study measures each group at 15, 20 and 25 °C. The cold-conditioned group swims at 8, 12 and 11 cm/s; the warm-conditioned group at 4, 9 and 14 cm/s. The groups have different performance patterns: the cold-conditioned group performs better at lower and intermediate temperatures while the warm-conditioned group performs better at the highest measured temperature. These data are compatible with an acclimatory shift in the relationship between performance and temperature. They do not establish precise optima because the temperature grid is sparse and the true maxima may fall between or beyond tested points. The result also does not demonstrate that performance changes are beneficial for survival in nature without additional endpoints.

Several mechanisms can contribute to acclimatory changes. An animal may alter the amount or activity of enzymes that support metabolism, change transport capacity at membranes, adjust the properties of tissues involved in exchange, or modify the balance between metabolic demand and environmental supply. In an ectotherm living in cooler conditions, changes in metabolic machinery may partially compensate for the direct slowing of temperature-sensitive processes. Such compensation is not universal or complete. Some physiological rates may change little after acclimation, while others shift in either direction. The general principle is that the relationship between an environmental variable and biological performance can be modified by previous exposure.

Acclimatization in nature is often more difficult to attribute to a single cause. A bird moving from summer to winter conditions encounters altered temperature, day length, food availability and activity patterns. Any measured seasonal change could involve one or several of these factors. Field observations identify ecologically meaningful changes, but laboratory experiments can help identify which cues or conditions produce them. A combination of field and laboratory approaches is often stronger than either alone. In a field study, repeated measures of individuals can help distinguish within-individual adjustment from seasonal differences in the composition of the sampled population.

Reversal experiments provide another way to examine physiological plasticity. After conditioning an animal to a new environment, return it to the original environment and measure the trait over time. If the trait moves toward its former value, the observation supports a reversible component. A failure to return immediately does not prove a genetic change. The original state may require longer recovery, some developmental effects may be persistent, or the exposure may have caused damage. The investigator must consider the biology and duration of the experiment before interpreting an apparently permanent effect.

The distinction between plasticity and injury is particularly important. Suppose prolonged heat exposure reduces an animal's maximum locomotor performance. A change in phenotype has occurred, but it is not automatically an adaptive acclimation. The decline may reflect damage or energy depletion. In another experiment, prior moderate exposure improves subsequent performance under a matched challenge. This result is more consistent with a protective acclimatory change, especially if control animals were handled identically and the endpoint measures useful function. Plasticity describes the capacity for environmental responsiveness; whether the response is beneficial requires independent evidence.

A useful comparison across timescales distinguishes immediate adjustment, acclimation over an exposure period, developmental plasticity and evolutionary change across generations. These categories need not be separated by universal time thresholds. Instead, they are separated by the causal question. Immediate responses involve the current challenge. Acclimatory effects involve prior environmental history within an individual. Developmental effects involve the influence of conditions during development. Evolutionary effects involve inherited differences and changes in populations. A rapidly altered phenotype is not necessarily a short-lived one, and a slow change is not necessarily evolutionary. The key is whether the relevant inherited composition of the population has changed.

9. Evolutionary adaptation and the evidence needed to establish it

Evolutionary adaptation is a process through which heritable variation associated with differential survival or reproduction becomes more common across generations in a particular environment. The term adaptation can also refer to a trait shaped by natural selection that contributes to fitness in that context. Physiologists sometimes use adaptation more broadly for beneficial adjustment within an individual. For examination reasoning, specify which meaning is intended. An individual can acclimate to a changed environment, but its acquired adjustment is not by itself evidence that its population has evolved.

Natural selection operates when individuals differ in heritable characteristics and those differences affect reproductive success. Environmental stress can impose selection because some variants maintain function or reproduce more successfully than others under the challenge. However, an exposure does not generate the particular inherited variant that an individual needs simply because that variant would be useful. Genetic variation arises through processes such as mutation and recombination, and selection changes the representation of inherited variants through differential reproductive success. A beneficial plastic response may improve survival and thereby affect the conditions under which selection operates, but this is distinct from the direct inheritance of every acquired physiological adjustment.

Consider two hypothetical populations of an animal occupying environments with contrasting salinity. Animals collected from the more saline environment maintain better performance when exposed to salt in a laboratory. This observation is consistent with several explanations. The populations may differ genetically in salt tolerance. The sampled individuals may have acclimatized to their local conditions. Their developmental histories, diet or age distributions may differ. The initial comparison does not distinguish these explanations because population origin and exposure history are confounded.

A common-garden experiment reduces this confounding by raising individuals from different populations under the same controlled conditions. Ideally, researchers compare offspring raised from early development in a shared environment, with adequate replication across families and populations. If differences persist under common conditions, inherited differences become a more plausible explanation. Nevertheless, maternal effects, environmentally influenced inherited states and other transgenerational influences can also persist. Additional generations under common conditions or appropriately designed breeding studies can strengthen an inference of a genetic basis. Persistence alone also does not prove that the trait was shaped by natural selection rather than another evolutionary process.

A common-garden design can be extended by raising individuals from each population in more than one environment. This reveals the reaction norm, the relationship between environmental conditions and the phenotype for a specified genotype or genetic group. A perfectly horizontal reaction norm indicates no measured plasticity of that trait across the tested range, not the absence of plasticity in every trait or environment. A sloped reaction norm indicates environmentally dependent variation. Different intercepts can represent differences in the phenotype at the reference environment, while different slopes indicate differences in the pattern of environmental responsiveness.

Suppose individuals from populations A and B are raised under low and high salinity in a common-garden experiment. Their hypothetical growth scores are A: 80 and 60, B: 60 and 85. Both populations show a change with salinity, so both exhibit an environmentally dependent phenotype in this example. The opposite directions of the changes indicate that the relationship between population origin and growth depends on the environment. The lines cross when plotted as reaction norms, which is a clear qualitative example of an origin-by-environment interaction. If the difference is shown to have a genetic basis, it may be described as genotype-by-environment interaction. The numerical scores do not constitute proof of local adaptation unless they are related to fitness and the environments represent relevant native conditions.

Reciprocal transplant experiments examine performance or fitness of individuals from contrasting origins in both their home and alternative environments. The four comparisons are essential: origin A in environment A, origin A in environment B, origin B in environment A and origin B in environment B. The design distinguishes an origin effect, an environment effect and their interaction. To provide evidence for local adaptation, a useful criterion is whether residents have higher fitness than foreign individuals in each relevant home environment, taking replication and uncertainty into account. Comparing one population only between its home and foreign environment can be misleading because one environment may simply be better for all populations.

In a hypothetical reciprocal test, animals from a low-salinity habitat have reproductive scores of 90 in low salinity and 50 in high salinity; animals from a high-salinity habitat have scores of 65 in low salinity and 85 in high salinity. The low-salinity residents outperform foreign animals in low salinity, while high-salinity residents outperform foreign animals in high salinity. This pattern supports local adaptation when alternative explanations have been controlled and the scores validly represent reproductive success. In contrast, if population A scores 90 and 80 and population B scores 60 and 50, A performs better in both environments. The latter pattern suggests an overall difference in performance, not the reciprocal home advantage of the first example.

Selection for a trait cannot be inferred simply from its apparent usefulness. A thick covering, a particular osmotic tolerance or a metabolic response may appear beneficial, but several traits could contribute to survival and reproduction simultaneously. A study measuring only one trait cannot always establish that the trait itself causes a fitness advantage. Stronger evidence can come from comparing trait variation with performance and reproductive outcomes across relevant environments, while accounting for correlated traits and population history. At NET/SET level, the essential reasoning is to distinguish evidence of heritable differences, evidence of environment-dependent performance and evidence of differential fitness.

Evolutionary adaptation is context dependent. A trait improving function under one stressor can carry a cost under another condition. For example, maintaining physiological capacity for exceptional environmental demands may consume resources that could otherwise support growth or reproduction. Whether such a cost occurs is an empirical question; it should not be assumed solely from an intuitive trade-off. The fitness consequences of physiological traits depend on the frequency, severity and predictability of environmental challenges as well as the other demands of the animal's life cycle.

Plasticity and evolved adaptation are therefore not opposites. A population can evolve a capacity for acclimation, and individuals can use that capacity when conditions change. The immediate response belongs to the individual's physiology; variation in the response across inherited backgrounds provides material for evolutionary change. A population living in a variable environment may benefit from plastic responses if useful cues are available and adjustments can occur quickly enough. If a response is costly or environmental cues are unreliable, greater plasticity need not be favourable. Stable or unpredictable environments can favour different response patterns depending on the specific biological constraints.

A final distinction concerns the timescale of evidence. Rapid change in a population mean can occur because individuals acclimate, because the composition of the sampled individuals changes, or because inherited variants become differentially represented. Selection can operate over relatively few generations in some organisms, so it is inaccurate to say that evolutionary adaptation always requires thousands of years. Conversely, a physiological change that takes months within an individual is not evolutionary merely because it develops slowly. The correct classification follows from the process and the evidence, rather than an arbitrary elapsed time.

10. Comparative animal examples and biological trade-offs

Comparative physiology asks how different animals solve related physiological problems under different environmental conditions. The goal is not to memorise lists of unusual species, but to identify the functional challenge, the strategy used to meet it and the limitations of that strategy. Vertebrates and invertebrates share basic needs for suitable cellular conditions, energy supply and removal of metabolic wastes. Their anatomical structures and physiological pathways differ, and those differences influence the options available to them. A stress framework should therefore not assume that mechanisms characteristic of mammals are universal in animals.

Temperature provides a useful starting example. In ectotherms, environmental heat exchange strongly influences body temperature, although behaviour can regulate exposure and some species generate or retain metabolic heat in particular contexts. In endotherms, metabolic heat production is a major source of body heat, and physiological and behavioural mechanisms can maintain body temperature over a range of environmental conditions. A rise in ambient temperature can therefore create different immediate challenges. An ectotherm may experience changing tissue reaction rates as its body warms; an endotherm may need to increase heat dissipation to prevent a similar rise in core temperature. The distinction does not imply that ectotherms never regulate temperature or that endotherms maintain an absolutely fixed temperature.

A useful invertebrate example is rapid cold hardening. Experiments reported by Lee, Chen and Denlinger in 1987 established that brief prior cooling can enhance subsequent resistance to chilling injury in insects. Later studies investigated this phenomenon in Drosophila. The significance is that physiological tolerance can change over a relatively short interval in an individual's life, not solely through long seasonal preparation or genetic evolution. A controlled comparison tests animals with and without a mild conditioning exposure using a common subsequent cold challenge. Improved performance or survival in the conditioned group supports a protective plastic response, subject to appropriate controls. The result should not be generalised to every insect, every developmental stage or all types of cold injury.

Cold injury also illustrates the difference between a physical limit and a functional endpoint. Some insects are injured by chilling at temperatures above those at which their body fluids freeze. Measuring the temperature at which ice first forms therefore does not necessarily reveal the temperature at which useful function fails. An animal can survive without freezing but still experience physiological disruption. A cold-tolerance study must identify its endpoint, such as survival after recovery, restoration of coordinated movement or another relevant measure. This is a general principle: a convenient physical marker may not represent the biological outcome of interest.

In aquatic environments, salinity influences water and ion movements across exchange surfaces. Many marine invertebrates have extracellular fluid osmolality that tracks environmental osmolality to varying degrees, while some animals maintain larger differences between internal and external concentrations. Osmoconformity describes a relationship between total internal and external osmotic concentration; it does not imply that every ion is unregulated or that intracellular composition is identical to seawater. Osmoregulation describes active maintenance of internal osmotic conditions despite environmental differences. Animals may combine elements of conformity and regulation depending on species, tissue and environmental range.

The advantage of these differing strategies depends on the habitat. In a relatively stable environment, conforming aspects of internal fluid composition can reduce the need to defend a large osmotic gradient. In fluctuating conditions, regulating internal composition may preserve cellular function across environmental changes but can require transport work and associated energy. A species capable of tolerating fluctuations in internal conditions may use a different balance of these strategies. The relevant question is which variables are protected and at what cost. Detailed ion transport mechanisms were introduced in earlier osmoregulatory lessons and are not repeated here.

Freshwater and marine fish provide another comparison. A freshwater fish generally experiences osmotic water entry and ion loss relative to its surroundings, whereas many marine teleosts tend to lose water and gain salts relative to seawater. Their physiological mechanisms therefore face different balances of transport and water exchange. A euryhaline fish can tolerate a broader range of environmental salinities than a stenohaline fish, but broad tolerance need not mean that the animal performs equally well everywhere or changes instantly between extremes. Time for acclimatory adjustment may be important. Measuring survival, ion balance and energetic expenditure after transfer distinguishes several aspects of physiological capacity.

Reduced oxygen availability is another widespread challenge. Animals can respond by increasing oxygen uptake or delivery, changing behaviour, reducing energy demand or tolerating a period of reduced aerobic performance. Different strategies can produce a superficially similar survival outcome. A fish that moves into oxygen-rich water avoids the exposure. An animal that increases oxygen delivery to tissues compensates for it. An animal that reduces metabolic demand may maintain viable cellular energy balance at lower supply. These strategies are not mutually exclusive. Detailed oxygen-sensing mechanisms and specialised diving or hypoxia adaptations are developed in 7.H.04.

The distinction between resistance and tolerance can be applied to oxygen limitation. If one animal maintains tissue oxygen availability better than another under identical environmental oxygen, it may resist the internal disturbance more effectively. If both experience a similar decrease in tissue oxygen but one maintains essential function longer, the latter may tolerate that internal condition better. Measurement of environmental oxygen alone cannot separate these possibilities. Measurements of oxygen delivery, metabolic demand and function are needed. This example illustrates how the same conceptual framework applies across thermal, osmotic and respiratory challenges without requiring identical underlying mechanisms.

Energy allocation links these examples. Animals must allocate resources among maintenance, activity, growth, reproduction and storage. During a challenge, additional maintenance expenditure or reduced feeding may alter the resources available for other functions. A response can improve immediate survival while reducing growth or reproductive output during the same period. This trade-off is not automatically maladaptive; its significance depends on the animal's life history and the likelihood of survival without the response. A short challenge may justify a large temporary allocation to protection, whereas persistent demands may require changes in behaviour or physiology that reduce ongoing costs.

A simple illustrative energy budget demonstrates this principle. Suppose an animal assimilates 100 energy units per day and uses 55 for maintenance and 20 for activity, leaving 25 for growth, storage or reproduction. During an exposure, maintenance expenditure increases to 70 units while assimilation and activity remain unchanged. The residual falls to 10 units. The response may maintain internal function but reduce the energy available for other processes by 15 units. If the animal also reduces feeding so assimilation falls to 85, the budget becomes negative by 5 units unless activity or another expenditure is reduced or stored energy is mobilised. The numbers are hypothetical; actual energy budgets require measurement of the relevant inputs and outputs.

Physiological strategies must be judged against environmental context. A behavioural strategy that avoids a brief stressor may cost little if shelter is nearby, but it may be ineffective if the entire habitat changes. A costly physiological response may be useful during an unavoidable event but unnecessary when avoidance is possible. Animals differ in mobility, body size, exchange surfaces, life histories and access to resources, so the same physical environment can impose different functional challenges. Comparative reasoning begins with these differences and then asks which measurements would discriminate the strategies. It does not treat every animal as a modified mammal or every stress response as evidence of the same endocrine mechanism.

A particularly instructive distinction is between the capacity for an adjustment and the cue that recruits it. Consider a population of insects in a habitat that experiences cool nights and warm days. A mild decline in temperature during the evening may precede a more severe chill later at night. If a prior mild cooling period enhances protection against the later challenge, a response to the earlier cue can be useful even before the severe exposure occurs. This is a case in which environmental predictability and individual plasticity can operate together. A laboratory test should include an unconditioned group and a group receiving only the conditioning exposure; the subsequent challenge must be identical for the relevant comparisons. The unconditioned challenge group estimates impairment without preparation, while the conditioned challenge group estimates impairment after preparation. A conditioning-only group reveals whether the prior exposure itself changes the baseline endpoint. The biological result need not be expressed as a new tolerance limit: a shorter recovery interval or improved survival at one matched challenge can also demonstrate useful protection. Conversely, if a cue becomes unreliable because severe chilling no longer follows mild cooling, the benefit of preparatory adjustment may be reduced. This reasoning connects environmental timing, response costs and the ability to modify physiology within an individual's lifetime, without assuming that the population has undergone genetic change.

A comparable logic applies to animals adjusting to a predictable reduction in available resources. Preparatory changes in activity or energy allocation can help preserve later function, but the advantage depends on the accuracy of the cue and the capacity to reverse the adjustment if conditions improve. An animal that commits resources to protection too early may reduce its current opportunity to feed or reproduce. An animal that responds too late may encounter a disturbance before compensatory pathways reach effective capacity. These are testable physiological trade-offs: vary the time between the cue and the challenge, measure the actual onset of the response, and assess the functional endpoint and resource expenditure. The shortest or strongest response is not automatically the most effective strategy. The useful response is one that improves the specified outcome under the conditions and costs that the animal encounters.

11. Experimental design: separating response, protection and injury

A strong experiment in stress physiology begins with a precise question. Asking whether an animal is stressed is often too general to guide measurement. Asking whether prior exposure reduces the decline in a specified physiological function during a standardised challenge is more useful. The latter question identifies the prior exposure as an independent variable, the subsequent functional change as a dependent variable and the standard challenge as a condition that must be controlled. A further experiment can then ask how the protective effect arises. Good experimental reasoning advances from a defensible observation to progressively narrower mechanistic explanations rather than treating a broad correlation as a complete cause.

Consider a study in which animals are held under two prior conditioning environments before exposure to a standard challenge. A comparison group must receive comparable handling and remain under the reference conditioning environment. An additional group tested without the challenge is often useful, because conditioning itself might change baseline performance. If the conditioned animals score higher after the challenge but also scored higher before it, a simple comparison of post-challenge scores may exaggerate the difference in protection. Measurements of baseline and change from baseline allow more informative interpretation. An appropriate analysis may also account for differences in starting values rather than relying exclusively on percentage changes.

Random assignment reduces systematic differences between treatment groups. If animals are assigned to conditioning environments based on their initial performance, group differences after treatment may partly reflect pre-existing variation. Randomisation cannot guarantee perfectly matched groups in a small sample, but it prevents the deliberate or systematic association of initial traits with treatment. Replication helps estimate variation and distinguish a reproducible response from chance differences. In a tank-based experiment, the tank rather than each animal may be the independent experimental unit if the exposure is applied to the entire tank. Ten fish in one tank do not provide ten independently assigned temperature treatments. Replicate tanks are needed to distinguish treatment effects from conditions peculiar to a particular tank.

The importance of measuring variation can be illustrated with two hypothetical studies. In the first, the mean performance values of control and conditioned groups are 70 and 75, with nearly all individuals clustered near their group mean. In the second, the means are also 70 and 75, but individuals vary widely and the sample is small. The mean difference is identical, yet the uncertainty differs substantially. Without measures of spread and independent sample size, it is impossible to evaluate the reliability of the estimated difference. Standard deviation describes variation among observations, while the standard error describes uncertainty in an estimated mean under appropriate assumptions. A larger sample can reduce uncertainty about a mean without necessarily reducing the biological variation of individual animals.

The timing of measurements can introduce an additional confound. Suppose a researcher measures the circulating concentration of a stress-associated mediator in all control animals in the morning and all experimental animals in the evening. A difference might reflect the treatment, natural daily variation or both. Matching sampling times, randomising measurement order and considering handling effects improve the design. Collection procedures can themselves constitute a challenge, so a purported baseline measurement may actually include part of an acute response. The appropriate procedure depends on the species and measurement. The general lesson is that the act of measurement must be considered as a possible physiological perturbation.

An increased level of a physiological mediator may correlate with reduced performance under a challenge. It is tempting to conclude that the mediator causes the reduced performance. However, at least three explanations are possible. The mediator might impair the measured function. The impaired physiological state might stimulate the mediator. Or both might result from the intensity of the exposure. A fourth possibility is that the mediator limits a larger deterioration that would otherwise occur. In that case its positive association with impairment would not imply that reducing the mediator improves function. The animals with the greatest challenge might have both the strongest protective response and the poorest remaining performance.

Mechanistic intervention can help distinguish such alternatives. Imagine two groups exposed to a standard disturbance; a selective experimental manipulation reduces activity of one candidate protective pathway in one group, while the other receives an appropriate control manipulation. If both groups experience the same exposure but loss of pathway activity produces greater internal deviation, the result supports a contribution of that pathway to compensation. It remains important to check whether the manipulation changes baseline physiology or has additional effects. An intervention that alters multiple pathways cannot unambiguously identify one mechanism. A complementary restoration experiment, where feasible, can strengthen the causal inference. These principles are conceptual and do not require knowledge of specialist laboratory protocols.

Protection should also be separated from avoidance. If conditioned animals experience an environmental challenge in a chamber where they can move away from the most intense region, improved survival might result from changed behaviour rather than greater tissue tolerance. That behavioural protection is biologically meaningful, but it addresses a different question from whether tissues can function under the same internal disturbance. Researchers can measure the conditions actually encountered by the animals or compare performance under matched exposure. A design that permits natural avoidance answers an ecologically relevant question, whereas a tightly controlled design can isolate particular physiological responses. The choice depends on the purpose of the experiment.

Recovery measurements are particularly valuable for separating reversible adjustment from damage. A reduction in locomotor performance during exposure might be a protective decrease in activity that conserves resources, or it might result from injury. If animals promptly regain performance when normal conditions return, reversible adjustment is a plausible explanation. If performance remains depressed, damage, depleted resources or persistent physiological reorganisation may be involved. Persistence is evidence that a short-lived acute response does not fully explain the outcome; it does not identify the cause without further observations.

A simple experimental sequence integrates these considerations. First, establish baseline internal state and function under standard conditions. Second, record the exposure quantitatively. Third, follow a small set of appropriate response variables during the disturbance. Fourth, measure functional outcomes and, where relevant, indices of damage. Fifth, follow recovery after the challenge ends. Finally, compare results across properly replicated control and treatment groups. The sequence need not be followed mechanically in every study, but it provides a framework for determining whether physiological adjustment actually preserves function and whether the effect persists after removal of the challenge.

Historical evidence also requires this discipline. Selye's observation of common tissue changes after severe challenges supported a shared component of response. Modern studies can distinguish common from stimulus-specific mechanisms by comparing exposures under defined conditions and measuring multiple response systems. Similarly, cold-hardening experiments support a protective effect of prior temperature history when conditioned and unconditioned animals encounter a matched subsequent challenge. They do not imply that every environmental exposure is beneficial. Different experiments answer different questions, and an appropriate interpretation should not go beyond the variables and outcomes actually examined.

A useful final principle is to distinguish the experimentally measured physiological variable from the biological construct it represents. A hormone concentration is a measured chemical quantity; stress is a broader physiological concept. Swimming speed is a measured performance endpoint; fitness includes reproductive contributions over relevant timescales. A return toward baseline is a measured trajectory; resilience may encompass broader recovery of function. Measurements are indispensable, but their interpretation depends on the question and the assumptions linking them to the construct. This is particularly important in Part C questions, where the correct answer may be the one that states what the experiment supports without making an unjustified generalisation.

12. Integrative worked examples and prediction from evidence

The following worked examples use hypothetical teaching data rather than findings from published experiments. Their purpose is to integrate the concepts of exposure, regulation, response capacity, tolerance and recovery. A good approach is to identify the biological question before performing a calculation, then check whether the result supports the proposed physiological interpretation. Numbers make a comparison precise, but a numerical difference does not automatically identify the mechanism responsible for it.

In the first example, two groups of aquatic animals are exposed to a standard increase in external salinity. Their baseline haemolymph osmolality is 300 mOsm/kg. At the end of exposure, group A measures 330 mOsm/kg and group B measures 390 mOsm/kg. The deviations are 30 and 90 mOsm/kg respectively, equivalent to ten and thirty percent of baseline. Group A therefore maintains this internal variable closer to its starting condition. If both groups have similar baseline locomotor performance but group A retains 80 percent and group B retains 95 percent of performance during the challenge, the results do not justify saying A is superior in every respect. A better resists the measured osmotic disturbance, whereas B preserves the chosen performance endpoint despite a larger deviation.

The result suggests several follow-up questions. Group B may tolerate internal osmotic disturbance more effectively, or the observed performance may depend on another variable that differs between groups. To examine tolerance more directly, compare performance at matched internal osmolalities, preferably across multiple levels of disturbance. The investigators should also assess recovery and whether retained short-term performance is associated with subsequent costs. If group B appears functional during exposure but exhibits slower restoration or lower later growth, the time interval used for assessment changes the interpretation. This example shows why comparing a single internal measurement and a single performance measurement is a useful beginning, but not necessarily sufficient for a comprehensive causal conclusion.

In a second example, an environmental challenge lasts for thirty minutes. Two groups have identical baseline values of an internal variable, and the deviation is recorded at 0, 10, 20 and 30 minutes after the challenge ends. Group A has deviations of 20, 10, 5 and 2.5 units. Group B has deviations of 10, 8, 6.4 and 5.12 units. Both trajectories are compatible with exponential recovery under the simplified model discussed earlier. Group A retains one-half of its preceding deviation during each ten-minute interval; group B retains four-fifths. Their approximate rate constants are respectively ln(2)/10 = 0.0693 min⁻¹ and −ln(0.8)/10 = 0.0223 min⁻¹. A therefore has a faster proportional recovery under this model, despite experiencing greater initial displacement.

Suppose the experimental criterion for functional recovery is a deviation no greater than 5 units. Group A reaches the criterion at twenty minutes. Group B remains slightly above it at thirty minutes and would reach it at approximately 31 minutes if the model continues to apply. The estimate follows from 5 = 10e^(−0.0223t), yielding t = ln(2)/0.0223. The interpretation depends on the assumption that the original baseline remains appropriate and that there is no renewed disturbance. If later measurements reveal a persistent plateau above baseline, the exponential model should be revised rather than used to force a numerical conclusion.

A third example examines conditioning and acute temperature effects separately. Animals from a single population are randomly assigned to two prior temperatures, and individuals from each group are then tested under both a lower and a higher temperature using a design that avoids confounding test order with treatment. In hypothetical data, cold-conditioned animals produce performance scores of 70 and 90 under the lower and higher test temperatures. Warm-conditioned animals produce scores of 50 and 110. Both groups perform better at the higher test temperature, indicating a direct association with the test condition. At the lower temperature the cold-conditioned group performs better; at the higher temperature the warm-conditioned group performs better. The magnitude and direction of the conditioning effect depend on the test temperature.

The crossing performance pattern matters because a conclusion based on only one test condition can be misleading. If investigators test only at the lower temperature, they might conclude that cold conditioning universally improves performance. If they test only at the higher temperature, they might reach the opposite conclusion. A factorial design exposes the conditional nature of the response. The difference between warm- and cold-conditioned groups is −20 units at the lower test temperature and +20 units at the higher one, a difference of differences of 40 units. This is an illustrative interaction contrast, not a statistical significance test. Replicated observations and an appropriate analysis are needed to estimate uncertainty.

A fourth example concerns evolutionary interpretation. Two populations are sampled from habitats with different thermal conditions, and their offspring are reared in common laboratory conditions before testing. Under the cooler test condition, descendants of the cool-habitat population have mean reproductive output of 40 surviving offspring, compared with 25 for the other population. Under the warmer test condition, the corresponding means are 20 and 45. This pattern is consistent with a reciprocal local fitness advantage if the test conditions accurately represent the native habitats. Common rearing makes a simple explanation based on immediate adult field exposure less plausible. However, if only one generation has been reared in common conditions, maternal effects may remain. The data do not identify a particular gene or demonstrate that any specified cellular trait caused the fitness difference.

A fifth example considers a response signal and a functional outcome. Three groups receive a mild, intermediate or severe challenge. A measured response mediator increases from 2 to 5 to 9 arbitrary units across the groups, while performance declines from 95 to 75 to 35 percent of baseline. The association between mediator concentration and reduced performance is strong in this hypothetical dataset, but the exposure itself changes across groups. The mediator could be a marker of challenge intensity, a protective response, a contributor to damage or part of a more complex causal network. To distinguish these explanations, a useful experiment alters the mediator or its pathway while holding exposure intensity constant and independently evaluating baseline state and function. Correlation across different intensities is not enough to assign causal direction.

The final example integrates resource allocation. An animal living under reference conditions assimilates 150 units of energy per day, spends 80 on maintenance and 30 on activity, and has 40 units available for storage, growth and reproduction. During repeated challenges, maintenance rises to 100 and activity remains 30, while feeding is disrupted so assimilation falls to 120. The budget is now negative by 10 units per day before any allocation to storage, growth or reproduction. Over four days, assuming unchanged rates, 40 units must come from stored energy or be compensated by a change in intake or expenditure. A stable internal variable during this period would not prove that the challenge has no cost. The animal may have preserved short-term function by drawing upon resources required for later activity or reproduction.

These examples can be combined into a general reasoning framework. Identify whether the observed difference concerns a stressor, an internal state, an effector response, a functional outcome or an accumulated cost. Compare equivalent exposure conditions before inferring differences in physiological capacity. Use a suitable baseline and time course to distinguish initial resistance from subsequent resilience. Separate environmental history from immediate test conditions when evaluating acclimation. Separate inherited differences from acquired experience when interpreting population comparisons. Finally, ask whether a proposed mechanism has been directly tested or remains a plausible explanation. The next chapter examines the neural and endocrine organisation of stress responses; the conceptual distinctions established here provide the basis for interpreting those mechanisms without equating a single signalling pathway with every form of stress.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. A stressor and a stress response are the same.

Correct reasoning: An exposure is the challenge; a response is the resulting adjustment.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: A single term is often used for both in ordinary language. On a question, identify the manipulated input separately from the measured effector.

2. Any stress response is pathological.

Correct reasoning: Acute adjustments can protect function.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Students focus on chronic injury. Test the duration and functional outcome before judging a response.

3. Homeostasis means an absolutely fixed internal value.

Correct reasoning: Regulation maintains context-appropriate ranges and may allow transient deviations.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Textbook set-point diagrams can suggest an unchanging line. Interpret rhythms and feedback delays rather than equating variation with failure.

4. Negative feedback always decreases the effector.

Correct reasoning: Its net effect opposes the initiating deviation.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: The word negative is confused with inhibition. An increase in heat loss can oppose increased temperature.

5. Feedforward is positive feedback.

Correct reasoning: Feedforward is predictive control; positive feedback amplifies an initiating change.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Both may involve activation. Identify whether the signal anticipates a disturbance or reinforces its consequence.

6. Allostasis replaces homeostasis.

Correct reasoning: Allostatic adjustment can support homeostatic stability.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Terminological contrast can be mistaken for mutually exclusive processes. Compare what is regulated with which supporting processes change.

7. All stressors produce the same response.

Correct reasoning: Common components can coexist with stimulus-specific patterns.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: GAS is often memorised as a universal sequence. Use the experimental stimulus and measured endpoint to identify differences.

8. Exhaustion inevitably follows every stressor.

Correct reasoning: Recovery or sustained compensation may occur; severe exposure can exceed capacity early.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: A three-stage schematic is mistaken for a compulsory biological law. Check exposure duration and whether the challenge is removed.

9. Equal cumulative exposure means equal injury.

Correct reasoning: Peak, duration, threshold and recovery can matter independently.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Students assume linear dose–response. Equal areas under an exposure curve do not guarantee equal functional outcomes.

10. Resistance and resilience are interchangeable.

Correct reasoning: Resistance limits displacement; resilience concerns restoration after disturbance.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: A single final value hides the initial response. Compare peak deviation and recovery kinetics separately.

11. Equal external exposure proves equal internal burden.

Correct reasoning: Different animals can regulate internal conditions differently.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: External dose is easy to measure. Test tolerance at matched internal burden if that is the causal question.

12. Every acclimatory change is beneficial and reversible immediately.

Correct reasoning: Plastic changes may be beneficial, neutral, costly or persistent; reversal takes time.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: The word adaptation suggests improvement. Examine function, exposure history and recovery rather than assuming benefit.

13. Acclimation is a genetic adaptation.

Correct reasoning: Individual plastic adjustment is distinct from inherited population change.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Both words describe adjustment. Look for evidence of heritable variation and differential fitness across generations.

14. A common-garden difference proves natural selection.

Correct reasoning: It supports persistent origin differences, but maternal effects and nonadaptive divergence remain possible.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Controlling environment is confused with proving selection. Seek inherited and fitness evidence.

15. An optimum is the same as a critical limit.

Correct reasoning: An optimum maximises a selected function; a limit is an endpoint-defined threshold.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Both come from performance curves. Identify axis, criterion and exposure protocol.

16. Osmoconformers regulate nothing.

Correct reasoning: Total osmolality can track the environment while individual ions remain regulated.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Total solute concentration is confused with specific ion composition. Distinguish overall osmotic relationship from transport mechanisms.

17. A hormone level measures stress directly and universally.

Correct reasoning: A biomarker reports one part of a context-dependent response.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Biomarkers are convenient but affected by timing and species. Compare exposure, regulated state and functional outcome.

18. More animals in one tank solve every replication problem.

Correct reasoning: If treatment is assigned to tanks, independent replicate tanks are required.

Why it is tempting and how to solve the question: Individuals are mistaken for independent treatment units. Identify the level at which the manipulation is applied.

Original examination practice

All questions below are original teaching questions, not previous-year examination questions. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

CSIR-NET Part B: concepts

1. Which is the stressor in an experiment that transfers fish to water with lower dissolved oxygen?

  1. Reduced dissolved oxygen
  2. Increased ventilation
  3. Reduced swimming performance
  4. Post-exposure recovery
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Reduced dissolved oxygen

Reduced environmental oxygen is the imposed challenge. Ventilation is a compensatory response; swimming performance and recovery are outcomes.

2. Which quantity is a regulated variable rather than an effector in a water-conservation response?

  1. Urine water excretion rate
  2. Drinking rate
  3. Collecting-duct water permeability
  4. Extracellular osmolality
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Extracellular osmolality

Osmolality is the internal condition being constrained. The other quantities describe processes that alter water balance.

3. What makes a physiological feedback loop negative?

  1. Every intermediate signal decreases
  2. It always inhibits the effector
  3. Its net effect opposes the initiating deviation
  4. It produces tissue injury
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Its net effect opposes the initiating deviation

Negative describes the sign of the completed causal loop. An increase in effector activity may oppose a deviation, as when sweating increases during heat exposure.

4. Which statement correctly describes steady state in an open biological compartment?

  1. All fluxes cease
  2. Input and output rates balance, although both may be nonzero
  3. The compartment has no energy cost
  4. Concentration can never change
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Input and output rates balance, although both may be nonzero

A zero net accumulation rate can coexist with active exchange. Steady state is not necessarily thermodynamic equilibrium or permanent invariance.

5. Which best illustrates feedforward regulation?

  1. A sensor detects an existing deviation and corrects it
  2. A reliable cue initiates an adjustment before the predicted disturbance
  3. An effector amplifies the original deviation
  4. A damaged organ ceases to respond
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. A reliable cue initiates an adjustment before the predicted disturbance

A predictive cue can recruit preparation before the regulated variable changes. Error correction is feedback; amplification is positive feedback.

6. Allostasis is most appropriately associated with which idea?

  1. Coordinated changes in physiological activity to support stability under changing demands
  2. Absence of any physiological variation
  3. A universal fixed hormone concentration
  4. Replacement of homeostasis by disease
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Coordinated changes in physiological activity to support stability under changing demands

Allostasis emphasises active and sometimes anticipatory adjustment. It is complementary to homeostasis, not synonymous with pathology.

7. Which statement about stress is scientifically defensible?

  1. Every stressor causes permanent injury
  2. All stress responses are emotional
  3. An elevated effector is always pathological
  4. A transient stress response can protect physiological function
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. A transient stress response can protect physiological function

Short-lived responses can preserve function. Their meaning depends on the challenge, magnitude, duration and effectiveness of compensation.

8. Which sequence represents the conventional general adaptation syndrome?

  1. Resistance, exhaustion, alarm
  2. Exhaustion, alarm, adaptation
  3. Alarm, resistance, exhaustion
  4. Acclimation, mutation, resistance
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Alarm, resistance, exhaustion

The conventional historical scheme describes initial alarm, sustained compensation and possible loss of capacity. It is not an obligatory trajectory for every exposure.

9. Which finding motivated Selye's original stress framework?

  1. Every animal had an identical stress hormone profile
  2. All mild stressors enhanced reproductive success
  3. Several severe, diverse challenges in rats produced partly similar physiological changes
  4. Stress was exclusively psychological
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Several severe, diverse challenges in rats produced partly similar physiological changes

The original work described common patterns of tissue changes after damaging exposures. It did not establish universality across all stressors or species.

10. Which is an appropriate interpretation of the exhaustion stage of GAS?

  1. Every acute stressor inevitably leads to death
  2. Effective compensation may fail during severe or sustained challenge
  3. A single universal adaptation-energy molecule is depleted
  4. All stress mediators must disappear
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Effective compensation may fail during severe or sustained challenge

Exhaustion is a conceptual stage of failure of compensation, not evidence of a universal fixed energy store or inevitable outcome.

11. Which comparison best tests resistance to an internal disturbance?

  1. Internal deviation at identical external exposure
  2. Maximum hormone level across unequal stressor doses
  3. Recovery time alone after different exposures
  4. The name of the stressor
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Internal deviation at identical external exposure

Resistance is operationally assessed here by the extent of internal displacement under matched challenge. Recovery time instead informs resilience.

12. Which comparison most directly tests tolerance of an internal disturbance?

  1. Internal deviation at matched external challenge only
  2. Time required to detect a cue
  3. Number of distinct stress hormones
  4. Performance at matched levels of internal disturbance
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Performance at matched levels of internal disturbance

Tolerance concerns retained function despite a specified burden. Equal external challenge alone does not separate resistance from tolerance.

13. What is resilience in the operational sense used in this chapter?

  1. Complete absence of initial disturbance
  2. Presence of an inherited mutation
  3. The maximum environmental temperature
  4. Capacity to restore a specified function after disturbance
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Capacity to restore a specified function after disturbance

Resilience concerns recovery, which is distinct from limiting initial disruption or tolerating a continuing disturbance.

14. Which is the most appropriate interpretation of a thermal performance optimum?

  1. The best temperature for every life process
  2. The maximum survivable temperature
  3. The temperature maximising the selected endpoint under stated test conditions
  4. A value independent of acclimation history
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The temperature maximising the selected endpoint under stated test conditions

Different traits can have different optima. Experimental conditions and history affect the measured performance relationship.

15. Which distinguishes controlled acclimation from evolutionary adaptation?

  1. Acclimation requires changing population allele frequencies
  2. Acclimation is an individual adjustment to prior environmental conditions
  3. Evolutionary adaptation is always reversible in the individual
  4. Acclimation is necessarily harmful
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Acclimation is an individual adjustment to prior environmental conditions

Acclimation concerns environmentally induced adjustment within an individual. Evolutionary adaptation involves heritable variation and selection across generations.

16. Phenotypic plasticity refers to which property?

  1. Environmental dependence of phenotype within an inherited background
  2. An obligatory change in DNA sequence following exposure
  3. Absence of genetic influence on phenotype
  4. A trait that cannot be environmentally modified
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Environmental dependence of phenotype within an inherited background

Plasticity describes environment-dependent expression of a phenotype; the capacity for that plasticity can itself be influenced by genotype.

17. Which terminology is conventionally appropriate for naturally occurring seasonal physiological adjustment?

  1. Acclimatization
  2. Artificial selection
  3. Genetic drift
  4. Speciation
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Acclimatization

Natural seasonal adjustment is generally termed acclimatization. Acclimation commonly denotes a controlled exposure, although terminology varies.

18. What is the most useful feature of a common-garden experiment?

  1. Every animal is tested only in its native habitat
  2. All inherited differences are eliminated
  3. All animals necessarily have identical phenotypes
  4. Different origins experience shared rearing conditions
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Different origins experience shared rearing conditions

Shared rearing reduces environmental-history confounding. It does not remove genetic variation or guarantee that all maternal effects disappear.

19. What is a reaction norm?

  1. A universal stress hormone threshold
  2. The time required for species formation
  3. The phenotype expressed by a given genotype or genetic group across environments
  4. A single physiological measurement at one temperature
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The phenotype expressed by a given genotype or genetic group across environments

A reaction norm describes the phenotype–environment relationship, allowing plasticity and differences in environmental responsiveness to be examined.

20. Which statement about osmoconforming animals is accurate?

  1. All intracellular solutes are identical to seawater
  2. Tracking external total osmotic concentration does not imply that every ion is unregulated
  3. They cannot possess active ion transport
  4. They are necessarily insensitive to salinity
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Tracking external total osmotic concentration does not imply that every ion is unregulated

Total osmolality and individual ion composition are different properties. An osmoconformer can still regulate particular ions and cellular conditions.

21. Which observation is characteristic of rapid cold hardening?

  1. An insect must evolve a new species before gaining cold tolerance
  2. Brief prior mild cooling can increase resistance to subsequent chilling injury
  3. All cold injury requires tissue freezing
  4. Cold protection is impossible without winter diapause
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Brief prior mild cooling can increase resistance to subsequent chilling injury

Rapid cold hardening demonstrates individual physiological plasticity. Chilling injury can occur without freezing, and the response varies by species and stage.

22. What is the appropriate meaning of allostatic load?

  1. Cumulative physiological burden associated with repeated or dysregulated adjustments
  2. A universally measurable single hormone concentration
  3. The total amount of water in an organism
  4. An inevitable outcome of every brief exercise bout
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Cumulative physiological burden associated with repeated or dysregulated adjustments

Allostatic load concerns the costs of repeated or inappropriate adjustment. It is not equivalent to one biomarker or every normal transient response.

CSIR-NET Part C: analytical reasoning

23. A hypothetical compartment receives 14 mmol/h and loses 11 mmol/h for two hours. If these rates remain constant, what is the net change in amount?

  1. Increase of 28 mmol
  2. Increase of 6 mmol
  3. Decrease of 6 mmol
  4. No change
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Increase of 6 mmol

Net rate is 14 − 11 = 3 mmol/h. Multiplication by two hours gives +6 mmol. Neither individual flux equals net accumulation.

24. A compartment contains 240 mmol in 0.8 L. It loses 0.2 L of pure water with no solute loss. What is the resulting concentration?

  1. 400 mmol/L
  2. 300 mmol/L
  3. 320 mmol/L
  4. 200 mmol/L
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. 400 mmol/L

Remaining volume is 0.6 L; concentration = 240/0.6 = 400 mmol/L. The solute amount is unchanged but the denominator falls.

25. A variable deviates by 16 units and then halves every five minutes after the disturbance ends. What is the predicted deviation after 15 minutes?

  1. 4 units
  2. 8 units
  3. 0 units
  4. 2 units
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 2 units

Three five-minute intervals give 16 × (1/2)^3 = 2. Exponential recovery approaches zero rather than necessarily reaching it in finite time.

26. Group A has initial deviation 15 and ten-minute deviation 5; group B has initial deviation 8 and ten-minute deviation 6. Which interpretation is best?

  1. A is superior in both traits
  2. B has faster fractional recovery
  3. B limits initial deviation better; A shows faster fractional recovery
  4. Neither group shows recovery
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. B limits initial deviation better; A shows faster fractional recovery

A removes 10/15 = 66.7% of its deviation; B removes 2/8 = 25%. B starts nearer baseline, so resistance and recovery rank differently.

27. In a linear model dx/dt = u − kx, u = 4 units/min and k = 0.5/min. What steady deviation is predicted under constant input?

  1. 2 units
  2. 4.5 units
  3. 8 units
  4. 0.125 units
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 8 units

At steady state dx/dt = 0, so x = u/k = 4/0.5 = 8 units. The rate constant has inverse-time units; a residual deviation is expected.

28. A mild heat exposure is 2 °C above reference for 30 min; a second is 6 °C above reference for 10 min. What can be concluded from their equal 60 degree-minute indices?

  1. The same number of cells must be injured
  2. Their integrated excess temperatures match, but biological outcomes may differ
  3. The peak exposure is identical
  4. Both exposures necessarily induce acclimation
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Their integrated excess temperatures match, but biological outcomes may differ

The index integrates magnitude over time but ignores thresholds, nonlinear responses, onset and recovery. It is not a universal damage measure.

29. In a factorial experiment, control performance is 100; warming gives 90; hypoxia gives 75; both give 55. What is the interaction contrast relative to simple additive losses?

  1. Combined performance is 10 units below additive expectation
  2. Combined performance is exactly additive
  3. Combined performance is 20 units above expectation
  4. The data identify the oxygen-sensing pathway
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Combined performance is 10 units below additive expectation

Expected additive value = 100 − 10 − 25 = 65; observed 55 is 10 lower. Replication is required to establish reliability, and mechanism is not identified.

30. A stressor adds 10 deviation units per episode. Between episodes, 60% of the current deviation is removed. What is the second peak if the first starts from baseline?

  1. 20 units
  2. 16 units
  3. 4 units
  4. 14 units
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 14 units

The first peak is 10; 40% remains, giving residual 4. The next episode adds 10 to 4, producing 14.

31. An animal's effector signal becomes smaller across five identical stimuli, but a matched novel stimulus produces a strong response. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. All response systems have permanently failed
  2. The animal has acquired a new allele
  3. Chronic disease is proven
  4. Stimulus-specific habituation is plausible; generalized exhaustion is less likely
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Stimulus-specific habituation is plausible; generalized exhaustion is less likely

Retained response to a novel challenge is consistent with stimulus-specific response reduction. It is not absolute proof because pathways and stimulus properties may differ.

32. Control and treated groups have the same exposure, but the treated animals show a larger stress mediator concentration and better maintained performance. What inference is justified?

  1. The mediator must be harmful
  2. The treatment necessarily eliminated the exposure
  3. The mediator might contribute to protection, but causal intervention is needed
  4. The mediator is the regulated variable
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The mediator might contribute to protection, but causal intervention is needed

A protective mediator may increase with successful compensation. Group differences in mediator and performance alone do not establish its causal role.

33. Group A maintains osmolality within 5% of baseline while B deviates by 20%; their performance is equal under matched external exposure. Which next test best separates tolerance from resistance?

  1. Measure ambient salinity again only
  2. Compare performance across matched internal osmolality levels
  3. Compare hormone concentrations at unequal times
  4. Assume equal survival proves identical mechanisms
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Compare performance across matched internal osmolality levels

A already shows greater resistance of osmolality. Matching internal burden isolates the performance–burden relationship needed to evaluate tolerance.

34. Two groups start with a score of 100 and fall to 50 and 80. After recovery their scores are 85 and 90. Which group recovers a larger fraction of its initial loss?

  1. First group: 70% versus 50%
  2. Second group: 90% versus 85%
  3. Both recover exactly 35%
  4. First group: 35% versus 10%
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. First group: 70% versus 50%

A recovers (85−50)/(100−50) = 0.70. B recovers (90−80)/(100−80) = 0.50. Final absolute score is a different metric.

35. Cold-conditioned fish are tested at 15 °C and warm-conditioned fish at 25 °C; the warm group swims faster. Which design change is most informative?

  1. Test both conditioning groups across identical test temperatures
  2. Increase the warm group's sample size only
  3. Remove the cold group
  4. Measure body mass only after warming
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Test both conditioning groups across identical test temperatures

Conditioning history and immediate test temperature are confounded. A crossed design can distinguish acute test effects from prior conditioning.

36. In a crossed conditioning study, cold-conditioned animals score 75 and 95 at low and high test temperatures; warm-conditioned animals score 55 and 125. What is the warm-minus-cold interaction contrast?

  1. 20 units
  2. 10 units
  3. 0 units
  4. 50 units
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. 50 units

Warm-minus-cold differences are −20 at low and +30 at high; their difference is 50 units. The sign change shows the conditioning effect depends on test environment.

37. Two populations differ in cold tolerance when collected directly from nature. Offspring raised together show no difference. Which conclusion is warranted?

  1. Evolutionary adaptation is conclusively demonstrated
  2. All genetic variation has disappeared
  3. The original difference is compatible with environmental history; genetic difference is not established
  4. Cold tolerance cannot be plastic
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. The original difference is compatible with environmental history; genetic difference is not established

Loss of the difference under common rearing suggests environmental or developmental contributions. A null result does not prove absolute absence of genetic differences.

38. In a reciprocal habitat experiment, A produces 30 offspring in A and 20 in B; B produces 25 in A and 40 in B. Which interpretation is supported?

  1. A is universally fitter
  2. Residents outperform foreign individuals in both habitats
  3. B is universally fitter
  4. Only habitat quality differs, with no origin interaction
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Residents outperform foreign individuals in both habitats

A exceeds B in habitat A (30 > 25) and B exceeds A in habitat B (40 > 20). This pattern supports local advantage with suitable controls and replication.

39. In another reciprocal experiment A scores 80 and 100 in environments A and B, while B scores 40 and 60. What pattern is present?

  1. Reciprocal home advantage
  2. A has higher scores in both; B environment improves both by 20
  3. Crossing reaction norms
  4. No environment effect
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. A has higher scores in both; B environment improves both by 20

Both origins improve by 20 in B and A exceeds B by 40 in both. These parallel patterns do not show reciprocal local superiority.

40. A thermal performance study samples only two temperatures and reports that group A performs best at the higher one. Why is the true optimum uncertain?

  1. The maximum may lie between or outside the sampled temperatures
  2. An optimum always equals the lowest tested temperature
  3. Performance cannot depend on temperature
  4. Two measurements establish a full curve
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. The maximum may lie between or outside the sampled temperatures

Sparse sampling gives information at tested points only. Identifying an optimum requires sufficient sampling across an appropriate range.

41. Animals with prior mild cooling survive a later standard chill exposure better than unconditioned controls. What would most strengthen the interpretation as acclimatory protection?

  1. Test preconditioned animals at a milder later temperature
  2. Compare different species without controls
  3. Measure only the number of treatments
  4. Matched handling, common later exposure, and pre/post measures with independent replication
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Matched handling, common later exposure, and pre/post measures with independent replication

Equivalent later exposure and handling isolate the prior conditioning effect. Baseline and recovery measures help distinguish protection from pre-existing differences.

42. An experiment uses one heated tank containing 20 fish and one control tank containing 20 fish. What is the main inferential limitation?

  1. There are 20 independently assigned tank treatments per group
  2. Fish cannot be used for physiology experiments
  3. Temperature treatment is confounded with tank identity; independent replicate tanks are needed
  4. The design proves all tank effects are absent
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Temperature treatment is confounded with tank identity; independent replicate tanks are needed

The manipulation is applied at tank level. Fish within one tank share conditions; treating all as independent treatment replicates inflates apparent evidence.

43. During a four-day challenge, daily assimilation is 90 units and maintenance plus activity costs 102 units. If rates are unchanged, how much stored energy or equivalent compensation is required?

  1. 12 units
  2. 408 units
  3. 48 units
  4. 360 units
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. 48 units

Daily deficit is 12 units; over four days it totals 48. Other allocations are not supported by this budget unless intake rises or expenditure falls.

44. A stress mediator is sampled in controls at dawn and experimental animals at dusk. The treatment group has higher values. What is the best corrective design?

  1. Infer the treatment effect immediately
  2. Sample both groups at matched circadian phases with balanced handling
  3. Measure only treated animals at dusk
  4. Ignore sampling time if means differ
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Sample both groups at matched circadian phases with balanced handling

Daily rhythms and handling can affect physiological signals. Matching phase and procedures reduces confounding before a treatment effect can be interpreted.

45. An animal exposed to low oxygen reduces metabolic demand and preserves essential function despite reduced tissue oxygen availability. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

  1. Reduced demand can improve tolerance without restoring the original oxygen supply
  2. Oxygen delivery must have increased
  3. The exposure has disappeared
  4. The animal has necessarily evolved
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Reduced demand can improve tolerance without restoring the original oxygen supply

Demand reduction changes the balance between supply and use. Retention of function despite internal disturbance is compatible with tolerance.

46. A 30-minute challenge is removed, but an effector signal remains high while the internal variable returns near baseline. What can be concluded?

  1. The animal has fully recovered every tissue and energy store
  2. The original stressor is necessarily still present
  3. Negative feedback is impossible
  4. The signal and internal variable have different dynamics; persistent signal alone does not prove injury
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. The signal and internal variable have different dynamics; persistent signal alone does not prove injury

The internal variable may normalise before effector activity or other recovery processes. Additional functional and cost measurements are needed.

  4. Assertion false, reason true

Correct answer: C. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion

Flux adjustment can prevent accumulation or loss, allowing internal stability despite changing organ activity.

48. Assertion: Acclimation is evidence that a population's allele frequencies have changed. Reason: Acclimation may occur within a single individual after environmental exposure.

  2. Assertion false, reason true
  3. Both are false
  4. Assertion true, reason false
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Assertion false, reason true

An environmentally induced change in an individual does not establish genetic evolution in the population.

49. Assertion: A chronic stressor can be present even if a sampled hormone is not elevated. Reason: Response magnitude depends on sampling time, feedback and previous exposure.

  1. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both true, but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Both false

Correct answer: A. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion

A single hormone reading is not a direct measure of exposure. Adaptation, dynamics and feedback can alter observed levels.

50. Assertion: A fish that moves away from warm water demonstrates intrinsic tissue heat tolerance. Reason: Avoidance reduces actual exposure.

  1. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both false
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Assertion false, reason true

Behavioural avoidance is protective but does not show that tissue can function at the same high internal temperature.

51. A controlled study finds an animal retains 80% of its performance during stress and recovers to 95% afterwards. Which report is most precise?

  1. It had no stress
  2. It was fully genetically adapted
  3. It demonstrated permanent injury
  4. It retained 80% during exposure and recovered to 95% of baseline at the stated time
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. It retained 80% during exposure and recovered to 95% of baseline at the stated time

Describe the measured endpoint and time; the remaining 5% deficit does not establish permanence, and neither value establishes inherited adaptation.

52. An animal maintains a regulated variable despite losing stored energy. Which biological interpretation is best?

  1. Homeostasis has failed because energy use increased
  2. Stable variables prove no challenge occurred
  3. Successful regulation may incur a resource cost
  4. Energy expenditure must be zero at steady state
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Successful regulation may incur a resource cost

The cost of maintaining stability may become evident in resource reserves or later performance even when the immediate variable is preserved.

Revision and mastery

Physiological stress involves a challenge to suitable internal function. A response is the set of adjustments, not the challenge or injury itself. Homeostasis is supported by feedback and coordinated effector fluxes; allostasis emphasises dynamic, predictive and context-dependent adjustment. The historical general adaptation syndrome describes alarm, sustained resistance and possible exhaustion, but does not prescribe a universal biological trajectory.

Resistance, tolerance and resilience are distinct endpoints. Compare internal displacement under matched external challenge, performance at matched internal burden, and restoration after exposure, respectively. Prior conditioning can alter a performance curve through plasticity; inherited adaptation requires evidence of heritable variation and differential fitness. Common-garden and reciprocal transplant designs answer different causal questions. Compare vertebrate and invertebrate examples by functional problem rather than assuming universal mammalian stress pathways.

Quantitative review

Net accumulation = input − output. Concentration = amount / volume. For a first-order recovery model, x(t) = x(0)e−kt, with recovery half-time ln(2)/k. Under constant forcing in the linear model, xsteady = u/k. Relative deviation = change / baseline × 100%, if the baseline permits ratio interpretation. Fractional recovery of a functional deficit = amount regained / initial deficit. Daily residual energy = assimilation − measured expenditures. State units, assumptions and endpoints in every calculation.
