1. Why expression must be controlled

A cell's genome provides the instructions for building RNA molecules and proteins, but the possession of an instruction does not imply that the corresponding molecule must be manufactured continuously. A bacterium that encounters a different source of carbon may need a new transporter and a different set of enzymes without acquiring new genetic information. A liver cell and a neuron can have broadly similar inherited genomes yet produce very different sets of proteins. These differences illustrate the distinction between genetic information and its expression. Regulation determines when, where and to what extent a functional gene product is made. The term applies to functional RNAs as well as to proteins; a gene encoding a ribosomal RNA does not require a translation step to generate its major functional product.

The same protein-coding gene can generate different amounts of product in two cells through several distinct mechanisms. One cell may produce more messenger RNA per minute. Another may produce RNA at the same rate but remove it more slowly. A third may use each available RNA more efficiently for protein synthesis. A fourth may retain the protein for longer after synthesis. Measuring protein abundance at one time cannot distinguish these explanations by itself. The central question throughout this chapter is therefore not simply whether expression rises or falls, but which molecular step is responsible.

The central dogma describes the usual transfer of sequence information, not a fixed rate of synthesis. A protein that binds DNA and alters transcription is not transferring its amino acid sequence back into DNA. The regulator changes the probability or rate of an information-using process. It can act on a gene without altering the sequence of the gene's product. This distinction explains why cells can respond reversibly to environmental signals on a timescale much shorter than that needed for genetic evolution. A reversible expression response also differs from selection, in which cells carrying different inherited properties may become differently represented in a population.

The statement that different cells share the same genome is a useful generalisation rather than an absolute rule. Somatic mutation, chromosome changes and immunoglobulin gene rearrangement provide exceptions; mature mammalian erythrocytes lack nuclei. Differential use of genetic information nevertheless explains a major part of cell specialisation. An inherited gene can remain available even when its expression is low in a given cell. The following chapters will examine how regulatory factors, chromatin and stable expression programmes help maintain such differences.

A further distinction is between the amount of a protein and its biological activity. If a protein is an enzyme, the reaction rate depends not only on how much enzyme is present but also on substrate, inhibitors, cofactors and catalytic state. Adding a ligand may change activity immediately without changing transcription or translation. Increasing enzyme abundance may not increase overall metabolic flux when a different reaction is limiting. A gene expression experiment therefore needs a clearly specified readout. The measured phenotype may reflect several intervening steps between the gene and its eventual biological effect.

2. Rates, abundance and functional output

The amount of new RNA produced per unit time is a rate. The amount of RNA present at a particular moment is an abundance. A gene can produce RNA continuously even while the abundance remains constant, because synthesis replaces molecules lost through turnover. In an analogous manner, the number of proteins made in one minute is not the same as the accumulated amount of protein in the cell. This distinction between a flux and a stock is essential to quantitative reasoning about gene expression.

Suppose a culture contains an average of sixty messenger RNA molecules per cell. In condition A, twelve new molecules are produced each minute and twelve are removed. In condition B, twenty-four are produced and twenty-four are removed. Both conditions have the same RNA abundance, but their rates of synthesis and removal are different. A measurement of total RNA cannot reveal this difference. A measurement of newly synthesised RNA during a controlled interval or an estimate of RNA lifetime would add a different kind of information.

The quantity of mature RNA may also differ from the quantity of precursor RNA. In a eukaryotic system, a transcript can be processed, exported or removed before it becomes available for efficient translation. If a perturbation interferes with RNA processing, total transcriptional initiation may continue at its original rate while the amount of mature messenger RNA decreases. In another case, two mature splice forms can change in relative abundance without a large change in the total transcript pool. A measurement that detects a region shared by both forms may conceal this change. The molecular identity of the readout matters as much as its numerical value.

Imagine that a treated cell contains half as much of a particular protein as an untreated cell, while the measured RNA amount is unchanged. A reduction in translation is plausible, but it is not the only possibility. Protein degradation may have increased; the protein may have moved into a compartment not represented in the measurement; or the measured RNA may include species unavailable for translation. An experiment that measures newly produced protein, protein lifetime and the appropriate RNA pool can distinguish these alternatives. The correct conclusion is no stronger than the available evidence.

Normalisation adds another layer of interpretation. A tissue sample can contain a different number or mixture of cells after a treatment. Total RNA from a tissue may rise even if no individual cell has increased its transcription rate. A reference transcript used for comparison may itself change under the treatment. Expression per cell, per unit total RNA and relative to a reference gene are not universally interchangeable. A student interpreting an expression graph should first identify the denominator and the population represented before assigning a regulatory mechanism.

The language of genetic perturbation similarly requires precision. A gene knockout may eliminate a functional product while some transcription from the locus persists. A knockdown lowers expression without necessarily removing the gene. Overexpression refers to output above a specified reference level, but does not identify the mechanism that produced the increase. A mutation can change the activity of a protein while leaving the amount of protein intact. These distinctions prevent misleading conclusions when a phenotype is described in general terms without the underlying molecular measurements.

3. Constitutive and regulated genes

The expression of a housekeeping gene is often relatively stable across the conditions in which its product is required. Such condition-independent behaviour is described as constitutive expression in that context. Other genes are expressed at different levels according to nutrient supply, stress, developmental state or other signals. Constitutive does not necessarily mean completely unregulated. A promoter that appears constant during one experiment may respond to a condition that has not yet been tested. Similarly, a housekeeping transcript is not guaranteed to be invariant across cell types, growth rates or treatments.

Making RNA and protein consumes cellular resources. A cell must invest nucleotides, amino acids and energy, as well as the use of its transcriptional and translational machinery. When an alternative nutrient is absent, continuous high production of a transporter and enzymes specific for that nutrient may have little immediate benefit. Induction upon nutrient availability can therefore conserve resources while allowing adaptation. However, completely eliminating basal expression may delay a response if a small amount of transporter is required to detect or import the nutrient. Regulation balances economy with readiness.

A biosynthetic pathway presents a complementary problem. When the end product is scarce, increased synthesis of the enzymes that make it may be advantageous. When the product is abundant, reducing the production of these enzymes can prevent unnecessary consumption of precursors and energy. Such a relationship can create negative feedback at the level of gene expression. Negative feedback is a system property, describing how a change produces an opposing effect. Negative transcriptional control is a different classification, describing the effect of a particular active regulator on target transcription. The two terms must not be confused.

Regulation of an existing enzyme and regulation of enzyme production can cooperate. An end product may bind and inhibit an existing enzyme rapidly, decreasing metabolic activity without changing protein abundance. The same product or a related molecule may reduce transcription of pathway genes, decreasing future enzyme production. The first change can act quickly, while the second may be more important over a sustained period because proteins already present must be removed or diluted before their abundance changes substantially. A short assay of enzyme activity cannot establish the presence of a transcriptional response without further evidence.

Consider three hypothetical genes tested under matched growth conditions before and after addition of a signal. Gene A produces eighty RNA units in both states; gene B produces five units before the signal and one hundred after it; gene C produces one hundred twenty before and twelve after. Gene A behaves constitutively in this comparison, gene B is induced, and gene C is repressed. These observed classes do not reveal whether the signal acts through an activator, a repressor or a combination of factors. That question requires a molecular model and additional perturbations.

In a multicellular organism, selective expression can produce stable cell identities as well as transient responses. A cell may maintain a set of proteins suited to its function while limiting production of other proteins. Changes in chromatin accessibility, regulatory factors and RNA processing can contribute. The later nodes on eukaryotic regulation and epigenetic memory explain how these states are established or maintained. The present chapter introduces the general logic without assuming that all differences between cells are explained by a single regulatory mechanism.

4. The experimental roots of regulation

Early studies of microbial enzymes posed a conceptual problem. A particular enzyme could appear in larger amounts when its substrate was available. One explanation was that the compound activated enzyme already present; another was that the compound changed production of the enzyme. A more specific hypothesis proposed that genetically specified regulatory components determine whether a structural gene is expressed. Genetic crosses and measurements over time helped distinguish these alternatives.

In 1959 Arthur Pardee, François Jacob and Jacques Monod reported experiments examining the control of beta-galactosidase synthesis in Escherichia coli. In an informative genetic cross, a functional structural gene entering a cytoplasm lacking normal repressor function could be expressed transiently without added inducer before repression was established. The result supported a distinction between the genetic capacity to make an enzyme and the activity of a diffusible regulatory substance. It also supported the idea that the regulatory gene acts through a product, rather than simply changing the sequence of the structural gene. The details of the lactose system are reserved for the next chapter.

Jacob and Monod's 1961 model brought together observations on the regulated synthesis of proteins. It proposed that a regulatory product could act at a linked control region to influence expression of structural genes. The framework made predictions about mutations that affect the regulatory protein and mutations that alter its DNA target. A regulator defect can influence more than one compatible copy, whereas a defect in a linked regulatory site may remain restricted to the target on that copy. The historical importance of the model is its experimentally testable genetic logic, not the claim that every gene is organised into a bacterial operon.

A related advance concerned messenger RNA. In 1961 Brenner, Jacob and Meselson supplied evidence that newly produced unstable RNA could associate with pre-existing ribosomes during bacteriophage infection. The ribosome need not be recreated as a special template for every newly expressed protein. Instead, informational RNA can be supplied to reusable translation machinery. Changing the supply and lifetime of informational RNA therefore provides a route to changing protein synthesis. This helps explain why rapid transcriptional responses can occur without permanently altering cellular DNA.

These historical experiments should be interpreted carefully. Transient enzyme production after genetic transfer supported a diffusible regulatory product, but did not itself directly show a purified repressor bound to a particular operator. The association of newly made RNA with pre-existing ribosomes supported the messenger hypothesis but did not reveal every molecular detail of modern translation. The later identification of particular molecular components required additional biochemical and genetic experiments. Strong reasoning separates what an observation directly establishes from what a model predicts and from what later studies independently confirm.

5. Cis-regulatory sequences and trans-acting products

A cis-regulatory element is a sequence that influences expression of a physically linked target on the same DNA molecule in the relevant regulatory context. Promoters, operators and many enhancers or silencers are examples. Cis describes the linkage of action. It does not mean that a sequence must be immediately upstream of the transcription start site. A local DNA sequence provides information about which target is to be controlled, and other molecular components can recognise or act through that sequence.

A promoter is a region involved in organising transcription initiation. An operator in a classical bacterial system is a region recognised by a regulatory factor, often a repressor. These functions can overlap in physical sequence. A mutation that alters promoter function can affect basal transcription even in the absence of the regulator. A mutation that disrupts a repressor-binding site while leaving initiation intact can remove repression from the linked target. If the site overlaps the promoter, a mutation may influence both. Genetic reasoning must therefore use the specified functional defect rather than assume every mutation in a region has one predictable consequence.

A trans-acting factor is a regulatory product capable of acting on compatible targets independently of the physical linkage of the gene encoding it to the target. Many such factors are proteins that bind DNA or interact with transcription machinery. Regulatory RNAs can also act in trans when they influence separate targets. A regulatory protein encoded next to a target can still act in trans if its product is diffusible. A distant enhancer can act in cis when its effect depends on physical linkage and chromosomal organisation. Distance alone does not establish the distinction.

Imagine two distinguishable target copies inside a single bacterium. Both carry a wild-type recognition site for a diffusible repressor. If the site on copy A is mutated so that repressor cannot bind, while basal transcription remains functional, copy A can escape repression even though copy B retains normal regulation. A wild-type recognition sequence on another DNA molecule cannot simply replace the mutated sequence in copy A. In contrast, if the bacterium lacks functional repressor because of a mutation in its regulator gene, introducing a functional copy can supply repressor to both compatible target sites.

The genetic comparison requires the outputs of the two copies to be distinguishable. If both copies encode identical enzyme molecules and the experiment measures only total activity, persistent production from one copy can conceal normal repression of the other. Allele-specific RNA measurements or distinct reporter labels can make the local effect apparent. The relevant experimental condition is that both copies share an appropriate regulatory environment. If they differ in accessibility, localisation or copy number, the result may require additional interpretation.

A loss-of-function mutation in a diffusible repressor may be complemented by a functional copy, but this is not a universal rule for all trans-acting mutations. A mutant factor that interacts with and interferes with the wild-type protein may produce a dominant-negative phenotype. A ligand-insensitive repressor may continue repressing despite the presence of a normal regulator. Conversely, a cis regulatory mutation can have a persistent local effect even when the corresponding wild-type site exists elsewhere. Complementation, dominance and cis-trans action describe related but distinct experimental concepts.
