1. Why expression must be controlled

A cell's genome provides the instructions for building RNA molecules and proteins, but the possession of an instruction does not imply that the corresponding molecule must be manufactured continuously. A bacterium that encounters a different source of carbon may need a new transporter and a different set of enzymes without acquiring new genetic information. A liver cell and a neuron can have broadly similar inherited genomes yet produce very different sets of proteins. These differences illustrate the distinction between genetic information and its expression. Regulation determines when, where and to what extent a functional gene product is made. The term applies to functional RNAs as well as to proteins; a gene encoding a ribosomal RNA does not require a translation step to generate its major functional product.

The same protein-coding gene can generate different amounts of product in two cells through several distinct mechanisms. One cell may produce more messenger RNA per minute. Another may produce RNA at the same rate but remove it more slowly. A third may use each available RNA more efficiently for protein synthesis. A fourth may retain the protein for longer after synthesis. Measuring protein abundance at one time cannot distinguish these explanations by itself. The central question throughout this chapter is therefore not simply whether expression rises or falls, but which molecular step is responsible.

The central dogma describes the usual transfer of sequence information, not a fixed rate of synthesis. A protein that binds DNA and alters transcription is not transferring its amino acid sequence back into DNA. The regulator changes the probability or rate of an information-using process. It can act on a gene without altering the sequence of the gene's product. This distinction explains why cells can respond reversibly to environmental signals on a timescale much shorter than that needed for genetic evolution. A reversible expression response also differs from selection, in which cells carrying different inherited properties may become differently represented in a population.

The statement that different cells share the same genome is a useful generalisation rather than an absolute rule. Somatic mutation, chromosome changes and immunoglobulin gene rearrangement provide exceptions; mature mammalian erythrocytes lack nuclei. Differential use of genetic information nevertheless explains a major part of cell specialisation. An inherited gene can remain available even when its expression is low in a given cell. The following chapters will examine how regulatory factors, chromatin and stable expression programmes help maintain such differences.

A further distinction is between the amount of a protein and its biological activity. If a protein is an enzyme, the reaction rate depends not only on how much enzyme is present but also on substrate, inhibitors, cofactors and catalytic state. Adding a ligand may change activity immediately without changing transcription or translation. Increasing enzyme abundance may not increase overall metabolic flux when a different reaction is limiting. A gene expression experiment therefore needs a clearly specified readout. The measured phenotype may reflect several intervening steps between the gene and its eventual biological effect.

2. Rates, abundance and functional output

The amount of new RNA produced per unit time is a rate. The amount of RNA present at a particular moment is an abundance. A gene can produce RNA continuously even while the abundance remains constant, because synthesis replaces molecules lost through turnover. In an analogous manner, the number of proteins made in one minute is not the same as the accumulated amount of protein in the cell. This distinction between a flux and a stock is essential to quantitative reasoning about gene expression.

Suppose a culture contains an average of sixty messenger RNA molecules per cell. In condition A, twelve new molecules are produced each minute and twelve are removed. In condition B, twenty-four are produced and twenty-four are removed. Both conditions have the same RNA abundance, but their rates of synthesis and removal are different. A measurement of total RNA cannot reveal this difference. A measurement of newly synthesised RNA during a controlled interval or an estimate of RNA lifetime would add a different kind of information.

The quantity of mature RNA may also differ from the quantity of precursor RNA. In a eukaryotic system, a transcript can be processed, exported or removed before it becomes available for efficient translation. If a perturbation interferes with RNA processing, total transcriptional initiation may continue at its original rate while the amount of mature messenger RNA decreases. In another case, two mature splice forms can change in relative abundance without a large change in the total transcript pool. A measurement that detects a region shared by both forms may conceal this change. The molecular identity of the readout matters as much as its numerical value.

Imagine that a treated cell contains half as much of a particular protein as an untreated cell, while the measured RNA amount is unchanged. A reduction in translation is plausible, but it is not the only possibility. Protein degradation may have increased; the protein may have moved into a compartment not represented in the measurement; or the measured RNA may include species unavailable for translation. An experiment that measures newly produced protein, protein lifetime and the appropriate RNA pool can distinguish these alternatives. The correct conclusion is no stronger than the available evidence.

Normalisation adds another layer of interpretation. A tissue sample can contain a different number or mixture of cells after a treatment. Total RNA from a tissue may rise even if no individual cell has increased its transcription rate. A reference transcript used for comparison may itself change under the treatment. Expression per cell, per unit total RNA and relative to a reference gene are not universally interchangeable. A student interpreting an expression graph should first identify the denominator and the population represented before assigning a regulatory mechanism.

The language of genetic perturbation similarly requires precision. A gene knockout may eliminate a functional product while some transcription from the locus persists. A knockdown lowers expression without necessarily removing the gene. Overexpression refers to output above a specified reference level, but does not identify the mechanism that produced the increase. A mutation can change the activity of a protein while leaving the amount of protein intact. These distinctions prevent misleading conclusions when a phenotype is described in general terms without the underlying molecular measurements.

3. Constitutive and regulated genes

The expression of a housekeeping gene is often relatively stable across the conditions in which its product is required. Such condition-independent behaviour is described as constitutive expression in that context. Other genes are expressed at different levels according to nutrient supply, stress, developmental state or other signals. Constitutive does not necessarily mean completely unregulated. A promoter that appears constant during one experiment may respond to a condition that has not yet been tested. Similarly, a housekeeping transcript is not guaranteed to be invariant across cell types, growth rates or treatments.

Making RNA and protein consumes cellular resources. A cell must invest nucleotides, amino acids and energy, as well as the use of its transcriptional and translational machinery. When an alternative nutrient is absent, continuous high production of a transporter and enzymes specific for that nutrient may have little immediate benefit. Induction upon nutrient availability can therefore conserve resources while allowing adaptation. However, completely eliminating basal expression may delay a response if a small amount of transporter is required to detect or import the nutrient. Regulation balances economy with readiness.

A biosynthetic pathway presents a complementary problem. When the end product is scarce, increased synthesis of the enzymes that make it may be advantageous. When the product is abundant, reducing the production of these enzymes can prevent unnecessary consumption of precursors and energy. Such a relationship can create negative feedback at the level of gene expression. Negative feedback is a system property, describing how a change produces an opposing effect. Negative transcriptional control is a different classification, describing the effect of a particular active regulator on target transcription. The two terms must not be confused.

Regulation of an existing enzyme and regulation of enzyme production can cooperate. An end product may bind and inhibit an existing enzyme rapidly, decreasing metabolic activity without changing protein abundance. The same product or a related molecule may reduce transcription of pathway genes, decreasing future enzyme production. The first change can act quickly, while the second may be more important over a sustained period because proteins already present must be removed or diluted before their abundance changes substantially. A short assay of enzyme activity cannot establish the presence of a transcriptional response without further evidence.

Consider three hypothetical genes tested under matched growth conditions before and after addition of a signal. Gene A produces eighty RNA units in both states; gene B produces five units before the signal and one hundred after it; gene C produces one hundred twenty before and twelve after. Gene A behaves constitutively in this comparison, gene B is induced, and gene C is repressed. These observed classes do not reveal whether the signal acts through an activator, a repressor or a combination of factors. That question requires a molecular model and additional perturbations.

In a multicellular organism, selective expression can produce stable cell identities as well as transient responses. A cell may maintain a set of proteins suited to its function while limiting production of other proteins. Changes in chromatin accessibility, regulatory factors and RNA processing can contribute. The later nodes on eukaryotic regulation and epigenetic memory explain how these states are established or maintained. The present chapter introduces the general logic without assuming that all differences between cells are explained by a single regulatory mechanism.

4. The experimental roots of regulation

Early studies of microbial enzymes posed a conceptual problem. A particular enzyme could appear in larger amounts when its substrate was available. One explanation was that the compound activated enzyme already present; another was that the compound changed production of the enzyme. A more specific hypothesis proposed that genetically specified regulatory components determine whether a structural gene is expressed. Genetic crosses and measurements over time helped distinguish these alternatives.

In 1959 Arthur Pardee, François Jacob and Jacques Monod reported experiments examining the control of beta-galactosidase synthesis in Escherichia coli. In an informative genetic cross, a functional structural gene entering a cytoplasm lacking normal repressor function could be expressed transiently without added inducer before repression was established. The result supported a distinction between the genetic capacity to make an enzyme and the activity of a diffusible regulatory substance. It also supported the idea that the regulatory gene acts through a product, rather than simply changing the sequence of the structural gene. The details of the lactose system are reserved for the next chapter.

Jacob and Monod's 1961 model brought together observations on the regulated synthesis of proteins. It proposed that a regulatory product could act at a linked control region to influence expression of structural genes. The framework made predictions about mutations that affect the regulatory protein and mutations that alter its DNA target. A regulator defect can influence more than one compatible copy, whereas a defect in a linked regulatory site may remain restricted to the target on that copy. The historical importance of the model is its experimentally testable genetic logic, not the claim that every gene is organised into a bacterial operon.

A related advance concerned messenger RNA. In 1961 Brenner, Jacob and Meselson supplied evidence that newly produced unstable RNA could associate with pre-existing ribosomes during bacteriophage infection. The ribosome need not be recreated as a special template for every newly expressed protein. Instead, informational RNA can be supplied to reusable translation machinery. Changing the supply and lifetime of informational RNA therefore provides a route to changing protein synthesis. This helps explain why rapid transcriptional responses can occur without permanently altering cellular DNA.

These historical experiments should be interpreted carefully. Transient enzyme production after genetic transfer supported a diffusible regulatory product, but did not itself directly show a purified repressor bound to a particular operator. The association of newly made RNA with pre-existing ribosomes supported the messenger hypothesis but did not reveal every molecular detail of modern translation. The later identification of particular molecular components required additional biochemical and genetic experiments. Strong reasoning separates what an observation directly establishes from what a model predicts and from what later studies independently confirm.

Historical sources: Pardee, Jacob and Monod (1959); Jacob and Monod (1961); Brenner, Jacob and Meselson (1961).

5. Cis-regulatory sequences and trans-acting products

A cis-regulatory element is a sequence that influences expression of a physically linked target on the same DNA molecule in the relevant regulatory context. Promoters, operators and many enhancers or silencers are examples. Cis describes the linkage of action. It does not mean that a sequence must be immediately upstream of the transcription start site. A local DNA sequence provides information about which target is to be controlled, and other molecular components can recognise or act through that sequence.

A promoter is a region involved in organising transcription initiation. An operator in a classical bacterial system is a region recognised by a regulatory factor, often a repressor. These functions can overlap in physical sequence. A mutation that alters promoter function can affect basal transcription even in the absence of the regulator. A mutation that disrupts a repressor-binding site while leaving initiation intact can remove repression from the linked target. If the site overlaps the promoter, a mutation may influence both. Genetic reasoning must therefore use the specified functional defect rather than assume every mutation in a region has one predictable consequence.

A trans-acting factor is a regulatory product capable of acting on compatible targets independently of the physical linkage of the gene encoding it to the target. Many such factors are proteins that bind DNA or interact with transcription machinery. Regulatory RNAs can also act in trans when they influence separate targets. A regulatory protein encoded next to a target can still act in trans if its product is diffusible. A distant enhancer can act in cis when its effect depends on physical linkage and chromosomal organisation. Distance alone does not establish the distinction.

Imagine two distinguishable target copies inside a single bacterium. Both carry a wild-type recognition site for a diffusible repressor. If the site on copy A is mutated so that repressor cannot bind, while basal transcription remains functional, copy A can escape repression even though copy B retains normal regulation. A wild-type recognition sequence on another DNA molecule cannot simply replace the mutated sequence in copy A. In contrast, if the bacterium lacks functional repressor because of a mutation in its regulator gene, introducing a functional copy can supply repressor to both compatible target sites.

The genetic comparison requires the outputs of the two copies to be distinguishable. If both copies encode identical enzyme molecules and the experiment measures only total activity, persistent production from one copy can conceal normal repression of the other. Allele-specific RNA measurements or distinct reporter labels can make the local effect apparent. The relevant experimental condition is that both copies share an appropriate regulatory environment. If they differ in accessibility, localisation or copy number, the result may require additional interpretation.

A loss-of-function mutation in a diffusible repressor may be complemented by a functional copy, but this is not a universal rule for all trans-acting mutations. A mutant factor that interacts with and interferes with the wild-type protein may produce a dominant-negative phenotype. A ligand-insensitive repressor may continue repressing despite the presence of a normal regulator. Conversely, a cis regulatory mutation can have a persistent local effect even when the corresponding wild-type site exists elsewhere. Complementation, dominance and cis-trans action describe related but distinct experimental concepts.

6. The sign of a regulator and the direction of a response

When we speak of positive or negative control we are describing the effect of a molecular regulator on a specified target. An activator increases the rate of productive expression relative to the appropriate unactivated state. A repressor decreases it relative to the corresponding unrepressed state. These are relational definitions. They do not mean that the activator is beneficial or that the repressor is harmful. Both kinds of regulator can conserve resources and improve adaptation. The same protein can even behave differently at different targets if its interactions or binding positions differ.

To make the distinction precise, imagine a DNA region capable of supporting transcription at a basal rate of twenty RNA molecules per minute. In the presence of an active activator, the rate rises to eighty. Removing this activator removes the additional stimulation, but the promoter may still produce its basal output. In another system the basal rate is eighty, and an active repressor reduces it to twenty. Removing the repressor exposes the higher basal rate. It would be misleading to call both proteins activators merely because a particular experimental treatment raises expression in both systems. The regulator sign is defined by what the regulator does when it is active.

The molecular basis of activation is often an increase in the probability that transcription machinery assembles productively or proceeds through a limiting step. A DNA-bound activator might stabilise an interaction with RNA polymerase, facilitate recruitment of a required component or alter the local regulatory environment. Repression can reduce productive access to a promoter or interfere with an essential interaction. It need not always involve physical coverage of the start site. The position of a regulatory element and the function of the associated protein must be established for the system being discussed.

A factor that binds to DNA does not necessarily change expression. For example, a mutant activator may retain its DNA recognition domain but lose a surface needed to interact with the transcription machinery. A binding experiment could show normal occupancy, whereas transcription remains near its basal rate. A different mutant might lose DNA binding and also produce low output. The same expression phenotype can therefore arise from two different molecular lesions. Measuring both binding and transcription provides stronger evidence than either measurement alone.

The direction in which an environmental signal changes expression is a separate issue. Suppose a signal activates a positive regulator. The target increases its output because an activating influence has become stronger. Suppose instead that the signal inactivates a repressor. The target again increases output, but the causal path contains two inhibitory steps: signal inhibits repressor, and repressor inhibits expression. Induction occurs in both cases. A signal can also decrease expression either by activating a repressor or by inactivating an activator. Thus an increasing or decreasing wild-type curve alone does not establish the molecular sign.

A useful qualitative procedure is to draw the signal, regulator and target in causal order. For a simple chain, an activating interaction preserves the sign of a change whereas an inhibitory interaction reverses it. Two inhibitory links can therefore give a net positive response. This sign rule is useful for examination problems involving a small number of defined components. It is not sufficient for a network with feedback, parallel interactions or regulators that change more than one step. In those systems the magnitude and timing of competing influences matter.

Consider two experiments using independent hypothetical genes X and Y. Both wild-type genes give six RNA units in the absence of signal and ninety-six in its presence. In X, deletion of the sole regulator gives approximately ninety-six units in both conditions. In Y, deletion gives approximately six units in both conditions. Provided that both promoters remain functional and there are no compensating changes, X is consistent with negative inducible control and Y with positive inducible control. The signal direction is identical in the two cases; the diagnostic evidence is the effect of removing the regulatory protein.

Even this genetic inference needs appropriate qualifications. Removing a regulator from a cell can affect growth, nutrient uptake and other regulatory factors. A target may respond indirectly to such changes. A regulator-null phenotype supports the simplest direct model when the relevant interactions are specified, but independent evidence of target recognition and restoration by a functional regulator strengthens the conclusion. An examination problem often explicitly states that the regulator is the only relevant one. In a real experiment the directness of its action is a separate hypothesis to test.

Positive control should also not be confused with positive feedback. A positive regulator can be part of a negative-feedback circuit. For instance, an activator could increase expression of a biosynthetic pathway, whose product subsequently inhibits the activator. The activator is positive with respect to its target; the complete feedback loop opposes the initial increase in product. Conversely a negative regulator can participate in a positive-feedback circuit if it inhibits another negative influence. The sign of a component and the sign of a complete circuit are different logical levels.

The distinction is valuable because it prevents memorisation of misleading associations. In an unfamiliar problem, first identify the output under consideration. Then ask what an active regulator does to that output, and only afterward ask how the signal changes regulator activity. This separates direct control from the overall observed response and provides the foundation for predicting genetic perturbations.

7. How an environmental signal becomes a molecular instruction

A regulatory system can respond to a nutrient, metabolite, stress or developmental cue only if information about that condition reaches a molecule capable of changing expression. The signal may interact directly with the relevant regulatory protein or act through intermediate reactions. In a simple bacterial system a small metabolite can bind a transcription factor; in a more elaborate system a signal changes the modification state, amount or localisation of one or more regulatory molecules. The relationship between signal and output depends on all intervening steps.

An effector is a molecule that alters the behaviour of a regulatory component in the defined context. A classical inducer promotes expression of the target. In a negative inducible system the inducer can reduce the activity of a repressor. In a positive inducible system it can increase the activity of an activator. The term inducer describes the effect on expression rather than a universal molecular structure or binding mechanism. A substance that induces one pathway need not induce every target in the cell.

A classical corepressor is a molecule that promotes the ability of a repressor to reduce expression. When a pathway's end product is abundant, binding of that product to a regulatory protein may reduce the production of the enzymes needed to make it. This links the requirement for the product to future biosynthetic capacity. The word corepressor is also used in eukaryotic gene regulation for a protein participating in repression, often through interactions with DNA-binding factors. This latter usage does not necessarily mean a small molecule. Correct interpretation requires the molecular context.

Allostery explains how a ligand can change regulatory activity without changing the total number of regulator molecules. A regulatory protein can occupy different conformational states with differing abilities to recognise DNA or interact with another component. Binding of a ligand stabilises some states relative to others and shifts the population of conformations. An inducer may therefore weaken DNA binding without destroying the repressor or altering the DNA sequence. Removing the ligand can allow the regulatory state to return toward its earlier distribution. Such reversible control is especially useful when environmental conditions alternate.

Suppose the total concentration of a regulator is one hundred nanomolar. In the absence of a signal, eighty percent is in a state capable of repressing a target, giving eighty nanomolar active regulator. In the presence of signal, only five percent is active, giving five nanomolar. A measurement of total protein shows no change, while the active pool falls sixteenfold. If binding sites are scarce relative to the regulator, the free active concentration may be approximated by the active pool; if many sites bind it, the free pool can be smaller. Expression depends on the relevant molecular state and its interactions, not solely on the protein abundance.

It is important to identify the chemical species that actually acts as the regulator ligand. Extracellular lactose is the nutrient used by the classical lactose-utilisation system, but allolactose is its physiological inducer. The external nutrient must be made available to the intracellular regulatory mechanism, and in this instance an intracellular derivative provides the direct signal. If a mutant cannot take up or convert the nutrient appropriately, induction can fail even when the regulator and target control region are intact. The detailed molecular case will be developed in the next chapter.

This observation leads to an important experimental design principle. A failed response to an external treatment does not prove that the target regulatory machinery is defective. One must determine whether the signal reached the relevant compartment, whether the intracellular effector was produced and whether it could alter regulator activity. A failure at any of these steps may give the same final expression phenotype. Supplying a suitable accessible effector or independently measuring the intracellular signal may discriminate between upstream and downstream lesions, provided that the design controls the other variables.

A ligand-recognition mutation and a DNA-recognition mutation can have opposite effects in a negative inducible model. A repressor that cannot respond to inducer but retains its repressive activity may keep the target low despite signal. A repressor that cannot recognise its target may fail to repress regardless of signal. In an activator-based model, loss of DNA recognition generally removes stimulation, whereas a ligand-independent active state can produce constitutive stimulation. These predictions arise from the specific functions retained by each mutant rather than from the name of the regulatory locus.

The available concentration of a ligand can change gradually rather than switching abruptly between zero and a saturating level. As signal increases, the fraction of molecules in a relevant state may gradually shift. Target binding can also approach saturation. An expression curve can therefore have an initial range of low sensitivity, an intermediate range of stronger sensitivity and a plateau where further increases produce little response. The location and shape of the curve depend on both ligand recognition and the subsequent steps connecting regulatory activity to output.

A plateau should not be interpreted as evidence that the total ligand concentration equals the total regulator concentration. It indicates that the measured response is approaching a limit under the tested conditions. The limit might arise from saturation of binding, limited transcription machinery, maximum promoter output or a downstream constraint. A robust explanation specifies which limit the evidence supports. This distinction between a molecular input and a measured output is one of the recurring themes of the chapter.

8. The four ideal regulatory architectures

The terms inducible and repressible refer to the direction in which a specified signal alters target expression. The terms positive and negative refer to the effect of the active regulator. Combining these axes produces four ideal architectures. They are useful reference models because each has a characteristic pattern of wild-type and mutant expression. They do not imply that a natural gene must be controlled by a single regulator or that every response is binary.

In negative inducible control, the repressor is active in the absence of inducer and limits target output. Inducer weakens repression, allowing output to rise. In a pure one-repressor model, removing the repressor gives comparatively high expression even without signal, provided that the basal promoter is functional. A mutation specifically preventing repressor recognition at the target can produce a similar local expression phenotype. A mutation that keeps the repressor functional but insensitive to inducer may instead cause low output in both conditions.

In positive inducible control, an activator is relatively ineffective in the absence of inducer. Signal promotes its active state, raising transcription. If the sole necessary activator is removed, expression remains at the low basal level whether inducer is present or absent. If the activator becomes effective even without ligand, output can remain high in both conditions. Thus the constitutive behaviour of a gene can result from removal of an inhibitory influence or from persistent stimulation; constitutive expression does not uniquely identify a molecular lesion.

In negative repressible control, a repressor is relatively ineffective without the corepressor. When the corepressor appears, the regulator becomes better able to repress the target, and expression falls. Loss of the sole repressor can leave the target highly expressed despite abundant corepressor. This is an intuitive arrangement for a biosynthetic pathway that is needed when its product is scarce. Real pathways may include additional regulatory steps that cannot be represented by this one interaction. The tryptophan pathway will be analysed in detail in a subsequent node.

Positive repressible control can arise when an activator is effective in the absence of an inhibitory signal but is inactivated by that signal. Expression is high before the signal and low afterward. Removing the activator produces low output in both conditions, unlike the regulator-null phenotype of a negative repressible system. An activator that remains effective despite the inhibitory signal may instead yield persistent high expression. The repressible classification describes the overall response and does not require the presence of a classical negative repressor.

A compact comparison can be constructed by writing the regulator's active state first. A negative regulator lowers expression when active; a positive regulator raises it. In a negative inducible system the signal decreases the active fraction; in a positive inducible system it increases it. In a negative repressible system the signal increases the active fraction; in a positive repressible system it decreases it. Once these relationships are specified, the relative expression states follow without memorising four unrelated lists.

Consider an unknown system whose wild-type output changes from ninety to eight units when signal S is added. A verified regulator deletion gives ninety units in both conditions. This is consistent with negative repressible control: the signal normally establishes an effective negative influence that the deletion removes. If the deletion instead gives eight units in both conditions, the observations support positive repressible control: the signal normally disables a positive influence, and deletion removes that influence in either condition. A mutation in the promoter might generate low output by a different route, so the statement that the deletion specifically eliminates the regulator is essential.

The terms high and low are relative to the experimental comparison. Low expression may be sufficient to maintain a small amount of transporter needed for sensing a nutrient. High expression may still be below the maximum possible rate if another factor is limiting. If a student equates low with zero automatically, predictions can become inconsistent when basal production is detected. A quantitative problem should use the stated values rather than assume an ideal complete switch.

Inducible and repressible behaviour can also depend on the reference signal. A gene could be induced by one nutrient but repressed by another, because each acts through a different regulatory interaction. Describing the whole gene as simply inducible may then hide the second input. The more precise description specifies the signal, its effect on the relevant regulator and the conditions of the comparison.

For example, suppose a target has a basal rate of ten units when no active repressor is present. An active activator increases the rate to one hundred, but an active repressor limits output to two regardless of activator state. If signal A activates the activator and signal B inactivates the repressor, the four environmental conditions give two, two, ten and one hundred units depending on the states of A and B. Signal A may appear ineffective when repression remains active. Signal B can produce modest induction without A, while maximal expression requires both signals. The response to one signal is conditional on the state of another component.

This example illustrates the conceptual difference between relief of repression and activation above basal output. A repressor-null mutation can remove a barrier to transcription without providing the stimulation required for high output. An activator-null mutation can leave a gene responsive to changes in repression but prevent it from reaching the high activated state. These distinct possibilities are central to interpreting combined nutrient controls in later bacterial chapters.

The purpose of the four models is not to force complex biology into rigid categories. It is to provide a disciplined method for reading an unfamiliar regulatory diagram. Identify the target and baseline, distinguish the sign of the regulator from the direction of the signal response, and track each component through the relevant perturbation. When the observations do not match the simple model, determine which assumption may have failed.

9. From recognition affinity to graded transcription

A regulatory protein can influence a DNA target only when the relevant interaction occurs. In a reversible binding model a site can switch between unoccupied and occupied states. The fraction of time or proportion of equivalent sites occupied depends on the molecular association and dissociation processes. A population average may therefore change smoothly even when each individual site is described by only two states. Graded expression does not necessarily imply that an individual promoter has a continuously adjustable instantaneous output.

For a simple reversible association write R + D ⇌ RD, where R is free active regulator, D is free target and RD is the complex. Using the dilute concentration approximation, the dissociation constant Kd is [R][D]/[RD]. This expression can be rearranged as [RD]=[R][D]/Kd. The total concentration of target is the sum of free and complexed target, [D]total=[D]+[RD]. Substituting the relation for RD into the fraction [RD]/[D]total gives θ=[R]/(Kd+[R]). The derivation assumes one class of independent binding sites, equilibrium and a specified free regulator concentration.

The fraction θ is dimensionless and ranges from zero to one. Both [R] and Kd must be expressed in compatible concentration units. If [R]=Kd, the site is half occupied. If [R]=9Kd, the fraction is 0.90. If [R]=Kd/9, the fraction is 0.10. At fixed free concentration a lower Kd gives greater occupancy, corresponding to greater affinity within the model. These simple relationships are useful for recognising qualitative changes without a calculator.

For a particular binding site with Kd=10 nM, free regulator concentrations of one, ten and one hundred nanomolar give occupancies of approximately 0.091, 0.50 and 0.909. The change from one to ten nanomolar has a large effect, whereas the change from an already saturating concentration of one hundred to one thousand nanomolar has less effect on occupancy. The relationship is not linear across the whole range. The concentration at which the cell operates therefore matters when predicting the effect of a mutation or ligand.

Suppose a mutation weakens regulator recognition by raising Kd from ten to forty nanomolar, while the free active regulator remains twenty nanomolar. The fraction falls from twenty divided by thirty, or two thirds, to twenty divided by sixty, or one third. If the regulator is a repressor this can raise transcription, whereas if it is an activator it can lower transcription. The binding calculation supplies only the occupancy change. The sign of the output change requires knowledge of what the occupied state does.

To relate occupancy to expression, suppose the site has an unoccupied production rate u and an occupied production rate b. If the states are representative and their rates are stable, average transcription is T=(1−θ)u+θb. This is a weighted average because the fractions of the two states sum to one. In a negative regulatory model b is less than u; in a positive regulatory model b is greater than u. If b equals u, occupancy has no effect on output even though specific binding may occur.

For an illustrative repressor, let u=100 and b=5 RNA units per minute. At occupancy 0.80, average production is 0.20×100+0.80×5=24. At occupancy 0.10 it is 0.90×100+0.10×5=90.5. Even a fully occupied site would produce five units in this model, rather than zero. It is important to retain the occupied-state rate when a question specifies residual transcription. An assumption of complete repression would give the wrong numerical answer.

For an illustrative activator, let u=10 and b=80. At occupancy 0.25, production is 7.5+20=27.5; at occupancy 0.75, it is 2.5+60=62.5. Binding increases with free active concentration in both the positive and negative models, but the response has opposite slopes because the rates assigned to the occupied and free states differ. This gives a quantitative expression of the distinction between binding affinity and regulatory activity.

A ligand can change occupancy indirectly by modifying the free active fraction of a regulator. Suppose the relevant free active repressor concentration falls from eighty to five nanomolar while its target has Kd=20 nM. Occupancy falls from 0.80 to 0.20. In the model with u=100 and b=5, output rises from twenty-four to eighty-one units per minute. The total number of repressor molecules can remain unchanged. This calculation links ligand-driven state change to site occupancy and then to transcript production, with each stage represented by a different variable.

The free concentration assumption needs particular attention when a cell contains many binding sites. If regulator is abundant relative to target sites, binding may remove only a small fraction of the free pool. If targets are numerous relative to regulator, they may compete for it. Extra copies of a regulatory sequence can therefore reduce the amount of regulator available to another target without directly changing its total cellular abundance. The equilibrium occupancy formula remains applicable to the actual free concentration in its stated domain, but that concentration may need to be determined using a mass balance rather than assumed equal to the total.

Different targets can have different affinities for the same regulator. In the simplest model this can create different response ranges. At a free concentration of ten nanomolar, a site with Kd=1 nM is about 91 percent occupied, whereas a site with Kd=100 nM is about nine percent occupied. This offers a conceptual explanation for why a common regulatory factor can affect its targets at different levels of signal. In a living cell, the binding position, local DNA environment and interacting partners can also differ, so affinity alone does not determine the hierarchy of expression.

A steeper response can arise when binding events or downstream steps are cooperative. A Hill-type equation θ=Rⁿ/(Kⁿ+Rⁿ) is a useful descriptive comparison. At R=K it gives one-half occupancy for a positive exponent. An exponent greater than one produces a steeper transition around that point than the one-site expression. The fitted exponent need not equal the number of molecules bound. A steep expression curve may also reflect multiple regulatory transitions between binding and measured output. Molecular stoichiometry cannot be established from a response slope alone.

A final limitation concerns time. Equilibrium expressions do not by themselves describe how quickly binding equilibrates after a change in ligand. The observed RNA or protein pool may also respond slowly even if regulator occupancy changes quickly. If a measured expression curve has a delay, the delay may arise at one or more of several stages. The next sections introduce the production-and-removal models needed to distinguish these possibilities.

10. Where gene expression is controlled

A change in the production of a functional protein is often described in terms of the gene that encodes it. Yet several molecular processes connect the gene to the final product. In a typical nucleated eukaryotic cell, the DNA region must support transcription, the precursor transcript must yield a mature RNA, the RNA must be available to ribosomes, the polypeptide must be produced and then become part of the relevant protein pool. The distinction among these stages allows an investigator to identify the primary regulatory step rather than merely report a change in the final product.

Transcriptional regulation changes the production of RNA from DNA. A factor that reduces the frequency of productive initiation can reduce the number of new RNA molecules. A factor that increases premature termination can also reduce the number of full-length transcripts, even if initiation occurs at the same rate. These two mechanisms can be distinguished by considering what portion of the RNA an assay detects. A measurement near the beginning of a gene can include initiated molecules that never reach its end, whereas a measurement requiring the completed transcript can reveal a reduction in successful transcriptional completion.

Consider a hypothetical transcription unit with one hundred initiation events per minute. Initially ninety percent of the initiated transcripts reach the normal termination region, producing ninety completed RNAs per minute. After a regulatory perturbation, initiation remains at one hundred but only thirty percent of the transcripts reach the end. The completed output drops to thirty per minute. If a question describes a threefold reduction in complete RNA production, a decrease in initiation is not necessarily the explanation. The probability of successful completion is a distinct variable. The example also prepares the later discussion of bacterial attenuation without teaching the details of that particular pathway here.

Post-transcriptional regulation acts on RNA after it has been made or while it is being processed. A single primary RNA may give rise to different mature molecules through alternative processing. Changing the proportions of these forms can alter the products made without necessarily changing the initial rate of transcription. A measurement that recognises a region shared by two RNA forms may show no overall difference, although one isoform becomes scarce and the other more abundant. A student must therefore know whether the measured RNA is a specific mature transcript, the precursor, or a mixture of related species.

Imagine that a precursor is made at a rate of twenty molecules per minute. In condition A, three quarters of the precursors yield mature RNA type X, and the remaining quarter yields type Y. In condition B, the precursor production rate remains twenty but the fractions are reversed. The production rates of X and Y change from fifteen and five to five and fifteen molecules per minute. If their lifetimes are equal, their combined steady abundance remains the same. If they encode different protein products, the biological outcome may change substantially even though a measurement of the combined RNA pool shows no difference. This is a concrete illustration of regulation through product identity rather than only total output.

The availability of mature RNA is also affected by transport, localisation and stability. An RNA retained in the nucleus may contribute to a whole-cell measurement without providing an equivalent supply of template to cytoplasmic ribosomes. A transcript transported to a particular region of a cell may be translated there, allowing a local increase in protein production. If the experimental readout uses the total RNA pool, it may fail to reveal a change in the local accessible pool. The cellular compartment and molecular form of the measured transcript therefore matter.

Translational regulation concerns the production of polypeptide from available RNA. A cell can maintain a messenger RNA in a relatively inactive state and increase its use after receiving a signal. Such a response need not wait for transcription of a new template. The regulatory mechanism may influence initiation, access to a ribosome-binding region, or another aspect of productive translation. The earlier chapters explain the translation machinery; the concern here is how the output of that machinery differs for the same supply of available RNA.

If an RNA pool of fifty molecules generates one hundred completed polypeptides per minute in one condition and twenty-five in another, the effective production rate per RNA falls from two to one half per minute. Provided the available RNA molecules are comparable in the two conditions, the result is consistent with reduced translational efficiency. If the measured fifty molecules include an unknown number of inaccessible transcripts, the apparent change per total RNA may instead arise from reduced RNA availability. A well-designed interpretation distinguishes the measured pool from the functional template pool.

Protein maturation and turnover influence the amount of protein detected after synthesis. A cell may produce the same number of new polypeptides per minute in two states yet have different accumulated protein abundance because degradation differs. Another protein may be produced and retained in equal amounts but become more active after a modification or ligand-binding event. Such control of an existing protein is often rapid and biologically important. It should not be mistaken for evidence that its gene has been transcribed or translated more rapidly.

The division among regulatory levels is conceptual, not an assertion that each process is physically independent. In bacteria, translation can begin while transcription is still in progress. A change in ribosome behaviour can influence RNA structure or the probability of transcription termination. In eukaryotes, processing can accompany transcription, and RNA-associated proteins can influence subsequent export, translation or decay. The primary site of perturbation can thus have secondary consequences at other levels. Describing the earliest demonstrated molecular effect helps avoid confusing the initiating cause with its downstream readouts.

An instructive way to think about these levels is to distinguish rates, fractions and accumulated amounts. Initiation and completion establish a production rate; RNA processing and availability can determine what fraction of newly produced RNA enters a relevant pool; translation establishes a rate per available template; removal and dilution determine how long products remain. The final protein amount depends on all these quantities. Biological activity can add yet another variable, the fraction of protein that is functional in the relevant environment. A single measurement seldom identifies all the controlling parameters.

This layered perspective also explains why two genes may respond differently to a global perturbation. A general change in transcriptional machinery could alter the production of many RNAs, whereas a factor that recognises a particular untranslated region might affect a subset of transcripts. A change in general nutrient availability can affect both translation and transcription, making a gene-specific readout difficult to interpret without control genes. The later detailed chapters will examine particular regulatory mechanisms, but all of them can be placed on this common sequence of information use.

11. Deriving the RNA production–removal model

To make the distinction between transcription rate and RNA amount quantitative, let M(t) represent the mean number of molecules of a specified RNA per cell at time t. Let s be the production rate of that RNA in molecules per cell per minute. The loss of RNA can depend on degradation and, depending on how the measurement is normalised, dilution. A useful first approximation is that removal is proportional to the amount of RNA present. If the effective first-order loss constant is δ in inverse minutes, the loss rate is δM. The production and removal terms have the same units, molecules per cell per minute.

The net change in abundance is therefore dM/dt = s−δM. This balance is not a statement that the molecules themselves are stable; it describes how the population of molecules changes. If production exceeds loss, M rises. If loss exceeds production, M falls. If the rates are equal, the abundance is constant even though individual molecules can be continually replaced. The concept of a dynamic steady state is essential for understanding why equal RNA quantities can conceal very different production rates.

Set dM/dt=0 to determine the steady mean. The resulting equation s=δMss gives Mss=s/δ. At fixed removal constant, doubling production doubles the steady amount. At fixed production, doubling the removal constant halves the steady amount. If both parameters double, steady abundance remains unchanged. The relation is valid for a steady state with positive removal constant, constant input and approximately first-order loss; a sample collected during a rapid transition need not obey the steady-state ratio.

Suppose an RNA is produced at twelve molecules per cell per minute and δ=0.20 min⁻¹. Its predicted steady abundance is sixty molecules per cell. A second state produces twenty-four molecules per cell per minute with δ=0.40 min⁻¹, again giving sixty. The second state has twice the replacement flux of the first. Measuring only total RNA gives the same result. A suitably interpreted measurement of newly synthesised RNA and a separate determination of lifetime can distinguish the two states.

Now suppose a signal increases RNA production from twelve to eighteen molecules per minute but simultaneously increases removal constant from 0.20 to 0.60 min⁻¹. The new predicted steady abundance is eighteen divided by 0.60, or thirty molecules per cell. Transcriptional production has increased by fifty percent, but steady RNA abundance has decreased by fifty percent. This apparently paradoxical result follows directly from the balance equation. It is an important warning against treating measured RNA abundance as a direct measure of transcription rate.

The first-order loss constant can be related to RNA lifetime. When production of the measured RNA is stopped and no additional input enters the pool, dM/dt=−δM. Separating variables gives dM/M=−δdt. Integration from the initial abundance M₀ at time zero to M at time t gives ln(M/M₀)=−δt. Exponentiation yields M(t)=M₀ exp(−δt). The quantity inside the logarithm is a ratio and hence dimensionless. The exponent is likewise dimensionless because δ has units of inverse time.

The half-life is the interval at which M/M₀ equals one half. Substitution into the integrated expression gives −ln(2)=−δt½, so t½=ln(2)/δ. For δ=0.20 min⁻¹, the half-life is approximately 0.693/0.20=3.47 minutes. For δ=0.40 min⁻¹, it is approximately 1.73 minutes. The state with the faster replacement flux in the earlier example has the shorter RNA half-life, even though its steady abundance is identical.

The exponential model also gives a straightforward method for interpreting data. If the initial abundance is eighty molecules and the amount after four minutes is twenty, two half-lives have elapsed, assuming production has stopped and the first-order model holds. The half-life is two minutes and δ is approximately 0.3466 min⁻¹. If the original sample was at steady state, the inferred production rate is 0.3466×80≈27.7 molecules per minute. This estimate uses both a decay measurement and the original steady abundance; neither quantity alone would determine the production rate.

When new RNA synthesis continues at a different rate after a regulatory signal, the simple decay-to-zero formula cannot be used directly. Suppose the rate changes abruptly to snew and then remains constant while δ is unchanged. Define Mss,new=snew/δ. The equation becomes dM/dt=−δ(M−Mss,new). The difference from the new steady abundance, rather than the abundance itself, now decays exponentially. Integrating yields M(t)=Mss,new+(M₀−Mss,new)exp(−δt). This result applies to an increase or decrease in production under the same assumptions.

Take a gene initially producing four RNAs per minute with δ=0.10 min⁻¹. Its initial steady abundance is forty. A signal raises production to sixteen per minute without changing removal. The new steady abundance is one hundred sixty. After one half-life, approximately 6.93 minutes, the remaining difference is half of the initial difference, giving 160−120/2=100. After two half-lives, abundance is 160−120/4=130. The final fourfold induction is not observed immediately. A sample at the first half-life shows a 2.5-fold increase, even though the production rate has already quadrupled.

Consider the reverse kind of response. A steady RNA pool of one hundred molecules is subject to a signal that reduces production to a level supporting a new steady abundance of twenty. If removal constant is unchanged, after one half-life the abundance is twenty plus half of the initial difference of eighty, which equals sixty. After two half-lives it is forty. It would be incorrect to predict fifty and twenty-five by repeatedly halving the entire RNA abundance, because production continues and the new steady level is not zero.

The time required to approach a new level can be expressed in terms of the remaining difference. After n half-lives, the remaining fraction is 2⁻ⁿ. To complete ninety percent of a step response, the remaining fraction must be 0.10. Solving exp(−δt)=0.10 gives t=ln(10)/δ. For δ=0.10 min⁻¹ this is approximately 23.0 minutes, or about 3.32 half-lives. The approach is asymptotic in the ideal mathematical model, so an exact finite time at which the new steady abundance is reached is not predicted.

This relationship helps explain a biological tradeoff. At fixed production, an RNA with slow removal accumulates to a larger steady amount but responds more slowly to a step in production. An RNA with rapid turnover can follow changing production more promptly, but maintaining the same steady abundance requires greater synthesis. Cells can exploit different lifetimes for different purposes. A transcript needed for a short transient response may benefit from rapid removal, whereas a transcript that supports a sustained programme may persist. These are functional possibilities, not universal rules about the lifetime of every particular RNA.

Suppose a short-lived and long-lived RNA both have a steady abundance of fifty molecules. The short-lived RNA has δ=0.50 min⁻¹ and the long-lived RNA δ=0.05 min⁻¹. Their required production rates are twenty-five and 2.5 molecules per minute, respectively. If production is switched off, their half-lives are about 1.39 and 13.86 minutes. The short-lived RNA is more expensive to maintain in terms of replacement flux but its abundance declines much sooner after the signal. These estimates quantify the connection between resource investment and responsiveness discussed earlier.

If a perturbation changes δ rather than s, a new steady state still follows from the same balance equation, provided that the new δ is constant. Suppose production is ten RNA molecules per minute, initially δ=0.20 min⁻¹, giving fifty RNA molecules. A stabilising signal lowers δ to 0.10 min⁻¹, producing a new steady level of one hundred and a new half-life of 6.93 minutes. After that interval, the abundance is seventy-five. The rising RNA curve can resemble a transcriptional induction curve, but the underlying mechanism is longer lifetime. A comparison of production and loss measurements distinguishes them.

A simple model is informative only when its assumptions are understood. A measured RNA concentration can change because the cell volume changes, even when molecule number is unchanged. A growing population can dilute long-lived molecules as cells divide. Different molecular subpopulations may have distinct lifetimes, making a single exponential approximation inadequate. Removal may depend on a limiting enzyme rather than being strictly first order across all concentrations. The balance principle remains valid, but the particular mathematical form must be adjusted if the evidence contradicts the assumptions.

Examination problems often provide a steady-state RNA abundance and a half-life in each condition. In such cases compute δ from ln(2)/t½, then calculate s=δMss. Compare the resulting production rates rather than comparing abundance alone. When a problem gives a time after a signal and specifies continuing transcription, use the step-response expression rather than a decay-to-zero model. This choice of equation follows from the biological situation and the stated assumptions, not merely from recognition of a familiar numerical pattern.

12. Translation, protein turnover and the final output

The RNA pool supports translation, but its abundance is not the only variable controlling protein production. An RNA molecule can be translated repeatedly while it remains available. Different transcripts can have different effective rates of polypeptide production, and the same transcript may be translated differently in different conditions. If the relevant messenger RNA pool contains M molecules per cell, and each available RNA produces an average of k protein molecules per minute, the total production rate is kM protein molecules per cell per minute. The parameter k represents an effective rate of completed production, not simply the number of bound ribosomes.

Let P(t) denote a measured protein abundance and γ the first-order removal constant in inverse minutes. The protein balance is dP/dt=kM−γP. This equation assumes that the available RNA pool is represented by M, that each molecule has the stated mean production rate, and that newly produced proteins contribute to the measured pool without an additional explicit maturation delay. The loss term may include degradation and effective dilution, depending on the way abundance is measured.

When both RNA and protein have reached steady states, kMss=γPss. Thus Pss=kMss/γ=ks/(δγ). This expression shows that production at the transcriptional level, removal at the RNA level, production per RNA at the translational level and removal at the protein level all determine final abundance. A change in one factor can be masked or amplified by changes in another. Equal steady protein levels do not imply equal transcriptional or translational rates.

Suppose a cell maintains fifty available messenger RNA molecules and each produces two protein molecules per minute. The total protein production rate is one hundred molecules per minute. With γ=0.05 min⁻¹, steady protein abundance is one hundred divided by 0.05, or two thousand. If k is halved while M and γ remain fixed, the production rate becomes fifty and steady protein abundance becomes one thousand. If instead γ is doubled while k remains unchanged, steady protein abundance is also one thousand, but production remains one hundred per minute. New-protein synthesis and protein decay measurements distinguish the two alternatives.

Changes at several levels can combine multiplicatively. Imagine that a signal doubles the transcriptional production rate, halves the RNA removal constant and raises translation per available RNA by a factor of 1.5, while leaving protein removal unchanged. The RNA abundance becomes 2/(0.5)=4 times the original. The protein abundance becomes 4×1.5=6 times the original. Adding the changes as though they were independent percentage increments would not capture the result. The multiplicative relationship follows from the steady-state balance expression under the specified assumptions.

Conversely, transcriptional and translational changes can cancel each other. Suppose RNA production doubles while RNA turnover remains the same, causing a twofold increase in M. If translation rate per RNA falls by half, the total protein production rate remains unchanged. With unchanged protein removal, the steady protein amount remains unchanged. Thus an apparently stable protein can conceal substantial regulation of its RNA and translational output. The lack of a protein abundance response does not establish that the gene has escaped regulatory control.

The direction of a protein change can differ from that of the RNA change. For example, a signal increases RNA abundance threefold but reduces k to one quarter of its original value. If protein loss is unchanged, steady protein abundance becomes three quarters of the original, a twenty-five percent decrease. A simplistic rule that more messenger RNA means more protein would fail. The correct result follows from the product kM and the removal rate of the protein pool.

A protein can also retain a memory of earlier RNA production through its finite lifetime. If transcription stops, RNA abundance begins to decrease, but the remaining transcripts may continue supporting translation until they are removed or become inactive. Existing proteins can persist much longer. Therefore a measurement at an early time may show a strong RNA reduction but only a modest reduction in protein. This does not necessarily indicate that translation has increased to compensate. Different lifetimes alone can produce the pattern.

A simple limiting model illustrates the protein response time. If the RNA pool changes instantaneously to a fixed new value Mnew and k and γ remain constant, the protein approaches its new steady value Pss,new=kMnew/γ according to P(t)=Pss,new+(P₀−Pss,new)exp(−γt). The model is directly analogous to the RNA step-response equation. It does not describe a general coupled response exactly, because the RNA pool itself ordinarily changes over time. If RNA dynamics are much faster than protein dynamics, the approximation can nevertheless be useful.

For a protein with γ=0.02 min⁻¹, the half-life is approximately 34.7 minutes. For another with γ=0.20 min⁻¹, the half-life is approximately 3.47 minutes. Under an instantaneous change in their synthesis input, the second protein approaches its new steady level more quickly. If both are measured ten minutes after the signal, they may show very different fractions of their eventual response even though their synthesis rates have already changed. The difference can reflect turnover rather than a different initial regulatory signal.

The effect of protein removal can also be examined by a controlled change in production. Suppose a protein pool has an initial amount of eight hundred and production stops while removal remains first order. After one half-life, four hundred remain; after three half-lives, one hundred remain. If production is not stopped, the abundance instead approaches a nonzero steady state. Just as with RNA, the appropriate decay expression depends on whether there is continuing input.

The steady-state expression is not defined when γ is exactly zero. In an ideal fixed cell with no protein removal and constant positive protein production, the amount would increase linearly with time rather than reach a finite steady state. In a growing cell population, division and growth may create an effective dilution even for chemically stable proteins. Similar considerations apply to an RNA pool with negligible loss. A finite steady abundance requires an opposing process or another constraint that balances the input.

It is also necessary to distinguish translation initiation from production of completed polypeptide. A messenger RNA associated with more ribosomes might indicate increased initiation, but ribosome accumulation could also result from slower elongation. The same observation can have different implications for completed protein production. A measurement of newly completed protein provides complementary evidence. The earlier translation chapters explain why ribosome occupancy and synthesis flux are not generally interchangeable; here this distinction prevents misidentification of a regulatory checkpoint.

For an illustrative comparison, two conditions have equal available RNA abundance and equal numbers of ribosomes associated with that RNA, but completed protein production is lower in the second condition. One possibility is that ribosomes take longer to finish or fail to complete translation more frequently. Another possibility is that a larger fraction of the detected ribosomes is not productively translating. The number of associated ribosomes is a snapshot, while completed production is a flux. A statement about translational control should specify which aspect the data actually establish.

Finally, the measured protein abundance is not always the biologically active abundance. Suppose P is the total amount of a protein and f is the fraction in a functional state. If each active molecule contributes the same activity in the specified assay, activity is proportional to fP. A shift from f=0.25 to f=0.75 triples measured activity without altering protein abundance. Tripling P at unchanged f could produce the same activity change. The distinction can be explored by measuring total protein, its molecular state and activity under controlled conditions. It prevents the conclusion that a signal necessarily changes expression simply because an enzyme assay changes.

The complete model thus connects four measurable variables—RNA synthesis, RNA lifetime, protein synthesis and protein lifetime—to a fifth variable, functional activity. In a real cell some of these variables depend on each other and on the environmental condition. In a well-specified examination problem, the simplified equations provide a powerful framework for identifying the direction and size of changes while keeping the molecular explanation explicit.

13. Predicting regulatory mutations without memorising a named operon

The relationship between a control element and a regulatory factor can be analysed by considering which molecular function a mutation removes. A promoter mutation may alter the basal capacity to initiate transcription. A regulatory-site mutation may prevent recognition by a factor while leaving basal transcription intact. A mutation in a regulator gene may prevent production of the regulator, alter its stability, impair its recognition of the target, impair its response to ligand or change its ability to act once bound. These molecular defects are not interchangeable. Predicting their effects requires identifying the function that the altered component normally supplies.

Begin with a hypothetical negative inducible model in which active repressor binds a regulatory site and reduces expression from ninety to six units. The inducer normally inactivates the repressor. A deletion that removes all repressor function should leave expression close to ninety in either condition. A mutation in the recognition site that prevents repressor binding, while preserving promoter function, should produce a similar expression pattern for the linked target. A mutation in the repressor that leaves it active despite inducer instead maintains expression near six. The three predictions follow from whether effective repression can be established under each condition.

The phenotype of persistent high expression is not sufficient to identify a cis or trans defect. Imagine that the original target is labelled A and a normal second target is labelled B. Both targets occupy the same regulatory environment and respond to the same diffusible protein. If the original cell lacks repressor, both A and B should remain relatively high without inducer. Supplying a functional copy of the repressor should restore repression of both compatible targets. If instead A has a site defect and the cell retains a functional repressor, B should respond normally while A remains high. Supplying more normal repressor does not repair A's altered recognition sequence in the ideal model.

This logic depends on distinguishing the two outputs. If A and B produce identical protein and only total enzyme activity is measured, the output from the constitutive copy may obscure normal regulation of the other copy. A distinguishable reporter or allele-specific measurement can reveal the local effect. This is why a two-copy problem should be solved for each linked gene or transcription unit before total activity is calculated. A local regulatory defect can make the total cellular activity high without making every target in the cell constitutive.

Consider an ideal quantitative example. Without inducer, each normal target contributes five units of output, and with inducer each contributes fifty. A mutation makes target A constitutive at fifty while B retains normal regulation. The total is fifty-five units without inducer and one hundred with inducer. If both targets were derepressed by loss of a shared repressor, total output would be one hundred in both conditions. Observing some residual induction in the first case is compatible with constitutive expression from one copy, because the other copy continues responding. The word constitutive applies to a specified target, not necessarily the sum.

A failure of complementation also requires caution. If a wild-type regulator is supplied but the abnormal output persists, a cis-site defect is one possible explanation. However, the mutant regulator might interfere with wild-type protein function, or another component required for normal regulation might be absent. An introduced gene may not be expressed at the necessary concentration or may not reach the appropriate compartment. A failure to rescue is therefore not conclusive unless the experimental conditions ensure that functional wild-type activity is actually supplied. A successful rescue under controlled conditions is more informative about sufficiency than a poorly controlled failure.

A mutation that makes a negative regulator insensitive to an inducer illustrates another complication. Supplying a normal regulator does not necessarily eliminate the effect of the mutant protein. If the mutant continues binding a target under inducing conditions, it may still reduce output even when the normal protein is inactivated. Whether this phenotype is dominant depends on the molecular interactions and relative amounts of the proteins. The later lactose-operon chapter will analyse this specific genetic logic using defined alleles. At this stage, the key point is to distinguish a nonfunctional protein from a protein that retains one activity while losing another.

Positive control can be analysed in the same way. A hypothetical gene produces eight units without active activator and eighty units when stimulation is established. A mutation destroying the activator's recognition of DNA prevents the increase. A mutation preserving recognition but preventing interaction with transcription machinery may have a similar output phenotype even though a binding assay gives a different result. A constitutively active activator can produce eighty units without inducer, while a ligand-insensitive inactive activator can prevent induction. Again, the loss or retention of particular molecular functions is what determines the prediction.

Now consider a mutation in a promoter that lowers both the unactivated and activated rates. If a target initially produces ten units without activator and one hundred with activator, a promoter mutation might lower these values to two and twenty while retaining a tenfold response. The target still responds to the regulator, but its absolute output is reduced. In contrast a mutation that specifically abolishes activation might produce ten in both conditions. A comparison of absolute levels and fold changes provides information that one measure alone cannot. A low-output mutant is not automatically a regulator-binding mutant.

Gene dosage can further complicate interpretation. Adding extra copies of a target can raise total output without changing the rate per copy. It can also change the availability of a limiting regulator if the added sites bind it. A comparison that normalises output per gene copy may be necessary to distinguish these effects. In an examination problem, the stated copy numbers and whether regulators are limiting should be noted explicitly. Otherwise a two-copy strain may appear to have altered control merely because it has more transcription templates.

The appropriate approach to a mutation problem is to specify the reference phenotype, identify the altered molecular function, predict the state of the regulator under each signal condition, evaluate the target's response and finally calculate the actual readout. If the observation disagrees with the prediction, identify which premise is unsupported. This strategy generalises to later operon, phage and eukaryotic examples and avoids the fragile practice of memorising phenotypes without understanding their causal basis.

14. Experimental evidence: separating regulatory hypotheses

An experiment establishes a regulatory mechanism most convincingly when alternative explanations make different predictions. Suppose an extracellular signal causes a large increase in the amount of a protein. One possible explanation is that the corresponding gene is transcribed faster. Another is that RNA becomes more stable. A third is that existing RNA is translated more efficiently. A fourth is that the protein is removed more slowly. These explanations are all compatible with the initial endpoint observation, yet they differ in the intermediate quantities that should change. The next experiment should distinguish those quantities rather than simply repeat the endpoint measurement.

A short measurement of newly synthesised RNA can estimate RNA production during the sampled interval, provided the readout is interpreted with attention to labelling, RNA removal and the molecular species detected. It is not identical to measuring total RNA. A decay experiment can estimate RNA lifetime if continuing production is prevented or accounted for and if the intervention does not substantially alter the decay process itself. Combining production and lifetime evidence with steady abundance allows a more complete evaluation of the RNA balance.

Imagine a signal causes steady RNA abundance to double. New RNA production remains unchanged, while the measured half-life also doubles under conditions consistent with first-order loss. This supports increased RNA stability as an explanation for the higher abundance. If production instead doubles and lifetime remains unchanged, the same final abundance supports increased production. If both production and half-life double, the simple model predicts a fourfold increase at steady state. A measured twofold increase in that case might indicate that steady state has not been reached, that one measurement represents a different RNA pool or that the simple model is inadequate. A numerical prediction can reveal inconsistencies rather than merely supply a mechanism label.

A reporter assay provides a different way to test regulation. In a transcriptional reporter, a regulatory DNA region is linked to a measurable indicator gene through a defined downstream context. A change in indicator output when the sequence is altered can provide evidence that the region influences expression in the construct. However, the reporter signal also depends on RNA stability, translation, protein maturation and removal. A persistent fluorescent signal after the original regulatory event has ceased may reflect the lifetime of the reporter rather than ongoing transcription. The actual measured variable must be specified.

A translational reporter may retain native RNA regions that influence the use of an RNA template. For example, two constructs can share the same promoter, but one retains a particular untranslated region from the gene of interest. If a signal leaves the RNA amount of both constructs unchanged but reduces newly produced reporter protein only from the construct retaining the native region, the result supports a regulatory effect involving that RNA region under suitable controls. If the experiment measured only accumulated protein, a difference in turnover could also be possible. The construct design and measurement together determine the strength of the inference.

A reporter may differ from its native gene in copy number, chromatin context, surrounding DNA sequences or RNA features. A sequence that is necessary in one construct may not be sufficient in another. For instance, a regulatory fragment can support signal responsiveness when combined with a compatible promoter but fail in an altered configuration. This need not mean the original sequence was irrelevant. Regulatory effects can depend on spacing, orientation, nearby factors and the functional promoter. An experiment using an isolated reporter establishes what happens in that construct; extending the result to the endogenous gene requires appropriate additional evidence.

A binding experiment addresses a different question: can a regulator recognise the relevant DNA or RNA under the defined conditions? If a purified regulator binds the normal sequence but not a mutant sequence, that result supports specific recognition. If mutation of the same sequence changes output in cells, the two findings reinforce a proposed relationship. However, binding in an isolated system does not prove that the site is accessible or occupied in the native cell, and cellular expression changes alone do not prove direct binding by a particular factor. Mechanistic confidence increases when molecular interaction, perturbation and output evidence agree.

Consider a hypothetical activator mutation that leaves its binding to the target unchanged in a controlled assay. The target reporter nevertheless loses stimulation. This result suggests that recognition alone is not sufficient and that another function of the activator may be disrupted. A test of its interaction with an appropriate transcription component could discriminate this explanation from a defect in the cellular availability of the factor. The conclusion should not be that all binding measurements are uninformative; rather, each type of measurement addresses a separate step in the proposed causal chain.

A time-course experiment can add information about sequence and causation. Suppose a signal changes active regulator occupancy before a rise in new RNA production, followed later by a rise in total RNA and protein abundance. This order is consistent with a causal path from regulator to transcription to accumulated RNA to protein. Temporal order alone does not prove that each step causes the next, because a common upstream signal might affect more than one process independently. A perturbation that specifically prevents the early regulator change and also prevents the later expression response strengthens the causal model.

A rapidly changing protein activity with no change in total abundance may instead suggest regulation of an existing protein. If new RNA and protein synthesis are unchanged during the response, the evidence does not support a primary transcriptional increase. The absence of a detected change in one assay must still be interpreted in light of its sensitivity and time window. A transient earlier transcriptional event may be missed by a later sample, and a local change may be concealed by a bulk measurement. Controls and sampling times are part of the biological interpretation.

The problem of population averaging is particularly important when interpreting smooth induction curves. Imagine one culture in which every cell produces approximately fifty reporter units, and a second culture in which half the cells produce one hundred units and half produce almost none. Both have a mean near fifty, but the distributions differ. A population average cannot distinguish graded changes within cells from changes in the fraction of cells occupying different expression states. The simple binding model explains one possible route to a graded mean, not the only possible route. If the question supplies single-cell evidence, use it rather than assuming uniformity.

Normalisation can change the apparent response. Suppose a target RNA doubles from ten to twenty units per cell, while a reference RNA also doubles from fifty to one hundred. The target-to-reference ratio remains 0.20. A relative measurement using only that reference would suggest no change, although the target abundance per cell increased. Conversely a constant target can appear elevated if the reference decreases. A reference is useful only if its behaviour is appropriate for the comparison or is independently accounted for. The correct denominator is dictated by the biological question.

When reading a regulatory experiment, identify whether the measured variable is production, abundance, occupancy, localisation or activity. Then establish whether the samples represent steady states or transients, whether the appropriate controls are present and whether a perturbation directly changes the proposed component. These questions are not laboratory procedural details; they are the logical foundation for interpreting the types of data encountered in NET and SET problems.

15. A shared framework across bacteria, phages, viruses and eukaryotes

Bacteria often encounter changing conditions over relatively short periods. A new nutrient may become available, oxygen availability may change, or an environmental stress may alter the value of particular cellular functions. Gene regulation can adjust the supply of transporters, enzymes and other proteins to match these conditions. The ability to reduce unnecessary production and raise production when a pathway becomes useful illustrates the resource-allocation principle introduced earlier. However, the response is not necessarily confined to a single gene or a single regulatory level.

A bacterial operon can place multiple linked coding regions within a shared transcription unit. One control region can thereby influence the production of a common RNA, allowing coordinated supply of products contributing to a related function. The shared RNA does not require equal protein abundance for every encoded product. Different coding regions can have different effective translation rates, and their proteins may have different lifetimes. The quantitative RNA-protein framework explains how equal transcriptional input to linked coding regions can yield unequal protein outputs. This distinction is important when interpreting an experiment that measures one protein as a representative of the whole operon.

A regulon is a collection of genes or transcription units under a common regulatory influence. Its members need not be contiguous. A single diffusible factor can influence several operons and independently transcribed genes, provided compatible regulatory relationships are established. Thus an operon describes linked transcriptional architecture, whereas a regulon describes coordinated regulation. Both can coexist: a local repressor may govern one operon, while a wider regulatory factor influences multiple targets in response to the cell's broader physiological state. The later carbon-source node will illustrate the difference between local and broader controls.

The idea of resource conservation helps explain the contrasting logic of catabolic and biosynthetic systems, although it is not an absolute law. A catabolic pathway may be induced when its substrate or an informative derivative becomes available, so the cell can produce the enzymes needed to exploit it. A biosynthetic pathway may be repressed when its end product is abundant, avoiding excessive production of the required enzymes. The regulatory signals can be indirect, and additional mechanisms can influence the outcome. What matters is the relationship between the signal and the required cellular capacity.

Temperate bacteriophages illustrate another role of regulation. The genome of a phage can support alternative expression programmes. In the lambda system, regulatory relationships govern the establishment or maintenance of lysogeny and the transition to productive lytic development under appropriate conditions. The importance of this example is that gene control can select between coordinated programmes rather than simply change the amount of one metabolic enzyme. Regulatory proteins can influence their own expression and that of other factors, allowing feedback to contribute to the stability or alteration of a state. The detailed CI and Cro switch belongs to its dedicated chapter.

Feedback in a regulatory programme can be understood without assuming the details of a particular phage. A factor that promotes its own production can help sustain an expression state, provided the overall conditions support that positive feedback. A factor that represses its own production can oppose changes in its abundance. Whether a particular circuit has stable alternative states depends on the strengths and kinetics of its interactions, not just the presence of one positive-feedback arrow. The conceptual point is that the products of regulated genes may themselves participate in regulation, creating connections across time.

Viruses use the expression machinery available in infected cells to varying degrees. Their genome type and encoded components determine how translatable RNA is produced. Positive-sense RNA can serve as a translation template under suitable conditions; other viral genomes must provide a route to an appropriate RNA. Some viral systems organise expression temporally so that earlier products contribute to processes needed for later events, and later products contribute to structural assembly or other late functions. This is a broad comparison, not a claim that all viruses have the same early-late scheme. Specific genome-dependent mechanisms will be taught in the viral-regulation node.

In a nucleated eukaryotic cell, transcription occurs in the nucleus and the availability of mature RNA in the cytoplasm introduces opportunities for further control. Chromatin organisation can influence access to regulatory sequences, and combinations of factors can influence whether a target is expressed in a particular cell type. Processing and localisation of RNA can alter the identities or availability of translation templates. The same external signal can produce different responses in two cell types because the required regulatory factors or DNA accessibility differ. A signal is therefore not a complete instruction without a receptive cellular context.

Stable cell identity illustrates how regulation can persist across time. A cell may retain a broadly similar genome while maintaining an expression programme suited to its function. Continued regulatory factor activity, chromatin state and other mechanisms can contribute to that persistence. A stable expression difference need not imply a mutation in the coding sequence. Conversely not every persistent expression change is necessarily an example of a heritable epigenetic state. The later chromatin and epigenetic chapters will distinguish these mechanisms and their evidence.

A comparative question can often be solved by recognising which common principle applies. A bacterial operon problem may emphasise cis-trans genetic reasoning; a phage problem may emphasise feedback and alternative regulatory states; a viral problem may emphasise temporal expression and RNA availability; a eukaryotic problem may emphasise combinations of regulatory factors and RNA processing. In all cases, the target, regulatory interaction, signal and measured output must be identified. The diversity of organisms does not invalidate the underlying logic, but their distinct molecular architectures determine how that logic is realised.

The final lesson is to avoid universal claims based on one familiar model. Bacteria use translational and RNA-mediated control as well as transcriptional control. Eukaryotes can respond rapidly through transcription as well as through regulation of existing RNA and proteins. Viruses are not uniform in their expression strategies. The four ideal regulatory architectures and the RNA-protein balance model are tools for reasoning about clearly specified relationships, not replacements for the molecular evidence of each system.

16. Integrating environmental response, molecular states and output

Consider a hypothetical bacterium with a gene needed to use an alternative nutrient. Its basal transcription in an unrepressed state is twenty RNA molecules per minute. An active repressor reduces the rate to two, and an active activator increases the unrepressed rate to eighty. Suppose repression dominates activation in the ideal model. A nutrient signal lowers repressor activity, while a second physiological signal promotes activation. In the absence of both signals the target produces two RNA molecules per minute; with the nutrient signal alone it produces twenty; with both signals it produces eighty. The model distinguishes relief of repression from activation above the basal level.

If the RNA removal constant is 0.20 min⁻¹ in all three states, the corresponding predicted steady abundances are ten, one hundred and four hundred RNA molecules. Suppose each available RNA produces one protein per minute and the protein removal constant is 0.05 min⁻¹. The respective steady protein amounts are two hundred, two thousand and eight thousand. These predictions connect the signal conditions to regulatory states, transcriptional flux, RNA accumulation and protein accumulation. The multiplication of effects is a consequence of the assumed kinetic parameters, not a general assertion that every induction yields this numerical amplification.

Now imagine that the nutrient signal also reduces RNA stability, raising δ from 0.20 to 0.40 min⁻¹ in the nutrient-present conditions. The transcriptional rates remain the same, but the corresponding steady RNA abundances with nutrient alone and both signals fall to fifty and two hundred. Protein abundance at steady state becomes one thousand and four thousand. A student who calculated only promoter output would overestimate the final protein pool. A student who saw the smaller RNA abundance and inferred weaker transcription would also be mistaken. The hypothetical system deliberately separates changes at distinct levels.

Finally suppose an experiment is sampled shortly after the two signals are applied. Regulator occupancy and nascent RNA production have already changed, but the RNA and protein pools have not reached steady state. Calculating the full steady output at this early time would be inappropriate. The RNA follows the step-response equation under constant new parameters, and the protein follows the changing RNA input. A delayed protein response may reflect these molecular lifetimes rather than failure of the regulatory system. The same causal sequence must be evaluated differently depending on the time at which data are collected.

When moving to the lactose operon, retain the questions illustrated by this example. Identify the physiological effector, the state of the repressor, the linked regulatory sequence, the basal transcriptional capacity and any other inputs required for maximal expression. Predict the phenotype of a molecular lesion before consulting a memorised mutation table. Keep RNA production distinct from steady RNA and protein output. The detailed operon architecture provides an experimentally grounded instance of these principles, rather than an unrelated list of components.

Part B conceptual practice

Original examination-style questions. Numerical data are hypothetical teaching examples, not previous-year questions.

1. Which statement distinguishes possessing a gene from expressing it?

  1. Possession guarantees high protein abundance.
  2. A retained gene may have low functional output.
  3. Only protein-coding genes are expressed.
  4. Expression always changes DNA sequence.
Answer and explanation

B. A retained gene may have low functional output. — A gene can remain in the genome without high output. Possession does not guarantee protein abundance; functional RNA genes are expressed too; changing production need not alter DNA.

2. What makes a gene constitutive in a defined experiment?

  1. Its expression is relatively independent of the conditions tested.
  2. Its promoter is incapable of regulation under any condition.
  3. It is never transcribed.
  4. It has no transcription machinery.
Answer and explanation

A. Its expression is relatively independent of the conditions tested. — Constitutive describes approximately condition-independent output in the specified comparison. It does not imply absence of all regulation, transcription or machinery.

3. Which quantity is a production flux?

  1. RNA molecules present at sampling.
  2. Total protein molecules present.
  3. Fraction of sites occupied.
  4. New RNA molecules made per minute.
Answer and explanation

D. New RNA molecules made per minute. — A flux measures change or production per unit time. RNA and protein abundance are stocks; occupancy is a dimensionless fraction.

4. Which is a cis-regulatory element in a classical bacterial system?

  1. A diffusible repressor protein.
  2. An inducer molecule.
  3. An operator DNA sequence controlling a linked target.
  4. A free ribosomal protein.
Answer and explanation

C. An operator DNA sequence controlling a linked target. — Cis refers to the linked sequence. The repressor is a trans product; inducer is a signal; ribosomal protein is not the specified local DNA element.

5. A trans-acting factor is defined primarily by what property?

  1. It must be encoded on the opposite DNA strand.
  2. It can act on compatible targets independently of linkage of its encoding gene.
  3. It must be a small metabolite.
  4. It can act only on the DNA that encodes it.
Answer and explanation

B. It can act on compatible targets independently of linkage of its encoding gene. — Trans refers to the mobility and independent target action of a product. Strand, chemical class and strict physical linkage do not define it.

6. An active negative regulator directly does what?

  1. Reduces the output of its specified target.
  2. Always reduces the abundance of its own gene product.
  3. Always binds a nutrient.
  4. Necessarily makes expression exactly zero.
Answer and explanation

A. Reduces the output of its specified target. — The sign describes target inhibition. Autoregulation and ligand recognition are optional properties, and repression can be incomplete.

7. A signal induces a target by disabling a repressor. The direct regulator is:

  1. Positive.
  2. Neither, because the target is induced.
  3. Positive only when ligand is absent.
  4. Negative.
Answer and explanation

D. Negative. — The active repressor lowers target output. Its inactivation increases expression, so induction and negative control coexist; regulator sign is not changed by the signal.

8. In a simple negative repressible system, a corepressor:

  1. Always inhibits binding of a repressor to DNA.
  2. Directly translates mRNA.
  3. Promotes the repressive state of the regulator.
  4. Eliminates the target DNA.
Answer and explanation

C. Promotes the repressive state of the regulator. — A corepressor promotes repression. Inhibition of repression describes a negative inducible arrangement; translation and DNA elimination are not required.

9. Why can total regulator abundance stay constant while gene output changes?

  1. Binding always destroys the target.
  2. The fraction in a functionally active state can change.
  3. RNA polymerase must be newly encoded.
  4. Gene copy number must increase.
Answer and explanation

B. The fraction in a functionally active state can change. — Allosteric or other state changes can alter active regulator at fixed total amount. The other options introduce unnecessary or incorrect mechanisms.

10. The physiological inducer of the classical lac system is:

  1. Allolactose.
  2. Tryptophan.
  3. ATP as a universal inducer.
  4. The operator sequence.
Answer and explanation

A. Allolactose. — Allolactose is the intracellular inducer. Tryptophan is relevant to another system, ATP has broad energetic roles, and the operator is a DNA site.

11. What does a smaller Kd imply at the same free regulator concentration in the one-site model?

  1. Lower fractional occupancy.
  2. Zero transcription in every system.
  3. A larger number of DNA copies.
  4. Greater fractional occupancy.
Answer and explanation

D. Greater fractional occupancy. — Lower Kd corresponds to stronger binding and greater occupancy. Output depends on the regulatory sign; DNA copy number is not inferred from affinity.

12. Which observation is sufficient to classify a signal as inducing a target in a defined comparison?

  1. A factor binds DNA regardless of output.
  2. The target is located near the origin of replication.
  3. The relevant measured expression output rises after the signal.
  4. Protein abundance is exactly zero in both conditions.
Answer and explanation

C. The relevant measured expression output rises after the signal. — Induction concerns the direction of the specified output response. Binding and location alone do not establish induction; unchanged zero output does not show an increase.

13. A change in the ratio of mature splice isoforms at fixed precursor production illustrates:

  1. Increased DNA replication.
  2. Post-transcriptional regulation.
  3. Obligatory translational repression.
  4. A change in amino acid sequence of the regulator by mutation.
Answer and explanation

B. Post-transcriptional regulation. — Processing changes RNA identities. It does not require replication, a change in translation, or a mutation in the regulator.

14. Which measurement most directly concerns translation output?

  1. Newly completed polypeptides per unit time.
  2. Total genomic DNA.
  3. Steady RNA abundance alone.
  4. Total protein abundance alone.
Answer and explanation

A. Newly completed polypeptides per unit time. — Completed protein production is a translation flux. DNA and RNA amounts and accumulated protein do not measure the flux by themselves.

15. A stable RNA pool can have ongoing turnover because:

  1. All RNA molecules must be permanently stable.
  2. Translation always stops at steady state.
  3. The promoter cannot initiate at steady state.
  4. Production can exactly balance removal.
Answer and explanation

D. Production can exactly balance removal. — Steady state is a dynamic balance; neither permanent stability nor cessation of production or translation follows.

16. An operon is best distinguished from a regulon by:

  1. Always having only one coding region.
  2. Always being eukaryotic.
  3. Linked transcriptional organisation rather than merely shared control.
  4. Never producing a polycistronic RNA.
Answer and explanation

C. Linked transcriptional organisation rather than merely shared control. — Operons can coordinate linked coding regions in a transcription unit; regulons may contain dispersed targets. Operons are common in bacteria and can produce polycistronic RNAs.

17. Which is the strongest reason that the same hormone may induce a gene in one cell type but not another?

  1. All genes must have different coding sequences in every cell.
  2. The required regulatory context can differ.
  3. Hormones always directly bind DNA.
  4. Translation is absent from every unresponsive cell.
Answer and explanation

B. The required regulatory context can differ. — Regulatory factors and accessibility may differ between cells. Genome identity need not change, direct DNA binding by the signal is not required, and unresponsiveness does not establish global absence of translation.

18. A repressor-site mutation prevents recognition but preserves promoter activity. Its primary expected effect is:

  1. Loss of repression of the linked target.
  2. Loss of every repressor in the cell.
  3. Loss of all ribosomes.
  4. Obligatory loss of transcription initiation.
Answer and explanation

A. Loss of repression of the linked target. — The defect is local and preserves promoter capacity. It does not remove the shared factor or ribosomes; the target can still initiate.

19. Why is a transcriptional reporter's fluorescence not a pure instantaneous transcription-rate measurement?

  1. Fluorescence always measures genomic DNA.
  2. A reporter cannot be regulated.
  3. Reporter protein cannot persist.
  4. It also depends on reporter RNA, translation, maturation and turnover.
Answer and explanation

D. It also depends on reporter RNA, translation, maturation and turnover. — Reporter abundance and activity integrate several processes. The other options incorrectly identify the readout or deny normal reporter behaviour.

20. Which historical inference was supported by the PaJaMo genetic crosses?

  1. Every gene is an operon.
  2. DNA replication is semiconservative.
  3. A genetically specified diffusible repressive activity can control enzyme synthesis.
  4. The exact atomic structure of a repressor was determined.
Answer and explanation

C. A genetically specified diffusible repressive activity can control enzyme synthesis. — Transient expression and subsequent repression supported a diffusible regulatory substance. The study did not establish universal operons, replication mechanism or atomic structure.

Part C analytical practice

Original examination-style questions. Numerical data are hypothetical teaching examples, not previous-year questions.

21. A regulator has Kd = 15 nM and free active concentration 45 nM. What is occupancy?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.75
  3. 3
  4. 0.33
Answer and explanation

B. 0.75 — θ=45/(15+45)=0.75. 0.25 is the unoccupied fraction, 3 is R/Kd rather than a fraction, and 0.33 inverts the concentration ratio.

22. Unbound transcription is 60 units/min and repressor-bound transcription is 8. Occupancy is 0.50. What is mean output?

  1. 34
  2. 30
  3. 8
  4. 68
Answer and explanation

A. 34 — Equal weighting gives (60+8)/2=34. 30 assumes complete repression, 8 assumes full occupancy, and 68 adds both rates without weighting.

23. For an activator, unbound rate is 12 and bound rate is 92. θ rises from 0.25 to 0.75. What is the output increase?

  1. 20 units/min
  2. 80 units/min
  3. 60 units/min
  4. 40 units/min
Answer and explanation

D. 40 units/min — The change is Δθ×(92−12)=0.5×80=40. 20 and 60 are separate contributions of the activation increment at the two occupancies; 80 is the full bound-versus-free difference.

24. Free active repressor is 12 nM and Kd=4 nM. A mutation increases Kd to 36 nM. What are the old and new occupancies?

  1. 0.25 and 0.75
  2. 0.75 and 0.75
  3. 0.75 and 0.25
  4. 3 and 0.33
Answer and explanation

C. 0.75 and 0.25 — 12/(4+12)=0.75 and 12/(36+12)=0.25. The reverse confuses affinity direction; unchanged ignores the mutation; R/Kd is not occupancy.

25. RNA production is 24 molecules/min and δ=0.30 min⁻¹. What is steady RNA abundance?

  1. 7.2
  2. 80
  3. 24
  4. 0.0125
Answer and explanation

B. 80 — Mss=s/δ=80 molecules. 7.2 multiplies rather than divides, 24 is the production rate, and 0.0125 is its reciprocal with incompatible units.

26. An RNA declines from 160 to 20 molecules in 9 min after production stops. What is the half-life?

  1. 3 min
  2. 9 min
  3. 4.5 min
  4. 1 min
Answer and explanation

A. 3 min — 160→80→40→20 is three halvings in nine minutes, hence three minutes. Nine assumes one halving; 4.5 assumes two; one assumes nine.

27. RNA is 30 at its initial steady state; a signal raises the new steady amount to 150 with unchanged loss. After two half-lives, what is M?

  1. 37.5
  2. 75
  3. 150
  4. 120
Answer and explanation

D. 120 — The initial difference is 120; one quarter remains, so 150−30=120. 37.5 is a quarter of the final amount, 75 halves the final amount, and 150 ignores the transient.

28. A gene goes from 100 to a new steady RNA level of 20. After one RNA half-life, what is the abundance?

  1. 50
  2. 10
  3. 60
  4. 20
Answer and explanation

C. 60 — Half the initial difference of 80 remains above the new level: 20+40=60. 50 assumes production stops, 10 halves the new steady level, and 20 assumes instantaneous equilibration.

29. At steady state, RNA production rises 2-fold while RNA loss constant rises 4-fold. What is the RNA fold change?

  1. 2
  2. 0.5
  3. 8
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

B. 0.5 — Mss is proportional to s/δ: 2/4=0.5. Other choices ignore the denominator or multiply changes that oppose one another.

30. RNA abundance is 80, translation rate is 3 proteins/RNA/min, and protein loss is 0.12 min⁻¹. What is Pss?

  1. 2000
  2. 240
  3. 28.8
  4. 666.7
Answer and explanation

A. 2000 — Protein production is 240/min; divide by 0.12 to obtain 2000. 240 is flux, 28.8 multiplies by loss, and 666.7 ignores translation rate.

31. At steady state, s triples, δ halves, k halves and γ doubles. What is the protein fold change?

  1. 6
  2. 3
  3. 0.75
  4. 1.5
Answer and explanation

D. 1.5 — Pss ratio = 3×0.5/(0.5×2)=1.5. Six describes RNA alone; three ignores protein removal; 0.75 mishandles RNA stability.

32. Protein production is unchanged; protein abundance halves at steady state. Which change alone could explain it?

  1. A halving of protein removal.
  2. A doubling of RNA with unchanged translation per RNA.
  3. A doubling of the effective protein removal constant.
  4. A halving of RNA with unchanged translation per RNA.
Answer and explanation

C. A doubling of the effective protein removal constant. — With fixed production, Pss=production/γ; doubled γ halves abundance. Lower γ raises abundance, and the RNA alternatives change production if translation per RNA is fixed.

33. A wild-type target produces 4 units without signal and 80 units with signal. Deleting the sole regulator gives 4 in both states. Which architecture fits?

  1. Negative inducible.
  2. Positive inducible.
  3. Positive repressible.
  4. Negative repressible.
Answer and explanation

B. Positive inducible. — Signal increases output, so the response is inducible. Removing the regulator removes stimulation, so control is positive. Negative inducible predicts high after deletion, and repressible predicts a decreasing wild-type response.

34. Wild type produces 90 without signal and 9 with signal; regulator deletion gives 90 in both. Which model is consistent?

  1. Negative repressible.
  2. Positive inducible.
  3. Negative inducible.
  4. Positive repressible.
Answer and explanation

A. Negative repressible. — Signal decreases output; loss of factor prevents repression. The inducible alternatives have wrong signal direction; positive repressible predicts low after activator deletion.

35. A negative inducible system has two distinguishable targets A and B. A remains high without inducer, B is low. Extra wild-type repressor affects B but not A. Which lesion best fits the ideal model?

  1. Loss of the shared repressor.
  2. An inactive promoter on A.
  3. Loss of inducer uptake alone.
  4. A cis recognition-site defect on A.
Answer and explanation

D. A cis recognition-site defect on A. — Selective failure of repression supports a linked site defect. Loss of shared repressor would affect both; inactive promoter gives low A; uptake failure would retain repression in both.

36. A wild-type activator binds its site and stimulates output. A mutant binds equally in a controlled assay but loses stimulation. What is the most direct inference?

  1. The mutant must have lost all DNA affinity.
  2. The promoter must be deleted.
  3. Recognition is retained but some function needed for activation may be impaired.
  4. The mutant is proven to be a repressor.
Answer and explanation

C. Recognition is retained but some function needed for activation may be impaired. — Binding evidence contradicts loss of all affinity. A missing activation interaction is consistent; promoter deletion and a sign reversal are not established.

37. A transcription unit initiates 120 times/min. Completion falls from 75% to 25% with unchanged initiation. What are completed RNA fluxes?

  1. 120 and 120/min
  2. 90 and 30/min
  3. 30 and 90/min
  4. 75 and 25/min
Answer and explanation

B. 90 and 30/min — Multiply initiation by completion: 120×0.75=90 and 120×0.25=30. The alternatives ignore completion, reverse the states, or confuse percentages with counts.

38. Two reporter constructs share a promoter. Only the construct retaining a native UTR shows reduced newly completed protein with unchanged available RNA. What is supported under matched controls?

  1. A UTR-dependent effect on translation.
  2. A mandatory reduction in both promoters' transcription.
  3. Deletion of the shared promoter.
  4. A change in protein abundance alone proves increased degradation.
Answer and explanation

A. A UTR-dependent effect on translation. — The selective reduction in production per available RNA implicates the retained region. Shared promoter changes should affect both; deletion contradicts RNA availability; degradation is not the measured new-production flux.

39. Target RNA changes from 12 to 24 per cell and the reference from 40 to 80. What happens to target/reference?

  1. It doubles.
  2. It halves.
  3. It becomes 0.60.
  4. It stays 0.30.
Answer and explanation

D. It stays 0.30. — Both ratios equal 0.30. Relative normalisation conceals the target's absolute doubling. The other choices treat the reference as invariant or miscalculate the ratio.

40. An ideal two-input promoter has output 2 with active repressor regardless of activator, 10 without either active factor, and 100 with activator alone. Signal A activates activator; signal B disables repressor. Which gives maximal output?

  1. A only.
  2. B only.
  3. Both A and B.
  4. Neither signal.
Answer and explanation

C. Both A and B. — A alone is masked by repression (2); B alone gives basal output (10); neither gives 2; both produce the activated unrepressed state (100).

  1. 1-c, 2-a, 3-d, 4-b.
  2. 1-b, 2-d, 3-a, 4-c.
  3. 1-a, 2-b, 3-c, 4-d.
  4. 1-d, 2-c, 3-b, 4-a.
Answer and explanation

C. 1-a, 2-b, 3-c, 4-d. — The rate is a flux, δ is an inverse-time constant, M is an amount per cell, and θ is a fraction. Other assignments give inconsistent units.

49. A target RNA doubles at steady state, new RNA production is unchanged, and protein abundance is unchanged while protein loss is fixed. Assuming all RNA remains available, what pair of changes can explain the data?

  1. RNA loss doubles and translation doubles.
  2. RNA loss constant halves and translation rate per RNA halves.
  3. Transcription doubles and protein loss doubles.
  4. RNA loss halves and translation doubles.
Answer and explanation

B. RNA loss constant halves and translation rate per RNA halves. — Fixed s and doubled M imply half δ. Fixed P and γ imply constant kM, so k halves. The other pairs conflict with measured production, turnover or protein abundance.

50. A signal gives a steep population-average induction curve. Which conclusion is justified without further evidence?

  1. The measured mean changes steeply; its molecular origin and single-cell distribution require more evidence.
  2. Exactly four regulator molecules bind cooperatively.
  3. Every cell must switch at the same concentration.
  4. The target must be under positive control.
Answer and explanation

A. The measured mean changes steeply; its molecular origin and single-cell distribution require more evidence. — Steepness alone does not determine stoichiometry, the single-cell distribution or regulatory sign. Cooperative interactions, coupled steps or population mixtures may produce such a curve.

Examination traps

  • Constitutive is context-dependent, not necessarily unregulated forever.
  • Induction describes the output direction, not the sign of the regulator.
  • Repression does not require absolutely zero RNA.
  • An active factor may be present without occupying its site.
  • A ligand may change active fraction without changing total protein.
  • Allolactose is the physiological inducer in the classical lac system.
  • A cis lesion may affect several linked genes, not necessarily only one coding region.
  • Failure to complement does not by itself prove a cis lesion.
  • Binding is not equivalent to activation.
  • Steady RNA is not transcription rate.
  • A stable pool can have rapid replacement flux.
  • Ribosome occupancy is not automatically completed protein production.
  • A nonzero new steady state requires a step-response formula, not decay to zero.
  • A reporter includes downstream RNA and protein kinetics.
  • A Hill exponent is not automatically molecular stoichiometry.

Active recall and mastery checklist

Why can two cells with similar inherited genomes express different proteins?

Different regulatory states, RNA processing or translation and turnover can alter use of shared information.

What are the two independent axes of the four ideal regulatory models?

The regulator's direct sign and the direction of the signal-induced output change.

How does a cis recognition-site defect differ from loss of a trans regulator?

A local site affects a linked target; loss of a shared factor can affect compatible targets across DNA copies.

What does Kd represent in the single-site model?

The dissociation constant; free active concentration equal to Kd gives half occupancy.

How can a repressor be disabled without a decline in total abundance?

Ligand binding can alter its active conformational fraction or interaction competence.

Why can increased transcription coexist with reduced RNA abundance?

Removal may increase by a larger factor than production.

What is the difference between decay after stopping synthesis and a step in continuing synthesis?

Decay approaches zero in the ideal no-input model; a step approaches a generally nonzero new steady state.

What determines steady protein abundance in the simple model?

RNA production, RNA loss, translation per available RNA and protein loss.

How can equal RNA levels conceal different regulatory states?

Different production and removal rates can balance at the same abundance.

Why does an operon not guarantee equal protein abundance from all coding regions?

Translation rates and protein lifetimes may differ despite shared transcription.

Mastery checklist

  • Classify all four architectures from wild-type and mutant data.
  • Explain cis and trans using two distinguishable targets.
  • Derive single-site occupancy and calculate state-weighted transcription.
  • Derive steady RNA, decay and step-response equations.
  • Separate translation from protein loss using flux and lifetime evidence.
  • Interpret reporter and nutrient-response data without assuming a named operon.

Formula sheet

Single-site equilibrium: θ = R/(Kd+R), with free active R in the same concentration units as Kd. Mean state-weighted transcription: T=(1−θ)u+θb.

RNA production and first-order loss: dM/dt=s−δM; Mss=s/δ; t½=ln(2)/δ; M=M₀exp(−δt) after production stops; M=Mss,new+(M₀−Mss,new)exp(−δt) after a constant production step.

Protein: dP/dt=kM−γP; Pss=ks/(δγ) when both pools have reached steady state. All rate constants must use the same time unit.
