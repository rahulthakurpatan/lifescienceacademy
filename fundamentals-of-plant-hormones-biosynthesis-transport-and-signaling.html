The problem of coordination in a growing plant

A germinating seed, a newly expanding leaf and a mature root all possess the genetic instructions required for plant life, yet they perform different tasks. In a germinating seed, the immediate problem is to establish growth before reserves are exhausted. In a mature leaf, water loss must be balanced against the carbon gained through open stomata. In a root, the direction of growth determines whether the expanding tip reaches water and mineral resources. Changes in these activities must occur at the appropriate place and time. The plant cannot rely on a fixed program that makes every cell perform the same activity whenever a particular environmental condition changes.

Consider a seedling whose shoot emerges from the soil. Its buried portion elongates rapidly, but once the shoot reaches light, its developmental program changes. The young leaves expand and acquire photosynthetic capacity; stem elongation becomes differently regulated. The incoming light is information about the environment. A photoreceptor is one of the molecular devices that detects this information. A hormone can then help translate the detection into changes in cell division, elongation or gene expression. It would be inaccurate to say that the light itself is the hormone, or that all effects of the light must pass through one hormone. The terms signal, receptor and response describe different positions within a causal explanation.

A plant hormone is an endogenous chemical messenger that influences physiological or developmental processes by acting on a responsive biological system, usually at low concentrations. Endogenous means produced within the organism, rather than necessarily supplied by the surrounding environment. A plant may synthesize the same hormone in multiple tissues; some of it may act near its site of production while some moves elsewhere. There is no requirement for a specialized endocrine gland or for transport through a circulatory system equivalent to animal blood. In plants, the ability of cells to make and respond to signals is distributed throughout the organism.

A chemical compound does not become a hormone merely because it changes growth when a large amount is supplied in a laboratory. An acid at a damaging concentration can stop root elongation without behaving as a normal hormone. Evidence for hormonal function is stronger if the compound is naturally produced, if its endogenous availability can be regulated, if its effect is observed in a physiologically relevant concentration range, and if perception or response depends on identifiable biological components. These criteria are complementary rather than a single rigid definition. Some compounds have several biological roles, and the evidence for a new signal may accumulate in stages.

The term plant growth regulator is wider than the term naturally occurring plant hormone. A synthetic chemical may mimic a natural hormone or interfere with a hormone pathway without itself being a normally synthesized plant hormone. For example, naphthaleneacetic acid is a synthetic auxin used to study and influence growth. It can activate parts of auxin response, but its transport and metabolism need not match those of indole-3-acetic acid, the major natural auxin in many plants. A result obtained with one compound therefore cannot automatically be assigned to every member of the same functional class.

Distinguishing hormones from nutrients is particularly useful. Nitrate provides nitrogen for amino acids, nucleotides and other metabolites; it can also supply information about nutritional status. A cytokinin may convey part of the response to nitrogen availability without furnishing the nitrogen needed to construct a new leaf. Supplying cytokinin to a severely nitrogen-starved plant cannot replace all the nitrogen atoms missing from its metabolism. The regulatory molecule may change how resources are acquired or distributed, but the resources themselves remain necessary. Some nutrients and metabolites also have signaling functions, so the distinction concerns their role in a particular mechanism rather than a claim that all metabolic compounds are chemically incapable of signaling.

A second useful distinction is between a hormone and an environmental stimulus. Soil drying changes the water status of a plant. Changes in water relations can trigger abscisic acid production and alter the responsiveness of cells. Abscisic acid then influences processes such as stomatal closure. Soil water availability is an external variable; the hormone is an internally regulated chemical component of the response. Removing the environmental stimulus and removing the hormone are different experimental interventions. A drought response may contain both hormone-dependent components and other components controlled by hydraulic or electrical changes.

Small quantities of a hormone can have substantial effects because molecular recognition is coupled to amplification. One activated receptor may influence the activities of multiple downstream molecules. Changes in transcription can lead to many copies of a messenger RNA, and each RNA can be translated more than once. Enzymes produced or activated by signaling may process many substrate molecules. Such amplification does not mean that the hormone creates energy or matter. Energy and building materials still come from cellular metabolism. The signal changes the organization and timing of biochemical work.

Hormonal control should not be pictured as nine independent switches, each connected to exactly one visible trait. Auxin can contribute to growth, vascular development and responses to directional stimuli. Cytokinins can influence division, nutrient signaling and organ development. Abscisic acid can regulate seed dormancy and water balance. These effects overlap and depend on developmental stage. A seed and a guard cell use different output machinery even if both perceive abscisic acid. The central question of the chapter is therefore how information is generated, moved, interpreted and terminated, not simply which name belongs beside a particular plant response.

The distinction between regulation and execution also prevents incorrect causal shortcuts. If a hormone increases root elongation, the actual increase in length depends on cells dividing or expanding. Expansion requires water uptake, a suitable wall and mechanical conditions that allow the cell to enlarge. A receptor does not directly stretch a cell wall. It may initiate changes that modify wall properties or alter transport and metabolism. When an experiment measures root length, the final measurement integrates all these processes and should not be mistaken for a direct measurement of receptor activity.

In a modular organism, each organ can occupy a different state while contributing to the same whole. The shoot tip may inhibit the outgrowth of some axillary buds, a developing fruit may become a strong sink, and an older leaf may begin to mobilize nutrients. Hormones help link these local decisions, but the response remains constrained by position, vascular connectivity and cell identity. The rest of the chapter develops a framework for tracing the path from the production of a chemical message to the physiological outcome and for recognizing where the chain can fail.

Historical evidence for a mobile growth signal

The first compelling demonstrations of plant chemical communication emerged before scientists could identify the structures of hormones. Grass seedlings provided a convenient system because the coleoptile, a protective sheath surrounding the emerging shoot, grows in a readily measurable direction. When a seedling is illuminated from one side, its coleoptile tends to bend toward the light. The question was whether the curved region directly sensed light or whether another region sensed the stimulus and sent information to the growing tissue.

Charles and Francis Darwin investigated this problem in experiments described in The Power of Movement in Plants in 1880. A normal coleoptile exposed to unilateral illumination bent toward the light. Removing the tip or shielding it with an opaque covering prevented or strongly reduced the normal directional response under their conditions. Covering the lower region while leaving the tip exposed could still permit curvature. These comparisons suggested a spatial separation between the region especially important for detection and the region carrying out differential growth. The experiments did not reveal a chemical structure, a receptor or a transport protein.

The opaque covering is informative only in relation to suitable controls. If a cap physically restricts growth, a straight seedling might result from mechanical obstruction rather than failure to detect light. A transparent covering with otherwise similar properties helps distinguish these possibilities. An uncovered, illuminated seedling establishes the normal response. A dark-grown control helps identify curvature unrelated to directional light. The conceptual lesson is that the absence of a response after a manipulation may arise because the stimulus was blocked, because growth was prevented or because communication was disrupted.

Peter Boysen-Jensen subsequently investigated the nature of the influence from the tip. A gelatin layer inserted between the excised tip and the remaining coleoptile could permit the transmission of the growth-promoting influence, whereas an impermeable barrier could interrupt it. This distinction supported the idea that some mobile material was involved rather than requiring uninterrupted living cellular contact across the cut. Gelatin did not prove that the substance was a specific molecule. It provided evidence consistent with a diffusible signal and narrowed the kinds of mechanisms that could account for the observation.

The experimental logic can be made explicit. Suppose two segments of tissue are physically separated by a hydrated layer through which dissolved substances can move. If a normal growth response is retained, the mechanism does not require continuous cytoplasm between the cut cells at that point. If the response is lost after inserting a barrier that blocks passage of dissolved material, a moving substance becomes a stronger explanation. But the barrier can also affect water supply or damage tissue. Experimental conditions must therefore preserve viability, and the conclusion should be expressed in proportion to what the comparison establishes.

Arpad Paál found that placing a coleoptile tip asymmetrically on the remaining stump in darkness could induce curvature. This was important because the direction of incident light was no longer needed to establish asymmetry during the response. The asymmetric position of the source was sufficient to generate unequal growth under the assay conditions. Light normally helps generate the asymmetry, but the tissue can execute differential growth when unequal chemical influence is produced experimentally. The result therefore separates the natural stimulus from the mechanism immediately responsible for the growth difference.

Frits Went used agar blocks to collect diffusible growth-promoting material from coleoptile tips and then applied these blocks to decapitated coleoptiles. A block positioned centrally could promote more symmetric growth; a block placed off-center could induce curvature. This approach helped convert an invisible biological influence into an assayable activity. The assay linked the presence of material transferred to agar with a measurable growth response. Subsequent chemical investigation and physiological research established the identity and role of indole-3-acetic acid. It is important not to credit the earliest curvature observations with a chemical identification that they could not have made.

Imagine that an agar block contains growth-promoting activity and is placed on the left side of an otherwise symmetrical coleoptile stump. If elongation increases on the left side relative to the right side, the organ bends toward its shorter side, which is the right side. The direction of bending is opposite to the more rapidly extending side. An examination question can exploit the intuitive but incorrect claim that an organ bends toward whichever side has the most elongation. A bending organ is a geometric consequence of unequal longitudinal extension, not evidence that the signal itself pulled the tissue toward its source.

Classic assays established a particularly important distinction between a biological activity and a molecular identity. Two compounds can produce similar curvature even though they have different chemical structures, because both can activate overlapping responses. Conversely, a single compound can produce little visible curvature if both sides receive the same effective dose. A bioassay measures the capacity of a sample to cause an output under specific conditions; a chemical analytical method measures the abundance of a chemically defined compound. Bioassays and chemical analyses complement one another but are not interchangeable.

The historical experiments also show that directionality has more than one meaning. Light arrives from a particular direction. Hormone molecules may move along a tissue or become distributed asymmetrically across it. Cells in different positions then expand at different rates. Those directions need not coincide. The demonstration that material can move through agar does not establish which membrane transporters direct its distribution in a living shoot. Nor does it show that every hormone uses the same route. The next chapter develops the molecular basis of polar auxin movement; here the experiments illustrate how the idea of a mobile messenger emerged.

Modern approaches extend the same causal reasoning. A mutation affecting a biosynthetic enzyme can reduce the active hormone pool. A mutation affecting a receptor can reduce the response even when the hormone is present. A reporter can reveal spatial differences in pathway output, and mass spectrometry can measure specific molecules. These independent measurements address questions that were inaccessible in the original bioassays. Their value comes from separating steps in the mechanism rather than merely providing more sophisticated images of the same visible curvature.

Chemical families: diversity within a common regulatory logic

Plant hormones are not a chemically uniform family. Their structures reflect their metabolic origins, and those structures affect their solubility, stability and recognition by proteins. A volatile hydrocarbon behaves differently from an ionizable weak acid; a steroidal compound behaves differently from a peptide. Grouping them by regulatory function is useful because the organism uses them to coordinate growth and physiology, not because all have the same functional groups or travel in the same form.

Auxins include indole-3-acetic acid, commonly abbreviated IAA, a weak organic acid with an indole ring. In many plants a major biosynthetic pathway uses tryptophan, converting it to indole-3-pyruvate through TAA/TAR aminotransferases and subsequently to IAA through YUCCA flavin-containing monooxygenases. IAA can act in local patterning or participate in directional redistribution through tissues. Its active concentration is influenced by synthesis, transport, conjugation and oxidative metabolism. Auxin illustrates how a gradient or a local maximum in hormone action can encode spatial information rather than merely signal that a whole plant should grow faster.

Cytokinins are principally substituted adenine derivatives. Examples include isopentenyladenine and trans-zeatin. Biosynthesis may begin with the attachment of an isoprenoid side chain to adenine nucleotides. Further metabolic steps and release of the free base can yield receptor-active forms. Cytokinin forms differ in activity and transport behavior; cytokinin ribosides and nucleotides should not all be counted as equally active ligands. Cytokinins are especially important in cell proliferation, meristem regulation, nutrient-linked root-to-shoot communication and the regulation of leaf longevity. The relationship with auxin is a recurrent theme in organogenesis.

Gibberellins are diterpenoid-derived compounds formed from geranylgeranyl diphosphate through a series of cyclization and oxidative steps. The large number of structurally identified gibberellins includes both bioactive forms and intermediates or products with little activity in a given assay. GA1 and GA4 are examples of naturally active gibberellins in many species; GA3 is widely used experimentally. A change in total gibberellin-like material may not accurately report a change in the bioactive pool. The regulation of stem elongation and cereal seed reserve mobilization depends on the biologically effective forms and the signaling components available to recognize them.

Brassinosteroids are steroidal regulators derived from plant sterols. Brassinolide and castasterone are prominent examples. Their steroid structure might suggest an analogy with animal steroid hormones, but the major plant brassinosteroid receptor BRI1 is a membrane-associated receptor kinase. The site of perception therefore cannot be inferred solely from the fact that a molecule is a steroid. Brassinosteroids contribute to cell expansion, differentiation and normal reproductive development. Many effects depend on local production and perception, rather than the hormone functioning as a universally long-distance endocrine messenger.

Strigolactones are carotenoid-derived signaling compounds that participate in regulation of branching and in communication between roots and other organisms in the soil. A series of carotenoid modifications gives rise to carlactone, which is subsequently converted into diverse products. Chemical diversity matters because distinct derivatives can differ in activity inside a plant and activity as signals released outside the root. Some compounds released by roots encourage arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal activity; they can also be detected by certain parasitic plants. These ecological interactions are not evidence that every molecule released by the root is a hormone within the plant.

Abscisic acid, abbreviated ABA, is formed in higher plants mainly through the oxidative cleavage of particular carotenoid precursors followed by subsequent conversion of the cleavage product. ABA is important for responses to water limitation and for seed maturation and dormancy. It is a particularly useful example of the relation between environmental conditions and controlled hormone availability. An increase in ABA during dehydration may arise from new synthesis, release from conjugated forms, altered movement or reduced removal. The pathway therefore cannot be fully understood by measuring expression of a single biosynthetic gene.

Ethylene is a gaseous two-carbon alkene. It is produced from methionine through S-adenosylmethionine and the intermediate 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid, abbreviated ACC. ACC oxidase catalyzes formation of ethylene from ACC and requires oxygen. Ethylene can diffuse through gas spaces and exchange with the surrounding atmosphere. Its precursor ACC can move through aqueous tissues and may also be conjugated or have signaling roles. The chemical distinction between ACC and ethylene explains why an aqueous precursor measurement is not a direct measurement of the gas that reaches a receptor.

Jasmonates derive from oxygenated fatty acid metabolism. In a well-characterized pathway, alpha-linolenic acid gives rise to 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, or OPDA, in plastids, followed by peroxisomal conversion toward jasmonic acid. Conjugation with isoleucine produces JA-Ile, a major ligand for canonical jasmonate signaling in Arabidopsis. Some other oxylipins can have biological effects of their own. Jasmonate signaling is prominent in responses to tissue injury and herbivory and in several developmental processes. The fact that JA-Ile is a conjugate is an immediate warning against treating all hormone conjugation as inactivation.

Salicylic acid is a phenolic weak acid with important roles in defense responses, including those associated with many biotrophic pathogens. Its biosynthesis can involve isochorismate-derived or phenylalanine-associated routes; relative contributions differ among species and circumstances. Conjugation can alter its free concentration, and conversion to derivatives can influence movement or subsequent regeneration of salicylic acid. Salicylic acid signaling involves NPR proteins, with NPR1 especially important for transcriptional regulation of many defense genes. Its relationship with jasmonate signaling is conditional rather than a universal pair of mutually exclusive switches.

Plants also use peptide hormones. These are generally encoded as precursor proteins or polypeptides and processed to biologically active forms. Some undergo modifications that influence receptor recognition. Examples include CLE peptides involved in meristem regulation and systemin in particular plant defense contexts. Many peptide signals are perceived by cell-surface receptor complexes. Unlike a small molecule generated through several metabolic enzymes, a peptide signal may originate from transcription, translation, proteolytic processing and secretion. Peptides are therefore introduced as an additional major signaling category, without suggesting that every small signaling peptide shares the same biological role.

Chemical classification is a foundation for reasoning, not a substitute for it. A student who knows that ABA and strigolactones have carotenoid-related origins should not conclude that they bind the same receptor. A student who knows that auxin and salicylic acid are weak acids should not infer identical transport specificity. Chemical similarity can suggest testable possibilities, but ligand recognition depends on the architecture of the proteins and on the cellular system in which those proteins operate. The physiological identity of each signal is established through evidence about synthesis, perception and response.

Biosynthesis, activation and the active hormone pool

Biosynthesis is the set of enzymatic reactions through which living cells construct a molecule from precursor compounds. A biosynthetic pathway does not necessarily end at the first compound that resembles a hormone. It may produce intermediates that must be modified before a receptor recognizes them effectively. A precursor can accumulate when the next enzyme is absent, even though the amount of the active product falls. This distinction provides the basis for interpreting mutants that affect metabolism rather than perception.

Consider a pathway in which enzyme E1 converts a precursor P into intermediate I, and enzyme E2 converts I into bioactive hormone H. If E1 activity is lost, both I and H may decrease. If E2 is lost, I may increase while H decreases. Supplying I could rescue an E1 mutant if E2 remains active, whereas supplying I would not necessarily rescue a complete E2 mutant. Supplying H could bypass either biosynthetic block provided that H enters the relevant cells, persists long enough and is recognized normally. Such a rescue is evidence that the downstream pathway can respond, although it does not exclude additional functions of the affected gene.

Hormone production is spatially organized because the enzymes involved are expressed differently across tissues and because their substrates are not uniformly available. In a growing root, distinct groups of cells can produce or remove auxin; in a leaf undergoing dehydration, ABA biosynthetic capacity may change. A hormone can be synthesized near its target or imported from elsewhere. A spatial pattern of active hormone concentration therefore cannot be deduced from a biosynthetic promoter alone. The promoter reveals one component of potential production, not the combined effect of transport, metabolic conversion and removal.

Compartmentation within cells also matters. Plastids are sites of carotenoid metabolism and early stages of several hormone pathways. The cytosol, endoplasmic reticulum and peroxisomes contribute to other reactions depending on the class of hormone. When a product formed in one compartment must be processed in another, movement across an organelle boundary can affect pathway flux. The formation of an intermediate in a plastid does not imply that the receptor detecting the final hormone must be plastid-localized. Biosynthetic localization, storage localization and receptor localization are distinct properties.

Plants can regulate production by changing enzyme abundance, activity or substrate availability. An increase in the messenger RNA encoding a biosynthetic enzyme may precede an increase in hormone content, but the relationship is not necessarily immediate or proportional. The existing protein must be considered, and the enzyme may need a substrate or cofactor that becomes limiting. Other pathway enzymes may be slow, and removal or export may compensate for increased production. A transcript measurement is a useful indicator of regulation but does not directly quantify molecular flux.

Some environmental stimuli change hormone production rapidly. Mechanical injury can lead to accumulation of jasmonate-related compounds; dehydration can enhance ABA biosynthesis; developmental and environmental stimuli can increase ethylene production through changes in ACC synthase and other steps. The timing of hormone accumulation depends on the tissue and stimulus. A leaf might alter its response before a large increase is detectable in a whole-leaf extract, especially if the relevant changes occur in a small group of cells. Sampling time and spatial resolution are therefore essential to the interpretation of biochemical data.

The biosynthetic origin of a hormone can help identify likely experimental interventions. A lesion in a carotenoid cleavage enzyme can affect the production of a hormone derived from carotenoids, but carotenoid metabolism also serves other processes. A broad inhibitor of carotenoid formation may alter photoprotection and pigment composition in addition to reducing certain hormone pools. Such a treatment should not be interpreted as selectively removing only ABA or only strigolactones. A more specific mutant, measurement of the relevant metabolites and rescue with the active hormone can provide stronger causal evidence.

A useful way to organize the active pool is to distinguish formation rate from abundance. If a tissue contains 20 picomoles of a hormone, this number states how much was present at the moment of sampling. It does not state whether the tissue synthesized 20 picomoles in the preceding hour. A tissue can maintain a stable amount while producing and removing many more molecules than are present at any one time. The molecules turn over even though the pool size remains steady. The notion of steady state is therefore a statement about balanced rates, not about inactivity.

A further distinction concerns amount and concentration. If a growing organ maintains the same amount of hormone while its volume doubles, the mean concentration falls by half. Conversely, a water loss that reduces tissue volume can change concentration even without immediate synthesis or removal. Experimental measurements reported per gram fresh mass, per gram dry mass, per cell or per unit volume answer different questions. Under dehydration, fresh mass itself is changing, so apparent increases in hormone per gram fresh weight should be evaluated with care. This does not invalidate such normalization; it defines the limitations of its interpretation.

The active chemical form must also be specified. Free auxin can be distinguished from several conjugated forms. Free cytokinin bases can be distinguished from nucleotides and ribosides. Bioactive GAs can be distinguished from precursors and inactive oxidative products. Ethylene must be distinguished from ACC. A method that hydrolyzes conjugates during sample preparation may measure a larger potential pool than a method designed to quantify only the free hormone. Both measurements can be meaningful, but they cannot be compared without knowing what chemical forms are being detected.

Hormonal activation is not limited to synthesis of a free small molecule. A precursor protein can be cleaved to produce a peptide hormone; a conjugation reaction can generate JA-Ile; an inactive cytokinin nucleotide can be converted into a receptor-active base by a LOG enzyme. These examples establish a general principle that activation is defined by the acquisition of biological signaling activity in a particular system. It is not synonymous with one chemical reaction, such as hydrolysis. The chemistry and receptor preferences of the particular family determine which conversion is activating.

The consequences of altering a pathway can be assessed at several levels. Metabolite analysis can establish whether active product abundance changes, gene-expression analysis can assess selected downstream outputs, and physiological assays can establish whether growth or function changes. Each contributes a different piece of evidence. Measuring a short stem does not identify the deficient hormone; measuring a low hormone concentration does not show whether that deficiency is the only cause of the phenotype. Combining independent measurements with targeted interventions makes the causal account more convincing.

Conjugation, storage, degradation and hormone turnover

An organism must be able to end a hormonal response as well as initiate one. A signal that remains active indefinitely after a transient stimulus would fail to convey information about the current state of the organism. A seed that has completed germination, for example, should not continue to behave as though it were still in an ABA-maintained dormant state. Hormone availability is therefore determined by biochemical reactions that introduce or remove active molecules. Transport changes where those molecules are available, but metabolic control determines their chemical form and lifetime.

Conjugation is the covalent attachment of another chemical group to a molecule. For plant hormones, the attached group may be a sugar, an amino acid or another moiety. Conjugation can change the polarity of the parent compound, its ability to cross membranes, its susceptibility to enzymatic degradation and its recognition by receptors. These consequences depend on the identity of both the hormone and the attached group. The word conjugate describes a chemical relationship; it does not by itself identify a physiological function.

An important example is the relationship between free IAA and IAA conjugates. A group of enzymes known as GH3 acyl acid amido synthetases can attach amino acids to IAA. Some conjugates can be hydrolyzed by suitable enzymes, releasing free IAA; others tend to enter pathways of further catabolism. IAA conjugation can thus serve as a means of buffering active hormone or directing it toward removal. In Arabidopsis, IAA-aspartate and IAA-glutamate are commonly associated with inactivation and subsequent metabolism. It would be unsafe to infer that every amino acid conjugate acts as a reversible store.

Oxidative metabolism is another route of IAA inactivation. The formation of oxindole-3-acetic acid through DAO-related oxidation illustrates how a chemical modification of the hormone can decrease its capacity to participate in the canonical receptor response. A tissue exposed to increased IAA may respond by stimulating genes whose products promote conjugation or oxidation. Consequently, hormone exposure can increase the machinery that removes the signal. Such feedback helps limit sustained accumulation, although its time course and relative importance vary by tissue.

A different example is ABA glucose ester. ABA can be covalently linked to glucose, producing a conjugated form that may accumulate in cellular compartments including vacuoles or the endoplasmic reticulum-associated system depending on the plant and tissue. Hydrolytic enzymes can release free ABA from this conjugate. The reserve can contribute to rapid changes in active ABA availability without requiring every released molecule to be produced anew from carotenoid precursors. This is a useful physiological distinction when studying transient changes during dehydration.

ABA can also undergo oxidative catabolism, notably through ABA 8′-hydroxylation by enzymes of the CYP707A family. The initial hydroxylated product can rearrange to phaseic acid. These reactions participate in the decline of ABA during transitions such as seed germination or recovery from particular stresses. Some ABA metabolites retain activity in certain assays, so the label catabolite should not be read as a universal guarantee that no biological effect remains. The main point is that the original active pool is being chemically modified and its receptor-accessible availability changes.

Cytokinin illustrates multiple possible fates for related molecular forms. Free bases are major receptor-active compounds, while ribotides are important intermediates in production and metabolism. Glycosylation can generate forms with different stability and activity. Certain O-glucosides may serve as reversibly conjugated forms, whereas N-glucosides often have different metabolic properties and should not be treated as equivalent reservoirs. Cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase, abbreviated CKX, can cleave the side chain of susceptible cytokinins. By changing the balance of susceptible forms, tissues regulate the hormone pool even without changing the first biosynthetic step.

Gibberellin metabolism demonstrates the importance of a multicomponent chemical family. Some GA forms are immediate precursors of bioactive compounds; some are active; others are inactivated by reactions catalyzed by GA 2-oxidases. A mutation that changes the composition of the pool may produce a strong growth phenotype without a comparable change in the sum of all related metabolites. A measurement labeled total GA can therefore obscure the specific step that determines signaling. Exam reasoning should identify whether the measured species is a precursor, a ligand or a product of inactivation.

The most instructive exception to the simple notion that conjugation turns a hormone off is JA-Ile. The enzyme JAR1 conjugates jasmonic acid to isoleucine. The resulting jasmonoyl-isoleucine is a major active ligand in canonical COI1–JAZ signaling. A plant unable to carry out this conversion can accumulate its precursor and still have a defective response. JA-Ile can subsequently be hydroxylated or hydrolyzed in pathways that limit or modify the response. The same general type of chemistry, covalent attachment or removal of an amino acid, can thus have opposite effects in different hormonal systems.

Ethylene is another exception. The gas itself is not normally retained as a covalently bound glucose store analogous to ABA glucose ester. It is produced, diffuses and can escape into the atmosphere. The precursor ACC can be transported or conjugated, including formation of malonyl-ACC. Altering ACC metabolism can influence the capacity to make ethylene later or elsewhere, but it is not the same as storing ethylene molecules in a stable conjugated form. In a flooded organ, limited gas exchange and oxygen availability can independently affect ethylene accumulation and production.

Storage need not mean that molecules are chemically altered. A hormone or precursor can be compartmentalized, and changes in membrane transport can change its access to receptors. A molecule contained in a vacuolar pool is not necessarily available to an intracellular receptor in the nucleus at the same moment. The relevant distinction is accessibility rather than simply physical presence in the sampled tissue. Tissue extraction eliminates many normal compartment boundaries, which is why total extracted abundance must be interpreted in relation to localization and chemical form.

These examples can be unified as a balance between active molecules, convertible reserves and removal pathways. A short increase in synthesis need not produce a prolonged response if inactivation is rapid. A large stored pool need not produce a large response if release is restricted. A signal can increase without new synthesis if a reserve is activated. To test these alternatives, a researcher can measure free hormone and representative conjugates at several times, inhibit or genetically alter selected conversions, and assess whether downstream signaling follows the changes in the accessible active pool.

In examination problems it is especially important to distinguish reversibility on the timescale of the experiment from theoretical chemical reversibility. A conjugate may be hydrolyzable by an enzyme in vitro but fail to supply much active hormone in a tissue that does not express that enzyme. Conversely, a tissue may rapidly mobilize a small reservoir when the appropriate hydrolase is activated. The label storage is most meaningful when there is evidence that the stored form can contribute to physiological signaling under the conditions being considered.

Quantifying hormone homeostasis and interpreting turnover

A simple mass balance helps connect the biochemical descriptions to measurable outcomes. Let H be the amount of active hormone within a defined tissue, expressed in picomoles. During a short interval, synthesis and import add molecules to the pool; release from an inactive reserve can also add molecules. Conjugation into inactive forms, irreversible metabolic removal and export can subtract them. If the tissue is considered as a system, the rate of change of H is the sum of inputs minus the sum of outputs. This statement is a conservation accounting relation and does not assume any particular kinetic mechanism.

For a tissue with one production term S and a removal rate proportional to the amount present, the model becomes dH/dt = S − kH. Here S has units of picomoles per hour and k has units of inverse hours. The term kH therefore has units of picomoles per hour, as required. This model can represent a combined effective removal process when several pathways operate approximately linearly in the range of interest. It is not a claim that every plant enzyme exhibits first-order kinetics at every hormone concentration.

At steady state, dH/dt is zero, giving H* = S/k. If a tissue produces 6 pmol per hour and the effective removal coefficient is 0.3 per hour, the predicted steady-state amount is 20 pmol. The tissue is not inactive: it is producing and removing 6 pmol per hour at that state. If production doubles while k remains unchanged, the new steady state is 40 pmol. If removal also doubles, the new steady state returns to 20 pmol even though the turnover is twice as fast. These examples are illustrative calculations, not measurements from a specific species.

Consider now a brief hormone pulse followed by a period when new synthesis and import are negligible. Under the same first-order approximation, the rate equation is dH/dt = −kH. Separating the variables gives dH/H = −k dt. Integration from the initial amount H0 to the amount at time t yields ln(H/H0) = −kt, or H = H0 exp(−kt). The exponential describes the fraction of molecules remaining after a given time. The half-life is the time at which that fraction is one half, so t1/2 = ln(2)/k.

For a hypothetical tissue whose active pool decreases from 80 pmol to 40 pmol in two hours and then to 20 pmol after another two hours, the observed half-life under this model is two hours. Dividing ln(2), approximately 0.693, by two hours gives k ≈ 0.347 h−1. After six hours the prediction is 10 pmol. The calculation is valid only if there is no significant continuing synthesis, import or release from stores and if the removal coefficient does not change. A plant with an active feedback response may not obey this simple decay law.

A common experimental complication is that hormone abundance appears to change nonexponentially. Suppose a plant receives a stimulus that stops after an hour. Active hormone continues to be supplied from a conjugated pool, while its free form is also degraded. A plot of the free pool might decline slowly or temporarily remain constant. It would be inappropriate to conclude that degradation has stopped merely because the measured free amount is stable. The underlying removal may be balanced by release from the reserve. Measuring only the free pool cannot uniquely identify all fluxes that contribute to it.

Concentration requires a further term when the tissue volume changes. If H is the amount and V is the effective volume, concentration C = H/V. Differentiation gives dC/dt = (1/V)(dH/dt) − (C/V)(dV/dt). The second term describes dilution during volume expansion or concentration during volume contraction. A rapidly expanding cell can therefore show a falling hormone concentration even when its absolute hormone content rises, provided volume rises faster. The equation illustrates why the denominator must be identified before interpreting an apparent change in hormone concentration.

For example, a hypothetical tissue contains 30 pmol of hormone in 0.1 mL and later contains 45 pmol in 0.2 mL. The initial concentration is 300 pmol per mL, equivalent to 300 nM. The final concentration is 225 pmol per mL, equivalent to 225 nM. The amount rises by 50 percent while the mean concentration falls by 25 percent. No contradiction exists. The two measurements describe different quantities. This distinction matters in a growing organ because the rate of growth can itself depend on hormone signaling.

The simplest model ignores the reserve pool, but introducing a second variable clarifies what a storage pathway does. Let B denote the amount of a reversibly conjugated inactive form. If active hormone is converted to B at rate aH and released at rate bB, then these terms enter the active pool with opposite signs and the reserve pool with reversed signs. If no other processes affect B, adding the two rate equations cancels the conjugation and release terms. The combined pool changes only through net synthesis, import, degradation or export. Internal conversion redistributes material between forms without changing the total in this idealized accounting.

Suppose an extract contains 10 pmol free hormone and 90 pmol reversibly conjugated material. Release of 20 pmol from the reserve increases the active pool to 30 pmol while the reserve decreases to 70 pmol. The combined pool remains 100 pmol. An assay that measures total hormone after hydrolysis would detect no change, whereas a free-hormone assay would detect a threefold increase. This example demonstrates why two analytical methods may both be correct while giving apparently different results.

Real tissues add further complications. Different removal enzymes can compete for the same substrate; some become saturated, some are induced by signaling, and transport rates can depend on the membrane environment. For a saturable enzyme, a first-order approximation can be reasonable when substrate concentration is much smaller than the Michaelis constant, because rate is then roughly proportional to concentration. Near saturation, a further increase in substrate does not produce a proportional increase in removal. The simple linear model is a starting point for interpretation, not a universal quantitative law of plant hormones.

The value of mass-balance reasoning is that it forces a hypothesis to account for both inputs and outputs. A high hormone level could reflect high synthesis, low removal, increased import, reduced export or release from a reserve. A low level could reflect the opposite changes or dilution in a rapidly expanding compartment. To distinguish these hypotheses, time-course measurements, precursor labeling, chemical speciation and specific perturbations are more informative than a single endpoint. The objective is not to fit elaborate mathematical models for their own sake but to identify which biological process is consistent with the observations.

Local movement, diffusion and membrane transport

A hormone molecule formed in one cell may need to reach a different cell or a different compartment of the same cell. Movement is governed by physical properties as well as proteins that regulate membrane passage. In plants, neighboring cells are separated by plasma membranes and cell walls. The aqueous space outside plasma membranes, including accessible cell-wall space, belongs to the apoplast. The interconnected cytoplasms of cells joined by plasmodesmata form the symplast. These are different routes, and a molecule following one route may need to cross a membrane before entering the other.

Diffusion is the net movement that results from random molecular motion when a concentration gradient exists. For a neutral solute in a simple uniform medium, Fick's law describes flux density J = −D dC/dx. D is a diffusion coefficient, C is concentration and x is distance. The minus sign indicates net movement toward lower concentration under the assumed conditions. The law does not state that individual molecules all move in that direction; molecular motion occurs in both directions, but the number moving from the region of higher concentration is greater.

If concentration is expressed in moles per cubic meter, position in meters and D in square meters per second, the resulting flux density is in moles per square meter per second. Multiplying flux density by an appropriate cross-sectional area gives the net amount transferred per second. A thicker region between two maintained concentrations reduces the average concentration gradient in a simple slab model. This relation helps explain why diffusion is particularly effective over short distances but need not rapidly coordinate widely separated organs.

An approximate one-dimensional diffusion timescale is t ≈ L²/(2D), derived from the relation between mean squared displacement and time for unrestricted diffusion. Increasing the distance L tenfold increases the characteristic time one hundredfold if D remains constant. The calculation is not a direct prediction for a living tissue because barriers, binding, flow and metabolism can alter movement. It does illustrate why a plant may combine short-range diffusion or cell-to-cell transport with long-distance vascular movement. One mechanism need not account efficiently for every spatial scale.

Ethylene can diffuse through intercellular gas spaces and cross membranes relatively readily because it is a small nonpolar gas. Its local concentration depends not only on production but also on escape and tissue geometry. Enclosing a fruit in an atmosphere where ethylene cannot readily escape can increase exposure even if production remains unchanged. A similar enclosure can alter oxygen and carbon dioxide concentrations, so a controlled ethylene experiment requires comparisons that account for gas exchange. The fact that ethylene diffuses as a gas does not mean its aqueous precursor ACC travels by the same route.

Weak organic acids behave differently because their charge depends on pH. For a monoprotic weak acid HA in dilute solution, the Henderson–Hasselbalch relation is pH = pKa + log10([A−]/[HA]). The symbol HA denotes the protonated form and A− the deprotonated anion. When pH is equal to pKa, the two forms are present in equal concentrations under the stated approximation. When pH is one unit above pKa, the anion-to-neutral ratio is ten to one. The charged form generally crosses a lipid bilayer less readily by simple diffusion than the uncharged form.

IAA is a weak acid with a pKa near 4.75. In an illustrative extracellular solution of pH 5.5, the ratio of deprotonated to protonated IAA is 10^0.75, approximately 5.62. The neutral fraction is therefore 1/(1+5.62), approximately 15 percent. At a cytosolic pH of 7.0, the ratio is about 178, so the neutral fraction is only about 0.56 percent. A protonated molecule that enters the cytosol can become deprotonated in the more alkaline environment. This contributes to ion trapping, although transport proteins, membrane potential and proton gradients must also be considered.

The example must not be extended into a claim that only protonated IAA enters plant cells. AUX1/LAX family proteins participate in auxin influx, while PIN proteins and certain ABCB transporters contribute to efflux. Their activities and cellular locations influence auxin movement. The ionization calculation explains why the membrane is not simply an unrestricted barrier for all IAA forms; it does not quantify the total in vivo flux. The next chapter develops the chemiosmotic model and polar auxin transport in more detail.

Polar transport means that the net transport of a substance has a preferred direction along a biological axis. One way cells achieve this directionality is by distributing efflux carriers asymmetrically on their plasma membranes. Imagine a sequence of cells that take up auxin from the extracellular space and preferentially export it through one face. Repeating this arrangement along the sequence can produce directional flux even when each local step involves membrane crossing rather than bulk flow through a vessel. The orientation of the transporter distribution, not merely the existence of a concentration gradient, is critical to the result.

Transport proteins can be regulated by their abundance, activity or localization. A cell may change the direction in which auxin is exported by redistributing particular PIN proteins between membrane domains. A mutation that prevents correct localization can alter spatial hormone patterns even when the transporter protein remains detectable. Consequently, measuring transporter transcript abundance alone does not establish the direction of transport. Localization studies and functional transport assays address complementary aspects of the mechanism.

Several other hormone families also have identified transport proteins, including particular members of the ABCG, NPF and other transporter families. The fact that a protein family transports one plant hormone does not mean that every member transports that hormone, or that all hormones use an identical carrier. Substrate specificity, tissue localization, driving force and direction of transport must be established individually. Some proteins transport more than one related compound, and some hormone distributions reflect combinations of transporters and passive movement. A single universal hormone transporter is not an adequate model.

A gradient can arise from localized production, spatially distributed removal, directional transport or a combination. A source that continually supplies a diffusible hormone and a distant sink that removes it may maintain a concentration difference. If the sink is eliminated, the gradient may weaken or the average concentration may rise. If a directional transporter is disabled, the gradient can change position or shape without a large change in total hormone amount. A spatial map of hormone response should therefore be interpreted with attention to both the sources and the mechanisms that distribute and remove the signal.

When a reporter indicates stronger signaling on one side of an organ, two interpretations immediately arise: more receptor-accessible hormone may be present there, or the cells on that side may be more sensitive to an equal hormone concentration. A transport hypothesis becomes stronger if independent evidence shows a corresponding difference in hormone abundance or flux and if perturbing a specific transport process changes the pattern. Without such evidence, a response gradient alone cannot uniquely identify a concentration gradient. This distinction connects the physical movement of a signal to the biological interpretation of the signal.

Long-distance signals and the vascular system

The root and shoot of a vascular plant occupy very different environments. Roots encounter soil water and minerals, while leaves encounter atmospheric conditions and light. Their activities nevertheless need to remain coordinated. A change in soil nitrate availability can influence shoot growth; a change in water status can alter stomatal behavior; the demands of expanding organs can affect allocation to roots. Chemical signaling is one means by which these parts of a plant exchange information, but it operates alongside transport of water and nutrients, electrical signals and other forms of communication.

Xylem is associated principally with transport of water and dissolved solutes from roots toward transpiring shoots. Phloem is associated principally with the movement of assimilates from sources to sinks, and its direction depends on the source–sink relationship in particular conducting pathways. A hormone or its precursor can be loaded into vascular pathways and unloaded at target tissues. The presence of a compound in xylem sap or phloem exudate suggests that the route may contribute to its movement, but the presence of a compound alone does not establish its physiological significance at the destination.

Cytokinin provides a useful example of nutrient-linked vascular communication. Roots can synthesize cytokinins in response to nutrient conditions, and trans-zeatin-type cytokinin forms can participate in root-to-shoot signaling. Transporters including ABCG14 have been implicated in this movement in Arabidopsis. The physiological outcome depends on the form that reaches the shoot, subsequent metabolic conversions and the ability of shoot cells to perceive the signal. Cytokinin is also produced within shoots. It is therefore inaccurate to present the root as the exclusive endocrine gland that supplies all shoot cytokinin.

ABA participates in communication between tissues during water stress. Root-derived ABA can contribute to signals reaching the shoot in particular conditions, but leaves and other shoot tissues can also produce ABA. Changes in hydraulic status may initiate or interact with chemical signals. In a drought experiment, the observation that stomata close while a root is drying does not establish that root-synthesized ABA traveled to the leaf and directly caused every part of the response. Measurements of hormone synthesis in both organs, grafting experiments, tissue-specific manipulations and hydraulic measurements can help distinguish the possibilities.

Long-distance movement may involve a precursor rather than the final active ligand. In some conditions ACC produced in roots can be transported to aerial tissues and converted to ethylene. This distinction is especially helpful when oxygen availability differs between organs. ACC oxidase requires oxygen; the tissue in which ACC is formed may not be the tissue best able to convert it into ethylene. The chemical substance traveling through the plant is not necessarily identical to the substance finally detected by the receptor. A model of interorgan signaling must specify both the mobile species and the terminal active signal.

Long-distance auxin transport and local polar auxin transport should likewise be distinguished. Auxin can move in association with vascular tissues, and polar carrier-mediated movement can generate local and tissue-scale patterns. Bulk flow in a vascular stream is not identical to repeated directional efflux across cell membranes. The two routes can interact but have different physical mechanisms. The observation that a label travels from an upper shoot to a root does not by itself identify the contributions of phloem-associated movement and polar cell-to-cell movement.

Movement beyond the plant surface adds another dimension. Strigolactone-related molecules released by roots can be detected by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi. These compounds can also stimulate germination of some parasitic plant seeds. The root exudate is an external chemical signal in that interaction. Inside the plant, strigolactone-related pathways regulate traits such as shoot branching. The identity of a compound active in the rhizosphere need not be identical to the physiologically dominant mobile signal controlling a distant bud. The relationship between particular chemical species, transport routes and biological activities must be established rather than assumed.

The concept of mobility should also be separated from the concept of requirement. A molecule may be detectable in a distant organ without being necessary for the response there, because the distant organ can make its own hormone. Conversely, a mobile signal may be necessary even if its concentration is difficult to measure, provided that a specific intervention blocking its movement abolishes the response and suitable rescue restores it. Such experiments require controls for the ability of the destination tissue to respond. A failed long-distance response may reflect defective reception rather than defective movement.

A graft offers a useful experimental way of separating the genotype of root and shoot. Suppose a plant carries a loss-of-function mutation that reduces the synthesis of a particular hormone in roots. A wild-type rootstock joined to a mutant scion may restore some shoot phenotype if a suitable mobile signal from the rootstock reaches the scion. A mutant rootstock joined to a wild-type scion tests a different configuration. Reciprocal grafts, together with self-grafted controls, can reveal whether the phenotype follows the rootstock, the scion or both. However, rescue could arise from a mobile precursor, another signal that induces local synthesis or changes in nutritional conditions.

A convincing graft experiment therefore often requires additional evidence. Measuring the relevant active form and potential precursors in rootstock and scion helps establish what changes. A receptor mutant scion can test whether restored growth requires perception in the shoot. Isotope tracing, when interpreted with attention to metabolic conversion, may establish that labeled material from the root reaches the shoot. The conclusion should reflect what each measurement establishes rather than automatically equating graft rescue with transport of a specific molecule.

Bulk movement through a vascular stream also depends on loading, unloading and flow. An increase in transpiration may increase the amount of a solute delivered per unit time even if its concentration in the stream is lower. If a solute concentration is C and volume flow is Q, the delivery rate is approximately CQ when the stream is well mixed and the sampled concentration is representative. For example, a hypothetical stream of 20 nM hormone flowing at 0.5 mL per hour delivers 10 pmol per hour; a 10 nM stream flowing at 2 mL per hour delivers 20 pmol per hour. Concentration alone gives the opposite ranking from delivery rate in this example.

The framework of local and long-distance movement is therefore not a classification in which every hormone must be assigned to only one category. The same hormone family can include locally acting ligands, transportable precursors and compounds that travel in particular vascular routes. A plant can regulate source strength, transporter localization, vascular loading, local activation and receptor responsiveness independently. These levels of regulation explain why a hormone can be present throughout a plant yet produce highly localized developmental effects.
