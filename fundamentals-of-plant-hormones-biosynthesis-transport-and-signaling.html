The problem of coordination in a growing plant

A germinating seed, a newly expanding leaf and a mature root all possess the genetic instructions required for plant life, yet they perform different tasks. In a germinating seed, the immediate problem is to establish growth before reserves are exhausted. In a mature leaf, water loss must be balanced against the carbon gained through open stomata. In a root, the direction of growth determines whether the expanding tip reaches water and mineral resources. Changes in these activities must occur at the appropriate place and time. The plant cannot rely on a fixed program that makes every cell perform the same activity whenever a particular environmental condition changes.

Consider a seedling whose shoot emerges from the soil. Its buried portion elongates rapidly, but once the shoot reaches light, its developmental program changes. The young leaves expand and acquire photosynthetic capacity; stem elongation becomes differently regulated. The incoming light is information about the environment. A photoreceptor is one of the molecular devices that detects this information. A hormone can then help translate the detection into changes in cell division, elongation or gene expression. It would be inaccurate to say that the light itself is the hormone, or that all effects of the light must pass through one hormone. The terms signal, receptor and response describe different positions within a causal explanation.

A plant hormone is an endogenous chemical messenger that influences physiological or developmental processes by acting on a responsive biological system, usually at low concentrations. Endogenous means produced within the organism, rather than necessarily supplied by the surrounding environment. A plant may synthesize the same hormone in multiple tissues; some of it may act near its site of production while some moves elsewhere. There is no requirement for a specialized endocrine gland or for transport through a circulatory system equivalent to animal blood. In plants, the ability of cells to make and respond to signals is distributed throughout the organism.

A chemical compound does not become a hormone merely because it changes growth when a large amount is supplied in a laboratory. An acid at a damaging concentration can stop root elongation without behaving as a normal hormone. Evidence for hormonal function is stronger if the compound is naturally produced, if its endogenous availability can be regulated, if its effect is observed in a physiologically relevant concentration range, and if perception or response depends on identifiable biological components. These criteria are complementary rather than a single rigid definition. Some compounds have several biological roles, and the evidence for a new signal may accumulate in stages.

The term plant growth regulator is wider than the term naturally occurring plant hormone. A synthetic chemical may mimic a natural hormone or interfere with a hormone pathway without itself being a normally synthesized plant hormone. For example, naphthaleneacetic acid is a synthetic auxin used to study and influence growth. It can activate parts of auxin response, but its transport and metabolism need not match those of indole-3-acetic acid, the major natural auxin in many plants. A result obtained with one compound therefore cannot automatically be assigned to every member of the same functional class.

Distinguishing hormones from nutrients is particularly useful. Nitrate provides nitrogen for amino acids, nucleotides and other metabolites; it can also supply information about nutritional status. A cytokinin may convey part of the response to nitrogen availability without furnishing the nitrogen needed to construct a new leaf. Supplying cytokinin to a severely nitrogen-starved plant cannot replace all the nitrogen atoms missing from its metabolism. The regulatory molecule may change how resources are acquired or distributed, but the resources themselves remain necessary. Some nutrients and metabolites also have signaling functions, so the distinction concerns their role in a particular mechanism rather than a claim that all metabolic compounds are chemically incapable of signaling.

A second useful distinction is between a hormone and an environmental stimulus. Soil drying changes the water status of a plant. Changes in water relations can trigger abscisic acid production and alter the responsiveness of cells. Abscisic acid then influences processes such as stomatal closure. Soil water availability is an external variable; the hormone is an internally regulated chemical component of the response. Removing the environmental stimulus and removing the hormone are different experimental interventions. A drought response may contain both hormone-dependent components and other components controlled by hydraulic or electrical changes.

Small quantities of a hormone can have substantial effects because molecular recognition is coupled to amplification. One activated receptor may influence the activities of multiple downstream molecules. Changes in transcription can lead to many copies of a messenger RNA, and each RNA can be translated more than once. Enzymes produced or activated by signaling may process many substrate molecules. Such amplification does not mean that the hormone creates energy or matter. Energy and building materials still come from cellular metabolism. The signal changes the organization and timing of biochemical work.

Hormonal control should not be pictured as nine independent switches, each connected to exactly one visible trait. Auxin can contribute to growth, vascular development and responses to directional stimuli. Cytokinins can influence division, nutrient signaling and organ development. Abscisic acid can regulate seed dormancy and water balance. These effects overlap and depend on developmental stage. A seed and a guard cell use different output machinery even if both perceive abscisic acid. The central question of the chapter is therefore how information is generated, moved, interpreted and terminated, not simply which name belongs beside a particular plant response.

The distinction between regulation and execution also prevents incorrect causal shortcuts. If a hormone increases root elongation, the actual increase in length depends on cells dividing or expanding. Expansion requires water uptake, a suitable wall and mechanical conditions that allow the cell to enlarge. A receptor does not directly stretch a cell wall. It may initiate changes that modify wall properties or alter transport and metabolism. When an experiment measures root length, the final measurement integrates all these processes and should not be mistaken for a direct measurement of receptor activity.

In a modular organism, each organ can occupy a different state while contributing to the same whole. The shoot tip may inhibit the outgrowth of some axillary buds, a developing fruit may become a strong sink, and an older leaf may begin to mobilize nutrients. Hormones help link these local decisions, but the response remains constrained by position, vascular connectivity and cell identity. The rest of the chapter develops a framework for tracing the path from the production of a chemical message to the physiological outcome and for recognizing where the chain can fail.

Historical evidence for a mobile growth signal

The first compelling demonstrations of plant chemical communication emerged before scientists could identify the structures of hormones. Grass seedlings provided a convenient system because the coleoptile, a protective sheath surrounding the emerging shoot, grows in a readily measurable direction. When a seedling is illuminated from one side, its coleoptile tends to bend toward the light. The question was whether the curved region directly sensed light or whether another region sensed the stimulus and sent information to the growing tissue.

Charles and Francis Darwin investigated this problem in experiments described in The Power of Movement in Plants in 1880. A normal coleoptile exposed to unilateral illumination bent toward the light. Removing the tip or shielding it with an opaque covering prevented or strongly reduced the normal directional response under their conditions. Covering the lower region while leaving the tip exposed could still permit curvature. These comparisons suggested a spatial separation between the region especially important for detection and the region carrying out differential growth. The experiments did not reveal a chemical structure, a receptor or a transport protein.

The opaque covering is informative only in relation to suitable controls. If a cap physically restricts growth, a straight seedling might result from mechanical obstruction rather than failure to detect light. A transparent covering with otherwise similar properties helps distinguish these possibilities. An uncovered, illuminated seedling establishes the normal response. A dark-grown control helps identify curvature unrelated to directional light. The conceptual lesson is that the absence of a response after a manipulation may arise because the stimulus was blocked, because growth was prevented or because communication was disrupted.

Peter Boysen-Jensen subsequently investigated the nature of the influence from the tip. A gelatin layer inserted between the excised tip and the remaining coleoptile could permit the transmission of the growth-promoting influence, whereas an impermeable barrier could interrupt it. This distinction supported the idea that some mobile material was involved rather than requiring uninterrupted living cellular contact across the cut. Gelatin did not prove that the substance was a specific molecule. It provided evidence consistent with a diffusible signal and narrowed the kinds of mechanisms that could account for the observation.

The experimental logic can be made explicit. Suppose two segments of tissue are physically separated by a hydrated layer through which dissolved substances can move. If a normal growth response is retained, the mechanism does not require continuous cytoplasm between the cut cells at that point. If the response is lost after inserting a barrier that blocks passage of dissolved material, a moving substance becomes a stronger explanation. But the barrier can also affect water supply or damage tissue. Experimental conditions must therefore preserve viability, and the conclusion should be expressed in proportion to what the comparison establishes.

Arpad Paál found that placing a coleoptile tip asymmetrically on the remaining stump in darkness could induce curvature. This was important because the direction of incident light was no longer needed to establish asymmetry during the response. The asymmetric position of the source was sufficient to generate unequal growth under the assay conditions. Light normally helps generate the asymmetry, but the tissue can execute differential growth when unequal chemical influence is produced experimentally. The result therefore separates the natural stimulus from the mechanism immediately responsible for the growth difference.

Frits Went used agar blocks to collect diffusible growth-promoting material from coleoptile tips and then applied these blocks to decapitated coleoptiles. A block positioned centrally could promote more symmetric growth; a block placed off-center could induce curvature. This approach helped convert an invisible biological influence into an assayable activity. The assay linked the presence of material transferred to agar with a measurable growth response. Subsequent chemical investigation and physiological research established the identity and role of indole-3-acetic acid. It is important not to credit the earliest curvature observations with a chemical identification that they could not have made.

Imagine that an agar block contains growth-promoting activity and is placed on the left side of an otherwise symmetrical coleoptile stump. If elongation increases on the left side relative to the right side, the organ bends toward its shorter side, which is the right side. The direction of bending is opposite to the more rapidly extending side. An examination question can exploit the intuitive but incorrect claim that an organ bends toward whichever side has the most elongation. A bending organ is a geometric consequence of unequal longitudinal extension, not evidence that the signal itself pulled the tissue toward its source.

Classic assays established a particularly important distinction between a biological activity and a molecular identity. Two compounds can produce similar curvature even though they have different chemical structures, because both can activate overlapping responses. Conversely, a single compound can produce little visible curvature if both sides receive the same effective dose. A bioassay measures the capacity of a sample to cause an output under specific conditions; a chemical analytical method measures the abundance of a chemically defined compound. Bioassays and chemical analyses complement one another but are not interchangeable.

The historical experiments also show that directionality has more than one meaning. Light arrives from a particular direction. Hormone molecules may move along a tissue or become distributed asymmetrically across it. Cells in different positions then expand at different rates. Those directions need not coincide. The demonstration that material can move through agar does not establish which membrane transporters direct its distribution in a living shoot. Nor does it show that every hormone uses the same route. The next chapter develops the molecular basis of polar auxin movement; here the experiments illustrate how the idea of a mobile messenger emerged.

Modern approaches extend the same causal reasoning. A mutation affecting a biosynthetic enzyme can reduce the active hormone pool. A mutation affecting a receptor can reduce the response even when the hormone is present. A reporter can reveal spatial differences in pathway output, and mass spectrometry can measure specific molecules. These independent measurements address questions that were inaccessible in the original bioassays. Their value comes from separating steps in the mechanism rather than merely providing more sophisticated images of the same visible curvature.

Chemical families: diversity within a common regulatory logic

Plant hormones are not a chemically uniform family. Their structures reflect their metabolic origins, and those structures affect their solubility, stability and recognition by proteins. A volatile hydrocarbon behaves differently from an ionizable weak acid; a steroidal compound behaves differently from a peptide. Grouping them by regulatory function is useful because the organism uses them to coordinate growth and physiology, not because all have the same functional groups or travel in the same form.

Auxins include indole-3-acetic acid, commonly abbreviated IAA, a weak organic acid with an indole ring. In many plants a major biosynthetic pathway uses tryptophan, converting it to indole-3-pyruvate through TAA/TAR aminotransferases and subsequently to IAA through YUCCA flavin-containing monooxygenases. IAA can act in local patterning or participate in directional redistribution through tissues. Its active concentration is influenced by synthesis, transport, conjugation and oxidative metabolism. Auxin illustrates how a gradient or a local maximum in hormone action can encode spatial information rather than merely signal that a whole plant should grow faster.

Cytokinins are principally substituted adenine derivatives. Examples include isopentenyladenine and trans-zeatin. Biosynthesis may begin with the attachment of an isoprenoid side chain to adenine nucleotides. Further metabolic steps and release of the free base can yield receptor-active forms. Cytokinin forms differ in activity and transport behavior; cytokinin ribosides and nucleotides should not all be counted as equally active ligands. Cytokinins are especially important in cell proliferation, meristem regulation, nutrient-linked root-to-shoot communication and the regulation of leaf longevity. The relationship with auxin is a recurrent theme in organogenesis.

Gibberellins are diterpenoid-derived compounds formed from geranylgeranyl diphosphate through a series of cyclization and oxidative steps. The large number of structurally identified gibberellins includes both bioactive forms and intermediates or products with little activity in a given assay. GA1 and GA4 are examples of naturally active gibberellins in many species; GA3 is widely used experimentally. A change in total gibberellin-like material may not accurately report a change in the bioactive pool. The regulation of stem elongation and cereal seed reserve mobilization depends on the biologically effective forms and the signaling components available to recognize them.

Brassinosteroids are steroidal regulators derived from plant sterols. Brassinolide and castasterone are prominent examples. Their steroid structure might suggest an analogy with animal steroid hormones, but the major plant brassinosteroid receptor BRI1 is a membrane-associated receptor kinase. The site of perception therefore cannot be inferred solely from the fact that a molecule is a steroid. Brassinosteroids contribute to cell expansion, differentiation and normal reproductive development. Many effects depend on local production and perception, rather than the hormone functioning as a universally long-distance endocrine messenger.

Strigolactones are carotenoid-derived signaling compounds that participate in regulation of branching and in communication between roots and other organisms in the soil. A series of carotenoid modifications gives rise to carlactone, which is subsequently converted into diverse products. Chemical diversity matters because distinct derivatives can differ in activity inside a plant and activity as signals released outside the root. Some compounds released by roots encourage arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal activity; they can also be detected by certain parasitic plants. These ecological interactions are not evidence that every molecule released by the root is a hormone within the plant.

Abscisic acid, abbreviated ABA, is formed in higher plants mainly through the oxidative cleavage of particular carotenoid precursors followed by subsequent conversion of the cleavage product. ABA is important for responses to water limitation and for seed maturation and dormancy. It is a particularly useful example of the relation between environmental conditions and controlled hormone availability. An increase in ABA during dehydration may arise from new synthesis, release from conjugated forms, altered movement or reduced removal. The pathway therefore cannot be fully understood by measuring expression of a single biosynthetic gene.

Ethylene is a gaseous two-carbon alkene. It is produced from methionine through S-adenosylmethionine and the intermediate 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid, abbreviated ACC. ACC oxidase catalyzes formation of ethylene from ACC and requires oxygen. Ethylene can diffuse through gas spaces and exchange with the surrounding atmosphere. Its precursor ACC can move through aqueous tissues and may also be conjugated or have signaling roles. The chemical distinction between ACC and ethylene explains why an aqueous precursor measurement is not a direct measurement of the gas that reaches a receptor.

Jasmonates derive from oxygenated fatty acid metabolism. In a well-characterized pathway, alpha-linolenic acid gives rise to 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, or OPDA, in plastids, followed by peroxisomal conversion toward jasmonic acid. Conjugation with isoleucine produces JA-Ile, a major ligand for canonical jasmonate signaling in Arabidopsis. Some other oxylipins can have biological effects of their own. Jasmonate signaling is prominent in responses to tissue injury and herbivory and in several developmental processes. The fact that JA-Ile is a conjugate is an immediate warning against treating all hormone conjugation as inactivation.

Salicylic acid is a phenolic weak acid with important roles in defense responses, including those associated with many biotrophic pathogens. Its biosynthesis can involve isochorismate-derived or phenylalanine-associated routes; relative contributions differ among species and circumstances. Conjugation can alter its free concentration, and conversion to derivatives can influence movement or subsequent regeneration of salicylic acid. Salicylic acid signaling involves NPR proteins, with NPR1 especially important for transcriptional regulation of many defense genes. Its relationship with jasmonate signaling is conditional rather than a universal pair of mutually exclusive switches.

Plants also use peptide hormones. These are generally encoded as precursor proteins or polypeptides and processed to biologically active forms. Some undergo modifications that influence receptor recognition. Examples include CLE peptides involved in meristem regulation and systemin in particular plant defense contexts. Many peptide signals are perceived by cell-surface receptor complexes. Unlike a small molecule generated through several metabolic enzymes, a peptide signal may originate from transcription, translation, proteolytic processing and secretion. Peptides are therefore introduced as an additional major signaling category, without suggesting that every small signaling peptide shares the same biological role.

Chemical classification is a foundation for reasoning, not a substitute for it. A student who knows that ABA and strigolactones have carotenoid-related origins should not conclude that they bind the same receptor. A student who knows that auxin and salicylic acid are weak acids should not infer identical transport specificity. Chemical similarity can suggest testable possibilities, but ligand recognition depends on the architecture of the proteins and on the cellular system in which those proteins operate. The physiological identity of each signal is established through evidence about synthesis, perception and response.

Biosynthesis, activation and the active hormone pool

Biosynthesis is the set of enzymatic reactions through which living cells construct a molecule from precursor compounds. A biosynthetic pathway does not necessarily end at the first compound that resembles a hormone. It may produce intermediates that must be modified before a receptor recognizes them effectively. A precursor can accumulate when the next enzyme is absent, even though the amount of the active product falls. This distinction provides the basis for interpreting mutants that affect metabolism rather than perception.

Consider a pathway in which enzyme E1 converts a precursor P into intermediate I, and enzyme E2 converts I into bioactive hormone H. If E1 activity is lost, both I and H may decrease. If E2 is lost, I may increase while H decreases. Supplying I could rescue an E1 mutant if E2 remains active, whereas supplying I would not necessarily rescue a complete E2 mutant. Supplying H could bypass either biosynthetic block provided that H enters the relevant cells, persists long enough and is recognized normally. Such a rescue is evidence that the downstream pathway can respond, although it does not exclude additional functions of the affected gene.

Hormone production is spatially organized because the enzymes involved are expressed differently across tissues and because their substrates are not uniformly available. In a growing root, distinct groups of cells can produce or remove auxin; in a leaf undergoing dehydration, ABA biosynthetic capacity may change. A hormone can be synthesized near its target or imported from elsewhere. A spatial pattern of active hormone concentration therefore cannot be deduced from a biosynthetic promoter alone. The promoter reveals one component of potential production, not the combined effect of transport, metabolic conversion and removal.

Compartmentation within cells also matters. Plastids are sites of carotenoid metabolism and early stages of several hormone pathways. The cytosol, endoplasmic reticulum and peroxisomes contribute to other reactions depending on the class of hormone. When a product formed in one compartment must be processed in another, movement across an organelle boundary can affect pathway flux. The formation of an intermediate in a plastid does not imply that the receptor detecting the final hormone must be plastid-localized. Biosynthetic localization, storage localization and receptor localization are distinct properties.

Plants can regulate production by changing enzyme abundance, activity or substrate availability. An increase in the messenger RNA encoding a biosynthetic enzyme may precede an increase in hormone content, but the relationship is not necessarily immediate or proportional. The existing protein must be considered, and the enzyme may need a substrate or cofactor that becomes limiting. Other pathway enzymes may be slow, and removal or export may compensate for increased production. A transcript measurement is a useful indicator of regulation but does not directly quantify molecular flux.

Some environmental stimuli change hormone production rapidly. Mechanical injury can lead to accumulation of jasmonate-related compounds; dehydration can enhance ABA biosynthesis; developmental and environmental stimuli can increase ethylene production through changes in ACC synthase and other steps. The timing of hormone accumulation depends on the tissue and stimulus. A leaf might alter its response before a large increase is detectable in a whole-leaf extract, especially if the relevant changes occur in a small group of cells. Sampling time and spatial resolution are therefore essential to the interpretation of biochemical data.

The biosynthetic origin of a hormone can help identify likely experimental interventions. A lesion in a carotenoid cleavage enzyme can affect the production of a hormone derived from carotenoids, but carotenoid metabolism also serves other processes. A broad inhibitor of carotenoid formation may alter photoprotection and pigment composition in addition to reducing certain hormone pools. Such a treatment should not be interpreted as selectively removing only ABA or only strigolactones. A more specific mutant, measurement of the relevant metabolites and rescue with the active hormone can provide stronger causal evidence.

A useful way to organize the active pool is to distinguish formation rate from abundance. If a tissue contains 20 picomoles of a hormone, this number states how much was present at the moment of sampling. It does not state whether the tissue synthesized 20 picomoles in the preceding hour. A tissue can maintain a stable amount while producing and removing many more molecules than are present at any one time. The molecules turn over even though the pool size remains steady. The notion of steady state is therefore a statement about balanced rates, not about inactivity.

A further distinction concerns amount and concentration. If a growing organ maintains the same amount of hormone while its volume doubles, the mean concentration falls by half. Conversely, a water loss that reduces tissue volume can change concentration even without immediate synthesis or removal. Experimental measurements reported per gram fresh mass, per gram dry mass, per cell or per unit volume answer different questions. Under dehydration, fresh mass itself is changing, so apparent increases in hormone per gram fresh weight should be evaluated with care. This does not invalidate such normalization; it defines the limitations of its interpretation.

The active chemical form must also be specified. Free auxin can be distinguished from several conjugated forms. Free cytokinin bases can be distinguished from nucleotides and ribosides. Bioactive GAs can be distinguished from precursors and inactive oxidative products. Ethylene must be distinguished from ACC. A method that hydrolyzes conjugates during sample preparation may measure a larger potential pool than a method designed to quantify only the free hormone. Both measurements can be meaningful, but they cannot be compared without knowing what chemical forms are being detected.

Hormonal activation is not limited to synthesis of a free small molecule. A precursor protein can be cleaved to produce a peptide hormone; a conjugation reaction can generate JA-Ile; an inactive cytokinin nucleotide can be converted into a receptor-active base by a LOG enzyme. These examples establish a general principle that activation is defined by the acquisition of biological signaling activity in a particular system. It is not synonymous with one chemical reaction, such as hydrolysis. The chemistry and receptor preferences of the particular family determine which conversion is activating.

The consequences of altering a pathway can be assessed at several levels. Metabolite analysis can establish whether active product abundance changes, gene-expression analysis can assess selected downstream outputs, and physiological assays can establish whether growth or function changes. Each contributes a different piece of evidence. Measuring a short stem does not identify the deficient hormone; measuring a low hormone concentration does not show whether that deficiency is the only cause of the phenotype. Combining independent measurements with targeted interventions makes the causal account more convincing.
