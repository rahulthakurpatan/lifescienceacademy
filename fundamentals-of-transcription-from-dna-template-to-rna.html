1. Why cells require an RNA copy of genetic information

A cell faces two different demands that cannot be satisfied simply by making more DNA. It must retain genetic information with sufficient stability for transmission to daughter cells, and it must use particular parts of that information whenever their products are needed. A chromosome contains instructions for many activities, but a growing cell does not require all its possible products in equal amounts at every moment. Some genes must be expressed continuously, others must respond to a change in nutrient availability, and still others must remain almost silent. Selective synthesis of RNA provides a molecular solution to this difference between storing information and using it. Transcription is the production of an RNA molecule by reading a nucleic acid template; in the ordinary cellular process studied in this chapter, the template is DNA.

A DNA molecule does not have to be consumed to produce an RNA molecule. Its sequence is used to select the order of incoming ribonucleotides, which are connected into a new covalent chain. Once the chain is released, the DNA remains available for a subsequent round of transcription. It is therefore possible for one DNA region to give rise to many RNA molecules over time. An increase in RNA output does not require a corresponding increase in chromosome number. This property is central to the regulation of cellular physiology. A cell can change the use of its genome without first changing the sequence of that genome.

Imagine a bacterium whose chromosome contains the instructions for enzymes involved in utilization of two different nutrients. When only one of the nutrients is available, it would be wasteful to produce large amounts of both groups of enzymes. A suitable change in transcription can alter the amount of messenger RNA for one group relative to the other. The effect of this alteration depends on subsequent translation and protein lifetime, but it begins with the selective use of DNA. In a multicellular organism, a similar principle operates on a larger scale. Two differentiated cell types can possess essentially the same genomic sequences and nevertheless accumulate very different collections of RNAs and proteins. Differences in gene expression, rather than differences in the DNA sequence of every expressed gene, help explain their distinct functions.

RNA is not always an intermediate that will be translated into protein. Ribosomal RNA becomes a major structural and catalytic component of ribosomes. Transfer RNA participates in the selection and delivery of amino acids during translation. Other RNA products assist processing or regulation of nucleic acids. In these cases, the RNA molecule itself is an important functional product of gene expression. The general definition of transcription must therefore refer to RNA synthesis rather than to messenger RNA synthesis alone. The basic chemical reaction applies whether the completed RNA eventually serves as a messenger, adaptor, structural component or regulatory molecule.

In the usual diagram of genetic information transfer, replication is represented as DNA to DNA, transcription as DNA to RNA, and translation as RNA to protein. These arrows indicate the direction in which sequence information is used. They do not mean that DNA is chemically transformed into RNA or that RNA is transformed into protein. In each reaction, a template helps determine the order of a new chain assembled from its own substrates. Distinguishing information transfer from material conversion is particularly useful when interpreting radioactive labeling experiments. Label incorporated into newly synthesized RNA originates in the RNA precursor pool; it does not have to come from a labeled DNA backbone.

The following sections develop the fundamental consequences of this arrangement. First, the evidence for a messenger and a DNA-directed polymerizing enzyme establishes why the mechanism was proposed. Next, chemical structure explains how a nucleotide becomes attached to a growing RNA. Finally, the polarity of nucleic acid strands and the orientation of a promoter explain which DNA sequence is transcribed, where the transcript begins, and why a polymerase moves in a particular direction.

2. Discovery of the RNA intermediate

Once the structure of DNA was established, a major biological problem was to explain how the order of its bases could influence the order of amino acids in a protein. In eukaryotes, the bulk of the genetic material is in the nucleus, whereas much protein synthesis occurs in the cytoplasm. Even in bacteria, which lack a nuclear envelope, the DNA chromosome and the ribosomal machinery are functionally distinct systems. A direct association of every actively translated protein sequence with the chromosome seemed insufficient to explain how many copies of a protein could be produced. The existence of an intermediate carrying sequence information provided an attractive explanation, but an attractive explanation is not yet evidence.

One historical possibility was that each gene produced a particular ribosome specialized for synthesizing one protein. Under that model, a new kind of protein might require the assembly of a new kind of ribosome. Another possibility was that ribosomes were reusable machinery and obtained their specific instructions from a more temporary RNA molecule. An experiment capable of separating old ribosomes from newly synthesized RNA could discriminate between these alternatives. The crucial issue was not whether RNA was present in a ribosome fraction, since ribosomes themselves contain RNA. The issue was whether a newly supplied genetic program could direct protein synthesis using previously existing ribosomes.

In 1961, Sidney Brenner, François Jacob and Matthew Meselson published a study of RNA synthesized after bacteriophage infection of Escherichia coli. A bacteriophage introduces genetic information that leads to a change in the types of protein produced by the infected bacterium. The investigators used heavy nitrogen and carbon isotopes to distinguish ribosomes made before infection from newly synthesized material following a change to lighter medium. Newly made RNA was detected through radioactive precursor labeling. By combining molecular age and density information, they could test whether the new RNA appeared with ribosomes already present before the genetic program changed. The study supported the interpretation that a newly made, relatively unstable RNA associated with ribosomes that had existed before infection. A new set of specialized ribosomes was not required for every newly expressed protein. The important result was an association between new RNA information and old translational machinery, rather than a simple demonstration that bacteria contain RNA.

A student analyzing such an experiment should ask what each label identifies. A stable isotope changes the mass of a molecule without changing the identity of its chemical element. A radioactive isotope can be used to detect newly incorporated material. If the components of a system are labeled at different times, their association can be examined without assuming that all material in a separated fraction has been produced at the same time. A density gradient separates particles according to their physical properties under the chosen conditions; detection of radioactivity in a fraction supplies a second type of evidence. Appropriate controls are necessary to establish the positions of heavy and light ribosomes and to distinguish new RNA from background labeling.

The findings do not imply that every RNA molecule is short-lived. Ribosomal and transfer RNAs can persist much longer than many messenger RNAs under comparable conditions. Nor did the experiment by itself establish the detailed chemistry of nucleotide incorporation or identify all the factors required for promoter recognition. It answered a question about the relationship of an unstable RNA intermediate to an existing translational apparatus. Later experiments examining synthesis by isolated enzyme systems and the relationship between DNA and RNA sequences addressed the molecular copying mechanism. These are distinct levels of evidence.

The historical lesson remains useful for modern examination questions. When an observation is compatible with two competing models, look for a prediction that differs between them. A radioactive RNA appearing near ribosomes would be insufficient if both models predicted such an appearance. Its association with particles that were demonstrably made before the change in genetic program is more informative. Likewise, a change in RNA abundance after infection might indicate altered RNA synthesis, altered degradation, or both. An experimental conclusion should match the particular variable that the experiment actually measured.

3. Establishing DNA-directed RNA polymerization

The existence of messenger RNA creates a second question: how is its sequence produced? A plausible answer is that an enzyme uses DNA to order ribonucleotides by complementary base pairing. However, a chemical system containing DNA and RNA does not necessarily carry out such a reaction. RNA might be present as a contaminant, or an enzyme might add nucleotides without copying a template. Evidence for DNA-directed synthesis requires showing that the product is newly formed and that its formation or sequence depends on the DNA supplied.

By 1960, several independent research groups, including those of Samuel Weiss, Jerry Hurwitz, Audrey Stevens and James Bonner, had demonstrated activities supporting template-directed RNA synthesis. Early studies of nucleotide incorporation established an essential enzymatic step in the flow of genetic information. DNA dependence and sequence complementarity were further investigated using isolated reaction systems. The historically important finding was a DNA-stimulated polymerizing activity, not a claim that every initial enzyme preparation could reproduce normal promoter specificity. An informative biochemical experiment uses a preparation containing RNA polymerase and a defined DNA template, together with ribonucleoside triphosphates and appropriate reaction conditions. Formation of RNA can be followed by incorporation of labeled nucleotide into material that is separated from unincorporated small molecules. At this level, the experimental aim is to distinguish polymerized nucleotide from nucleotide that merely remains in solution. The appearance of a labeled macromolecular fraction is evidence of incorporation, provided that the procedure actually separates free label and that background controls are satisfactory.

The comparison becomes stronger when separate reactions omit the enzyme, the DNA template or an essential substrate. Without catalytically active polymerase, template-directed chain formation should be severely reduced or absent in an otherwise defined system. Removing the DNA should prevent DNA-directed synthesis, although residual activity in a crude preparation may reveal contaminating templates or other enzymes. Omitting one nucleotide species will limit extension through positions where that nucleotide is required. These controls address the causal contribution of each component. A single positive tube cannot establish which component was responsible for a detected product.

An RNase-sensitive labeled product is consistent with RNA formation, but RNase sensitivity alone does not identify the sequence of the product. To determine whether information has been copied from DNA, the investigator can compare the product with known template sequences or test specific hybridization between the RNA and complementary DNA. For example, if a defined template is changed at one position and the corresponding RNA base changes in the predicted manner, the evidence directly connects template sequence and product sequence. This reasoning is stronger than merely showing that total synthesis increases when DNA is added.

A further distinction concerns the enzyme's ability to initiate a chain. An enzyme that only extends a supplied oligonucleotide might still produce template-dependent RNA, yet it would not establish that transcription can initiate without a pre-existing primer. A suitable experiment provides an initiation-competent template, appropriate transcription machinery and the necessary ribonucleotides, but no added primer. Controls are required to exclude contaminating short nucleic acid primers if that is a plausible concern. Detection of products with newly formed 5′ ends under these conditions supports de novo initiation. The precise factors required for initiation differ across organisms and polymerases; the experimental principle does not depend on treating all RNA polymerases as identical.

These examples illustrate a hierarchy of conclusions. Incorporation of nucleotide label establishes that a chemical product is being made only if the detection procedure is valid. Product identity requires evidence that it is RNA. Template dependence requires comparison with reactions lacking or altering DNA. Copying fidelity requires a comparison between the known template and the RNA sequence. De novo initiation requires evidence that the first nucleotide does not come from an existing primer. In examinations, a distractor often jumps from one level of evidence to another. A researcher who measures total nucleotide incorporation has not necessarily measured the number of completed transcripts or established their sequences.

4. DNA and RNA as chemically related information carriers

The ability to copy DNA into RNA depends on two properties of nucleic acids. Their chains have a chemically defined direction, and their bases can recognize complementary partners. The first property constrains the direction of enzyme-catalyzed chain extension. The second makes the sequence of a template informative. Neither property depends on a base being a part of a protein-coding region. A nucleotide in a noncoding region can be copied using precisely the same chemical logic as a nucleotide in a coding region.

Each nucleic acid nucleotide contains a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar and phosphate. Adenine and guanine are purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines. A nucleoside consists of the base and sugar without phosphate. In a nucleotide, one or more phosphates are attached. DNA normally contains deoxyribose and thymine, whereas RNA contains ribose and ordinarily uracil. The structural difference between ribose and deoxyribose at the 2′ position is especially important for the chemical behavior of their polymers. RNA has a hydroxyl group at that position; DNA has hydrogen instead.

The 2′ hydroxyl contributes to the greater susceptibility of RNA to backbone cleavage under alkaline conditions. Under appropriate conditions, a deprotonated 2′ oxygen can attack the neighboring phosphate within the RNA chain, producing cleavage products through an intramolecular reaction. DNA lacks the corresponding 2′ hydroxyl and cannot follow the same pathway. This difference helps explain why RNA and DNA have different chemical stability profiles. It does not mean every RNA molecule degrades immediately in the cell. RNA folding, binding proteins and the regulation of nucleases all influence biological lifetime. Conversely, a DNA molecule can be damaged or degraded by other reactions despite lacking the 2′ hydroxyl.

The sugar carbons are numbered with primes to distinguish them from positions in the base. The 1′ carbon is attached to the base, the 3′ position provides a hydroxyl group for chain extension, and phosphate associated with the 5′ position is central to formation of the backbone. In a conventional polynucleotide, a phosphodiester linkage joins the 3′ oxygen of one sugar to the 5′ oxygen of the next sugar through a phosphate. A chain with such linkages is chemically asymmetric even if its sequence of bases happens to read the same in either direction. A 5′ end is not interchangeable with a 3′ end.

Complementary base pairing arises from the arrangement of chemical groups that can donate or accept hydrogen bonds and from the geometry of the bases. In standard RNA-DNA pairing, RNA adenine pairs with DNA thymine, RNA uracil with DNA adenine, and RNA guanine with DNA cytosine. RNA cytosine pairs with DNA guanine. These relationships are the foundation for sequence conversion problems, but it is the enzyme that helps select and position the correct substrate in the context of a template. Base pairing by itself does not form the phosphodiester bonds required to create a covalent RNA chain.

A double-stranded DNA molecule has two antiparallel strands. If the upper strand is drawn with its 5′ end at the left and its 3′ end at the right, the lower complementary strand runs from 3′ at the left to 5′ at the right. This arrangement allows both strands to serve as potential sources of template information, but in opposite directions. A gene is not required to use the upper or lower strand by convention. The orientation of the transcription unit establishes the relevant template. The role of a particular strand must always be defined relative to the gene under consideration.

5. The actual substrates: ribonucleoside triphosphates

RNA polymerase normally uses ATP, GTP, CTP and UTP as substrates for DNA-directed RNA synthesis. These are adenosine, guanosine, cytidine and uridine triphosphates. Each supplies a ribonucleoside monophosphate residue to the growing RNA after the appropriate bond-forming reaction. The triphosphate form has a chemical role beyond carrying the base: it makes the incoming nucleotide an activated substrate. A ribonucleoside alone lacks the phosphates needed for the usual polymerization chemistry.

The three phosphates of an incoming nucleoside triphosphate are designated alpha, beta and gamma, beginning with the phosphate closest to the sugar. During ordinary chain extension, the alpha phosphate becomes the new backbone phosphate. The beta and gamma phosphates depart together as pyrophosphate, often represented as PPi. This provides a useful way to remember what becomes part of the RNA. A triphosphate does not remain attached at every internal position. If it did, the backbone would contain three phosphates between successive sugars rather than the usual single phosphodiester-linked phosphate.

The initiating nucleotide is a special case. In typical de novo initiation by a cellular RNA polymerase using standard NTPs, the first nucleotide retains its 5′ triphosphate in the immediate primary product. Its 3′ hydroxyl then participates in making the first internucleotide linkage. For each later nucleotide, the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphate is retained in the backbone while the other two phosphates are released. This difference matters for labeling problems. A gamma-phosphate label can be retained at the original 5′ end if the appropriately labeled nucleotide is used to initiate transcription and that end remains unmodified; it is not normally retained in the backbone during elongation.

The same ATP molecule that serves as a building block for RNA is also used in many other cellular energy-transfer reactions. Its role depends on the reaction in question. When ATP is selected opposite DNA thymine in the polymerase active site, its adenine base becomes part of the RNA sequence. It is not simply hydrolyzed by a separate motor to cause the incorporation of some unrelated nucleotide. GTP, CTP and UTP likewise contribute their own residues and activated phosphate groups. Accessory proteins and other transcription-associated processes can consume nucleoside triphosphates separately, but an extra ATP hydrolysis is not an obligatory universal step for each nucleotide added by RNA polymerase.

Consider an RNA sequence written as 5′-AGCUA-3′. The sequence requires two adenine residues and one each of guanine, cytosine and uracil. If the RNA starts with ATP by de novo initiation and is not subsequently modified, the first ATP contributes the 5′ triphosphate as well as the first adenine-containing residue. The second through fifth nucleotides each cause the release of one pyrophosphate during formation of four linkages. This example illustrates why a count of RNA residues is not identical to a count of internucleotide bonds. It also illustrates why the chemistry of initiation must be stated when performing phosphate-label calculations.

Nucleotide concentrations can influence transcription, because a substrate must be available at the moment its complementary template position is encountered. However, it would be misleading to assume that a high concentration of only one NTP can generally replace a shortage of another. High concentrations do not change the standard correspondence between template bases and product bases. At a position requiring UTP, excess ATP cannot normally produce an accurate extension. A shortage of one substrate can lead to slower elongation, accumulation of paused complexes or premature products, depending on the system and conditions. Such effects can be interpreted only with knowledge of the template sequence and the experiment's measured output.

6. Formation of the phosphodiester bond and energetic coupling

The elementary elongation reaction can be represented as RNA(n) + NTP → RNA(n+1) + PPi, where RNA(n) is the growing chain containing n residues. The terminal 3′ hydroxyl is the reactive group that participates in formation of the next linkage. The incoming NTP is positioned by complementary interaction with the DNA template and by the enzyme's active site. The resulting product possesses a new terminal 3′ hydroxyl, so the chemical arrangement can be used again during the next step. This repetition explains how a polymerase can assemble a chain hundreds or thousands of residues long without changing the fundamental linkage chemistry.

Divalent metal ions, commonly magnesium ions, are essential to the catalytic mechanism of standard cellular RNA polymerases. A simplified two-metal-ion picture helps explain how the enzyme facilitates nucleophilic attack at the incoming nucleotide's alpha phosphorus and stabilizes charge during the reaction. The metal ions help organize reactive groups and lower the activation barrier. They are not consumed as a component of every RNA phosphodiester bond. A reaction may fail when magnesium is removed or chelated even when DNA and nucleotides are present, because the catalytic environment has been disrupted.

Catalysis and thermodynamic driving force are different concepts. An enzyme accelerates a reaction by providing a pathway with a lower activation barrier; it does not change the equilibrium constant for a specified reaction. The chemical potential of the nucleotide triphosphate and the concentration of products contribute to the driving force of chain formation. Hydrolysis of released pyrophosphate to two orthophosphate molecules can favor net polymerization by lowering the amount of PPi available for the reverse reaction. This subsequent hydrolysis is chemically distinct from formation of the RNA linkage. Describing both processes as a single ATP hydrolysis obscures what actually happens.

It is possible under appropriate conditions for a polymerase to carry out the reverse of a nucleotide-addition reaction in the presence of pyrophosphate. This reaction, called pyrophosphorolysis, illustrates that the chemical direction of the elementary reaction is not enforced by a rule that forbids reversal. Cellular conditions, substrate supply, product removal and the organization of the transcription complex promote net extension. The biological direction of RNA synthesis refers to how successive nucleotides are added to the growing chain, not to an absolute claim that every individual catalytic step is chemically irreversible.

The difference between the number of residues and the number of phosphodiester bonds can be derived from simple chain construction. A single nucleotide has no internucleotide linkage. Adding a second nucleotide creates one linkage. Adding a third creates one more, giving two linkages. Each subsequent addition increases both the residue count and the linkage count by one, preserving a difference of one. A linear, unbranched RNA with L residues therefore has L − 1 internucleotide phosphodiester bonds. Under the stated standard de novo mechanism, construction of that RNA releases L − 1 molecules of pyrophosphate during incorporation of the second through last nucleotide. Subsequent processing and nonstandard initiation chemistry can alter the ends or number of bonds in a final RNA product, so the relationship must be applied to the molecular form specified in the question.

For an unprocessed 800-residue primary RNA made by standard initiation, the number of incorporated ribonucleotides is 800 and the number of backbone linkages is 799. If all released PPi is subsequently hydrolyzed, 1598 orthophosphate molecules arise from that pool of PPi. No assumption about the composition of the sequence is needed for this calculation. In contrast, calculation of the amount of UTP incorporated requires information about the number of DNA adenines in the transcribed template segment. Chemistry determines the total bond count, whereas sequence determines the distribution among four substrate types.

7. Why RNA grows from 5′ to 3′

The direction of RNA synthesis is a consequence of the chemical arrangement of the growing chain and the substrates available to the enzyme. During elongation, the existing chain supplies its free terminal 3′ hydroxyl and the incoming nucleotide supplies an activated 5′ phosphate group. After the linkage forms, the incoming sugar becomes the new terminal residue and presents a new 3′ hydroxyl. The previous 3′ end is no longer the end of the chain. It has become an internal position connected to the next nucleotide. Repeated use of this arrangement makes the RNA progressively longer at its 3′ end. The chain is said to grow in the 5′ to 3′ direction even though the growing chemical bond is being formed at the 3′ end.

Students sometimes assume that a 5′ to 3′ polymerase must move along a DNA strand whose sequence is also written 5′ to 3′ in the same direction. This confuses product polarity with template polarity. The RNA and the DNA strand that it copies form antiparallel base pairs. If the RNA is growing toward the right in a diagram, its 5′ end lies to the left of its 3′ end. The template under that RNA must have its 3′ orientation to the left and its 5′ orientation to the right. The polymerase therefore reads successive template positions in the 3′ to 5′ direction while producing the RNA from 5′ to 3′. Both descriptions refer to the same molecular movement, not to two opposite polymerase motions.

Consider a very short template written 3′-TACG-5′. Suppose the first transcribed template position is the thymine at the left. RNA polymerase selects ATP, then UTP, then GTP, then CTP in the simplified sequence shown. The RNA product is 5′-AUGC-3′. The first base incorporated is adenine, and the last is cytosine. If a student reverses the RNA sequence because the two strands are antiparallel, the answer becomes incorrect: the direction has already been accounted for by placing the RNA 5′ end opposite the template 3′ end. The bases should be complemented from left to right when the template is already written 3′ to 5′.

The situation changes when the template is presented 5′ to 3′. For example, the same template segment above can be written 5′-GCAT-3′. The physical molecule has not changed; only the convention of writing it has changed. A polymerase reading from its 3′ end now moves from the rightmost symbol toward the leftmost symbol. To report the RNA in the conventional 5′ to 3′ form, first read the template in reverse order and then substitute complementary RNA bases. The answer remains 5′-AUGC-3′. The reversal is necessary because the template was originally displayed in the opposite direction from the order in which it is read.

Directionality must not be confused with the absolute orientation of a drawing or chromosome. A polymerase can move leftward on a particular map if the template for that transcription unit has its 3′ to 5′ polarity toward the left. The fact that the printed arrow points left does not indicate 3′ to 5′ RNA synthesis. It indicates the position and direction of transcription relative to the page. The new RNA still extends at its 3′ end. A useful habit is to label both ends of the DNA strands before attempting any question involving an arrow, inversion or neighboring gene.

This chemical explanation also helps identify a genuine distinction between RNA polymerase and a nuclease. A nuclease hydrolyzing a chain can remove residues in a direction defined by the end from which it begins; a polymerase adds residues to a growing chain. Saying that an enzyme acts in a 3′ to 5′ direction is incomplete unless the context specifies whether the enzyme is reading a template, extending a product or removing nucleotides. During transcription, the standard polymerization direction of the product is 5′ to 3′, and the complementary template is read 3′ to 5′.

8. Complementarity, the coding strand and the template strand

For a particular transcription unit in a double-stranded DNA molecule, the strand whose bases are read by the polymerase is the template strand, sometimes called the antisense strand in relation to that transcript. The complementary DNA strand is the non-template strand. For a protein-coding transcription unit, this strand is conventionally called the coding or sense strand because its sequence, read in the same 5′ to 3′ order as the primary RNA, matches the RNA sequence apart from the substitution of uracil for thymine and possible later alterations to the RNA. This terminology does not mean that the non-template strand is directly read during synthesis of that RNA.

For example, consider the DNA segment with coding strand 5′-ATGCCATTA-3′ and template strand 3′-TACGGTAAT-5′. If the entire displayed segment is transcribed in the direction from left to right, the primary RNA segment is 5′-AUGCCAUUA-3′. The coding DNA sequence contains thymine wherever the RNA contains uracil. The template has complementary bases at every position. These three sequences describe one copying relationship. They are not three independent information sources or three distinct transcription events.

The term coding strand is also used by convention in discussions of noncoding transcription units, although non-template is clearer when there is no protein-coding message. An RNA that will function without translation still resembles the non-template DNA sequence in the same local copying relationship. The word coding in coding strand should not be interpreted as evidence that the entire strand or every part of a transcript encodes amino acids. Even a protein-coding gene can contain transcribed regions that are not translated. Those regions follow the same base-pairing rules as the region that eventually specifies a polypeptide.

A sequence problem is easiest when the question supplies the coding strand in its conventional direction. Under the assumption that the displayed region is transcribed and the product is an unmodified primary sequence, replace T with U and preserve base order. If the question supplies the template strand in the 3′ to 5′ direction, complement each base and preserve order. If the template is supplied in the 5′ to 3′ direction, reverse the order and complement. A coding strand displayed 3′ to 5′ requires reversal without complementation before T is replaced with U. These are not four different biological processes. They are four ways of describing the same pair of antiparallel molecules.

To illustrate the fourth case, suppose a coding DNA segment is written 3′-TTACG-5′. Reversing the order produces 5′-GCATT-3′. Replacing thymine with uracil yields 5′-GCAUU-3′. Complementing the originally displayed sequence instead would generate a sequence associated with the opposite strand rather than the specified transcript. The end labels and the identity of the strand must both be considered. Memorizing a rule such as replace T with U without checking those two conditions will fail whenever the question changes the representation.

In practice, a double-stranded DNA sequence without a promoter or other directional information does not define a unique transcript. It defines two potential complementary sequence orientations. To infer which is used, the question must identify the template, the coding strand, a transcription arrow, a promoter orientation or experimentally determined transcript sequence. A DNA region can in principle contain multiple transcription units, some using one strand and some the other. In overlapping transcription, two distinct RNAs can even share a portion of the DNA coordinate interval while having different sequences because they are copied from opposite strands.

There is an additional qualification concerning the relationship between the RNA and the coding strand. The simple equality applies to the templated portion of the initial transcript, assuming accurate copying. Processing may remove internal sequences, alter bases or add nucleotides at an end. Thus a mature eukaryotic RNA may not be an uninterrupted sequence identical to one continuous region of coding DNA after T-to-U replacement. It is not necessary to learn the detailed processing mechanisms at this point; the important distinction is that the copying rule predicts a primary templated sequence, while the molecular history of the final RNA may include other reactions.

9. Sequence construction from incomplete and inverted representations

One valuable way to check a proposed transcript is to reconstruct the DNA duplex from it. Suppose an unmodified primary RNA segment is 5′-UAGGCU-3′. The corresponding non-template DNA segment, written 5′ to 3′, is 5′-TAGGCT-3′. The template can be written antiparallel beneath it as 3′-ATCCGA-5′. If a question requests the template in the usual 5′ to 3′ form, it becomes 5′-AGCCTA-3′. Checking the answer by aligning the RNA and template in opposite orientations avoids many mistakes that result from performing a mental reverse complement without tracking strand ends.

The same method can be used when some positions of a duplex have been omitted. If an RNA product contains guanine at its fourth position, the corresponding template position should contain cytosine, assuming faithful standard transcription. The non-template position contains guanine. This relationship allows a student to fill missing bases without having to reconstruct the entire sequence. However, if the supplied RNA is edited or if an error has occurred during transcription, the prediction may not hold. Questions should state or imply the standard unmodified case before a simple one-to-one relation is assumed.

Inversions are another source of confusion. Imagine that a promoter and the DNA region it transcribes are inverted together as an intact block. Relative to a fixed chromosome coordinate system, the transcription direction reverses. If the same promoter remains capable of recruiting its transcription machinery and the internal relationships between promoter and gene remain unchanged, the intrinsic RNA sequence may be unchanged. This is because the same information-bearing duplex is now located in the opposite orientation, rather than the gene being arbitrarily assigned to a different template. In contrast, reversing only a promoter while leaving the downstream sequence fixed may direct transcription into a different DNA region. The outcome depends on the actual orientation and location of all relevant elements.

Consider a defined educational map. A plus-reference DNA strand has the sequence 5′-ATGCCGTA-3′ across an interval. A rightward transcript that covers this entire interval has the sequence 5′-AUGCCGUA-3′. A leftward transcript covering the same interval instead uses the plus-reference strand as its template, read from the right. Its RNA sequence is 5′-UACGGCAU-3′. These RNAs are reverse complements of each other in the standard case. They are not identical simply because their coordinates overlap. The sequence of a transcript is determined by both the interval and the strand.

At the exam level, it is useful to distinguish three operations on a sequence. Reversal changes the order of symbols but not their chemical identities. Complementation changes every base to its pairing partner but does not by itself change the order in which symbols are written. A reverse complement performs both operations and is necessary when comparing two antiparallel strands written in the same conventional direction. A student should be able to explain which operation represents a change in notation and which represents a change in molecular identity. Reversal alone is a change in presentation, whereas the complementary strand is a different physical chain.

A sequence can be self-reverse-complementary over a particular interval. For example, the DNA sequence 5′-GAATTC-3′ has a complementary strand that reads the same in the conventional 5′ to 3′ notation. This is a property of that short sequence, not a general characteristic of DNA or a reason that strand orientation may be ignored. If a transcribed interval were exactly symmetric in that sense, the two hypothetical primary RNA sequences could coincidentally match. The transcription events would still have distinct directions and templates. Sequence identity in a special case is not proof that the same DNA strand was read.

When interpreting gene diagrams, it is often more reliable to annotate the transcription direction first and only then examine sequence details. Begin at the location of +1, follow the arrow to the end of the relevant interval, identify the strand that runs 3′ to 5′ along that path, and write its complementary RNA. This procedure unites spatial reasoning and chemistry. A diagram can be rotated, the reference strand can be exchanged, and the sequence notation can be reversed without changing the underlying molecular rule.

10. The transcription unit as a spatial and functional entity

A transcription unit is a region of DNA associated with the production of an RNA from a defined initiation region through a downstream transcribed region to the region where transcription ends. In a simplified introductory diagram, its principal features are a promoter, a transcription start site, the DNA region copied into RNA, and a termination region. The promoter provides information used by the transcription machinery to initiate at the appropriate position and orientation. The start site identifies the first nucleotide position that is actually transcribed. The copied region provides the sequence information of the transcript. Termination enables the synthesis event to end and the machinery to be released or reorganized for further use.

These elements are not necessarily separate pieces with visible boundaries in an actual chromosome. They are functional designations assigned on the basis of DNA sequence, molecular interactions and experimental evidence. Some promoter elements lie upstream of the start site; in some systems important promoter elements can overlap the start site or extend downstream. A gene diagram showing a promoter as a rectangular block entirely upstream is a useful simplification, but it is not a universal statement about promoter architecture. The exact organization of bacterial and eukaryotic promoter elements belongs to later chapters. Here the essential principle is that a promoter directs initiation rather than specifying an amino acid sequence.

A transcription unit may contain one protein-coding region, several coding regions or no protein-coding region at all. In bacteria, an RNA transcribed from one promoter can contain the information for several polypeptides. Such a transcript is commonly described as polycistronic. The existence of several protein-coding regions on one RNA does not require several separate transcription events, although an actual locus can contain additional internal promoters or other arrangements. Conversely, a gene giving rise to a functional noncoding RNA does not require a translation start codon in order for transcription to occur.

It is therefore important to distinguish the boundaries of a transcription unit from the boundaries of a protein-coding region. The first transcribed nucleotide can precede the translation start by a considerable number of bases. The RNA may also continue beyond the translation stop codon. In a simple mature messenger RNA these untranslated segments are called the 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions. The existence of these regions demonstrates that RNA polymerase does not identify its starting and stopping positions by applying the rules of codon recognition. It reads DNA in response to transcription-related signals. The ribosome subsequently interprets appropriate RNA features in the context of translation.

The term terminator also needs careful handling. In many simplified bacterial examples a defined DNA sequence, or the RNA sequence generated from it, participates in signaling the end of transcription. A primary transcript can contain bases derived from portions of a termination region. In a eukaryotic protein-coding gene, processing at the 3′ end of the RNA and cessation of RNA polymerase II transcription can occur at different positions. Consequently, a drawn terminator boundary cannot always be assumed to equal the end of the mature mRNA. The general idea of ending a transcription event is shared, but the molecular methods and relationships between ends differ.

A transcription unit is also distinct from the complete set of regulatory regions that influence its output. DNA sequences outside the copied interval may alter the probability of initiation. A regulatory element can therefore affect an RNA without being part of the RNA's sequence. The simplest examples involve promoter mutations that reduce initiation frequency without changing the sequence of the transcripts that still form. This is a powerful conceptual distinction in mutation experiments: the effect of a change in DNA can be informational, regulatory or both. Its location relative to the actual start site and the transcription machinery helps identify which possibility is most likely.

11. The +1 convention, upstream and downstream

The transcription start site is designated +1 in the conventional gene-relative coordinate system. It is the first nucleotide of the newly transcribed RNA, corresponding to a particular base pair in the DNA. The position immediately upstream is −1, and there is no zero position in this notation. Positions downstream of the start site are +2, +3 and so on. The negative positions denote locations before the start in the direction in which transcription proceeds. They do not denote physical positions below a reference plane or bases that are chemically negative. They are a coordinate convention chosen to make the start site a common point of reference.

The absence of zero has a small but important consequence for length calculations. Suppose a regulatory segment extends from −20 through −1, inclusive. It contains 20 base pairs. The interval from +1 through +30 contains 30 base pairs. Together the contiguous interval from −20 through +30 contains 50 base pairs, not 51. The ordinary formula endpoint minus start plus one applies directly when both positions are on the same uninterrupted side of the coordinate system. When an interval crosses from negative to positive gene-relative positions, the omitted zero must be accounted for. This is an example of a mathematical rule being valid only for a particular convention of labeling positions.

If transcription begins at +1 and proceeds continuously to a last incorporated base designated +400, the unprocessed transcript contains 400 residues, assuming that every position is copied once and no insertions or deletions occur. A promoter element occupying positions −35 through −30 is not included in that RNA merely because it is close to the start. A downstream promoter element at positive coordinates, however, could lie in a region that is copied into the transcript. Thus the proper criterion for inclusion in a primary RNA is whether the DNA position is within the actual transcribed interval, not whether the position is called a promoter element.

The words upstream and downstream are relative to the direction of transcription. Consider a chromosome map drawn with increasing reference coordinates from left to right. For a rightward gene, an upstream site lies to the left of +1 on that map. For a leftward gene, an upstream site lies to the right of +1. The same numerical chromosome coordinate can therefore be downstream of one gene and upstream of another. A statement that all upstream positions have lower chromosome coordinate numbers is incorrect whenever a gene is oriented toward decreasing reference coordinates.

Suppose gene A has a transcription start site at genomic coordinate 1000 and is transcribed toward increasing coordinates. Its position +20 is at coordinate 1019, and −20 is at coordinate 980. Gene B has its start site at coordinate 2000 and is transcribed toward decreasing coordinates. Its +20 lies at 1981, whereas its −20 lies at 2020. In both cases, moving from +1 to +20 requires movement by 19 positions. Moving from +1 to −20 requires movement by 20 positions because there is no zero. Writing down the direction before performing the arithmetic protects against off-by-one errors.

Some questions use absolute genomic coordinates rather than gene-relative notation. If an inclusive interval runs from coordinate 500 through coordinate 699, it contains 200 bases regardless of whether transcription moves from 500 to 699 or from 699 to 500. What changes is which endpoint corresponds to +1. For a rightward transcription unit starting at 500, +1 is coordinate 500. For a leftward unit starting at 699, +1 is coordinate 699. Under the same simplified assumptions, the two hypothetical primary RNAs would have equal lengths but potentially very different sequences. Orientation determines strand and order; the number of positions determines length.

Actual genes can use alternative initiation sites. A transcript population may therefore contain molecules starting at different nearby or distant positions rather than a single universal +1. In a teaching diagram, +1 refers to the particular start site under discussion. An experiment that maps several 5′ ends must establish whether they reflect multiple transcription starts, RNA processing or degradation. The coordinate convention remains useful, but it does not require every transcript from a locus to share one end.

12. Promoter, transcription start, translation start and termination signals

A promoter and a transcription start site answer different questions. The promoter answers where and in which orientation the transcription apparatus is recruited and organized to initiate. The start site answers which DNA position supplies the first nucleotide of the RNA under the conditions being considered. A change in promoter sequence may affect the probability of initiation, the selection of a start site or both. A mutation at the first transcribed nucleotide may change the sequence of the resulting RNA and can sometimes influence initiation itself. These effects should be evaluated from the experiment rather than assigned solely on the basis of a word such as promoter.

A translation start codon is an RNA sequence interpreted by the translational machinery in an appropriate context. In the familiar example, AUG can serve as the start codon and specify methionine during initiation, with the bacterial initiating amino acid often supplied as formylmethionine. However, not every AUG is a translation start, and transcription does not require AUG at its first three positions. RNA polymerase reads individual template positions according to nucleotide complementarity, whereas translation uses a decoding system that considers groups of three bases in a reading frame. The chemical operation and the recognition problem are different.

For an illustrative transcription unit, assume +1 corresponds to the first base of an RNA leader, and the first base of the selected translation start codon is +31. The leader contains 30 residues. If the first base of a stop codon is +331, there are 300 residues from +31 through +330, corresponding to 100 sense codons under the assumption of a continuous reading frame without interruptions. The stop codon occupies positions +331 through +333 and does not encode an additional ordinary amino acid. If the final nucleotide of the RNA in this simplified model is +400, another 67 residues follow that stop codon. Nothing in the arithmetic implies that RNA polymerase ceased synthesis at +333.

The distinction between translation termination and transcription termination is particularly important. Stop codons are recognized in the context of ribosomal decoding. A terminator is involved in cessation of transcription. A mutation changing a sense codon to a stop codon can produce a shorter polypeptide without necessarily causing RNA polymerase to stop at the mutated site. Secondary effects can occur in some biological systems, including effects of translation on downstream expression in bacteria, but the primary event must not be confused with direct recognition of a stop codon by RNA polymerase.

An analogous distinction applies to the start. Deletion of an appropriate promoter can strongly reduce the formation of RNA even if the coding sequence and its AUG remain intact. Mutating a translation start can strongly reduce production of the protein while leaving transcription initiation intact. If both mutations lead to the same low protein measurement, the protein measurement alone cannot locate the defect. Measuring RNA abundance, mapping its ends and assessing protein output relative to RNA can help distinguish the two mechanisms. A promoter that remains functional can generate an RNA even when the RNA is incapable of directing normal translation.

A further complication is that promoter activity cannot always be inferred from sequence resemblance to a known motif. A candidate promoter might resemble a common recognition sequence but fail to support initiation in its particular context. Conversely, a functional promoter may differ from a textbook consensus sequence. At the introductory level, the promoter is best defined by its role in initiation. Later chapters will explain how different transcription systems recognize promoter elements. A functional test that measures initiation from a candidate region is more informative than a purely visual judgment that a sequence looks like a promoter.

The four terms can now be organized into a causal order. A promoter helps organize initiation at a transcription start site. RNA polymerase synthesizes the primary transcript. A protein-coding RNA can subsequently be used by ribosomes, which select a translation start and eventually encounter a stop codon in the reading frame. Meanwhile, transcription itself ends according to mechanisms specific to its transcription system. In bacteria, transcription and translation of a given RNA can overlap in time, but this temporal coupling does not make the two sets of signals identical. Understanding these distinctions provides the basis for interpreting more detailed molecular diagrams in the next nodes.

13. Initiation as the creation of a new RNA chain

The first covalent bond of a new RNA presents a different problem from the bonds made during subsequent elongation. An elongating polymerase already has a chain whose terminal sugar supplies the necessary 3′ hydroxyl. At the beginning of a transcription event there is no such polymeric RNA end. Cellular DNA-dependent RNA polymerases solve this problem through de novo initiation. In the usual mechanism, an initiating ribonucleoside triphosphate is positioned opposite the first template base, and a second ribonucleoside triphosphate is positioned opposite the next template base. The 3′ hydroxyl of the first nucleotide participates in forming the bond to the second nucleotide. A new RNA chain now exists with a defined 5′ end and a free 3′ hydroxyl.

This explanation clarifies why the statement that RNA polymerase needs no primer does not contradict the requirement for a hydroxyl group in the polymerization reaction. A primer is a pre-existing nucleic acid chain that provides a suitable end for extension. The initial NTP in de novo transcription supplies a hydroxyl group as a substrate within the initiating complex rather than as a preformed oligonucleotide. Once the first linkage is made, the growing RNA itself supplies the terminal hydroxyl during elongation. The chemical requirement remains, but the source of the hydroxyl changes as the process proceeds.

DNA polymerases used in cellular genome replication generally require an existing primer-template junction with an accessible 3′ hydroxyl. During cellular DNA replication, primase or a primase-associated system generates the starting segment, after which a replicative DNA polymerase extends an appropriate end. The difference is therefore not that DNA synthesis lacks suitable nucleotides capable of forming phosphodiester bonds. It is that the usual replicative DNA polymerase requires a different substrate arrangement for productive catalysis. An RNA polymerase is equipped to organize the initial nucleotides without a pre-existing chain. Exceptions involving specialized polymerases, unusual priming strategies and viral enzymes do not invalidate the comparison of ordinary cellular transcription with ordinary chromosomal DNA replication.

A reaction containing DNA, all four NTPs and an RNA polymerase preparation does not automatically initiate at the intended site. The relevant polymerase must be capable of using the template under the supplied conditions; accessory factors may be necessary for proper promoter recognition or start site selection. Primer independence must not be confused with independence from all other molecular requirements. An RNA polymerase cannot usually begin accurate gene-specific transcription merely because a random piece of double-stranded DNA is present. The initiation apparatus must organize an appropriate template region and permit access to the bases that will be copied.

It is helpful to separate three events in an introductory representation of initiation: association of the transcription apparatus with a suitable promoter, local opening of the DNA duplex, and synthesis of the first short RNA. These are functional steps, not a claim that each system proceeds through an identical list of named complexes. A bound polymerase may not yet have a template strand correctly positioned for catalysis. An opened DNA region may not yet contain an RNA product. An initial RNA may be formed without progressing into a stable elongation event. Different experimental measurements can detect these distinctions, so it is inappropriate to infer completion of initiation from promoter occupancy alone.

The immediate RNA end formed through standard initiation is frequently triphosphorylated at the 5′ position. Its subsequent fate depends on the organism and transcript type. Eukaryotic messenger RNA precursors commonly undergo cap formation, and other RNA classes can undergo distinct end-processing reactions. Noncanonical initiating substrates and primer-dependent initiation occur in particular biological contexts, so the simplest description of the 5′ triphosphate should be qualified as the usual de novo reaction with standard NTPs. A primary transcript is defined by its origin in transcription, not by an unchanging chemical feature that all cellular RNAs must retain throughout their lifetime.

14. Selecting a promoter and gaining access to DNA bases

The bases of a double-stranded DNA molecule are normally engaged in complementary pairing within the duplex. For a polymerase to select an incoming ribonucleotide by the appropriate template base, that base must become accessible. Transcription therefore involves local separation of the two DNA strands. It does not require the entire chromosome to unwind or the two DNA strands to separate permanently. A short region is opened and maintained in association with the transcription machinery, while much of the DNA ahead of and behind the active region remains double-stranded. The local nature of this rearrangement helps preserve chromosome integrity during selective gene expression.

Promoter recognition provides information about where to establish this opening and how to orient the machinery. A sequence-specific interaction can distinguish one region of a DNA molecule from many other regions composed of the same four types of nucleotide. The spatial arrangement of recognized bases and associated DNA properties can orient the polymerase complex. An asymmetrically arranged promoter therefore provides a directional signal. The exact components that establish specificity differ between bacterial, archaeal and eukaryotic transcription systems, and their molecular identities will be treated in the relevant subsequent chapters.

DNA strand separation requires overcoming the interactions that stabilize the duplex. Hydrogen bonding contributes to complementary pairing, and base stacking contributes substantially to helix stability. Local opening also depends on the shape of the DNA and on the interactions between the DNA and the initiation complex. A simple count of hydrogen bonds alone is not sufficient to predict the melting behavior of an arbitrary promoter sequence. Sequence context, salt concentration, temperature and DNA topology can all affect the energetic balance. In the cell, proteins and other components of the transcription machinery can favor an opened state through their binding interactions and, in certain systems, through energy-dependent processes.

An intuitive but inaccurate model imagines that polymerase simply finds the first available exposed base and starts to copy indefinitely. This would produce many inappropriate RNAs and would not reliably connect transcript boundaries with cellular functions. The promoter helps solve the positioning problem, while start site selection determines the first incorporated base in the particular initiation event. Sequence features at or near the start and the geometry of the assembled machinery can influence the position selected. The consequences of a promoter mutation may therefore include a reduced number of transcripts, a shift in their starting positions or altered responsiveness to regulatory factors.

Consider an educational comparison of two DNA fragments identical throughout their intended transcribed regions but differing in a short upstream sequence. If one fragment produces much more correctly initiated RNA than the other under matched conditions, the upstream difference is evidence of an effect on the process leading to productive transcription. It does not show that the upstream sequence was incorporated into the RNA. To identify the precise defective step, it would be useful to examine whether the polymerase-containing complex binds, whether the DNA opens and whether short initiated products progress to longer ones. An effect on total product yield is compatible with defects at more than one stage.

The promoter is not consumed in a transcription cycle. After a polymerase progresses away from the initiation region or otherwise leaves it available, another productive initiation can occur. The DNA can therefore support repeated rounds of transcription. The actual interval between rounds is determined by factors such as availability of the machinery, promoter occupancy and regulation, rather than by a fixed stoichiometric relation between one DNA copy and one RNA copy. This is a central difference from the usual aim of chromosomal replication, in which the entire genetic complement must be duplicated in a coordinated manner.

15. The elongating transcription complex

Once a productive transcription complex has formed, it must maintain the relationship between the DNA template and the growing RNA while moving to successive positions. The active region of an elongating complex contains a locally opened segment of DNA, an RNA-DNA hybrid involving part of the newly synthesized RNA and its template, and a route through which the older part of the RNA separates from the DNA and emerges. Ahead of the active center, DNA bases that will be read must become accessible. Behind it, the two DNA strands can pair again as the RNA is displaced from the template.

The entire growing RNA does not remain paired to the DNA for the duration of transcription. If a polymerase has synthesized a thousand nucleotides, the hybrid within a typical elongation complex comprises only a short portion near the active center. The bulk of the RNA made earlier has already emerged from this region. This arrangement allows the product to become available for folding and interactions with other molecules while synthesis is still under way. The dimensions of the hybrid and the open region depend on the system and state of the complex; they should not be treated as invariant numbers applicable to all polymerases.

A useful picture of elongation is a repeating cycle. The appropriate NTP becomes available and is selected at the current template position. Its reactive groups and the growing RNA end become arranged for catalysis. Formation of a new linkage extends the product, and pyrophosphate is released. The polymerase and its nucleic acid components change their relative positions so that the next template base can participate in another round of incorporation. Actual polymerases explore different conformational and translocation states, and the individual microscopic events need not be captured by a rigid one-step mechanical model. At the examination level, the repeating chemical and positional changes explain the net progress of the enzyme.

The movement of the open region along the DNA creates a distinction between transcription and a stable RNA-DNA hybrid produced in an experimental hybridization assay. In an elongating complex, local hybrid formation and disruption are normal components of productive synthesis. A persistent RNA-DNA hybrid outside the usual moving active region can arise in certain biological circumstances and can affect DNA organization or stability, but it is not necessary for every transcript to remain attached to the chromosome after synthesis. The ordinary endpoint of a productive transcription event is release of the RNA from its transcription complex.

RNA chain extension also has consequences for DNA topology. As a polymerase moves along a helical DNA template, relative rotation of the DNA and the complex may be required. If that rotation is constrained, torsional changes can accumulate, often represented by increased positive supercoiling ahead and negative supercoiling behind the moving complex in a simplified model. Liu and Wang proposed the twin-domain model in 1987; subsequent cellular and biochemical experiments provided evidence that transcription can generate these local torsional domains. The sign of the change is predicted relative to the direction of motion and depends on constraints that prevent free rotational relaxation. Enzymes that manage DNA topology can influence the ability of transcription to proceed. This connection is especially useful after studying DNA structure and replication, because it demonstrates that transcription is not purely an abstract operation on a sequence of letters. It occurs on a three-dimensional molecule subject to physical constraints.

The term processivity describes the ability of a polymerizing enzyme to continue synthesis through multiple additions without dissociating from its substrate complex. It is not identical to the speed of nucleotide incorporation. A highly processive enzyme may extend a chain relatively slowly yet remain associated until a long product is formed. A fast but poorly processive enzyme may add several residues rapidly and then dissociate before reaching the normal endpoint. Experiments measuring RNA length distributions and experiments measuring incorporation rates can distinguish these properties when their designs permit. Processivity helps explain how a polymerase can produce an RNA much longer than the short DNA-RNA hybrid present at any one moment.

16. Transcription fidelity and the consequences of incorrect incorporation

The ability of RNA polymerase to choose the appropriate nucleotide is essential because an incorrect base can alter the biological activity of the RNA. A substitution in messenger RNA may alter a codon and, in some circumstances, the amino acid incorporated into the resulting protein. A substitution in a noncoding RNA may alter its folding or its recognition of a binding partner. Not every error has a detectable functional effect. Its significance depends on its position, the identity of the substituted nucleotide, the purpose of the RNA and the extent to which other cellular processes compensate.

Fidelity originates in several aspects of the transcription complex. Complementary pairing favors appropriate substrates, but pairing is not the sole determinant. The active center also constrains how nucleotide geometry, the template and the terminal RNA end are arranged for catalysis. Incorrect substrates may be less likely to reach a productive state. Some errors that nevertheless occur can be corrected when an elongation complex changes position and cleaves part of the newly formed RNA to restore an end suitable for extension. Detailed accessory factors and organism-specific mechanisms will be introduced in later treatments of transcription elongation. At this point it is sufficient to understand that transcription fidelity reflects selection and possible correction rather than an infallible chemical rule.

A transcription error is different from a mutation in the DNA template. If a DNA substitution is present at a given genomic position, every accurate transcript made from the affected template under comparable conditions can carry the corresponding altered base. If the DNA is unchanged and one polymerase makes a copying error, other transcripts produced from the same region need not contain that error. A transient RNA error does not automatically become a stable heritable change in the chromosome. This difference helps explain why cellular genome duplication and RNA production have distinct biological consequences even though both rely on templated nucleotide selection.

The distinction should not be overstated as a claim that transcriptional errors are biologically harmless. A single RNA may be used repeatedly by translational machinery. Consequently, an error in a messenger molecule can affect more than one protein molecule. Errors in an important functional RNA may also have physiological consequences. The key distinction is the normal route by which the error propagates. A chromosomal mutation can be transmitted through DNA replication to descendant cells, whereas an error in an ordinary cellular RNA usually affects products derived from that RNA and disappears when the molecule and its consequences are removed.

Consider a simplified model in which the probability of an incorrect incorporation is a constant p at each of L independent positions, with no subsequent correction. The probability of an entirely correct sequence is (1 − p) raised to the power L. The probability that at least one error occurs is one minus that expression. For small p and a sufficiently small product Lp, the latter probability is approximately Lp. The approximation follows from expansion of the product: the first-order term for one event is Lp, while terms involving multiple events are of higher order. The formula is useful only under the model's assumptions; different sequence positions may have different error probabilities and errors can influence subsequent elongation or be corrected.

For a hypothetical per-position error probability of one in one hundred thousand and a transcript length of one thousand positions, Lp is 0.01. The exact independent-position probability of at least one error is approximately 0.00995, or 0.995 percent. This numerical example is not a universal measured RNA polymerase error rate. It is an illustration of how a low probability per event can produce a higher probability for a long sequence containing many events. A transcript twice as long would have approximately twice the error probability only while the first-order approximation remained valid. The expected number of errors, Lp, is not generally identical to the probability of at least one error, since one transcript can contain several errors.

To distinguish a DNA mutation from a recurrent RNA sequence difference experimentally, both DNA and RNA information are relevant. A mismatch seen in a single RNA read can result from biological misincorporation, RNA modification, an error in conversion or sequencing, or other sources. A reproducible RNA difference absent from genomic DNA warrants further investigation but does not by itself establish a particular editing mechanism. The beginner's sequence-complementarity rule is a prediction about faithful primary synthesis, and the experimental scientist must consider whether the molecules being measured actually satisfy that assumption.

17. Pauses, productive progression and the meaning of transcription rate

A polymerase does not necessarily advance at the same speed through every DNA sequence. The probability of an addition and the time spent in different transcription complex states can vary with nucleotide availability, DNA sequence and interactions with other molecules. A pause is a temporary interruption or reduction in the net progress of an elongating complex. A paused complex may resume synthesis. A stalled complex may remain arrested for a longer period under the conditions studied. Neither term automatically implies that the RNA has been released or that the transcription event has ended.

It is important to distinguish a pause from termination because the two can produce superficially similar measurements. Imagine a short experiment in which many RNA molecules have a length of about 150 residues. This could indicate that the polymerases have temporarily accumulated at the corresponding template position. It could also indicate that the RNA products have been released at that position and cannot continue. A length measurement at a single time point does not decide between these alternatives. Evidence that the short chains subsequently become longer in a suitable continuation experiment would support pausing or arrest followed by recovery. Evidence that the RNAs were released and cannot be extended within the same complex would support a different interpretation.

A mutation can change the average time required to make a transcript without changing its final length. Suppose an RNA polymerase adds residues at an idealized rate of 40 nucleotides per second during active extension and produces a 1200-residue RNA. Ignoring the distinct timing of initiation, the active extension time estimated from length divided by speed is 30 seconds. If the polymerase also spends ten seconds in a pause, the overall synthesis time in this simple example becomes 40 seconds. The average effective rate across the entire interval is 30 nucleotides per second, even though the active extension rate remains 40 nucleotides per second. A lower observed average rate does not establish that the chemistry of every addition became slower.

The exact length convention deserves attention in very short examples. If a chain of n residues has already formed, an L-residue completed chain requires L − n additional incorporations. For an elongating 120-residue RNA that must reach 600 residues, there are 480 additions remaining. At a uniform model rate of 30 additions per second, these would take 16 seconds. Using 600 divided by 30 would overestimate the remaining time because it would count residues already present. For long transcripts initiated from nearly zero length, L divided by an average rate is often a useful approximate completion time. The assumptions should be explicit.

The speed at which one elongation complex advances must also be distinguished from the number of RNA molecules produced per unit time by a gene or cell. Several RNA polymerases can occupy different positions on one DNA region when initiation is sufficiently frequent and the physical arrangement allows it. In a simple steady pipeline, one complex may take a long time to complete a transcript while new complexes begin at shorter intervals. Once the pipeline is filled, completed transcripts can emerge at intervals closer to the initiation interval than to the transit time of a single polymerase. Slower elongation may increase the number of simultaneously occupied complexes if initiation remains unchanged and crowding is negligible, but it need not increase completed transcript output.

This distinction is crucial for interpreting a measurement of polymerase density. High density can result from frequent initiation, slow movement or long residence at a pause. A gene with many associated polymerases is not necessarily producing more full-length RNA per unit time than one with fewer polymerases. To estimate output, one must know whether the polymerases are productively elongating and completing transcripts, and how rapidly they enter and leave the transcription unit. An occupancy measurement is a snapshot of how many complexes are present; a rate measurement describes how many events occur during an interval. The two quantities are related only through additional assumptions about the dynamics of the system.

18. Termination as a distinct stage of RNA synthesis

A transcription event must eventually end. If a polymerase were to continue through a chromosome without appropriate limits, many unwanted sequences could be included in the RNA and the polymerase would remain engaged longer than necessary. Termination is the stage at which a productive transcription complex ceases further RNA synthesis as part of the normal transcription cycle. Release of the RNA and dissociation or reorganization of the polymerase complex allow the molecules to participate in subsequent cellular activities. This general description applies across systems, but their specific termination signals and pathways differ.

The physical problem of termination is distinct from simply running out of substrate. When an essential NTP is absent, a polymerase can stop at a position that requires that nucleotide. If the missing substrate is restored, a suitably retained complex may resume synthesis. A biological termination pathway, in contrast, promotes ending of the transcription event in response to relevant sequence or molecular signals. An experimentally truncated RNA can arise from either mechanism or from RNA cleavage after synthesis, which is why the presence of a short product does not by itself identify normal termination.

Bacterial and eukaryotic systems illustrate different ways of implementing this general stage. In familiar bacterial examples, particular sequences can generate RNA features or support the activity of factors that cause transcription to end. In nuclear eukaryotic transcription, termination of RNA polymerase II can be linked to events involved in forming the RNA's 3′ end, and the position where the polymerase stops need not coincide with the position where the mature messenger RNA ends. The details of intrinsic and factor-dependent bacterial termination, and of the various eukaryotic polymerase systems, are intentionally separated into the following nodes. Their shared conceptual point is that a signal changes the behavior of an elongation complex rather than merely instructing a ribosome to stop translation.

A failure of termination can cause a polymerase to continue beyond an expected endpoint, producing a readthrough RNA. Suppose two otherwise identical DNA fragments contain the same initiation region and transcribed sequence but differ at a candidate termination region. If one fragment yields many short RNAs of the expected length and the other yields a larger fraction of RNAs extending into downstream DNA, the difference is consistent with altered termination efficiency. To strengthen this conclusion, the investigator must consider the possibility that short RNA species were generated by cleavage or that the long products arise from an additional initiation site. Mapping transcript ends and analyzing whether the downstream RNA is physically continuous with the upstream transcript can help resolve these possibilities.

Termination efficiency is often expressed as a fraction of relevant transcription events that end at a specified region, provided that the experimental quantities correspond to events rather than merely to steady-state molecule counts. In a hypothetical assay, 180 out of 200 transcripts reaching a candidate terminator end there and 20 read through. The apparent termination efficiency is 180 divided by 200, or 90 percent. The readthrough fraction is 10 percent under the assumption that those two outcomes exhaust the events. If some complexes stall indefinitely or produce undetected products, the simple two-outcome calculation will no longer represent all complexes that reached the region.

A promoter and terminator can consequently influence different aspects of output. A weakened promoter may reduce the number of events entering a transcription unit, while a weakened terminator may alter the distribution of transcript endpoints. In a system with a downstream readthrough reporter, changing the initiation frequency can also change the reporter signal. It is therefore helpful to normalize readthrough to the amount of transcription entering the candidate region or compare upstream and downstream measurements under matched conditions. This reasoning illustrates the general need to identify the denominator in a molecular experiment before interpreting a percentage as a mechanistic property.

19. Comparing transcription with DNA replication

Transcription and replication share a foundational principle: the order of nucleotides in an existing nucleic acid strand guides the order of nucleotides in a newly synthesized chain. Both processes normally rely on complementary base recognition, activated nucleoside triphosphates, a polymerizing enzyme and extension at a 3′ end. These similarities explain why the concepts of template polarity and phosphodiester linkage formation recur in both chapters. They also provide a basis for distinguishing the biological purpose and organization of the two processes.

During chromosomal replication, the cell must duplicate the DNA genetic complement so that daughter DNA molecules contain the necessary genetic information. In the ordinary semiconservative mechanism, each resulting duplex contains a parental strand paired with a newly synthesized DNA strand. Both parental strands serve as templates, even though the antiparallel orientation of the strands creates distinct leading- and lagging-strand arrangements at a replication fork. Transcription has a different purpose. It produces an RNA from a particular transcription unit, and only one DNA strand serves as the template for that RNA at any given position. The other strand remains part of the parental DNA duplex and is not converted into a second RNA simply as a consequence of copying the first.

This difference explains why the terms leading strand and lagging strand are not appropriate labels for the two DNA strands of an ordinary transcription unit. They describe the relationship of continuous and discontinuous DNA synthesis to the direction of movement of a replication fork. In transcription, one RNA polymerase synthesizes one RNA chain along the selected template. It does not need to produce a complementary RNA on the non-template strand at the same time, so the requirement that leads to a lagging-strand solution at a replication fork is absent. Other transcription units may use either strand, but that is a consequence of their own directional organization rather than a requirement of each transcription event.

The substrate pools also differ. Replicative DNA polymerases normally incorporate deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates, while RNA polymerases normally incorporate ribonucleoside triphosphates. Both types of synthesis involve formation of phosphodiester bonds and release of pyrophosphate during chain extension. DNA replication generally uses a primer-generating system, whereas normal cellular transcription can begin de novo. The enzymes that solve these distinct molecular problems need not have the same active-site architecture or associated factors even though the basic chemical transformations are related.

An experimental perturbation that prevents formation of a new DNA replication primer need not directly prevent an ordinary RNA polymerase from initiating a transcript. Nevertheless, a perturbation of DNA metabolism may alter transcription indirectly through changes in nucleotide pools, cell viability or DNA integrity. In a whole-cell experiment, the specificity of an observed effect must therefore be distinguished from the specificity of its direct molecular target. A purified enzyme experiment and a cellular phenotype answer different questions. The former can isolate the immediate reaction, while the latter reveals the consequences of that reaction in a connected system.

The different outcomes of replication and transcription also help explain why the processes are regulated in different ways. A typical chromosome must be duplicated in a coordinated manner during a cell division cycle. A particular RNA may be made repeatedly whenever its function is needed. If an investigator doubles the initiation frequency of a promoter without changing the chromosome copy number, the RNA production rate can rise. If the number of chromosome copies increases but the transcription machinery becomes limiting, output may not rise in direct proportion to copy number. Neither a DNA concentration measurement nor an RNA concentration measurement alone determines the efficiency of the other process.

Finally, the chemical contrast between an RNA product and a DNA product permits useful analytical discrimination. Under suitable conditions an RNA-containing sample can be treated with an RNase, whereas DNase acts on susceptible DNA. The susceptibility depends on the enzyme, substrate structure and reaction conditions. Such treatments can help identify which nucleic acid carries a measured label, but appropriate controls are required. A loss of a signal after DNase treatment before transcription could indicate removal of the template; a loss after RNase treatment of the product could indicate that the measured material contains RNA. The order of treatment matters because removing a template and degrading a product are distinct interventions.

20. From synthesis to measured RNA abundance

A cell's RNA content is not determined by transcription alone. RNA molecules are formed, can undergo processing, and are eventually removed. A sample taken at a single moment reveals the amount that remains under the conditions examined, not directly how many molecules were made during a particular interval. This distinction is important because many biological experiments report RNA abundance and then discuss a change in gene expression. A change in expression may reflect altered transcription, altered RNA stability, or a combination of mechanisms. A foundational understanding of transcription should include the mathematical distinction between synthesis rate and RNA amount.

Let R be the number of molecules of a specified RNA per cell in a simplified model, and let s be its constant rate of production in molecules per cell per unit time. Suppose removal is proportional to the number of molecules present, with proportionality constant k having units of inverse time. Then the net change in RNA amount can be written dR/dt = s − kR. The first term counts formation of the species being measured, and the second counts removal. For a primary transcription product, s may reflect productive transcription into that measurable species. For a mature RNA, processing and maturation may contribute to the effective formation rate as well. The equation is a simplified mass balance, not a universal complete kinetic model.

At steady state, the amount does not change with time, so dR/dt is zero and the two opposing rates are equal. Rearranging gives R = s/k. It follows that doubling synthesis at unchanged k doubles the steady-state amount, but halving k at unchanged synthesis has the same effect. A measurement of the final amount alone cannot distinguish the two. The rate of removal at steady state is not zero: molecules can be continuously formed and removed even when the total number remains constant. This distinction between dynamic steady state and absence of activity is important throughout biology.

For a hypothetical RNA synthesized at ten molecules per cell per minute and removed with k = 0.2 per minute, the predicted steady-state abundance is fifty molecules per cell. If an intervention doubles synthesis to twenty molecules per minute, the new steady-state prediction is one hundred molecules per cell. If a different intervention instead reduces k to 0.1 per minute while synthesis remains ten, the same abundance is predicted. Measuring nascent synthesis or decay after a validated interruption of production can distinguish these possibilities. A simple comparison of two RNA abundance measurements cannot do so.

If production ceases at time zero and first-order removal continues with constant k, the equation becomes dR/dt = −kR. Separating variables and integrating gives R(t) = R(0)e^(−kt). The half-life is found by setting R(t) equal to one-half of the initial amount, so t½ = ln(2)/k. In the first hypothetical example, a k of 0.2 per minute predicts a half-life of approximately 3.47 minutes. Reducing k to 0.1 per minute increases the predicted half-life to about 6.93 minutes. This model assumes a homogeneous measurable RNA population and a constant removal probability per unit time; multiple molecular species, cell division, changing conditions or complicated maturation can violate those assumptions.

The synthesis and removal distinction also helps interpret transient experiments. Immediately after a promoter becomes more active, the RNA pool may still be small because time is required for new molecules to accumulate. If transcription ceases, a stable RNA may remain detectable for a considerable period. A cell can therefore retain a molecular record of earlier activity in its RNA population. Observing an RNA after a stimulus has disappeared does not prove that the promoter is still active. Measuring newly formed material over a defined interval provides more direct evidence of ongoing synthesis than simply measuring all existing RNA.

The same caution applies to estimates of protein output. A change in RNA production may precede a change in protein abundance, and the relationship depends on translation efficiency and protein turnover. A researcher studying a transcription mechanism should ideally measure a variable close to the proposed step, such as initiation, elongation or newly synthesized RNA, rather than assuming that every downstream phenotype is a direct measure of polymerase activity.

21. Experimental reasoning: mapping where RNA begins and ends

One of the first tasks in understanding a transcription unit is to identify the positions that contribute to the RNA. A DNA sequence can suggest where a promoter or coding region might be located, but the existence of a transcript and the position of its ends are experimentally testable questions. If an RNA's 5′ end is identified and aligned to a known genomic sequence, the alignment establishes a candidate location for its beginning. Whether that location is the original transcription start site requires attention to processing, degradation and the type of molecule measured. A mature RNA end can be different from the initial end created by RNA polymerase.

In a conceptual primer-extension experiment, a short oligonucleotide complementary to a known RNA region is annealed to an RNA sample. Reverse transcriptase extends the primer using RNA as a template, producing complementary DNA. If the RNA has a defined 5′ end and extension reaches that end, the length of the resulting DNA can reveal the distance between the known priming location and the RNA end. This technique is useful because a known internal sequence provides an anchor from which the position of the end can be inferred. The experiment uses reverse transcription as an analytical method, not as the normal mechanism by which the cell made the original RNA.

Consider an educational example in which the primer spans positions +51 through +70 on an RNA of known orientation. Its 3′ end is aligned opposite position +51, so extension adds complementary DNA corresponding to positions +50, +49 and so on through +1 if the original RNA begins at +1. Fifty new DNA residues are added to the twenty-residue primer, giving an idealized seventy-residue product. If the RNA instead begins at position +11 of the original coordinate system, forty new residues are added and the final DNA product contains sixty residues. This calculation assumes complete extension to the end and correct primer binding. Premature arrest of reverse transcriptase can generate a shorter product without demonstrating a different RNA start.

A complementary type of evidence is obtained by hybridizing an RNA sample with a labeled nucleic acid probe that recognizes a particular region. A probe targeting an upstream portion of a transcription unit can detect RNA containing that region; a probe targeting downstream DNA-derived sequence can detect RNAs that include that downstream region. The fact that two probes both detect RNA in a sample does not, by itself, prove that both regions are on the same physical molecule. If the question concerns readthrough from one promoter, continuity of the RNA or appropriate size information is needed to distinguish a long transcript from two separate transcripts.

The Northern blot provides an introductory example of how size and sequence-specific detection can be combined. RNA species are separated by size under suitable conditions and detected by hybridization with a complementary probe. A shorter or longer band can suggest a difference in transcript length, while band intensity under appropriately controlled conditions can give a relative indication of abundance. A shift in band size need not be due to a transcription termination defect; it might result from a different start site, altered processing or degradation. A single probe identifies molecules containing a complementary region but does not define every nucleotide of those molecules.

Orientation-specific detection requires careful probe design. Suppose a defined rightward transcript matches the plus-reference strand except for the substitution of uracil for thymine. A probe designed to hybridize directly to that RNA must have the complementary sequence in the proper antiparallel orientation. A single-stranded probe identical to the RNA is generally not the probe that detects it by ordinary complementary hybridization. If transcription occurs in both directions across the same DNA interval, orientation-specific probes or equivalent directional methods can help distinguish the products. The exact experimental conditions must ensure that the detected signal reflects the intended nucleic acid species rather than contaminating DNA or nonspecific binding.

These methods illustrate how several measurements can complement one another. A start mapping assay gives positional information about an RNA end, a hybridization assay gives evidence for particular sequences, and a size measurement gives information about the distribution of product lengths. None of these is identical to directly observing promoter recognition by a polymerase. Together, appropriate measurements can constrain a model of the transcription unit. The ability to connect an experimental observable to the relevant step is more important at this stage than memorizing a list of laboratory techniques.

22. Integrating the transcription cycle with cellular organization

The same fundamental copying logic operates in bacterial, archaeal and eukaryotic cells, despite major differences in their transcription machinery and cellular organization. In bacteria, transcription occurs in a cellular environment without a membrane-bounded nucleus. Ribosomes may begin translating suitable messenger RNAs while transcription is still in progress. This makes the emerging RNA a site at which transcription and translation can become functionally connected. Nevertheless, the polymerase produces the nucleotide sequence by reading DNA, while the ribosome reads codons in RNA. Their spatial proximity does not merge their reaction mechanisms.

In eukaryotes, transcription of most nuclear genes occurs within the nucleus, whereas translation of ordinary nuclear-encoded messenger RNAs occurs in the cytoplasm. RNA processing and transport therefore form important links between initial RNA synthesis and its use. A newly synthesized nuclear RNA may already begin to undergo processing before transcription finishes. It would be misleading to imagine that every transcript is first released as an entirely untouched chain and that every processing event begins only afterward. The distinction between primary sequence synthesis and subsequent chemical processing remains useful even when the events overlap in time.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts also contain genetic information and have systems for producing RNA. Their existence shows that eukaryotic RNA synthesis is not confined to the nucleus. The detailed transcription machinery varies among organisms and organelles, but ordinary DNA-directed RNA polymerization still requires an appropriate template, substrates and catalytic machinery. Archaeal transcription provides another useful comparison: the absence of a nucleus does not imply that the transcription apparatus must be identical to the familiar bacterial model. Cellular location and molecular ancestry are different criteria for classification.

There are biological cases in which RNA serves as a template for synthesis of RNA rather than DNA serving as the template. Some RNA viruses use RNA-dependent RNA polymerases, and cellular systems in some organisms also employ RNA-dependent RNA synthesis. Reverse transcription instead produces DNA using an RNA template. These reactions are not exceptions to the direction of information copying in the ordinary DNA-directed transcription mechanism defined in this chapter. They are related but distinct biochemical pathways. The exam question must be read carefully to identify the template and product rather than assuming that any polymerase called an RNA polymerase necessarily uses DNA.

The fundamental copying rules also explain the logic of a common biotechnology application: producing an RNA of a chosen sequence from a suitable DNA template. The desired sequence must be placed in the appropriate orientation relative to an initiation region recognized by the selected transcription system. A promoter that directs transcription into the opposite orientation can yield an unintended complementary RNA or fail to produce the intended product. Similarly, changes in the end of the transcribed region can alter the length or composition of the resulting RNA. Such applications rely on the same strand and coordinate reasoning used throughout this chapter, not on a separate set of base-pairing laws.

A meaningful final model of transcription should allow predictions at several levels. Changing a base within the copied interval changes the corresponding primary RNA position if faithful synthesis continues. Changing a promoter may alter the number of initiation events or their positions. Changing the concentration of a required substrate can alter productive extension under suitable conditions. Altering termination can change the distribution of RNA endpoints. Altering RNA removal can change RNA abundance without necessarily altering transcription. These predictions connect chemistry, sequence, kinetics and experimental interpretation in a coherent framework. They also prepare the student for the next chapter, where the bacterial RNA polymerase and promoter recognition machinery explain how the general initiation problem is solved in a specific cellular system.

Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The rightward RNA matches the plus-reference sequence with U replacing T. The leftward RNA is its reverse complement, UCGACU. Same interval does not imply same strand.

45. In a defined assay, the WT and mutant generate identical numbers of 8-nt initiated chains. WT makes 90 full-length RNAs and mutant makes 10. Which interpretation is best?

  1. Mutant cannot recognize any promoter
  2. A defect after the measured early initiation step is supported
  3. All mutant DNA is absent
  4. The full-length RNAs prove a translation defect
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Early initiation remains detectable; divergence in full-length product points to a later transition, elongation, release or product stability. More measurements are needed to locate the precise defect.

46. A reaction with polymerase, DNA and NTPs yields label in a high-molecular-weight fraction. Which control most directly tests whether that fraction contains RNA?

  1. Adding excess unrelated protein
  2. Counting promoter motifs
  3. Repeating without ribosomes only
  4. Testing susceptibility to an appropriate RNase against controls
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. RNase sensitivity supports product identity. Template-dependence controls are separately needed to demonstrate DNA-directed synthesis; ribosomes are not required for the polymerization reaction.

47. Only gamma-labeled GTP is supplied as a tracer. A primary RNA starts with A and contains several internal G residues. What is predicted for an unprocessed standard product?

  1. No gamma label retained through ordinary incorporation
  2. Every G residue retains gamma label
  3. Only the final G retains gamma label
  4. All four bases become labeled
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Internal GTP loses beta and gamma as PPi. A GTP start could retain gamma label, but the specified initiating base is A. This assumes no secondary exchange or modification.

48. A −25 mutation changes RNA output from 100 to 20 molecules/min while the mapped +1 and product sequence remain unchanged. Which inference is most defensible?

  1. The −25 base must appear in every RNA
  2. The mutant now transcribes 3′ to 5′
  3. A change in productive initiation is consistent with the data
  4. Translation must begin at −25
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The upstream mutation is not copied in the stated transcript. The unchanged start and sequence support a regulatory effect, although downstream stability controls are needed to prove altered initiation.

49. A 200-nt RNA band accumulates when a required NTP is absent. Upon restoration, the labeled short chains become 600-nt products. Which model is favored?

  1. Irreversible termination at 200
  2. Retained complexes resumed extension
  3. A DNA replication origin at 200
  4. Translation of the missing NTP
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Conversion of the previously labeled short RNA to longer product supports reversible arrest, assuming the pulse/chase tracks the same chains. Released terminal products would not normally extend in the same complex.

50. A mutation increases polymerase occupancy along a gene but decreases completed RNA output. Which explanation is compatible with both observations?

  1. Occupancy proves increased completion rate
  2. All polymerases move faster
  3. DNA is converted into protein
  4. Longer residence or stalling with fewer productive completions
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Occupancy reflects residence and entry. Accumulated stalled complexes can raise occupancy while lowering completion. Neither occupancy alone nor protein measures reveal the exact flux.

51. A downstream probe detects a 900-nt species after a terminator mutation. An upstream probe detects a 500-nt species. What additional evidence is most useful for demonstrating readthrough?

  1. Evidence that the 900-nt RNA also contains upstream sequence in the same molecule
  2. A larger cell size
  3. The presence of an AUG
  4. An RNase-free buffer alone
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Readthrough requires continuity from upstream initiation through the normal endpoint. The downstream RNA might instead originate at an independent promoter, so size and strand-specific sequence continuity matter.

52. A promoter and its entire transcription unit are inverted together in a simplified context. What is the expected change?

  1. RNA must be synthesized 3′ to 5′
  2. All coding bases are replaced with complementary RNA bases relative to their own gene
  3. Chromosomal direction reverses, while intrinsic primary RNA sequence can remain unchanged
  4. Transcription and replication become identical
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. An intact inverted block preserves internal promoter–gene orientation while changing direction relative to the reference chromosome. The prediction assumes no contextual disruption.

53. A protein becomes much less abundant after a start codon mutation, but primary RNA production and length are unchanged. What conclusion is best supported?

  1. The DNA template cannot be recognized
  2. A downstream defect in translation is plausible
  3. The transcription start is always AUG
  4. RNA polymerase has lost Mg²⁺ binding
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Unchanged primary transcription argues against a major initiation defect in the measured conditions. A start codon mutation can affect translation; protein stability could also contribute.

54. Two RNA species have the same steady-state abundance. Species X has half the removal constant of Y. In the simple model, what follows?

  1. X has twice the synthesis rate of Y
  2. Their synthesis rates must be equal
  3. X cannot be transcribed
  4. X has half the synthesis rate of Y
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. At steady state s=kR. With equal R and kX=kY/2, sX=sY/2. Equal abundance does not imply equal production.

55. A reproducible RNA sequence difference is absent from the corresponding genomic DNA. Which conclusion is justified first?

  1. Further controls are needed to distinguish RNA editing, copying error and analytical artifact
  2. A heritable DNA mutation has been demonstrated
  3. Every transcript must contain the difference
  4. The promoter is necessarily inverted
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. An RNA–DNA difference warrants investigation but is not by itself diagnostic of editing or transcription error. Independent sample and method controls are required.
