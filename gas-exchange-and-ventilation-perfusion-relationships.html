1. The biological problem: two flows and a diffusion barrier

A mammal must continuously replenish the oxygen consumed by its cells and remove the carbon dioxide produced during metabolism. The respiratory system does not accomplish this task merely by moving air in and out of the chest. Air must reach exchange surfaces, blood must flow close to those surfaces, and molecules must be able to cross the intervening tissues. Failure at any one of these three stages can reduce the amount of oxygen reaching the systemic circulation. A person may therefore have a normal breathing frequency and nevertheless exchange gas inefficiently. Similarly, a lung that expands easily may have abnormal gas exchange if the pulmonary blood supply is interrupted.

The movement of fresh gas between the external environment and the alveoli is called ventilation. The movement of blood through pulmonary capillaries is perfusion. Both are forms of bulk flow, meaning that many molecules travel together because of a pressure difference that drives the fluid. In contrast, diffusion is the net movement of individual molecules down their chemical potential gradient. Under the conditions used in elementary respiratory physiology, a difference in partial pressure provides a useful description of this driving force for the same gas on either side of an interface. Respiratory muscles generate airflow, the heart generates blood flow, and random thermal motion accomplishes diffusion. These three physical processes are not interchangeable.

Consider an isolated alveolus with no fresh gas entering it but an intact blood supply. Initially, oxygen diffuses into the passing blood and carbon dioxide diffuses in the reverse direction. Oxygen in the alveolus is progressively depleted and carbon dioxide accumulates. If enough time passes under idealized steady conditions, the alveolar gas approaches equilibrium with the mixed venous blood arriving at the region. The diffusion barrier may remain perfectly normal; the loss of exchange is caused by failure to maintain the gradients. Conversely, an alveolus supplied with fresh gas but no perfusion can have a very high oxygen partial pressure, yet it delivers essentially no oxygen to the circulating blood. High alveolar oxygen is not evidence of high oxygen uptake.

The pulmonary circulation and the systemic circulation work in series. Blood returning from the systemic organs mixes in the right heart and travels through the pulmonary arteries to alveolar capillaries. Blood from the lungs then enters pulmonary veins and returns to the left heart. In a steady state without major intracardiac or vascular bypasses, the average amount of blood pumped through the pulmonary and systemic circuits per minute must be approximately equal. The pulmonary arterial blood is not fully depleted of oxygen; it represents what remains after diverse tissues have extracted different amounts of oxygen. Mixed venous oxygen partial pressure is accordingly a useful starting point for the analysis of pulmonary exchange.

External respiration refers here to exchange between alveolar gas and pulmonary capillary blood; internal respiration refers to exchange between systemic capillary blood and tissue. Cellular respiration is the set of metabolic reactions that consumes oxygen in aerobic cells and produces carbon dioxide through oxidative pathways. These terms describe different levels of organization. A mitochondrion does not ventilate, an alveolus does not synthesize ATP for the body by consuming all the oxygen that enters it, and a pulmonary capillary is not a source of fresh atmospheric oxygen. Oxygen follows a series of connected compartments until it is consumed, whereas carbon dioxide usually moves in the opposite direction. Each compartment modifies the conditions encountered by the next.

This distinction is especially useful in interpreting experiments. A sample of arterial blood reveals the integrated outcome of the respiratory units whose blood contributes to the sample, whereas a sample of expired gas represents gas from the regions that communicate with the airways during expiration. These two samples are weighted by different physical flows. A region receiving little blood may dominate part of an expired-gas sample but contribute almost nothing to arterial blood. A region receiving much blood and little ventilation can substantially depress arterial oxygen while contributing relatively little gas to an expired sample. Comparing these measurements without considering their origins can lead to incorrect conclusions about the functioning lung.

The preceding chapter developed the relation between tidal volume, breathing frequency and effective alveolar ventilation. Here the same concept becomes a local one. A total alveolar ventilation of four litres per minute is meaningful for the lung as a whole, but it does not describe how that ventilation is distributed among millions of exchange units. The distribution of perfusion is similarly heterogeneous. Gas exchange is therefore a problem of local matching as well as total supply. To understand why matching matters, we first need a quantitative definition of the driving force for diffusion and of the amount of gas present in a liquid.

2. Partial pressure, Dalton's law and humidification

Gas pressure results from molecular collisions with a surface. In a mixture of approximately ideal gases, molecules of each species contribute to the total pressure according to their abundance and thermal motion. Dalton's law states that the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures of the individual species. For gas i with mole fraction Fi in a mixture of total pressure P, its partial pressure Pi is Fi multiplied by P. A mole fraction is a number between zero and one, not a percentage written as a whole number. If oxygen represents twenty-one percent of a gas mixture, its mole fraction is 0.21.

The usual approximate atmospheric pressure at sea level is 760 mmHg, equivalent to one standard atmosphere or about 101.3 kPa. Dry atmospheric oxygen has a mole fraction close to 0.2095. In a simplified problem using 0.21, its partial pressure is 0.21 × 760 = 159.6 mmHg. This quantity describes the pressure contribution of oxygen in the mixture, not a separate pressure compartment within the air. All of the gas molecules occupy the same physical volume. Nitrogen and oxygen can diffuse in different directions within a mixture if their individual gradients differ, even when total pressure is almost the same on both sides.

The ideal-gas equation offers a way to understand Dalton's law. For a mixture containing n total moles in volume V at absolute temperature T, P = nRT/V. The contribution of oxygen can be written PO2 = nO2RT/V under the ideal-mixture approximation. Dividing the second expression by the first gives PO2/P = nO2/n = FO2. The argument assumes that intermolecular interactions are negligible and that temperature is uniform. Near normal atmospheric pressures these assumptions are adequate for the gas-law calculations used in respiratory physiology. They should not be extended uncritically to very high pressures or to liquids, for which gas dissolution must be treated separately.

At an altitude where barometric pressure is 500 mmHg, the dry inspired oxygen partial pressure is approximately 105 mmHg if the atmospheric oxygen fraction remains 0.21. The air has not necessarily lost oxygen relative to nitrogen; both are less abundant per unit volume at the lower pressure. This is why statements about the percentage of atmospheric oxygen alone do not characterize the challenge of altitude. Conversely, at the same pressure, increasing oxygen fraction increases oxygen partial pressure. Pressure and gas composition are two separate variables that influence the partial pressure available at the respiratory surface.

The inspired gas encounters warm, moist airway surfaces. Water molecules enter the gas phase until the air becomes nearly saturated with water vapour under normal conditions by the time it reaches the distal airways. At a temperature of 37 °C the saturation vapour pressure of water is approximately 47 mmHg. This pressure is primarily a function of temperature when water vapour is saturated; it is not a fixed percentage of total barometric pressure. If the overall gas pressure is about 760 mmHg, the remaining dry-gas components together account for 713 mmHg. Oxygen supplies its fraction of that remaining pressure.

Consequently, the humidified inspired oxygen pressure PIO2 is FIO2(PB − PH2O), where FIO2 is the inspired oxygen fraction on a dry-gas basis, PB is barometric pressure, and PH2O is water vapour pressure. All pressures must use the same units. At sea level, 0.21 × (760 − 47) = 149.73 mmHg. At PB = 500 mmHg, the same calculation gives 0.21 × 453 = 95.13 mmHg. Water vapour subtracts 47 mmHg from the pressure budget in both examples, but the fraction of total pressure occupied by water is larger at the lower barometric pressure. One must not multiply 760 by 0.21 and then subtract 47; only the dry portion of the pressure budget is multiplied by the dry oxygen fraction.

There is an important distinction between humidified inspired gas and alveolar gas. The humidified inspired gas is a reference input to the respiratory system. The alveolar gas has additionally exchanged oxygen and carbon dioxide with the blood. It contains less oxygen and considerably more carbon dioxide than inspired air. Alveolar oxygen at sea level under typical resting conditions is around 100 mmHg rather than 150 mmHg. The difference cannot be explained by humidification alone. It reflects oxygen uptake by blood and the relation between metabolism and ventilation.

At the beginning of an inspiration, the conducting airways contain gas left from the preceding expiration. Fresh atmospheric gas subsequently enters and mixes with that gas. Alveoli also contain a substantial reserve of gas that persists between ordinary breaths, the functional residual capacity. As a result, alveolar partial pressures vary less during normal breathing than they would if the lung emptied completely after each expiration. The pressure figures used in many exam questions are approximations to average values, rather than measurements at every instant of the breathing cycle.

Mixed expired gas includes gas from the conducting dead space and from exchange units with varying ventilation and perfusion. It is not identical in composition to ideal alveolar gas. In a normally ventilating adult, mixed expired oxygen is generally higher than alveolar oxygen because gas from the conducting airways has undergone little or no oxygen extraction. Mixed expired carbon dioxide is generally lower than alveolar carbon dioxide for the corresponding reason. These differences explain why the source of a gas sample must be identified before a numerical value is interpreted.

3. Henry's law: dissolved concentration is not total gas content

The preceding equations explain oxygen pressure in a gas mixture. To cross from an alveolus into a capillary, however, oxygen must enter an aqueous environment. At equilibrium between an approximately ideal gas and a dilute liquid, Henry's law can be written C = αP, where C is the concentration of physically dissolved gas, P is the partial pressure of the gas in equilibrium with the liquid, and α is a solubility coefficient. Its numerical value depends on the gas, solvent, temperature and chosen concentration and pressure units. Henry's law is an equilibrium relation. It does not specify how quickly equilibrium will be reached.

The partial pressure or tension of dissolved oxygen in blood expresses the pressure that would be established by that dissolved oxygen in an equilibrated gas phase. It is not the total hydrostatic pressure of the blood. Pulmonary capillary blood can have an oxygen tension much larger than its hydrostatic vascular pressure without any contradiction. These quantities describe different physical phenomena. A blood sample may have a hydrostatic pressure of a few millimetres of mercury relative to the surrounding reference while its dissolved oxygen is in equilibrium with a gas having a partial pressure near 100 mmHg.

In plasma and blood at body temperature, a representative oxygen solubility coefficient is approximately 0.003 mL O2 per dL of blood per mmHg when oxygen volume is expressed using conventional physiological reference conditions. At an oxygen tension of 100 mmHg, physically dissolved oxygen is approximately 0.3 mL per dL. At 40 mmHg it is about 0.12 mL per dL. The amount of oxygen carried in blood is much greater because most is reversibly associated with haemoglobin. That chemically bound oxygen does not contribute to oxygen partial pressure in the same manner as freely dissolved oxygen. It can become available to the dissolved pool as equilibrium shifts, but it must dissociate before it can diffuse across the tissue.

This distinction solves an apparent puzzle. If two blood samples have the same oxygen partial pressure but different haemoglobin concentrations, they can have similar physically dissolved oxygen concentrations while containing quite different total amounts of oxygen. At the same time, if haemoglobin binds newly dissolved oxygen, it can help maintain a low free oxygen tension relative to the alveolar gas during the early stage of loading. Further oxygen can then enter the blood. The protein increases blood oxygen carrying capacity without directly raising the dissolved oxygen tension at a specified equilibrium pressure. The detailed saturation curve belongs to the next chapter; here we need the distinction to understand diffusion and mixing.

Carbon dioxide is considerably more soluble in biological fluids than oxygen. At the same partial pressure, the physically dissolved quantity of carbon dioxide is much greater than that of oxygen. Its aqueous chemistry includes conversion to bicarbonate, which is a separate chemical process and not simply an increase in physical dissolution. When considering diffusion through the respiratory membrane, the solubility of the molecular gas in the intervening tissue is an important determinant of transfer. It is therefore possible for carbon dioxide to cross readily despite having a smaller pressure difference than oxygen in typical pulmonary exchange.

The pressure convention is particularly useful across a boundary between unlike phases. A dissolved concentration expressed in moles per litre is not numerically comparable to a gas concentration expressed per litre of air without accounting for solubility and phase properties. At equilibrium, the partial pressure of a gas can be equal across the interface even though the concentration per unit volume differs considerably. Thus equality of oxygen concentration is not required for equality of oxygen chemical potential. Henry's law connects pressure to dissolved concentration in each medium. This is why a diagram of gas exchange should label partial pressure rather than imply that concentration per millilitre is identical in blood and air.

Suppose a liquid has oxygen solubility coefficient α = 0.003 in the units above and is equilibrated at PO2 = 80 mmHg. It contains 0.24 mL dissolved O2 per dL. Doubling the oxygen tension to 160 mmHg doubles the dissolved amount to 0.48 mL per dL if temperature, solvent and the coefficient remain constant. The amount of oxygen bound to haemoglobin need not double because reversible binding has a finite capacity and nonlinear pressure dependence. The linear law applies only to the physically dissolved fraction. This simple separation prevents many incorrect interpretations of oxygen supplementation, anaemia and regional gas exchange.

Gas molecules still move randomly when an interface has reached equilibrium. At equilibrium, the rate of molecules crossing in one direction equals the rate crossing in the other, so there is no net transfer. The term equilibrium does not imply that molecules have stopped moving. When metabolism removes dissolved oxygen from one side or ventilation replenishes it on the other, the equilibrium condition is disturbed and a net flux appears. The magnitude of that flux depends not only on the driving force but also on the properties of the barrier, as the next section develops.

4. Fick's diffusion law and the respiratory membrane

A diffusion barrier is a region that molecules must traverse between two compartments. For oxygen leaving an alveolus, the path includes the surface liquid, the thin alveolar epithelial cell, basement membrane and any intervening interstitial material, the capillary endothelium, plasma, and eventually the erythrocyte membrane and intracellular fluid before reaction with haemoglobin. Not every path is geometrically identical. The gas-exchange portion of the lung presents an enormous aggregate area because many alveolar surfaces lie alongside a dense capillary network. Thin regions of the barrier are particularly important for rapid molecular transfer.

In a planar homogeneous medium, Fick's first law is J = −D dC/dx. J is molar flux per unit area per unit time, D is a diffusion coefficient with dimensions length squared per unit time, C is concentration per unit volume, and x is distance. The negative sign expresses movement down the concentration gradient. If concentration falls along the positive x direction, dC/dx is negative and J is positive in that direction. If the sign of the gradient reverses, the net flux reverses. The equation does not describe molecules moving only in one direction; it describes the difference between two large random crossing rates.

For a membrane of effective area A and thickness T, assuming approximately steady one-dimensional diffusion, a constant coefficient and an approximately linear concentration profile, the magnitude of transfer can be written Ṅ = DA(C1 − C2)/T. If concentration within the membrane is proportional to the gas partial pressure by a solubility coefficient α, then Ṅ = DαA(P1 − P2)/T. The product Dα is sometimes represented as a gas-specific permeability factor in elementary physiology. Authors differ in how they define their diffusion constants, so the units and definition must be checked before combining formulas from different texts.

The expression predicts that increasing the effective surface area increases the transfer capacity if other variables remain fixed. Destruction of alveolar walls can reduce area and thus impair oxygen transfer. Increasing barrier thickness decreases transfer capacity; fluid accumulating within the interstitium or excessive connective tissue in the gas-exchange path can increase the effective diffusion distance. These are direct predictions under fixed pressure-gradient conditions. In the intact lung, other effects can occur simultaneously: capillary perfusion may change, ventilation may become uneven, and local partial pressures may shift. An observed arterial oxygen change therefore cannot be attributed automatically to thickness alone.

The solubility of a gas in the barrier and its molecular mobility are both important. A useful approximation for comparing respiratory gases is that diffusibility increases with solubility and decreases with the square root of molecular mass under specified conditions. Carbon dioxide is somewhat heavier than oxygen but has much greater solubility in water and biological tissues. Its effective diffusibility through the exchange membrane is consequently often described as about twenty times that of oxygen. This is an approximate comparison, not a universal ratio for all tissues, temperatures or gases. In a simple model, a carbon dioxide gradient of only 5 mmHg may support substantial transfer while the oxygen gradient is much larger.

Consider a hypothetical membrane with an arbitrary permeability factor K = Dα. Its area is 40 units and thickness is 1 unit, giving conductance proportional to 40K. If the area falls to 30 units and thickness doubles, the conductance becomes 15K. The new conductance is 15/40 = 0.375 of the original, a reduction of 62.5 percent. If the pressure difference is maintained, the transfer falls by the same fraction. If the gradient increases, some of the reduction can be offset. The exercise is not a clinical estimate of lung function; it isolates the geometrical dependence of diffusion.

Real alveolar exchange involves barriers and reaction steps arranged in series, not a single perfectly uniform slab. A gas traverses successive media before entering blood and binding to its chemical carrier. Resistance to transfer can therefore be described in terms of contributions from the membrane and from the blood itself. A highly permeable epithelial surface does not guarantee instantaneous uptake if erythrocytes are sparse or chemical binding is slow. Likewise, a larger number of erythrocytes cannot remove the resistance of a severely thickened interstitial layer. An effective diffusion measurement reflects the combined properties of the exchanging system.

The geometry of a lung also matters because total area and functional area are not necessarily equal. An alveolar surface that is ventilated but unperfused has little opportunity for sustained gas transfer into flowing blood. A perfused region that has collapsed may have both poor ventilation and altered exposed exchange area. Increasing blood flow can recruit capillaries and distend previously narrow vessels, making more of the available exchange surface functionally accessible. These changes help explain why measured pulmonary diffusing capacity is not a fixed anatomical constant across all physiological states.

Fick's diffusion law should not be confused with the Fick principle of whole-organ gas balance. Both are useful in respiratory physiology, but they answer different questions. The diffusion law describes local transport driven by concentration or pressure gradients and constrained by barrier properties. The Fick principle uses conservation of a substance to relate uptake to blood flow and the difference in blood content across an organ. An experiment measuring oxygen consumption and arterial and mixed venous oxygen contents can estimate cardiac output by mass balance; it does not thereby measure the microscopic diffusion coefficient of oxygen. Later sections use both relations for different purposes.

5. Alveolar gas composition and the alveolar gas equation

The air reaching the respiratory region continuously mixes with residual alveolar gas while oxygen is removed and carbon dioxide enters. At steady state the amount of oxygen extracted from the alveolar gas per minute reflects metabolic oxygen uptake, and the amount of carbon dioxide added reflects metabolic carbon dioxide elimination. These fluxes are not necessarily equal. The respiratory exchange ratio R is carbon dioxide output divided by oxygen uptake when both volumes are expressed under the same reference conditions. In a resting subject under stable mixed-substrate metabolism, 0.8 is a common illustrative value. Oxidation of glucose yields a ratio of 1, whereas oxidation of many fats yields a lower ratio near 0.7. The actual ratio can differ, especially during nonsteady conditions.

An elementary mass-balance derivation clarifies the alveolar gas equation. If inspired and expired alveolar dry-gas volume flows are approximated as equal, inspired carbon dioxide is negligible, and gas volume conventions are consistent, oxygen uptake is proportional to alveolar ventilation multiplied by the difference between inspired and alveolar oxygen fractions. Carbon dioxide output is proportional to alveolar ventilation multiplied by the alveolar carbon dioxide fraction. Dividing the second relationship by the first cancels the common ventilation term. The resulting approximation is R ≈ FACO2/(FIO2 − FAO2), where the fractions refer to dry gas. Rearranging yields FAO2 ≈ FIO2 − FACO2/R.

Multiplying by the dry alveolar gas pressure PB − PH2O gives PAO2 ≈ FIO2(PB − PH2O) − PACO2/R. Capital A in PAO2 denotes alveolar gas. Lowercase a in PaO2 denotes arterial blood. In many common physiological calculations arterial carbon dioxide tension PaCO2 is used as an approximation to ideal alveolar carbon dioxide tension. The simplified alveolar gas equation then reads PAO2 ≈ PIO2 − PaCO2/R. This distinction matters when the lung is heterogeneous: a measured arterial carbon dioxide pressure cannot be assumed to be the value in every alveolus.

The simplification is commonly useful when breathing room air and in stable conditions. A fuller correction accounts for the difference between inspired and expired gas volumes associated with a respiratory exchange ratio other than one. An often-used complete form, neglecting inspired carbon dioxide, is PAO2 = PIO2 − PACO2[FIO2 + (1 − FIO2)/R]. For R = 0.8 and FIO2 = 0.21, the bracket equals 1.1975 rather than 1.25. With carbon dioxide pressure 40 mmHg, the simplified equation differs by about 2.1 mmHg. When oxygen fraction is high, the correction is more important. Unless the problem specifies a full equation, the familiar simplified version is usually intended for introductory room-air examples.

For a resting room-air example at sea level, use PB = 760 mmHg, PH2O = 47 mmHg, FIO2 = 0.21, PaCO2 = 40 mmHg and R = 0.8. First calculate PIO2 = 149.73 mmHg. The carbon dioxide term is 40/0.8 = 50 mmHg. Subtraction gives PAO2 ≈ 99.73 mmHg. If a blood sample shows PaO2 = 95 mmHg, the alveolar–arterial oxygen difference in this example is approximately 4.73 mmHg. These numbers are illustrative, not universal normal values.

If alveolar ventilation falls while metabolic carbon dioxide output remains constant, arterial carbon dioxide tension usually rises in a relatively uniform lung. At steady state, the relationship PaCO2 ≈ 863 V̇CO2/V̇A is useful when carbon dioxide production is in litres per minute STPD and alveolar ventilation is in litres per minute BTPS, with pressure in mmHg. STPD refers to standard temperature and pressure, dry; BTPS refers to body temperature and ambient pressure, saturated with water vapour. The constant accounts for differences in these conventions. If both flow variables are expressed in mL/min instead, the same ratio and constant apply.

For a hypothetical resting subject producing 0.20 L/min carbon dioxide with an alveolar ventilation of 4.32 L/min, the estimated PaCO2 is 863 × 0.20/4.32 ≈ 40 mmHg. If alveolar ventilation falls to 2.88 L/min without a change in production, PaCO2 rises to approximately 60 mmHg. With unchanged inspired pressure and R = 0.8, the simplified PAO2 falls from about 100 to about 75 mmHg. A perfectly intact diffusion barrier cannot fully compensate for this lower supply of oxygen pressure. This is a mechanism of hypoxaemia distinct from the failure of oxygen to equilibrate with gas already present in an alveolus.

At a lower barometric pressure of 500 mmHg, inspired humidified oxygen tension is about 95.1 mmHg on room air. If carbon dioxide tension remained 40 mmHg and R were 0.8, the calculated alveolar oxygen tension would be only about 45.1 mmHg. If the subject increases alveolar ventilation so that carbon dioxide tension falls to 30 mmHg, estimated PAO2 rises to 57.6 mmHg. Hyperventilation improves alveolar oxygen pressure in this situation by reducing the carbon dioxide term. It does not restore the barometric pressure and cannot elevate alveolar oxygen indefinitely beyond the pressure of humidified inspired oxygen.

The alveolar gas equation is a model of the gas compartment rather than a direct measurement of arterial oxygen. The calculated PAO2 is the oxygen pressure expected under its assumptions, while PaO2 reflects blood leaving the entire lung and entering systemic arteries. A difference between these pressures can arise when some blood fails to equilibrate with well-ventilated gas, when exchange units have different V/Q ratios, or when oxygen transfer is diffusion limited. A student should not substitute PaO2 for PAO2 in the equation simply because both have similar values under favorable conditions.

6. Pulmonary and systemic exchange gradients

At the entrance to a pulmonary capillary, mixed venous blood typically has an oxygen tension near 40 mmHg and a carbon dioxide tension near 46 mmHg in a resting adult at sea level. Ideal alveolar gas under representative conditions has oxygen tension around 100 mmHg and carbon dioxide tension around 40 mmHg. These figures provide useful rounded examples; they vary with metabolic activity, cardiac output, inspired gas and ventilation. The oxygen gradient points from alveolar gas into blood. The carbon dioxide gradient points from blood toward alveolar gas. Both transfers can occur across the same surface at the same time because each gas follows its own gradient.

Oxygen dissolves at the alveolar surface, traverses the exchange membrane and enters plasma. It can diffuse into erythrocytes and associate reversibly with haemoglobin. As capillary blood flows farther along the exchange surface, its dissolved oxygen tension rises. The pressure difference between alveolar gas and blood becomes progressively smaller. If equilibrium is reached, there is little additional net uptake from that particular alveolar compartment, even though the erythrocyte continues to move along the capillary. A gas that has already equilibrated can be carried away in greater quantity if more blood with a lower initial content is delivered.

For carbon dioxide, the direction is reversed. Molecular carbon dioxide diffuses out of plasma and erythrocytes into the alveolar gas. The dissolved pool is replenished as chemically transported forms are converted back into molecular carbon dioxide. This maintains transfer during the passage of blood through the lung. Since carbon dioxide transfer is facilitated by its high solubility, pressure equilibration usually occurs readily in healthy lungs. However, elimination of carbon dioxide at the level of the whole body still depends on adequate effective alveolar ventilation. A rapidly equilibrating gas is not automatically eliminated efficiently if gas is not removed from the alveoli.

A normal pulmonary end-capillary oxygen tension may approach the local alveolar value. Arterial blood sampled after blood from many regions and small physiological venous admixtures has combined may show a somewhat lower oxygen tension. It is helpful to reserve the term end-capillary for blood leaving a specified exchange unit, and arterial for the integrated blood downstream of the lung. The two pressures cannot be assumed equal in every case. This is a central distinction in the study of shunt, diffusion limitation and ventilation–perfusion mismatch.

At the systemic tissue, mitochondria consume oxygen, lowering the intracellular dissolved oxygen concentration and establishing diffusion gradients from the capillary through interstitial fluid toward cells. Tissue oxygen tensions are heterogeneous because cells lie at different distances from vessels and have different rates of consumption. A single textbook value of 40 mmHg is best understood as a representative venous or regional extracellular value, not an invariant oxygen tension of all tissue cells. Oxygen pressure near active mitochondria may be considerably lower than in the arterial blood supplying the tissue.

When a muscle increases its oxygen consumption, oxygen tension in its immediate environment tends to fall unless oxygen delivery increases sufficiently. This strengthens the local driving force for diffusion and promotes unloading of oxygen from blood. It can also trigger local metabolic vasodilation, increasing flow and bringing additional oxygen. The eventual tissue tension is determined jointly by oxygen delivery, cellular consumption and diffusion distances. If perfusion is extremely low, a favorable local gradient cannot sustain high total oxygen delivery because insufficient oxygen enters the region in the blood.

Systemic carbon dioxide follows the complementary path. Carbon dioxide produced by metabolism diffuses through cells and interstitial fluid into blood, increasing the amount carried back to the lungs. Local carbon dioxide pressure and hydrogen ion concentration can change with metabolism and blood flow; chemical transport and buffering moderate the relationship between production and free molecular carbon dioxide pressure. The detailed biochemical steps belong to 7.C.07. Here, the essential point is that metabolism acts as an oxygen sink and carbon dioxide source, thereby maintaining tissue exchange gradients.

A simple steady-state mass balance gives oxygen consumption V̇O2 = Q(CaO2 − CvO2), where Q is systemic blood flow and C denotes total oxygen content per unit blood volume. If Q is in L/min and C in mL O2/L blood, consumption is in mL O2/min. In a hypothetical subject with Q = 5 L/min, arterial content 200 mL/L and mixed venous content 150 mL/L, oxygen uptake is 250 mL/min. These contents cannot be replaced by partial pressures in mmHg, because blood oxygen content is not a linear multiple of pressure over the full physiological range. The next chapter develops the underlying binding relationship.

The oxygen mass balance also illustrates why mixed venous blood is not a fixed input to the lung. If tissue consumption rises while cardiac output and arterial content stay unchanged, the arterial–venous content difference increases. Blood returns with less oxygen, and its oxygen tension may be lower. If pulmonary transfer capacity remains adequate, the lung can restore much of this oxygen before blood re-enters the arterial circulation. If transfer capacity or local matching is impaired, a lower mixed venous oxygen content can magnify the resulting arterial oxygen deficit. The respiratory and cardiovascular systems thus influence each other even when their primary defects occur in different organs.

7. Measuring diffusion capacity: what a transfer test actually measures

The exchange membrane is too extensive and structurally complex for a single microscope measurement of thickness to describe the function of an intact lung. A physiological approach is to administer a gas whose uptake depends strongly on the transfer properties of the alveolar–capillary interface, measure how much disappears from the alveolar gas, and relate its disappearance to the driving pressure. The resulting quantity is a conductance: it describes how much gas can be transferred per unit time for each unit of pressure difference. In respiratory physiology this conductance is called the pulmonary diffusing capacity or transfer factor.

For a gas X, the operational relationship is V̇X = DLX(PAX − mean PcX), where V̇X denotes transfer rate, DLX denotes lung diffusing capacity for X, PAX denotes alveolar gas tension, and mean PcX denotes an appropriate mean capillary blood tension over the exchanging path. If transfer is expressed as mL gas per minute and pressure in mmHg, DLX has units mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹. DL is not a molecular diffusion coefficient with units of area per time. It incorporates geometric properties, gas solubility, molecular movement and blood-side effects. A larger value means greater transfer conductance under the conditions of the measurement.

Measuring oxygen diffusing capacity directly is challenging because capillary oxygen tension changes substantially as blood becomes oxygenated. The gradient at the capillary entrance is larger than the gradient near the exit, and the average driving pressure is not simply the difference between alveolar oxygen and a measured systemic arterial value. Moreover, different lung regions have different local gas compositions and different transit times. One cannot place a single pressure sensor along all of the pulmonary capillaries simultaneously. A tracer whose capillary pressure remains low provides a useful way to simplify the measurement.

Carbon monoxide is used at a low tracer concentration in standardized pulmonary transfer tests because it binds strongly to haemoglobin. At the small exposure used in these tests, the dissolved CO tension of blood entering and traversing the capillary is usually low relative to the alveolar CO tension. Thus uptake can be related approximately to alveolar CO pressure, giving DLCO ≈ V̇CO/PACO. This is an approximation, not a claim that the capillary CO pressure is literally zero. Background carboxyhaemoglobin and prior exposure can alter the blood-side pressure and the number of available binding sites. The clinical measurement uses established safety and measurement standards; the examination concept is the physical basis of the test rather than the administration of a test gas.

In a representative single-breath method, the subject inhales a standardized dilute test mixture containing a small amount of CO and an inert tracer gas. The tracer helps estimate the alveolar volume reached by the inspired mixture and distinguish dilution by residual lung gas from removal of CO into blood. During a controlled breath-hold interval, CO is transferred out of the alveolar compartment into blood. A subsequent alveolar gas sample is analyzed for the remaining CO and the inert gas. Because the inert tracer is selected to undergo negligible uptake in the simplified model, changes in its concentration primarily reflect dilution rather than gas transfer. Comparing the two signals permits estimation of the amount of CO removed relative to the driving tension and time.

A crucial experimental distinction is between gas that has disappeared because it entered the blood and gas that has simply been mixed into a larger volume. If a test gas concentration fell by half immediately upon entering a lung with residual gas, that change would not prove that half the test gas had diffused across the membrane. An inert tracer provides information about the dilution process. A valid measurement also requires appropriate correction for conducting-airway dead space and gas-volume conventions. Poorly communicating regions may not participate fully in a single-breath measurement, so the accessible alveolar volume need not equal the total anatomical gas volume of the thorax.

A simplified numerical example illustrates the conductance interpretation without reproducing a clinical testing algorithm. Suppose an exchange system transfers 90 mL of a gas per minute and the effective mean pressure difference is 3 mmHg. Its transfer conductance is 90/3 = 30 mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹. If the measured rate becomes 60 mL/min at the same effective pressure difference, conductance has fallen to 20 in the same units. If instead the rate fell to 60 because the pressure difference fell to 2 mmHg, the conductance would still be 30. Comparing raw uptake rates without accounting for pressure would confuse a change in driving force with a change in transfer properties.

The analogy with electrical conductance is useful when used carefully. A large conductance allows a high current at a given voltage difference; a large gas transfer capacity allows a high transfer rate at a given gas tension difference. However, the respiratory barrier contains living structures and reactive blood, so its conductance can change with perfusion, recruitment, blood chemistry and time. It is neither a rigid resistor nor a direct measurement of oxygen delivery to all organs. The analogy supplies a way to distinguish driving force from conductance, not an exhaustive physical model.

A landmark refinement was developed by Roughton and Forster in 1957. They distinguished resistance of the membrane from the finite rate of reaction of the gas with haemoglobin. The classical relation is 1/DLCO = 1/DMCO + 1/(θCO Vc), where DMCO denotes membrane diffusing capacity, Vc is effective pulmonary capillary blood volume and θCO is the rate of CO uptake by blood per unit blood volume per unit CO pressure. Both DMCO and θCO Vc have dimensions of transfer conductance. Their reciprocals therefore have the dimensions of resistance to gas transfer. Adding those reciprocals corresponds to resistances in series.

This model explains why a normal membrane can give a reduced measured DLCO in a subject with reduced haemoglobin. The gas must encounter available haemoglobin in the capillary blood; decreasing the available reaction capacity increases the blood-side resistance. It also explains why pulmonary vascular abnormalities that reduce the available capillary blood volume can reduce DLCO. Conversely, increasing capillary blood volume can increase measured transfer capacity without necessarily changing the microscopic thickness of the alveolar epithelium. A transfer test cannot by itself assign every decrease to a single structural defect.

Roughton and Forster's approach used the fact that oxygen influences the reaction of carbon monoxide with haemoglobin. By changing alveolar oxygen tension under controlled conditions, investigators could alter the blood reaction component of CO uptake and examine how the total transfer resistance changed. The conceptual prediction is that if the membrane term remains approximately constant while the reaction term varies, measurements obtained under different reaction conditions can help separate the two contributions. The experiment requires assumptions about the stability of capillary blood volume, membrane conductance and reaction kinetics. It established the importance of a blood-side contribution rather than proving that every lung can be represented exactly by two spatially uniform resistors.

Consider an illustrative calculation with membrane conductance DM = 60 mL·min⁻¹·mmHg⁻¹ and blood reaction conductance θVc = 60 in the same units. The combined resistance is 1/60 + 1/60 = 1/30, so DL = 30. If the reaction conductance decreases to 30 while membrane conductance remains 60, the total resistance becomes 1/60 + 1/30 = 1/20 and DL = 20. A one-half reduction in one component of conductance has reduced total conductance by one-third rather than one-half. This pattern follows from the additive resistances; adding the two conductance values directly would be incorrect.

Measured diffusing capacity can increase during exercise as more pulmonary capillaries are recruited or distended and blood volume within the exchange region increases. The rise in transfer capacity helps accommodate the increased oxygen uptake required during activity. Conversely, loss of alveolar surface area, thickening of the barrier, reduced pulmonary vascular bed or decreased haemoglobin concentration can lower measured DLCO by different mechanisms. For interpretation, lung volume, haemoglobin, posture and measurement conditions matter. Comparison with reference values must be appropriate to the individual and method.

It is also useful to distinguish DLCO from the transfer coefficient KCO, often expressed as DLCO divided by an estimate of accessible alveolar volume VA. If the accessible volume is small, the ratio may be normal or high even when total transfer capacity is reduced. The ratio is not a universal correction that converts any low-volume measurement into a direct measure of membrane health, because transfer efficiency per unit accessible volume changes with inflation, distribution and capillary recruitment. These concepts support exam-level reasoning about gas uptake without requiring detailed specialist test interpretation.

8. Perfusion limitation and diffusion limitation: follow blood along a capillary

Imagine a graph with position along a pulmonary capillary on the horizontal axis and the blood tension of a particular gas on the vertical axis. Blood enters at the left and exits at the right. A horizontal line above or below the entering value represents the tension of the gas within the associated alveolus, assumed constant for the model. The blood tension changes as gas diffuses between the alveolus and blood. The shape of that curve tells us whether additional time in the capillary would produce appreciable extra transfer.

When a gas reaches approximate equilibrium with alveolar gas well before blood exits the capillary, the remaining portion of the capillary offers little additional net transfer at the same alveolar tension. Increasing the rate at which fresh blood arrives can increase total uptake, because more blood of lower initial gas content is exposed to the alveolus per unit time. Transfer is then described as perfusion limited. The term does not mean that diffusion is absent. Diffusion is indispensable, but it is sufficiently rapid that blood supply rather than incomplete equilibration chiefly limits net uptake under those particular conditions.

Nitrous oxide provides a classical conceptual example of perfusion-limited transfer. It is not appreciably bound to haemoglobin in the way carbon monoxide is, so its blood partial pressure rises as the gas dissolves. Under typical model conditions it rapidly approaches the alveolar tension, after which little extra gas can enter that portion of blood. Its uptake increases when more blood passes through adequately ventilated exchange regions, provided alveolar tension and incoming blood content are maintained. This contrasts with gases whose strong binding or slow transfer prevents rapid pressure equilibration.

Carbon monoxide illustrates an approximately diffusion-limited gas in the transfer-capacity measurement. Strong haemoglobin binding keeps the dissolved capillary tension low over the exchange path at the tracer concentrations used. The pressure gradient therefore persists and the gas continues transferring along the capillary. Increasing transfer conductance increases uptake. Simply increasing blood flow does not necessarily produce a proportional increase in uptake when the limiting factor is the conductance and the pressure gradient is already nearly maximal. In the real lung, flow can still alter capillary recruitment and blood volume, so these ideal categories should not be interpreted as absolute independence from every other physiological variable.

Oxygen behaves differently from either extreme because its dissolved pressure rises as blood is oxygenated, but its reversible association with haemoglobin gives blood a substantial capacity for oxygen. In a healthy resting adult breathing room air at sea level, pulmonary capillary oxygen tension typically approaches the local alveolar tension sufficiently early in the capillary transit that oxygen transfer is largely perfusion limited. Representative textbook estimates give total resting red-cell transit time near 0.75 seconds and near equilibration in roughly the first 0.25 seconds under favorable conditions. These are illustrative averages rather than constant times for every vessel or every subject.

The difference between total transit time and time needed for equilibration is a functional reserve. During exercise cardiac output rises and blood may move through some capillaries more quickly. If transfer conductance and the alveolar driving pressure are adequate, oxygen can still approach equilibrium within the shorter passage. Capillary recruitment and increased effective blood volume help preserve exchange capacity. The reserve is not unlimited. At very high flow, low inspired oxygen pressure, or increased diffusion resistance, capillary blood can leave an exchange region before its oxygen tension has approached alveolar tension.

In this last situation, oxygen transfer has acquired a diffusion-limited component. The measured end-capillary tension remains below the local alveolar value, showing that the pressure gradient persists at the exit. Increasing the time available for exchange or the transfer conductance can increase oxygen uptake per unit blood volume. However, the effect of slowing blood flow on total oxygen uptake is not determined by end-capillary tension alone. A longer passage may improve oxygen loading of each volume of blood while a smaller volume passes through the lung per minute. Total oxygen transfer depends on both loading and flow.

A simple mathematical model helps connect diffusion conductance and transit time without claiming to represent the entire nonlinear oxygen binding curve. Let a capillary segment contain blood with a constant gas capacity β, defined as the change in gas content per unit blood volume per unit change in gas tension. If blood in the segment has volume V and the conductance from alveolar gas to this blood is G, then its amount of gas changes at rate G(PA − Pc). With fixed PA, a linear gas capacity and well-mixed blood, conservation gives βV dPc/dt = G(PA − Pc). The resulting pressure approaches equilibrium exponentially: Pc(t) = PA − (PA − Pc0)e⁻ᵗ/τ, where τ = βV/G. The time constant has units of time, since βV represents gas amount per unit pressure and G represents gas amount per unit time per unit pressure.

After one time constant, the remaining pressure difference is about 37 percent of its initial value. After three time constants it is about 5 percent. The equation shows why the time required for pressure equilibration is greater when blood has a high gas capacity or conductance is low. A blood sample capable of taking up a large amount of gas before its tension rises requires more transfer to close the same pressure gap. For oxygen, the effective capacity changes with tension because of haemoglobin binding, so the true pressure curve is not a single exponential across all conditions. The model provides a principled explanation of equilibration rather than a precise clinical prediction.

For an illustrative comparison, let the entering tension be 40 mmHg, alveolar tension 100 mmHg, and time constant 0.1 seconds. At 0.3 seconds, the remaining difference is 60e⁻³, approximately 3 mmHg, so capillary tension is approximately 97 mmHg. If conductance decreases to one-third while other variables are held fixed, the time constant increases to 0.3 seconds. At the same 0.3-second transit time, the remaining difference is 60e⁻¹, approximately 22 mmHg, and capillary tension is only about 78 mmHg. The numerical dataset is hypothetical. It demonstrates how slower transfer can generate a persistent end-capillary gradient.

Low inspired oxygen pressure can reveal limits in oxygen transfer even if the barrier has not changed. At altitude the alveolar oxygen tension is lower, and incoming blood may also have lower oxygen tension. The absolute pressure difference available for uptake can be reduced, while the relevant portion of the haemoglobin saturation curve changes the effective blood oxygen capacity. During exercise, oxygen consumption also changes mixed venous oxygen content and transit time. These simultaneous effects explain why a lung that supports adequate exchange at rest may show incomplete equilibration under more demanding conditions.

The examination distinction is best made by asking whether the gas tension has equilibrated with alveolar tension before the end of the available exchange path. If yes, increasing perfusion can increase total uptake when other conditions are maintained. If no, increasing exchange time or conductance can improve loading. Neither definition refers simply to the chemical identity of a gas irrespective of environment. Oxygen may be predominantly perfusion limited in one situation and partially diffusion limited in another.

Finally, incomplete oxygen equilibration within an individual capillary must be distinguished from low oxygen tension caused by its own alveolus having little ventilation. A capillary may leave a poorly ventilated alveolus in excellent equilibrium with local gas at 55 mmHg. That is not diffusion limitation in the usual sense; the local pressure itself is low because ventilation is inadequate relative to perfusion. A capillary leaving an alveolus at 80 mmHg when local alveolar oxygen is 100 mmHg shows a different mechanism. These two cases may produce similar arterial results while requiring different explanations.

9. The ventilation–perfusion ratio and local steady state

An exchanging region receives a flow of fresh alveolar gas and a flow of blood. The ratio V̇A/Q̇ compares the alveolar ventilation supplying that region with its perfusion. The dot over a symbol indicates a rate rather than a stored volume. Because both quantities can be expressed in litres per minute, the ratio is dimensionless. The ventilation numerator excludes purely conducting-airway ventilation, while the denominator denotes blood traversing the corresponding pulmonary exchange region. Pairing ventilation of one region with perfusion of a different region produces a ratio that has little local physiological meaning.

In a representative healthy resting adult, total alveolar ventilation may be about 4 L/min while total pulmonary blood flow is about 5 L/min, giving a whole-lung ratio of 0.8. These values are illustrative and change with body size and activity. A ratio of 0.8 is not a claim that every alveolus has exactly that ratio. Regional ventilation and perfusion vary, and individual exchanging units can have ratios considerably above or below the whole-lung value. The overall value is a convenient descriptive quantity but it does not uniquely determine gas-exchange efficiency.

Local gas balance clarifies why the ratio matters. For oxygen, the uptake from a region's alveolar gas must equal the increase in oxygen content of blood flowing through it in steady state, assuming there is no other significant oxygen source or sink in the model. Thus Q̇(Cc'O2 − CvO2) is related to V̇A times the difference between inspired and local alveolar oxygen fractions, with appropriate gas-volume conversions and correction for unequal inspired and expired flows. Cc'O2 denotes end-capillary oxygen content and CvO2 denotes entering mixed venous oxygen content. Carbon dioxide balance similarly equates blood carbon dioxide loss with alveolar gas carbon dioxide removal.

Dividing the balance by blood flow reveals that the amount of oxygen supplied per unit blood flow depends on V̇A/Q̇ and the composition of inspired and alveolar gas. At high ventilation relative to perfusion, the inspired oxygen supplied to the alveolus can readily replace the amount extracted by its limited blood flow. Alveolar oxygen tension tends to rise toward humidified inspired oxygen tension, while carbon dioxide tension tends to fall because carbon dioxide delivery by blood is small relative to ventilation. At low ventilation relative to perfusion, the reverse occurs: more blood extracts oxygen and delivers carbon dioxide than the fresh gas flow can readily support.

This local balance is conceptually distinct from the force driving breathing. A region with high airway resistance can receive relatively little ventilation, despite a normal thoracic pressure change. A region with high vascular resistance can receive relatively little perfusion despite ventilation being normal. Both alterations can change the ratio, but the directions differ. Reducing ventilation at fixed blood flow decreases V/Q; reducing perfusion at fixed ventilation increases V/Q. An exam question specifying airway obstruction and another specifying pulmonary vascular obstruction therefore lead to different local gas changes.

Imagine two identical exchanging regions each with ventilation 2 L/min and perfusion 2.5 L/min. Each has a ratio 0.8, and the whole-lung ratio is also 0.8. Now redistribute ventilation so that region A receives 0.5 L/min and region B receives 3.5 L/min while perfusion remains equally distributed. Region A has V/Q = 0.2, region B has V/Q = 1.4, and total ventilation divided by total blood flow remains 0.8. The total numbers have not changed, yet oxygenation may worsen because blood leaving region A is relatively poorly oxygenated while extra ventilation of region B cannot necessarily compensate for all of that deficit.

The overall ratio is a perfusion-weighted mean of regional ratios for units with nonzero perfusion. Algebraically, ΣVA/ΣQ = Σ[Qi/(ΣQ)](VAi/Qi). It is not ordinarily the simple arithmetic mean of the individual ratios. Moreover, a truly unperfused region has an undefined ratio in the ordinary arithmetic sense; it is described as the limiting case V/Q tending to infinity, and its ventilation contributes to the total numerator without a corresponding perfusion weight. The algebraic mean expression must therefore be applied to regions with finite nonzero perfusion and handled carefully at the limit.

The presence of a normal ratio in a region does not guarantee that the region contributes normal total gas exchange. If both ventilation and perfusion are reduced proportionately to one-tenth of their original values, their ratio may be unchanged. Under a simplified model with similar entering blood and inspired gas composition and adequate diffusion, local alveolar and end-capillary tensions may be similar to before. But the region processes much less blood and fresh gas per minute, so its total contribution to whole-body oxygen uptake is small. A ratio expresses matching, not the scale of throughput.

There is no requirement that the optimal V/Q ratio be numerically equal to one. The relationship between oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide elimination involves blood gas contents, metabolic exchange ratio, inspired composition and the nonlinearity of haemoglobin binding. The biological objective is adequate oxygen loading and carbon dioxide removal given the available ventilation and perfusion, not matching equal numerical volumes of two different fluids for its own sake. Whole-lung values near 0.8 are common examples; local matching may be appropriate over a range of ratios.

A useful thought experiment examines a small alveolar region before and after partial airway blockage. Immediately after ventilation falls, blood continues extracting oxygen and delivering carbon dioxide. Over several gas exchanges, the local gas composition moves toward a lower oxygen tension and higher carbon dioxide tension. If vascular responses subsequently reduce local perfusion, the ratio rises toward a better match, although absolute exchange through that region may remain below baseline. This sequence distinguishes the initiating mechanical event, the change in gas composition and the later compensatory redistribution of blood.

The converse experiment involves an interruption of perfusion. Initially the alveolus contains residual carbon dioxide from previous blood delivery. Continued ventilation removes much of that carbon dioxide and replenishes oxygen, causing the gas composition to move toward that of inspired air. If blood flow remains zero, the gas can appear well oxygenated while no oxygenated blood emerges from that unit. This is a striking demonstration of the difference between oxygen abundance in gas and oxygen transfer to the organism.

These local balance principles form the basis for interpreting diagrams of V/Q. As the ratio varies from very low to very high, local alveolar oxygen tends to increase and local alveolar carbon dioxide tends to decrease, under fixed inspired and entering blood conditions and a sufficiently permeable barrier. The relationship is not a pair of straight lines; it is shaped by nonlinear blood content-pressure relationships and the coupled oxygen and carbon dioxide balances. The limiting gas tensions at the two ends provide reliable anchors even without exact intermediate values.

10. Gravity, regional lung mechanics and the distribution of perfusion

The lung is not a collection of identical units all exposed to identical mechanical forces. In an upright human, gravity affects pulmonary vascular pressures, the weight of lung tissue and the local expansion of alveoli. As a consequence, both ventilation and perfusion generally increase from the upper regions of the lung toward the lower regions, but the proportional increase in blood flow is often greater. The V/Q ratio is therefore typically higher near the apex and lower near the base in the upright resting lung.

The gravitational effect on perfusion can be understood using the hydrostatic relation ΔP = ρgh, where ρ is fluid density, g is gravitational acceleration and h is vertical height difference. For blood, a density near 1060 kg/m³ provides a useful approximation. A vertical separation of 0.30 m gives ΔP ≈ 1060 × 9.81 × 0.30 = 3120 Pa. Dividing by about 133.3 Pa per mmHg yields approximately 23.4 mmHg. This illustrative hydrostatic pressure difference is large relative to the low pressures of the pulmonary circulation. Real local vessel pressures depend additionally on vascular resistance, flow and external tissue pressures.

At the dependent base of the upright lung, vascular pressures tend to be higher because of the hydrostatic column. More capillaries may be open or distended, allowing greater regional perfusion. Toward the apex, vascular pressures are lower and the relationship between blood pressure and surrounding alveolar pressure can restrict flow. Pulmonary blood flow is not determined simply by the distance from the heart. A region can be nearer a supplying artery in anatomical space yet have different perfusion because the local driving and external pressures are different.

The classical West zone model organizes these relationships using three pressures: pulmonary arterial pressure Pa,v at a local vascular entrance, pulmonary venous pressure Pv,v at the local exit and alveolar pressure PA,g outside the vessels. The subscripts here distinguish vascular hydrostatic pressures from the gas tensions PaO2 and PAO2 discussed elsewhere. When alveolar pressure exceeds both local arterial and venous vascular pressures, gas-exchange vessels can be compressed and flow can become very low or absent. This idealized ordering is called zone 1. It is not necessarily present as a substantial region in the healthy upright lung at rest; reduced vascular pressure or increased alveolar pressure can make it more likely.

In zone 2, local arterial pressure exceeds alveolar pressure, which in turn exceeds venous pressure. In the simplified collapsible-vessel picture, the effective driving difference is arterial minus alveolar pressure, because the latter limits transmission of the downstream vascular pressure. In zone 3, both vascular pressures exceed alveolar pressure, with arterial pressure higher than venous. Blood can flow through an open vascular pathway and the effective pressure difference is arterial minus venous. These descriptions concern flow mechanics, not oxygen partial pressure differences. They are useful conceptual models but do not capture all contributions of extra-alveolar vessels, vessel geometry, tissue tension and pulsation.

A numerical exercise clarifies the orderings. Suppose local alveolar hydrostatic reference pressure is 0 mmHg, pulmonary arterial pressure is 8 mmHg, and pulmonary venous pressure is −2 mmHg, all expressed relative to the same atmospheric reference. This is zone 2 in the simplified model. The effective pressure difference controlling flow is approximately 8 − 0 = 8 mmHg rather than 8 − (−2) = 10 mmHg. If both vascular pressures rise and become 14 and 4 mmHg, the ordering corresponds to zone 3 and the effective difference is 10 mmHg. These values are hypothetical and are not gas tensions.

Gravity also influences regional ventilation through the pleural pressure gradient. At the top of an upright lung, intrapleural pressure tends to be more negative at functional residual capacity. The corresponding transpulmonary distending pressure is larger, so upper alveoli tend to be more expanded at the beginning of a quiet inspiration. At the base, the resting distending pressure is smaller and alveoli tend to start at a lower volume. Because the lung pressure–volume relation is nonlinear, a given increment in distending pressure may expand the less inflated dependent region more than the already distended upper region.

This explanation uses the slope of a local pressure–volume curve, not merely the initial size of an alveolus. Compliance is the change in volume per change in distending pressure under specified conditions. An alveolus located on a steep portion of the pressure–volume curve can gain more volume during inspiration than a larger alveolus located on a flatter portion. Thus it is possible for smaller resting alveoli at the base to receive more fresh ventilation per unit region during a quiet breath. The apparently paradoxical result follows from the distinction between existing volume and change in volume.

The pattern is influenced by posture and lung volume. When a person lies supine, the gravitationally dependent regions are no longer simply the anatomical bases in the same sense; posterior areas become more dependent. At very low lung volumes, dependent airways may close and ventilation may become impaired in regions that would otherwise have received considerable air. During positive-pressure ventilation, regional expansion and perfusion can differ from the spontaneous breathing pattern. Accordingly, apex-to-base comparisons should specify the upright resting condition rather than treating anatomical position as a permanent determinant of ventilation.

The common observation that basal V/Q is lower than apical V/Q does not mean basal ventilation is necessarily lower in absolute terms. A hypothetical two-region model may assign the apex ventilation of 0.8 L/min and perfusion of 0.5 L/min, giving V/Q = 1.6. The base receives ventilation of 1.6 L/min and perfusion of 2.5 L/min, giving V/Q = 0.64. The base receives twice the ventilation but five times the perfusion. Its oxygen tension tends to be lower than at the apex because the local ventilation is smaller relative to the blood flow.

The difference in oxygen and carbon dioxide pressures between regions also has consequences for the gases in expired air and the blood entering the left heart. The apical region may supply blood with higher oxygen tension, but the basal region carries a larger fraction of total perfusion and therefore has greater weight in arterial blood composition. Similarly, an alveolar region with abundant ventilation can influence expired gas more strongly than its blood flow would suggest. These different weightings help explain why averaged gas samples and arterial samples cannot be interconverted by simple arithmetic.

Regional distribution has been studied using radioactive tracers and other techniques that track ventilation and perfusion separately. The general experimental design is to observe where an inhaled marker distributes and where a blood-borne marker distributes under controlled posture and physiological conditions. A map of regional ventilation alone cannot establish matching; it must be compared with information about regional perfusion. Similarly, a map of blood flow cannot reveal whether the perfused units receive fresh gas. Tracer methods have limitations including spatial resolution, tracer properties and assumptions about steady distribution.

The classical gravitational description should be treated as a starting model. Local vascular branching, structural heterogeneity, regional tissue deformation and other factors also produce differences in perfusion and ventilation. Even at the same vertical level, nearby regions may receive different amounts of air and blood. A test question that reports a heterogeneous V/Q distribution within one horizontal plane is therefore not inconsistent with gravity influencing the overall vertical pattern. The lung is a three-dimensional branching organ, not a single stack of identical exchange chambers.
