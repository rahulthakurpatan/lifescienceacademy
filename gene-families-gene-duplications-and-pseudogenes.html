1. Why keep several related genes?

Human red blood cells must carry oxygen throughout life, but the circumstances under which oxygen is acquired change profoundly during development. Before birth, oxygen reaches the fetus through the placenta; after birth it enters the lungs. The oxygen-carrying protein hemoglobin is therefore not produced in exactly the same molecular form at every age. Embryonic, fetal and adult hemoglobins contain related but different globin chains. Their instructions are stored at several distinct positions in the genome, not simply as alternative spellings of one gene.

A second observation points in a different direction. Cells that make many ribosomes often possess numerous very similar ribosomal RNA genes. Their products need not have substantially different roles. Here the advantage of maintaining multiple genes is largely the ability to produce a large amount of a similar product. These two cases reveal distinct reasons why a genome may contain a family of related sequences: specialization of function and increased or coordinated production.

To explain either case, start with the idea that a gene occupies a physical DNA region called a locus. A genome is the set of genetic information of an organism; a haploid nuclear genome contains one representative chromosome set. A gene may produce a protein or a functional RNA. Its coding region, where present, is only part of the locus; promoter and other regulatory sequences help determine whether the information is used. In interrupted genes, introns separate exons in genomic DNA and are removed from the precursor RNA. These features matter because different duplication processes copy different portions of the original locus.

A gene family comprises related genes or gene-derived loci whose sequences descend from a common ancestral sequence. The members may remain close together on one chromosome or occupy different chromosomes. They may encode nearly identical products, specialized proteins, or no longer produce the ancestral functional product. A family is therefore an evolutionary relationship, not merely a count of repeated sequences. A repeated centromeric satellite sequence, for example, is not automatically a protein-coding gene family.

The central task in this chapter is to reconstruct the history and functional consequences of an additional gene copy from its position, sequence, exon organization, expression and phenotype. No single observation answers all of these questions. Two genes that are very similar may behave differently because their regulatory regions differ; a gene that is not transcribed in liver may be essential in another tissue; and a gene-derived sequence may retain an apparent coding region without making a useful protein. Learn to separate each level of evidence before combining them.

The prerequisite knowledge is basic DNA and RNA structure, the distinction between chromosomes and loci, and the meaning of transcription and translation. The preceding chapters on interrupted genes and repeated DNA supply the organizational background. This chapter develops the structural and elementary evolutionary consequences of duplication; the detailed enzymology of recombination and gene expression belongs to later chapters.

2. Loci, alleles, homologs, orthologs and paralogs

Consider a diploid organism with one locus called A on each homologous chromosome. The maternally inherited copy may have a base C where the paternally inherited copy has T. These are alternative alleles of A. Even though the nucleus contains two physical copies, the haploid chromosome set has one A locus. Calling the two alleles a two-member gene family would confuse inheritance with the origin of distinct genomic loci.

Now imagine that an ancestral chromosome has acquired a second locus B by copying A. A and B may initially be identical, but their positions can be distinguished using surrounding sequences. Each can be inherited, mutate or be expressed independently. After the duplication has become established, a typical diploid organism may possess two alleles at A and two alleles at B. It has four physical copies of these family loci but only two family members in its haploid reference genome. The four allelic copies need not all have different sequences. Different loci can encode an identical protein, and two alleles at the same locus can encode different proteins.

Homology describes descent from a common ancestral sequence. Orthologs and paralogs are two kinds of homologs defined by the event that separated their lineages. A speciation event separates the ancestral gene into descendants in different species, producing orthologs. A duplication event separates gene lineages into paralogs. In a simple case where a duplication A → A1 and A2 preceded a species split, species X and Y each retain two genes. X-A1 and Y-A1 are orthologs; X-A1 and X-A2 are paralogs. X-A1 and Y-A2 are also paralogs: the decisive event connecting those two gene lineages was duplication, not the later speciation.

These definitions avoid a common error: ortholog does not mean identical function, and paralog does not mean different function. An ortholog can change its role after species diverge. Paralogs may both produce the same essential RNA or protein for millions of years. In a lineage where duplication occurred only after speciation, two copies in one species can both be orthologous to the single corresponding gene in the other species. Orthology is not always a one-to-one correspondence.

Sequence identity is the proportion of compared positions that contain the same nucleotide or amino acid. Homology is a statement about ancestry, so it is inappropriate to say that genes are 80 percent homologous. They may have 80 percent identity over a specified alignment and be inferred to be homologous. Similarity can also include chemically conservative amino acid substitutions. A short motif shared by two unrelated enzymes is weak evidence; a long alignment across several conserved regions with matching structural features is stronger. Even high identity does not by itself identify whether the relevant ancestral event was duplication or speciation.

Gene families can be subdivided into more closely related subfamilies, and a broader superfamily may unite proteins sharing a conserved ancestral domain. A domain is a region of a protein with a recognizable structural or functional identity. A family relationship established for one domain should not automatically be extended to every part of a multidomain protein. For examination problems involving simplified full-length genes, follow the explicit ancestral events and locus maps rather than relying on gene names alone.

3. How gene families are arranged in chromosomes

The genomic organization of a family is often its first visible clue to origin. A tandem family contains adjacent copies of a related DNA unit, usually in the same orientation when described as head-to-tail repeats. Intervening DNA is not necessarily absent. Each copy may include its own regulatory region and transcription unit, or a repeated unit may itself contain more than one gene. A cluster is a broader positional description: a set of genes located close together in a chromosome region. Not every cluster is a regular tandem array, and neighboring genes are not necessarily homologous.

A dispersed family has members separated by substantial intervening DNA or on different chromosomes. It may originate when a segment is copied into another location, through large chromosome rearrangements, through RNA-mediated duplication or from a much larger duplication followed by loss of surrounding genes. The current position alone cannot prove which mechanism occurred. A tandem duplicate may later move; a dispersed copy may subsequently be duplicated in tandem.

There is a useful distinction between the physical map and the transcription map. The physical map shows genomic order, spacing and orientation. A transcription map shows which segments contribute to a single RNA precursor. An operon can contain several functionally related genes that have very different sequences and encode distinct proteins; it is defined by coordinated transcription. A globin cluster contains evolutionarily related genes but does not behave as a typical bacterial polycistronic operon. Each major globin gene is separately transcribed. Conversely, several homologous bacterial genes could occur within an operon; the two categories are not mutually exclusive.

A repeated gene family also differs from a series of short noncoding repeats. If the sequence unit is a four-base motif repeated twenty times, those twenty units are not twenty gene loci. If the unit is a functional ribosomal RNA transcription unit repeated twenty times, the count is different: there are twenty copies of that unit, each containing one or more identifiable RNA genes. Count the biological entity specified in the question, not merely the number of repeated rectangles.

Maps may be drawn in a reference direction. A horizontal line from left to right need not represent the chromosome's centromere-to-telomere direction or the transcriptional direction of every gene. An arrowhead marks a gene's orientation only when the legend explicitly assigns that meaning. Inverted duplicates can be related despite facing opposite directions. If a diagram shows X, Y and Z in order but says nothing about strand, transcription direction cannot be inferred. This discipline prevents positional evidence from being mistaken for regulatory evidence.

4. DNA-level duplication: how a second locus is created

A stable duplication is an increase in the copy number of a DNA region in a lineage. It is not the ordinary replication of DNA that precedes cell division. Normal replication produces sister chromatids carrying the same loci; segregation restores the original number of copies per haploid set. Duplication adds an extra copy to a chromosome set that can be inherited as part of its structure.

Unequal crossing-over provides a clear model of tandem expansion. During meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair and can exchange corresponding DNA segments. Normally, equivalent positions align, so exchange leaves the number of genes unchanged. When adjacent regions are sufficiently similar, however, a repeat on one chromatid may pair with a different repeat on another. The alignment is displaced. If crossing-over then joins non-equivalent positions, the two participating chromatids gain and lose the sequence between those positions.

Imagine two chromatids, each arranged L–A–A–R, where L and R represent unique flanking regions. One A unit on the first chromatid aligns with the other A unit on the second. Exchange within those aligned copies yields one chromatid L–A–A–A–R and one L–A–R. In a simplified reciprocal exchange, the total number of units on the participating chromatids remains four. The important new feature is the distribution: three on one chromatid and one on the other. The two chromatids that did not participate in a single meiotic exchange would retain their original two-copy structure.

Repeated DNA can also flank a single gene. If two directly repeated flanking sequences misalign, exchange can duplicate the intervening gene and surrounding region even though the gene had only one copy initially. Once an array exists, its long similar sequences can facilitate further misalignment. This creates a structural basis for expansions and contractions of gene families. There is no requirement that the expansion product survive or that the contraction product be harmful; the phenotype depends on the affected loci and on how much product is needed.

A DNA-level duplication can also occur during abnormal replication or repair, where an extra stretch of DNA is copied into a new or adjacent site. Such events can produce copies that share intron-exon organization and flanking noncoding DNA. A segmental duplication copies a region potentially containing multiple genes. Whole-genome duplication, familiar in plant evolution, initially increases the copy number of essentially every nuclear locus. These events differ in scale but provide related DNA material that can later diverge. Their detailed molecular mechanisms are not needed to infer their basic structural outcomes.

An important difference between small-scale duplication and whole-genome duplication is relative dosage. If one subunit of a multi-protein complex doubles but its partners do not, the extra product may be unused or harmful. Doubling the whole genome can initially preserve the relative number of genes for interacting subunits, even though absolute gene dosage and cellular physiology may change. Subsequent gene losses can again disturb those proportions. Thus doubling a gene is neither inherently beneficial nor inherently equivalent to doubling the whole genome.

For evolution of a sexually reproducing multicellular organism, the new copy must reach a lineage that contributes to subsequent generations. A somatic duplication may affect one tissue and can contribute to disease, but it does not normally establish a heritable species-wide gene family. Even a duplication transmitted to offspring may be lost through selection or chance. The origin of a copy and its persistence over evolutionary time are two separate events.

5. RNA-mediated duplication and its structural signatures

DNA-level duplication can preserve the entire genomic organization of an interrupted gene. RNA-mediated duplication usually preserves a different set of information. The distinction follows from the normal path from DNA to mature messenger RNA. A gene with three exons separated by two introns is first transcribed into a precursor containing those introns. Splicing connects the exons, and most mature eukaryotic messenger RNAs carry a poly(A) tail added to the RNA after transcription.

Occasionally a processed messenger RNA becomes a template for reverse transcription, the synthesis of DNA from RNA. Cellular retrotransposition machinery, notably that associated with LINE-1 in mammals, can generate and integrate DNA derived from such an RNA. The resulting locus is called a retrocopy. Because the template was usually a spliced RNA, exon sequences in the new DNA are contiguous. The original promoter normally lies outside the transcribed sequence and is therefore not copied. A remnant of the poly(A) tail and short duplicated target sequences at the insertion site may survive as evidence of the origin.

The absence of the parent's promoter explains why many retrocopies initially fail to be expressed. Even when all coding bases are present, the genome does not automatically supply a promoter or a suitable regulatory environment. Some insertions are incomplete or acquire mutations that disrupt the reading frame. A nonfunctional coding-gene-derived retrocopy is called a processed pseudogene. A rare retrocopy can instead acquire expression from a nearby promoter, retain or recover a useful product, and become a functional retrogene. Thus an intronless copy is not synonymous with a pseudogene.

The term processed refers to the RNA intermediate, not to whether a protein was modified after translation. A DNA-level duplication and an RNA-mediated duplication can produce copies with very high sequence identity. Exon organization and nearby DNA help distinguish them. An intronless copy of a parent that was already intronless provides little structural evidence by itself. Loss of introns, erosion of ancient insertion signatures, and rearrangement of surrounding DNA also complicate interpretation. A classification should be supported by several independent features, not by an absolute rule.

A useful comparison starts with a known parent containing exons E1, E2 and E3 separated by introns I1 and I2. A related locus containing E1–I1–E2–I2–E3 plus similar upstream sequence favors DNA-level duplication. Another locus with E1–E2–E3 in a single continuous stretch, an A-rich remnant and unrelated genomic flanks favors retrocopy formation. Whether either new locus is a pseudogene requires an additional functional assessment. The mechanism of mobile element movement is studied later; the structural signature is the relevant lesson here.

6. Why some duplicates survive and others disappear

At the moment of a complete duplication, the two loci may encode the same product. Their shared function can provide a degree of redundancy, meaning that either copy can contribute to an ancestral requirement. Redundancy is not necessarily complete. If one copy cannot produce enough product, both may already be important. If two copies are both expressed and the additional product disturbs a tightly balanced system, the duplication may reduce fitness from the beginning.

One straightforward outcome is retention for dosage. A cell may need a large amount of a particular RNA or protein in a limited time. Increasing the number of usable templates can raise potential production when each template is accessible and other required factors are available. Repeated ribosomal RNA genes and many histone genes illustrate this logic. However, doubling gene number does not guarantee doubled output: promoters can be silent, polymerases limiting, RNA turnover altered or feedback regulatory mechanisms activated.

A second outcome is loss of one copy. If the ancestral function can still be performed by the remaining gene, a mutation that disables the duplicate may have little immediate effect. The disabled copy can accumulate further changes, become a pseudogene, or eventually be deleted. This is often called nonfunctionalization or pseudogenization, although a pseudogene can later acquire another role. A surviving ancestral-function copy does not have to be the one occupying the original physical position. Either lineage can retain or lose functionality.

In subfunctionalization, two duplicates partition parts of an ancestral function. Suppose an ancestral gene was expressed in both liver and muscle. Copy A loses a liver-specific regulatory element while preserving muscle expression; copy B loses a muscle-specific element while preserving liver expression. Together the two descendants reproduce the ancestral expression pattern. Neither alone now supplies the full ancestral requirement. Selection can retain both copies because loss of either would remove a necessary component. No new biochemical reaction needs to evolve. The same principle can apply to multiple ancestral biochemical activities, developmental windows or cellular compartments.

Neofunctionalization occurs when one descendant acquires a function absent from the ancestor while the ancestral role remains available, commonly through the other copy. A change in a catalytic site might permit use of a new substrate; a change in a regulatory sequence might provide expression in a new physiological setting. Merely detecting a difference between paralogs does not demonstrate neofunctionalization. To make that inference, one needs a defensible reconstruction of the ancestral state, for example using an appropriate related species with a single ortholog and supporting functional evidence.

A hypothetical family in which both descendants produce smaller amounts of the ancestral functional product illustrates yet another possibility: partial loss of function, or hypofunctionalization. Both copies might together meet the required activity without a division into qualitatively distinct functions. This is different from the clear partitioning in the liver and muscle example. These models are idealized endpoints. Real genes may undergo more than one process over time, and selective pressures can change.

The most useful principle is that duplication creates opportunity, not intention. Mutations are not produced because an organism foresees a new requirement. Changes arise in the copies, and selection, dosage constraints, chance and inheritance influence their eventual persistence. Some gene families expand substantially; others contract. A functional family is the set of surviving descendants, not a complete record of every duplication that ever occurred.

In a multicomponent complex, maintaining balanced ratios can matter as much as maintaining one enzyme's output. Suppose proteins P and Q assemble in a 1:1 ratio. If a P duplication raises P production from 100 to 200 molecules while Q remains at 100, the maximum number of complete complexes remains 100 under the assumption of quantitative assembly. The extra P cannot produce a complete complex without Q. If both production levels double, as a simplified model of retained whole-genome duplication, the maximum becomes 200. This example illustrates the dosage-balance concept without asserting that real protein production always scales linearly with gene number.

7. How scientists establish family membership and divergence

Before complete genome sequences were available, scientists could compare purified proteins. Globins are particularly informative because proteins from different tissues, developmental stages and species can be distinguished and their amino acid sequences compared. Related globins share a recognizable globin fold and conserve residues essential for positioning the heme group. Similarities distributed throughout the sequence, combined with similarities in structure, are consistent with descent from an ancestral globin. The discovery of distinct related globin genes in physical clusters strengthened the duplication-and-divergence explanation. These are converging lines of evidence, not a single experiment that independently reconstructed the entire family history.

A sequence comparison starts by aligning positions thought to correspond through ancestry. Insertions and deletions may require gaps. For an elementary ungapped alignment, count matching positions M and divide by the number of compared positions L. Percentage identity = 100 × M/L. Both counts are dimensionless; the result is a percentage. If 108 of 120 aligned amino acids match, identity is 100 × 108/120 = 90%. If another pair matches all 12 positions of a short fragment, that is 100% identity over 12 positions but not necessarily stronger family evidence. The alignment coverage and biological context matter. In gapped alignments, the denominator convention must be stated.

For a protein, amino acid substitutions can preserve chemical properties even when exact identity decreases. Two proteins can share a fold because the residues stabilizing the structure remain similar. Conversely, unrelated proteins exposed to similar selective pressures may evolve short similar motifs, a phenomenon of convergence. A conserved catalytic motif or DNA-binding sequence is therefore a starting point, not sufficient evidence of full-length common ancestry. Similarity across multiple regions, conserved exon boundaries and equivalent surrounding genes make a proposed relationship more persuasive.

Classical DNA hybridization exploits complementarity. A labeled nucleic-acid probe made from part of a known gene can bind related genomic fragments if their sequences are sufficiently complementary. In a Southern blot, genomic DNA is cut with a restriction enzyme, fragments are separated by size, transferred to a membrane and probed. Multiple hybridizing bands suggest multiple related fragments. The test does not directly count genes: one gene can be split by a restriction site into two probe-binding fragments, two genes can occur on one fragment, and distinct fragments can have the same length and co-migrate. Hybridization conditions also influence which divergent relatives are detected.

To distinguish these explanations, compare digests made with different restriction enzymes, use a second probe in a different region, and examine the genomic restriction map or sequence. A known single-copy control helps assess sample loading and general hybridization quality; a negative or unrelated probe tests nonspecific binding. If a probe that recognizes a unique flanking sequence identifies one locus but a conserved coding probe identifies three fragments, the pattern supports related sequences outside the unique region. Yet the functional status of those sequences still requires evidence from expression or function.

A comparison of genomic and complementary DNA, or cDNA, adds another layer. A cDNA is DNA synthesized from an RNA template. If a transcript derived from a multi-exon parent has junctions matching an intronless genomic copy, that copy may have arisen through retroposition. However, RNA from the original parent naturally has the same exon junctions; detecting the junction in RNA does not demonstrate expression from the retrocopy. One needs a locus-specific feature, such as a distinguishable sequence in the transcript or a unique flanking region incorporated into it. This distinction is especially important when studying highly similar pseudogenes.

The appropriate evidence depends on the claim. A genomic map tests copy number and position. Sequence alignment tests similarity and coding integrity. An RNA assay tests expression in a particular sample. Protein detection can establish production of a polypeptide but not necessarily its useful biological role. A carefully controlled loss-of-function and rescue experiment can test contribution to a phenotype. For example, removal of one locus with no phenotype followed by removal of both loci with a clear defect supports functional redundancy under the tested conditions; it does not prove the genes are redundant in every tissue or developmental stage.

8. The globin gene family: from a common fold to developmental specialization

The globin family makes the connection between DNA duplication and physiological specialization unusually clear. Globin proteins contain a heme-binding fold. Heme is an iron-containing prosthetic group capable of reversible oxygen binding in appropriate globins. Myoglobin is principally a monomeric oxygen-binding protein in muscle. Hemoglobin in vertebrate red blood cells is a multimeric protein that transports oxygen. Their related sequences and common structural features support an ancestral relationship, but their different molecular organization and physiological tasks show how related proteins can diversify.

Human hemoglobin generally contains four globin chains arranged as two pairs. In the predominant adult hemoglobin HbA, two alpha chains associate with two beta chains (α₂β₂). Fetal hemoglobin HbF contains two alpha and two gamma chains (α₂γ₂), while the minor adult HbA₂ contains two alpha and two delta chains (α₂δ₂). These symbols describe polypeptide subunits, not the number of corresponding gene loci. A tetramer assembled from two alpha chains does not need two separate alpha loci to supply those two molecules; repeated transcription and translation can produce many copies from one locus.

The major alpha-like gene cluster is on human chromosome 16 and the beta-like cluster on chromosome 11. Both groups descend from ancient globin gene duplications, followed by additional duplication, divergence and rearrangement. Within the alpha-like cluster, the principal developmental sequence is the embryonic zeta gene HBZ followed by the adult and fetal alpha genes HBA2 and HBA1. Other related sequences, including pseudogenes and additional alpha-like loci such as HBM and HBQ1, also occupy the region; a simplified diagram of the major hemoglobin-forming loci must not be mistaken for the entire genomic map. HBA1 and HBA2 encode the same usual alpha-globin protein sequence but are distinct loci, not alleles.

The beta-like cluster contains the embryonic epsilon gene HBE1, two fetal gamma genes HBG2 and HBG1, and the adult delta and beta genes HBD and HBB in that relative order. HBG2 and HBG1 are distinguishable as Gγ and Aγ, named for glycine and alanine respectively at a characteristic position. A related pseudogene HBBP1 lies between HBG1 and HBD. The developmental order broadly follows the genomic order among the principal beta-like functional genes, but this does not mean the whole cluster is transcribed as a single RNA that moves from one gene to the next. The genes have their own transcription units; shared regulatory regions and developmental changes influence their expression.

During early embryonic development, combinations involving zeta, epsilon, alpha or gamma chains form embryonic hemoglobins. Later, HbF becomes predominant in fetal circulation. Around birth and during infancy, gamma-globin expression declines relative to beta-globin expression, and HbA becomes predominant. The switch is gradual rather than an instantaneous replacement at delivery. In healthy adults HbA₂ is a small component and HbF generally persists at a low level. The underlying globin loci remain present in nucleated cells irrespective of which gene is most active. This is a change of expression, not a developmental deletion or new duplication of the genes.

Why is gamma-globin expression advantageous before birth? Fetal hemoglobin binds the red-cell metabolite 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate less strongly than adult HbA. Under comparable physiological conditions, this reduces stabilization of the low-oxygen-affinity state, giving HbF greater oxygen affinity. This helps the fetus acquire oxygen across the placenta, together with other physiological factors. The developmental specialization is possible because structurally related genes encode alternative chains with distinct biochemical properties and are expressed at different times. Detailed cooperative oxygen binding and the molecular regulatory switch are studied in physiology and gene-expression chapters.

The organization also has an evolutionary implication. A family with multiple loci can alter the timing or location of expression of one member while keeping another member available. It does not follow that every developmental change is necessarily a neofunctionalization event; reconstructing the ancestral expression program is required for that conclusion. What the modern globin arrangement demonstrates directly is the coexistence of related sequences, divergent expression profiles and physiological roles. The inferred historical duplication model is supported by comparisons of proteins, exon organization and genomic maps.

A direct test of functional divergence

Comparing the adult and fetal hemoglobins shows that related proteins can have different oxygen-binding properties, but comparison alone does not identify which sequence differences cause that behavior. In a 1997 study, researchers produced human HbF and engineered HbF variants whose gamma chains carried selected substitutions towards the corresponding adult beta-chain residues. They compared oxygen-binding properties of the engineered and normal proteins with adult hemoglobin, both with and without 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate. Normal HbF and HbA provided reference behaviors; the tests without 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate helped distinguish effects on intrinsic oxygen affinity from effects involving the metabolite. A variant with three gamma-chain substitutions showed oxygen affinities closer to HbA than another variant with two substitutions. These observations support a causal contribution of particular amino acid differences to the divergent properties of fetal and adult hemoglobin. They do not by themselves reconstruct the historical order of mutations in the globin gene family or explain developmental expression. The experiment isolates a biochemical aspect of diversification, while gene maps and RNA evidence address genomic organization and expression.

9. Globin dosage, chain balance and copy-number reasoning

The two principal alpha loci on each chromosome 16 make an instructive exception to the habit of equating a single protein with a single gene. In a diploid human carrying the usual arrangement, there are four principal alpha-globin gene copies: two HBA2 and two HBA1 allelic copies. The principal adult beta-globin gene HBB occupies one locus per haploid set, yielding two allelic copies in the diploid individual. This is a count of loci and DNA copies, not a statement that alpha chains are produced at twice the beta-chain rate. Differences in per-copy expression, RNA stability and other regulatory processes determine chain output.

A deletion of one of the alpha loci can arise through misaligned recombination between similar sequences. Its consequence is a decrease in the number of usable alpha-globin templates, potentially reducing alpha-chain production. The beta-like genes remain on chromosome 11. If beta-like chains are produced in excess relative to alpha chains, they cannot all be assembled into normal alpha-containing hemoglobin. The resulting imbalance underlies alpha-thalassemia. The reciprocal structural event can generate additional alpha copies, which may aggravate an existing deficiency in beta-globin production. Beta-thalassemia, in contrast, involves impaired beta-globin synthesis; its molecular causes need not be deletions. Both cases show why relative dosage matters.

A simplified chromosome pair might have the arrangement αα/αα, where each group represents one chromosome and each α represents one functional principal alpha locus. After a single deletion, −α/αα contains three functional copies. Two-copy loss may occur as −α/−α (one remaining copy on each chromosome) or −−/αα (both missing from one chromosome). Both genotypes have two remaining copies, yet their inheritance patterns differ because the distribution across chromosomes differs. This is a distinction between total dosage and chromosomal phase. Clinical severity and inheritance are not determined by this notation alone; nondeletional variants and unequal expression between loci also matter.

For a simple quantitative example, assume every alpha copy produces 30 arbitrary units of chain per hour, every beta copy produces 60 units per hour, and complete HbA assembly requires equal alpha and beta chain amounts. The usual four alpha and two beta copies give 120 units per hour of each chain. Losing one alpha copy reduces alpha output to 90, leaving beta output 120. The alpha:beta ratio is 90/120 = 0.75, and at most 90 units of beta chains can be matched under the model. Thirty units remain unmatched. This is an illustrative model of chain imbalance, not a prediction of an individual's hemoglobin level.

A second example tests the difference between loci and gene products. If a diploid cell has two copies of HBG1 and two copies of HBG2, it has four gamma-gene copies even if only one member contributes strongly at the sampled developmental stage. If 60 gamma-chain molecules and 100 alpha-chain molecules are available, the maximum number of HbF tetramers that can be assembled from these pools is min(60/2, 100/2) = 30. Twenty pairs of alpha chains remain available for other compatible partners. Gene copy number did not enter the calculation directly because the measured chain quantities already summarize expression.

These examples also explain why HbA, HbA₂ and HbF are not alleles of one hemoglobin gene. Each tetramer is a molecular assembly of products encoded at different loci. A sequence variant at HBB is an allele of HBB; HBD is a separate paralogous locus. Confusing these levels leads to wrong predictions about how a mutation in one gene can influence another protein species. Gene family structure provides the map from inherited DNA to possible protein combinations.

10. rRNA gene families: multiple templates for the same essential task

Ribosomes are needed in large numbers in actively growing cells. Their RNA components contribute structural and catalytic functions, so a cell must synthesize substantial quantities of ribosomal RNA. Many eukaryotes organize the genes for their major ribosomal RNAs in long tandem arrays. This creates numerous potential transcription templates for products that retain broadly similar functions, rather than a series of specialized enzymes.

In humans and many other eukaryotes, one major rDNA repeat unit includes a region transcribed into a long precursor that contains 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNA sequences. These sequences are separated by transcribed spacers and bounded by additional spacer regions. Processing releases the mature rRNAs; the intergenic spacer separates neighboring transcription units. The 5S rRNA gene is typically transcribed separately by RNA polymerase III and is not part of the human major rDNA precursor. The major precursor is transcribed by RNA polymerase I. There are exceptions among eukaryotes to the arrangement of 5S genes and the general rDNA architecture.

One repeat is therefore not simply one mature RNA gene in the same sense as a single globin transcription unit. If a haploid genome contains 120 complete major rDNA units, it contains 120 copies of the 18S sequence, 120 of the 5.8S sequence and 120 of the 28S sequence, or 360 mature-rRNA coding regions distributed across 120 major transcription units. The count excludes 5S genes. A precursor containing one of each rRNA can supply one molecule of each after successful processing. The final number of functional ribosomes also depends on 5S RNA, ribosomal proteins, assembly and other limiting factors.

Human major rDNA arrays occur on the short arms of the five pairs of acrocentric chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22. These regions are associated with nucleolar organizer regions. A nucleolus forms where active ribosomal RNA synthesis and early ribosome assembly are organized, as described in the nucleus chapter. The number of rDNA copies varies between individuals and chromosome arrays. Not every copy is transcriptionally active at one time. Additional inactive copies may be present as a reserve and play roles in genome organization; the actual output reflects regulation and processing as well as template number.

A further question is why members of some large rDNA arrays remain remarkably similar even after long evolutionary periods. Independent evolution would allow each copy to accumulate different changes. Recombination can counter this process: unequal crossing-over can increase the number of copies carrying one sequence variant while decreasing others. Gene conversion, a nonreciprocal transfer of sequence information between homologous regions during recombination or repair, can replace part of one repeat with sequence corresponding to another. Repeated exchanges and turnover can spread a variant through the array, so copies within a species may resemble each other more than corresponding repeat variants in another species. This pattern is called concerted evolution.

Purifying selection also preserves sequences needed for ribosomal function. It does not by itself explain every instance of homogenization, particularly similarity in less constrained spacer regions. Both selection and molecular exchange can contribute, and the relative importance varies among organisms and families. Unequal exchange changes copy number, whereas a simple gene-conversion event can alter sequence identity without increasing or decreasing the number of units. The mechanisms should not be treated as synonyms.

Classical work on Drosophila rDNA provides a biological link to dosage. The bobbed phenotype is associated with inadequate functional ribosomal DNA dosage, and studies of rDNA magnification established that repeat number could increase in certain deficient genetic backgrounds. These observations are consistent with repeated DNA being a dynamic genomic array rather than a fixed list of independent single-copy genes. They do not establish that every rDNA change uses one exclusive molecular pathway. The evidence is valuable precisely because copy number, sequence organization and phenotype can be connected.

11. Histone gene families: dosage and specialization together

The need for a large supply of histones arises from DNA packaging. Histones are proteins that associate with nuclear DNA to form chromatin. When a cell duplicates its chromosomes during S phase, new DNA must be packaged along with the redistribution of pre-existing histones. Large-scale production of histones is therefore required in proliferating cells. The organization of many canonical histone genes in clusters supports coordinated production of several core histone types and linker histones.

Canonical replication-dependent histone genes commonly lack introns in animals and have unusual messenger RNA 3′ ends that normally terminate in a conserved stem-loop instead of a conventional poly(A) tail. Many histone variants, in contrast, are expressed outside S phase as well and have conventional polyadenylated transcripts. Some have introns and occur outside the principal replication-dependent clusters. These are common patterns, not universal diagnostic laws. Gene organization, expression pattern and protein sequence must be examined together to classify a histone locus.

Histone families show both conserved functions and divergence. Multiple genes can encode closely similar canonical H3 proteins used broadly in chromatin, whereas related variants such as H3.3 have characteristic sequence differences and deposition patterns. A histone variant is not necessarily merely an allele of a canonical histone gene. It may be encoded at a distinct homologous locus, and different family members may encode an identical variant protein. The detailed functions of variants and the histone octamer will become clearer in the next chromatin chapter.

Histone clusters also illustrate why an entire physical cluster need not be one family of paralogs. A cluster can contain genes for H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. These products are distinct histone types; close genomic linkage alone does not make every pair members of the same narrowly defined gene family. One may discuss the H3 family within a larger cluster of histone genes. As with bacterial operons, physical grouping and evolutionary relationship are different questions.

Imagine a simplified histone region containing three H3 loci and two H4 loci. This does not mean that each nucleosome contains three H3 and two H4 molecules. Gene copy counts concern DNA; a nucleosome has a characteristic protein stoichiometry determined by assembly. Transcription, translation and assembly bridge those levels. If a question asks how a histone variant can continue to be made in a nondividing cell, its own expression program is more informative than whether it resembles a canonical histone sequence. If it asks why many canonical copies exist, the high demand associated with replication and coordinated dosage offer a relevant explanation.

Compare rDNA and histone families with globin families. Repeated rRNA genes mainly illustrate the maintenance of numerous highly related functional RNA templates and concerted evolution. Histone genes reveal both high-copy production and specialized variants. Globins demonstrate prominent developmental and biochemical diversification of related proteins. All three are examples of the same general evolutionary principle, duplication with subsequent retention or divergence, but the biological problem that favors maintenance is different.

12. Pseudogenes: recognizing loss of an ancestral coding function

A genomic sequence that resembles a functional protein-coding gene but no longer produces the expected functional protein is commonly annotated as a pseudogene. The prefix pseudo does not mean that the DNA is imaginary or that it cannot have any effect. It refers to the loss or absence of the conventional coding function that would be expected from its relationship to a functional gene. Some pseudogenes are not expressed, others produce RNA, and a subset may acquire biologically important activities.

To understand how an ordinary coding locus can become disabled, consider translation. A coding sequence is read in successive groups of three nucleotides called codons. An insertion or deletion of a number of bases not divisible by three changes the reading frame downstream unless compensated by another event. A base substitution can turn an amino-acid codon into a premature stop codon. A mutation can remove a required splice site or disrupt a promoter. A large deletion may eliminate an essential domain. Each lesion affects a different step in the production of a functional protein, so its consequence should be interpreted with reference to the gene's actual structure.

Not every mutation that changes the coding sequence establishes pseudogene status. A three-base deletion might remove one dispensable amino acid and leave an active protein. A one-base deletion in an untranslated region does not automatically change the coding reading frame. A premature stop near the end of a gene can have a different effect from one near the beginning. Alternative transcript structures and translation initiation sites may sometimes preserve a useful product. Similarly, a gene that is not expressed in one tissue could be expressed elsewhere. Several independent indications of loss of normal coding ability make annotation more reliable.

There are three commonly distinguished origins. A duplicated, or unprocessed, pseudogene forms when a DNA-level copy loses ancestral coding function. Because the template is genomic DNA, the copy may retain introns, exon arrangement and some original flanking sequence. A processed pseudogene originates from a processed RNA-derived retrocopy and often has contiguous exon sequences, a remnant of a poly(A) tail or target-site duplications. A unitary pseudogene arises when a previously functional gene is disabled at its original locus without a surviving equivalent functional parent from that duplication event in the same lineage. It need not have arisen from a new duplication. A functional ortholog in another species at a corresponding genomic position can help reveal this history.

An example of the unitary category is the inactive human GULOP locus (the human remnant of GULO). In many other mammals, the corresponding enzyme contributes to the synthesis of vitamin C. In humans and several other lineages, loss of the functional biosynthetic capacity means that vitamin C must be obtained from the diet. The important inference is that a pseudogene need not sit beside a functional parent in the same genome. Related functional sequences in other species may reveal its former identity. Its physiological consequence also shows that loss of a coding function can be tolerated when the environment or other biological processes supply the necessary product.

The human globin clusters contain pseudogene loci such as HBBP1. Their presence alongside functional globin genes helps illustrate that duplicate copies can follow different trajectories. A physical family map can retain evidence of both retained and lost ancestral functions. Unlike a DNA deletion, pseudogenization can leave recognizable sequence material behind. Over time, further mutations and deletions may make the sequence harder to recognize, and eventually a locus can disappear from the genome entirely.

Use the phrase functional pseudogene carefully. A locus can lack the ancestral protein-coding function but produce an RNA that influences another gene or be involved in a different regulatory process. Detection of an RNA alone is insufficient to establish such a role. An experimental comparison must demonstrate that changing the locus or its product causes a specific biological effect, ideally with appropriate controls and rescue. Some annotated pseudogenes may later be reclassified as protein-coding genes if evidence establishes a functional protein. Annotation is a scientific conclusion that can be revised, not an immutable property conferred by a name.

Comparative evidence for loss at an ancestral locus

Comparative studies of human unitary pseudogenes use the corresponding intact gene in another species to locate a disabled human sequence. The human GULOP reference annotation contains remnants of a gene whose rodent counterpart encodes a functional enzyme in vitamin C biosynthesis. Mutations and missing coding regions support loss rather than mere failure to detect transcription in one sample. In a complementary experimental approach, a 2004 study examined whether introducing functional mouse GULO cDNA into human cells could restore vitamin C synthesis. The logic of such a rescue is that restoration of a missing enzymatic step can test whether the deficient step accounts for the biochemical phenotype. Gene restoration is a functional test distinct from sequence comparison; it does not establish the exact historical mutation that originally disabled the human gene. This example makes it possible to distinguish a unitary pseudogene from a processed duplicate without relying solely on intron count.

13. Distinguishing expression, coding ability and biological function

The identification of a pseudogene or a functional duplicate is an inference from multiple tests. Begin with genomic DNA. A full-length alignment to a known coding gene identifies corresponding exons and possible disabling changes. Verify that the sequence is assigned to the correct locus rather than to another highly similar copy. A locus-specific flanking sequence can provide this distinction when conserved internal regions cannot.

Next examine RNA. If the candidate lacks detectable RNA in one cell sample, one can conclude only that expression was not detected under those conditions and within the assay's sensitivity. A positive signal also requires careful interpretation. In a reverse-transcription PCR assay, residual genomic DNA could amplify even without RNA-derived cDNA. A no-reverse-transcriptase control helps detect this possibility. A unique transcript sequence is needed to distinguish a candidate from its parent, especially when the candidate is an intronless retrocopy.

Protein detection poses a related problem. An antibody recognizing a conserved peptide can bind both paralogous proteins. A band of the expected size does not identify which gene produced it. A distinctive peptide or another locus-specific method gives stronger evidence. An intact open reading frame is a stretch of codons that can potentially encode a protein; it does not prove that transcription, translation and functional folding occur. Conversely, a truncated coding product may retain partial activity depending on the affected domain.

The strongest functional test asks what happens when the candidate changes while related genes remain intact. In a hypothetical experiment, a specific deletion of locus B reduces a cellular response by 40 percent; restoration of B at a controlled expression level rescues that response. If the neighboring loci are unchanged and appropriate controls are included, the result supports a functional contribution by B. It does not establish every possible role or prove that B acts through the same molecular mechanism as A. If the deleted DNA also contained an enhancer for A, the phenotype could instead reflect altered A expression. Measuring A and designing a rescue that distinguishes the RNA or protein product from the DNA region help resolve this alternative.

A negative result must be interpreted with equal care. If deletion of B produces no detectable phenotype under standard growth conditions, perhaps A compensates, perhaps B is active only under stress, or perhaps B truly has no relevant function. A double deletion revealing a phenotype that neither single deletion produces suggests redundancy under the tested conditions. A double deletion that affects nearby genes simultaneously is less informative unless those off-target or positional effects are excluded. In an exam, look for the explanation that the evidence warrants, not the most dramatic conclusion.

One example of the distinction between transcription and function comes from the GENCODE pseudogene catalogue, which combines genomic annotation with evidence for transcription and other features. Such catalogues identify many pseudogene-derived transcripts but do not thereby establish a biological role for every one. They also demonstrate why annotation must consider the genomic structure of the sequence instead of equating all transcription with conventional protein-coding gene function. The existence of exceptional functional products is not a reason to assume that most pseudogenes perform regulatory tasks.

The same reasoning applies when assessing a gene family from a genome assembly. A highly similar tandem array may be difficult to resolve into separate copies. The reported copy number can differ from the actual individual genome if repeated regions are collapsed during assembly. An apparent missing copy may therefore require independent verification. At the exam level it is sufficient to recognize this limitation and distinguish a measured physical copy count from a tentative annotation.

14. Worked quantitative reasoning: count the correct object

Most calculations in this topic arise from physical copy number, sequence identity or simple dosage assumptions rather than from a universal kinetic law of gene-family evolution. The first step is to state whether a number is per haploid set, per diploid nucleus before replication, per chromosome, per transcript or per protein complex. The second is to state whether the calculation counts all related DNA loci or only copies that retain the relevant function.

For an ordinary autosomal family with n loci per haploid chromosome set and no copy-number variation between homologs, a diploid G1 nucleus has 2n copies of those loci. This follows because each of two homologous chromosome sets contributes n. After S phase, each chromosome has two sister chromatids, so the number of physical DNA copies is 4n, but the number of distinct loci in the haploid reference does not change. For example, a three-member autosomal family has six physical DNA copies in G1 and twelve in G2. A structural duplication on one homolog changes the G1 count to seven, which becomes fourteen after replication. Do not describe replication alone as creation of new paralogs.

An unequal-exchange example: two participating chromatids contain five identical tandem units each. Misalignment by two units followed by reciprocal exchange within aligned repeat units produces arrays of seven and three units. The sum is 10 both before and after. If the other two chromatids in meiosis were unchanged, they each retain five units; the four products therefore have seven, five, five and three units. In this idealized event, the average number per product is (7 + 5 + 5 + 3)/4 = 5. An individual product can nevertheless transmit a substantial change in dosage.

For sequence comparison, suppose A and B have 150 matching positions in a 180-position ungapped alignment, while A and C have 156 matching positions in a 240-position alignment. The respective identities are 83.33% and 65%. A and B have greater percentage identity despite having fewer matching positions in absolute terms. If the first alignment covers only a shared domain while the second covers almost the entire protein, it is not legitimate to conclude from percentages alone that A and B are closer evolutionary relatives. Coverage and alignment context matter. No molecular clock or advanced evolutionary model is needed for this inference.

For a repeat-output problem, let n be the number of major rDNA transcription units and f the fraction active at the measured time. Let r be the production rate of complete precursors per active unit, measured in transcripts per minute per unit. Under the assumption that all active units operate at the same constant rate and that no other factor is limiting, the total precursor production rate is R = nfr transcripts per minute. This follows by adding r over nf active units. If n = 200, f = 0.30 and r = 4, there are 60 active units and R = 240 precursors per minute. If a second sample has 240 units but f = 0.25 with the same r, it also has 60 active units and produces the same modeled output. An increase in DNA dosage need not increase RNA production.

Suppose each of those 240 precursors gives one molecule each of 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNA, and the cell can supply 200 molecules of 5S rRNA per minute. Even with unlimited ribosomal proteins and perfect processing and assembly, at most 200 complete cytosolic ribosomes per minute can be supplied from these newly synthesized RNA pools. The maximum follows from the limiting component because each mature ribosome contains one of each of those four rRNA types. Real assembly has additional steps and losses, so the calculated value is an upper bound, not a measured rate.

Finally, consider a hypothetical protein family with four loci per haploid set, one of which is a pseudogene with no relevant product. In a diploid G1 cell there are eight related DNA copies but only six functional copies under the stated assumptions. If all six active copies produce 20 units of product per hour, the modeled output is 120 units per hour. A deletion of a pseudogene copy leaves seven related DNA copies but does not change that modeled output. A deletion of one functional copy reduces the output to 100, a decrease of 20/120 × 100 = 16.67%. This prediction assumes no regulatory compensation and no other function for the pseudogene. If the problem changes either assumption, reconsider the result.

The general lesson is not a formula for every gene family. It is a method: identify the counted entity, identify any limiting biological component, state the simplifying assumptions, and then carry out the arithmetic. Many difficult-looking Part C questions are solved by keeping these categories separate.

15. Exceptions, misconceptions and a method for reading unfamiliar gene maps

A family is not necessarily a tandem array, and a cluster is not necessarily a family. A family name indicates a relationship; a chromosome position indicates organization. Similarly, similarity of biological function is not sufficient evidence of common ancestry because unrelated proteins may solve comparable problems. When a question asks whether loci are paralogs, find evidence of duplication rather than assuming that two enzymes with similar substrates are related.

Gene number is not the same as allele number or chromosome number. A normal diploid organism with two alleles of one gene does not automatically possess a duplicated gene family, and a normal S-phase doubling does not create a new locus. If a duplication changes only one homolog, the count can be an odd number in a diploid nucleus. The position of copies matters when predicting how they will be inherited. Two genomes with the same total copy count can distribute copies differently among chromosomes.

The term repetitive DNA is about sequence multiplicity, whereas gene family is about ancestry and gene identity. A highly repeated rRNA family can belong to both categories. A simple satellite array without conventional gene units need not be a family of genes. A dispersed family with two related genes may be classed as a low-copy sequence family but still exemplifies duplication. There is no fixed copy-number threshold that turns an ancestral relationship into a gene family.

Do not treat an intronless gene as a processed pseudogene without evidence. Many legitimate genes, including numerous canonical histone genes, lack introns. A retrocopy may be functional; a duplicated pseudogene may retain introns. A unitary pseudogene may have no functional counterpart in the same genome. Conversely, a locus with an apparently intact coding sequence may lack expression or an essential function. Functional loss is not inferred solely from the presence of a gene-like name or one structural feature.

Do not assume all copies become more different with time. Concerted evolution can homogenize sequences through exchanges among repeats. Nor should all differences be described as new functions. A comparative map showing two different paralogs reveals divergence, but the ancestral function must be known or credibly inferred before distinguishing partitioning from innovation. A functional assay may show that two paralogs both catalyze the same reaction yet have different expression windows. This can be a biologically important specialization without a change in the basic reaction.

When facing a new map in an examination, first locate unique flanking regions and count the distinct loci. Then compare their orientation and exon organization. Determine whether the supplied history contains a duplication or a species split. Ask whether the evidence concerns genomic DNA, RNA, protein or phenotype. If a question invokes a functional loss, identify the molecular lesion and its location. If it invokes dosage, count active functional copies and consider whether output is limited by a partner or by regulation. Finally, select the strongest justified inference rather than an unsupported universal claim.

The next chapter changes scale from the sequence-level arrangement of genes to the physical packaging of DNA. A family cluster on a chromosome is not naked DNA in a living eukaryotic nucleus. Its accessibility depends in part on how DNA associates with histones and other chromatin proteins. Histone gene families introduced here provide a bridge: their products construct the nucleosomes that organize the very chromosomes containing the genes. This connection prepares the study of nucleosomes without confusing gene-family organization with chromatin structure itself.

16. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

These original examination-style questions test the concepts developed in this chapter.

Question 1

Which pair represents alleles rather than paralogs?

  1. Two sequence variants at HBB on homologous chromosomes
  2. HBA1 and HBA2
  3. HBG1 and HBG2
  4. HBD and HBB
Answer and explanation

A. Alleles occupy corresponding positions of one locus. The other pairs occupy distinct duplicated loci even when the proteins are closely related.

Question 2

An ancestral gene duplicated before a species split. X-A1 and Y-A2 descend from different copies. They are:

  1. Alleles
  2. Paralogs
  3. Necessarily orthologs
  4. Unrelated because they occur in different species
Answer and explanation

B. Their lineages separated at duplication. A is incorrect because the loci differ, C confuses species location with the decisive event, and D ignores ancestry.

Question 3

Which observation most directly supports an RNA-derived copy of an interrupted gene?

  1. Same chromosome
  2. Similar protein function
  3. Contiguous ancestral exon junctions and an A-rich insertion remnant
  4. A conserved promoter
Answer and explanation

C. The spliced RNA template explains the contiguous exons and tail remnant. A and B are compatible with either origin; D favors a DNA-level copy.

Question 4

A family of nearly identical rRNA genes is retained primarily because:

  1. Every copy catalyzes a different reaction
  2. All are necessarily active
  3. Each copy is a different allele
  4. Multiple templates can support abundant rRNA synthesis
Answer and explanation

D. Template multiplicity supports high output when needed. A invents specialization, B ignores inactive copies, and C confuses distinct genomic copies with alleles.

Question 5

Which chain composition identifies fetal hemoglobin HbF?

  1. α₂γ₂
  2. α₂β₂
  3. α₂δ₂
  4. ζ₂ε₂
Answer and explanation

A. HbF contains gamma and alpha chains. B is HbA, C HbA₂ and D embryonic Gower I.

Question 6

Which pair is associated with major human globin clusters?

  1. Alpha: 11; beta: 16
  2. Alpha: 16; beta: 11
  3. Both: 21
  4. Both: 13
Answer and explanation

B. The major alpha-like cluster lies on chromosome 16 and beta-like on 11. A reverses them; C and D confuse globin loci with acrocentric rDNA-bearing chromosomes.

Question 7

A unitary pseudogene differs from a duplicated pseudogene because:

  1. It is always transcribed
  2. It must lack introns
  3. It arose through inactivation of an existing functional locus without a new duplicate
  4. It is defined by a poly(A) remnant
Answer and explanation

C. Unitary classification concerns loss at the original locus. A is not required; B and D concern possible RNA-mediated signatures, not unitary origin.

Question 8

Which statement about an intronless retrocopy is justified?

  1. It necessarily encodes a functional protein
  2. It necessarily is nonfunctional
  3. It must lie beside the parental gene
  4. Its functional status requires additional evidence
Answer and explanation

D. Retrocopy origin does not determine function. A and B make opposite unsupported assumptions; C ignores dispersed insertions.

Question 9

Which is true of concerted evolution?

  1. Repeat copies may become homogenized by sequence exchange
  2. Every repeated gene evolves independently
  3. It creates a new chromosome set in every event
  4. It is identical to ordinary S-phase replication
Answer and explanation

A. Exchange and turnover can spread variants among repeat copies. B contradicts the homogenization pattern; C and D conflate unrelated processes.

Question 10

Assertion: Two genes in one physical cluster need not be paralogs. Reason: A cluster is defined by genomic proximity, while paralogy is defined by ancestral duplication.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. The reason directly distinguishes location from ancestry, explaining the assertion. B denies that connection; C and D incorrectly reject a true statement.

17. Part C — Gene maps, experiments and quantitative analysis

These original examination-style questions test the concepts developed in this chapter.

Question 1

An autosomal family has 4 loci per haploid set; one homolog carries an additional copy. How many DNA copies are present in G1 and G2?

  1. 8 and 16
  2. 9 and 18
  3. 5 and 10
  4. 9 and 9
Answer and explanation

B. G1 has 4+5=9 copies. S phase doubles physical DNA to 18. A ignores variation, C counts only one homolog and D ignores replication.

Question 2

Two chromatids each have six tandem units. Reciprocal exchange occurs with three-unit offset. What is the simplest predicted pair?

  1. 6 and 6
  2. 12 and 0
  3. 9 and 3
  4. 9 and 6
Answer and explanation

C. The offset adds three units to one and removes three from the other; total 12. A ignores offset, B assumes a six-unit shift, D violates conservation for this reciprocal model.

Question 3

A gene originally performed tasks X and Y. Copy 1 now performs only X; copy 2 only Y. Which model fits?

  1. Concerted evolution
  2. Unitary pseudogenization
  3. Dosage-only retention
  4. Subfunctionalization
Answer and explanation

D. Together the copies retain the ancestral tasks through partition. A concerns homogenization; B is loss at an original locus; C does not explain the distinct tasks.

Question 4

An outgroup has only ancestral reaction X. Descendant copy A retains X while B performs novel reaction Z. The best supported model is:

  1. Neofunctionalization
  2. Subfunctionalization
  3. Simple copy gain without divergence
  4. Allelic segregation only
Answer and explanation

A. Z is absent from the inferred ancestral state, while X remains. B partitions ancestral tasks, C ignores Z and D confuses different loci with alleles. The ancestral inference depends on the outgroup assumption.

Question 5

A probe recognizes two bands in a Southern blot. Which conclusion is warranted without a restriction map?

  1. Exactly two functional genes
  2. At least two distinguishable hybridizing fragment sizes
  3. Exactly one processed pseudogene
  4. Exactly two genes on separate chromosomes
Answer and explanation

B. Bands correspond to fragment sizes, not necessarily separate genes. A, C and D all require genomic or functional information not supplied.

Question 6

A and B share 96 of 120 aligned residues; A and C share 90 of 100. What is true?

  1. A–B 96%, A–C 90%
  2. A–B necessarily closer by ancestry
  3. A–B 80%, A–C 90%; coverage must be considered
  4. A–B and A–C have identical identity
Answer and explanation

C. 96/120=0.8 and 90/100=0.9. A uses counts as percentages, B ignores coverage and D contradicts the calculation.

Question 7

A diploid G1 genome contains 160 major rDNA transcription units, 40% active. Each active unit produces five complete precursors/min. What is the modeled rate?

  1. 800/min
  2. 64/min
  3. 400/min
  4. 320/min
Answer and explanation

D. 160 × 0.40 = 64 active units; 64 × 5 = 320 precursors/min. A assumes all units active, B gives active unit count not rate, and C uses a wrong active fraction.

Question 8

A diploid cell contains 150 major rDNA units. Each includes one 18S, one 5.8S and one 28S region. How many specified mature-rRNA coding regions occur?

  1. 450
  2. 150
  3. 600
  4. 300
Answer and explanation

A. 150 × 3 = 450. B counts transcription units, C incorrectly includes 5S, D omits one region per repeat.

Question 9

In a simplified alpha-globin dosage model, four copies produce 25 units each per hour and two beta copies produce 50 each. One alpha copy is deleted. What is the chain ratio α:β?

  1. 1:1
  2. 0.75:1
  3. 2:1
  4. 0.5:1
Answer and explanation

B. Alpha becomes 75, beta stays 100, giving 0.75:1. A ignores deletion, C uses an incorrect gene-count ratio, D represents a larger loss in this model.

Question 10

A locus retains introns and nearby ancestral DNA but has an early frameshift that eliminates the normal protein. Its functional parent remains. Most likely classification?

  1. Processed functional retrogene
  2. Unitary pseudogene
  3. Duplicated pseudogene
  4. Allele at the parent locus
Answer and explanation

C. The retained structure favors DNA duplication, and the lesion disables normal coding. A predicts RNA-derived organization and function, B omits the retained parent, D contradicts the distinct locus.

Question 11

RNA-seq detects reads shared exactly between a functional parent and a pseudogene. What would best establish expression of the latter?

  1. Higher total read count
  2. An antibody to a shared peptide
  3. Absence of parent expression in an unrelated tissue
  4. RNA evidence containing a feature unique to the pseudogene locus
Answer and explanation

D. A locus-specific RNA feature resolves ambiguous origin. A and B remain shared, C is irrelevant to the sampled tissue.

Question 12

Each of two paralogs can support growth. Single deletions show no defect; the double deletion causes failure and either gene rescues it under the same conditions. Best inference?

  1. Functional redundancy under tested conditions
  2. Neither gene has function
  3. One must be a pseudogene
  4. They are necessarily alleles
Answer and explanation

A. The double loss and independent rescues demonstrate overlapping contributions in this assay. B and C conflict with rescue; D ignores distinct loci.

Question 13

A protein complex needs one P and one Q. P increases from 80 to 160 molecules, Q remains 80. Maximum complexes under complete assembly?

  1. 160
  2. 80
  3. 240
  4. 40
Answer and explanation

B. Q limits assembly to 80. A counts P only, C sums molecules rather than pairing them and D incorrectly divides the limiting count by two.

Question 14

A candidate is absent as an intact gene in humans but has a disabled sequence at the corresponding locus and an intact ortholog in a related species. No equivalent functional copy remains in humans. Best explanation?

  1. Always a processed pseudogene
  2. Gene conversion alone
  3. Loss of ancestral function consistent with unitary pseudogenization
  4. A new allele of the related species' locus in humans
Answer and explanation

C. Inactivation at the corresponding position with no remaining functional equivalent supports unitary loss. A requires RNA-copy evidence; B does not explain the demonstrated loss; D confuses cross-species orthology and alleles.
