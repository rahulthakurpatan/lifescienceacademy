1. The tissue problem: which cell leaves descendants?

A normal tissue continually balances cell production against differentiation, migration and removal. In the bone marrow, mature blood cells have finite lifetimes, whereas less differentiated populations produce replacements. In intestinal epithelium, cells born near the base of a crypt move toward the surface and eventually disappear. The important distinction is between the production of cells and the persistence of the lineages that produce them. A mutation in a short-lived cell may disappear when that cell is shed, even if it briefly changes the cell's metabolism. The same mutation in a cell whose descendants remain in the tissue can be inherited through many successive divisions. The genetic starting point of a cancer therefore cannot be understood from a list of mutations alone. We must ask which cell acquired the change, what the cell normally does, and whether its descendants can persist.

A stem cell is capable of self-renewal and of producing more differentiated descendants. A progenitor cell is a descendant with a more restricted developmental potential and often a limited number of divisions. These definitions describe normal developmental behavior rather than a particular cell shape. A progenitor can undergo several rounds of proliferation while producing a specialized lineage, even if it does not normally maintain itself indefinitely. When a genetic alteration increases the probability that a progenitor leaves a proliferative daughter, the normal limit on its lineage may change. The original developmental label does not by itself decide whether the altered cell can initiate a neoplasm. Conversely, the ability of a cell to self-renew normally is not evidence of malignancy: ordinary stem cells are controlled by their niche and by molecular restraints.

The cell of origin refers to a normal or premalignant cell in whose lineage cancer-associated changes accumulate. A cancer stem cell, in contrast, is defined functionally within an existing tumor by its ability to sustain tumor propagation in an appropriate assay. These two concepts concern different times and different biological questions. A cancer stem-like population may have originated from a normal stem cell, but it may also have acquired self-renewing behavior after alterations in a more committed cell. Finding a stem-cell marker in a tumor does not establish the historical identity of the cell in which tumor evolution began. The marker might reflect a state acquired later or shared by several developmental populations. Lineage evidence is required to connect present phenotype to ancestry.

Consider two equally frequent changes that each reduce the normal requirement for an extracellular growth signal. One occurs in a terminally differentiated cell that cannot re-enter the cell cycle; the other occurs in a progenitor that is already dividing. Their immediate molecular effects could resemble one another, yet their consequences for tissue composition differ. The second cell has an opportunity to transmit the altered DNA to new cells. Even that opportunity is not a guarantee: differentiation, death, competition with normal neighbors and tissue architecture may prevent the lineage from persisting. A cell with an advantageous genotype must survive these restrictions before its genetic change becomes common in the tissue.

The term clone means a set of cells descended from a common ancestor. Clonality is not synonymous with cancer. Normal tissue can contain expanding clones as a consequence of development, repair or chance. A genetically abnormal clone may also persist for years without progressing to a malignant disease. The defining issue in cancer genetics is whether inherited cellular changes progressively allow a lineage to evade the controls that normally maintain tissue organization. A malignant population may contain multiple genetically distinguishable branches of the same ancestral clone. Some of its cells can divide rapidly, others may be relatively quiescent, and nonmalignant cells can occupy much of the same tissue. A biopsy is therefore a sample of a mixed population, not necessarily a uniform collection of identical genomes.

How tissue renewal determines the fate of a mutation

A lineage is best understood in terms of the behavior of its individual cells and the boundaries of its local environment. In a normal epithelial renewal compartment, a stem cell can generate two daughters with different future fates, or two daughters with similar fates followed by compensating events elsewhere in the population. At a population level the number of long-lived renewing cells can remain nearly constant while individual stem-cell lineages expand or disappear. A mutation that shifts the balance slightly toward retention of renewing daughters can increase the probability that a lineage remains in the renewing compartment. This is different from a mutation that increases the number of divisions of cells that are already destined to leave the tissue. The latter may increase cell output without establishing an indefinitely persistent clone.

A useful consequence is that selection can operate even when the total size of the tissue is unchanged. Imagine a renewing compartment with a fixed number of available positions. If descendants of one cell occupy positions vacated by another, the former lineage can become more common without increasing the total number of stem cells. The tissue may have an apparently normal appearance while its genetic composition changes. This process is analogous to competition for positions in a finite population. It helps explain why an early driver may be detectable in a region that lacks the histological features of a tumor. When a later alteration changes the relationship between cell production and loss beyond the local compartment, abnormal growth can become visible. The two transitions should not be collapsed into a single event.

A different experimental question concerns the cell in which an alteration is first expressed. In model organisms, lineage-specific genetic markers can identify descendants of particular developmental populations. If all descendants of a labeled cell carry the marker and an abnormal growth derives from that population, the finding supports a contribution from the labeled lineage. However, a marker may be active in more than one cell type, and the timing of labeling can influence the interpretation. If a marker switches on after a cell changes state, a labeled tumor does not necessarily reveal the state of the original cell. A comparison of labels established before and after the relevant change is conceptually stronger than classification based on one terminal specimen. Visvader's review on cells of origin provides a useful scientific context for this distinction [13].

Stem-like behavior can also depend on the environment in which it is measured. A cell that fails to persist in one setting may survive in a supportive tissue environment, and a cell with self-renewal activity in a transplantation model may not occupy the same functional state in its original tumor. Thus a functional assay of tumor propagation tests the potential of cells under the assay conditions. It cannot by itself recreate every historical transition that occurred during tumor initiation. This is why it is important to keep the terms initiating cell and propagating cell separate. A student interpreting an examination problem should identify whether the data trace ancestry, demonstrate persistence, or merely measure markers that correlate with differentiation.

Blood formation provides a useful observation of expansion before malignancy. Studies of age-associated clonal hematopoiesis have detected expanded lineages carrying somatic variants in individuals who do not have an established hematologic malignancy. An influential report by Jaiswal and colleagues in 2014 found an association between such alterations and increased subsequent blood-cancer risk [14]. That is not evidence that every detected clone progresses. The study demonstrates why the frequency of an altered clone and the presence of a cancer diagnosis are different variables. It also connects tissue renewal with time: a persistent progenitor lineage can preserve and expand genomic changes over a long period, increasing the opportunity for future variation without making the outcome inevitable.

2. Somatic inheritance and inherited predisposition

The word genetic is sometimes interpreted as meaning transmitted from parent to child. In cancer biology this is too narrow. A DNA sequence change is genetic because it affects the information carried by the genome. A somatic alteration originates after fertilization in a cell lineage and ordinarily is not transmitted through the germ line. When that cell divides, however, the altered sequence can be copied into both daughter genomes, subject to segregation and further changes. A cancer can consequently be genetic without being a hereditary disease [1]. This distinction is particularly important in a question comparing tumor DNA with constitutional DNA from the same individual.

A germline variant is present in the fertilized egg when inherited from a parent, or when it arises de novo in a parental gamete. It is therefore usually present in the tissues derived from that egg. Some inherited variants reduce the effectiveness of a pathway that ordinarily constrains proliferation or preserves genome integrity. The individual then begins life with a predisposition rather than with a malignant clone. Additional events in a particular cell lineage are often required before a cancer emerges. The fact that a predisposition is distributed throughout the body does not mean every tissue has the same risk. Developmental programs, gene expression, environmental exposure and the ability of a lineage to expand all influence which tissues are affected.

A comparison of matched constitutional and tumor material provides a useful framework. A variant observed in both may be constitutional; a variant found in tumor cells but absent from an appropriate constitutional sample is consistent with somatic acquisition. Neither conclusion is automatic. Mosaicism can produce a variant in a subset of normal tissues, and a blood sample can itself contain an altered hematopoietic clone. A rare tumor-specific variant might be missed by an insensitive assay. Thus the source of the normal comparator and the detection limits are part of the evidence. The biological question is the distribution of a variant across lineages, not simply whether a machine labels it inherited.

It is also necessary to distinguish genetic alteration from altered gene expression. A cell can temporarily increase transcription in response to a physiological signal without changing its DNA sequence. Its daughter may inherit some proteins or regulatory state, but the event need not involve a mutation. Changes in chromatin and DNA methylation can sometimes be maintained through division and contribute to cancer-associated phenotypes. In this chapter, genetic changes in sequence, chromosome structure and dosage are the central subject. Epigenetic regulation is mentioned to prevent the mistaken conclusion that every stable abnormal cellular state must be caused by a newly rearranged chromosome.

The heritability of a cellular phenotype also depends on how a mutation is expressed. If a change disrupts a promoter that is inactive in a particular cell, the immediate effect may be small even when the DNA alteration is permanent. If the altered gene later becomes expressed during differentiation, the phenotype can become apparent in a descendant. Conversely, a powerful molecular effect can be restrained by intact checkpoints. This is one reason that the sequence of events in a lineage is not equivalent to the order in which visible abnormalities appear. A mutation may be acquired before it contributes measurably to selection.

Alleles, mosaicism and the interpretation of genetic origin

At an ordinary autosomal locus a diploid cell has two homologous copies of a genomic region. They are not necessarily identical in sequence. An allele is one version of the sequence at that locus, whereas a homolog is a physical chromosome carrying many loci. When we say that an individual carries a constitutional heterozygous variant, we usually mean that one homolog contains the variant and the other contains a different sequence. This distinction becomes important when a tumor changes its chromosome composition: the variant can be lost, retained or duplicated depending on which physical chromosome segment changes. Even if the original inherited variant is present throughout normal tissue, its dosage can differ among the descendant tumor cells.

A germline predisposition can involve altered genome maintenance rather than a direct proliferative stimulus. If a repair-associated function is impaired, the immediate consequence may be an increased likelihood of persistent errors after a particular class of damage. A cell can retain ordinary growth behavior until additional changes alter pathways controlling division or survival. Alternatively, a predisposition can involve a protein that normally restricts proliferation. The loss of part of that restraint may alter tissue behavior sooner, depending on dosage and cellular context. These two examples illustrate the difference between changing the rate at which variation is produced and changing the consequences of an existing variant. Both can influence cancer risk without being equivalent molecular mechanisms.

Mosaicism arises when genetically different cell populations originate from the same fertilized egg. If a mutation occurs early in embryonic development, its descendants may contribute to multiple tissues. If it occurs much later in a restricted progenitor lineage, it may be present in only a small anatomical region. A postzygotic variant can consequently have a wider distribution than the word tumor-specific suggests. Conversely, the absence of a variant from one sample does not establish its absence from every normal tissue. A comparative study needs a suitable sample and enough sensitivity to distinguish a truly absent allele from a low-frequency allele. This is a biological sampling problem as well as a technical one.

Somatic change is different from a genetic polymorphism that is common in the population. A variant found relative to a reference genome may be present in the constitutional DNA of the person and need not have contributed to cancer. The reference sequence is a tool for comparison, not a declaration that every difference is pathological. If a tumor and the matched normal sample both carry a variant, the variant may be an ordinary constitutional allele, or it may be a predisposition-associated allele with functional consequences. Determining which requires evidence beyond the tumor-normal comparison. A large database of naturally occurring human variation can help, but biological interpretation also requires the relevant gene function and phenotype.

The temporal stability of an inherited DNA alteration should not be confused with permanence of its cellular consequences. A genetic lesion can be compensated by expression of another protein, suppressed by a regulatory signal or physically lost during later chromosome evolution. The genotype inherited by a cell is a starting condition for its current phenotype, not an immutable prediction of its behavior. If an altered allele is absent in a late tumor sample, this can reflect loss in the surviving lineage rather than absence from all earlier cells. To reason correctly about cancer evolution, separate the initial event, the subsequent chromosome history and the current functional state. This distinction will recur in the discussion of copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity and clonal ancestry.

3. DNA lesions, copying errors and fixed mutations

DNA is chemically stable enough to serve as hereditary material, but not chemically inert. Bases can undergo spontaneous transformations, oxidative products can modify nucleotides, and physical damage can disrupt the backbone. Replication also creates opportunities for incorrect nucleotide incorporation and errors in copying repetitive sequences. A lesion is a physical or chemical abnormality of DNA; a mutation is a change in the inherited sequence. A lesion may be removed accurately before replication, leaving the original sequence intact. If a damaged template is copied inaccurately or if repair changes the sequence, the resulting alteration can persist after subsequent replication. Thus an exposure that damages DNA is not equivalent to a fixed mutation in every exposed cell.

Consider spontaneous deamination of cytosine. The chemical product uracil is inappropriate in ordinary DNA and can be recognized by base-excision repair. If repair restores cytosine using the complementary strand, no heritable substitution is produced. If uracil remains when the region is copied, adenine can be incorporated opposite it. Further replication can establish a thymine–adenine pair where a cytosine–guanine pair originally existed. The example connects chemical identity, complementary pairing, replication and repair. It also shows why mutation probability depends on the time between lesion formation and successful repair. A lesion is an opportunity for an error, not an instruction that inevitably changes the genome.

Replication fidelity emerges from several layers. The polymerase prefers correctly paired substrates; proofreading can remove some newly incorporated incorrect nucleotides; mismatch repair can correct residual errors. These systems are not redundant copies of the same reaction because they act on distinct stages of the copying process. A deficiency at one stage can elevate the burden placed on later safeguards. In a dividing lineage, the errors that escape the combined systems can become stable sequence variants. The molecular details of polymerases and repair enzymes belong to Unit 3.A. The cancer-specific consequence is that a failure of genome maintenance increases the supply of potentially selectable variants without specifying which future variant will occur.

DNA double-strand breaks raise a different structural problem. Both backbones are interrupted, and the continuity of a chromosome is lost locally. A cell must either restore a viable chromosome structure or fail to transmit that chromosome normally. Homologous recombination can use an appropriate homologous template, often a sister chromatid, to guide accurate restoration. End joining reconnects broken DNA ends without requiring a long homologous template and is essential for normal genome maintenance. End joining is not intrinsically synonymous with inaccurate repair. Nevertheless, when multiple ends coexist or when ends have been processed, a different pairing or loss of sequence can generate rearrangements. The geometry and accessibility of breaks, the cell-cycle stage and pathway availability influence the possible outcomes.

Genome damage can activate responses that delay progression through the cell cycle, facilitate repair or remove severely damaged cells. These responses reduce the chance that an abnormal sequence or chromosome is passed to descendants. Selection can therefore favor changes that increase proliferation and changes that allow cells with damage to survive; these are distinct routes to increased representation of an abnormal lineage. A genome-maintenance defect may not directly stimulate division. It may instead change the probability distribution of future variants. It can also be costly because extensive damage compromises essential genes and chromosome segregation. A high mutation rate does not guarantee a high population growth rate.

Why different genome-maintenance failures leave different evidence

Replication errors do not all arise by the same molecular process. Repetitive DNA provides a particularly useful example because several neighboring sequence positions may be almost indistinguishable to a copying enzyme. A strand can transiently pair in a shifted register, creating a small loop of unpaired bases. If that intermediate is not corrected, the descendant DNA can contain a repeat-length change. A defect in mismatch repair can make such changes more frequent. Because repeats occur at many sites throughout the genome, repeated observations of altered repeat length can indicate a broader maintenance problem rather than independent selection for every individual repeat. Some changes are nevertheless functional if they occur in coding or regulatory regions.

The cell-cycle context changes the available response to a damaged chromosome. After replication, a sister chromatid can supply a closely matched sequence for restoration of a break. Before replication, that particular template does not yet exist. A homologous chromosome is not identical to a sister chromatid at every locus and is not generally the preferred mitotic repair template. End joining provides a way to reconnect ends without requiring the same extensive homology. These differences do not imply that one pathway is always accurate and the other always inaccurate. Rather, the probability of retaining the original sequence depends on the structure of the break, available template, processing and the actual joining event. A repair pathway can function appropriately in one molecular context and produce a changed sequence in another.

The relationship between chromatin organization and break joining can be understood without memorizing specialist repair factors. Chromosomes occupy structured territories in the nucleus, but DNA loci can move and contact other regions. Broken ends that remain near their original partners have a different set of potential encounters from ends that become spatially separated. If two pairs of breaks occur, joining the original partners restores the earlier linkage, whereas certain alternate combinations create new linkage relationships. Not every mathematical pairing yields a viable chromosome complement: products may lack a centromere or contain abnormal numbers of centromeres. The inherited rearrangements observed after cell division are therefore influenced by both the initial molecular event and the ability of its products to segregate.

DNA break repair is also an energetically constrained process. Polymerization requires nucleotide triphosphates, ligation requires activated intermediates, and chromatin remodeling or the recruitment of some repair machinery involves ATP-consuming activities. Accurate restoration is not obtained merely because the final double-stranded DNA structure is energetically favorable. Cells must coordinate substrate recognition, strand processing and enzyme activity in time. The thermodynamic stability of paired bases helps specify templates, while reaction kinetics and pathway regulation influence whether a particular intermediate is processed before replication proceeds. This explains why chemical principles and cellular timing are both relevant to mutation formation.

Environmental exposures may increase particular classes of lesions, but a tumor mutation pattern reflects more than the exposure itself. The lesions formed, their repair efficiency, the opportunities for copying, cell survival and subsequent selection all influence which sequence changes remain observable. A statistical association between an exposure and a pattern of variants is valuable, but the pattern is not a perfect record of each molecular event. Multiple processes can yield similar substitutions, and distinct tissues can process the same initial lesion differently. Examination questions that describe damage should therefore distinguish the chemical lesion from the observed DNA change and the eventual biological phenotype. A change in mutation rate alone does not justify claiming a specific driver has been produced.

Genome maintenance also illustrates the difference between a consequence and a cause of tumor evolution. A lineage may acquire a defect in a repair-associated pathway early, increasing the supply of later variation. Another lineage may first expand due to a growth-regulatory event and later display genomic instability. A sample taken after both changes have occurred may not establish which was earlier. Longitudinal or ancestry-based data can help resolve the order, while a single snapshot often cannot. At this stage the aim is to understand the sources and classes of genetic changes, leaving detailed repair biochemistry to Unit 3.A and the control of checkpoint responses to later cancer chapters.

4. Small sequence alterations: from base identity to function

The simplest sequence-level difference is a substitution of one nucleotide for another. A transition interchanges purines or interchanges pyrimidines; a transversion exchanges a purine and pyrimidine. These categories describe the chemistry of the substituted base, not the biological outcome. To decide what a substitution does, determine its position and the role of the affected sequence. Within a protein-coding region, a change in a codon may leave the amino acid unchanged, replace it with another residue or generate a premature termination codon. Outside coding regions, a substitution can change a regulatory element or interfere with RNA processing. Cancer-relevant effects therefore cannot be predicted reliably from the substitution class alone.

A missense substitution changes an amino acid. Its significance depends on the structural role of the original residue, the properties of the replacement, and the protein's normal behavior. Replacing a residue within a catalytic site can reduce activity; changing a residue that stabilizes an inactive conformation can increase the fraction of active molecules. A mutation in a binding interface may alter interactions without changing the catalytic center. These possibilities explain why sequence changes in different positions of the same gene need not have the same direction of effect. A recurrent missense change in a growth-regulatory protein is a useful candidate for functional study, but the label driver must follow evidence of a relevant cellular advantage.

Insertions and deletions add or remove nucleotides. In a protein-coding region, an insertion or deletion that is not a multiple of three changes the reading frame downstream of the event. The new sequence of codons can terminate prematurely or encode an abnormal tail. An in-frame deletion instead removes one or more amino acids while preserving the downstream reading frame. Either can be disruptive or, in some contexts, activating. The effect also depends on transcript processing: some premature stop codons trigger nonsense-mediated RNA decay, whereas others escape it. Do not infer protein abundance solely from the presence of a predicted premature stop.

The DNA sequence can remain unchanged throughout the coding region while transcription or splicing changes because of an altered nearby control sequence. A splice-site mutation can lead to exon skipping, retention of intronic sequence or use of an alternative junction. The resulting protein may be shorter, longer or otherwise altered. Regulatory mutations can change how much normal protein is produced rather than its primary structure. The common distinction between an altered molecule and an altered amount of molecule will become useful when we examine chromosome copy number. Both can alter the effective strength of a pathway, but their molecular evidence is different.

A sequence variant observed in a tumor is not necessarily a cause of that tumor. It may have occurred in a lineage that subsequently expanded because of another change. The observed frequency of the variant is then influenced by the ancestry of the sampled cells. Even a variant present in nearly every cancer cell might be functionally neutral if it was already present in their common ancestor. Conversely, a mutation confined to a small subpopulation can have a strong effect within that subpopulation. Functional significance and population prevalence are independent questions that must be joined with evidence rather than treated as synonyms.

Coding effects, regulatory effects and the meaning of functional mutation

The location of a sequence change within a gene is an important starting point for interpreting its effect, but even a coding change does not define its phenotype completely. A missense substitution that replaces a charged side chain with an uncharged side chain may remove an ionic interaction within a protein. If that interaction was essential for holding an active-site residue in position, activity may decrease. If it stabilized an inhibitory arrangement, activity may increase instead. The molecular consequences depend on the energetic and structural role of the interaction in the protein's different conformations. A sequence change cannot be classified as activating merely because the replacement is chemically substantial.

Silent or synonymous substitutions illustrate a different limitation of the genetic code. If two codons encode the same amino acid, the primary protein sequence can remain identical. Many such changes have little functional effect, but not every synonymous change is biologically neutral. The nucleotide sequence may also participate in RNA processing or influence transcript behavior. Thus the appropriate initial description is synonymous at the protein-coding level, not necessarily a passenger. The functional assessment should consider the relevant molecular product and avoid treating one annotation category as a complete prediction. Similarly, a noncoding variant should not automatically be regarded as insignificant when the locus includes a regulatory element required for normal gene expression.

A stop-gain alteration and a whole-gene deletion may both reduce the activity of a growth-restraining gene, but they are not necessarily identical. A deletion removes the template for production from the affected allele. A stop-gain alteration may instead produce an unstable transcript or a truncated protein that retains some domains and lacks others. If a truncated product can still associate with partners, its effects may differ from complete absence of the protein. For some proteins that function in complexes, an abnormal product can interfere with normal products from the other allele. The distinction between absence and presence of an abnormal product underlies some of the exceptions to simple loss-of-function rules taught in the next tumor-suppressor chapter.

The normal regulatory response of a signaling protein can also be altered without changing its maximum catalytic capacity. Consider a molecular switch that usually spends most of its time in an inactive state in the absence of a growth signal. A mutation could slow the return to the inactive state, causing more molecules to remain active for longer. The abundance of the protein need not change. The measured activity of the pathway increases because the distribution of conformations changes. This conceptual distinction between activity per molecule and amount of protein is important for comparing point mutation with copy-number gain. Later, RAS and other oncogenic switches will provide more specific examples; here the task is to distinguish the classes of genomic alteration that can produce different molecular outcomes.

Evidence of altered protein function is usually more persuasive when it can be linked to a cellular phenotype under a defined condition. Suppose two cells produce similar quantities of a signaling protein, but one carries a missense variant. If the variant cell shows a higher pathway output in the absence of a signal, that observation is consistent with an altered switch or catalytic property. It could also reflect differences elsewhere in the network if the cells have unrelated backgrounds. Comparing cells with a closely matched genetic background, or testing the protein's activity as a separate molecular question, can help distinguish those explanations. Examination reasoning should move from the observation to the most specific supported conclusion, without treating a predicted amino acid change as direct evidence of tumor formation.

5. Deletions, copy-number gains and amplification

A chromosome segment can be present in a different number of copies from the expected number. In a simplified diploid autosomal region, two copies represent the reference state: one on each homolog. Deletion of a segment on one homolog reduces the total to one. Duplication of a segment on one homolog may increase the total to three. Amplification refers to a larger increase in the number of copies of a genomic region; no single numerical threshold is universal across measurement methods and clinical contexts. These are dosage changes. Their immediate genomic consequence is an altered number of DNA templates, not necessarily a proportionate change in active protein.

Deletion can remove an entire gene, part of its coding sequence or a regulatory element. A large deletion may remove many genes with different functions. If one deleted copy belongs to a gene that normally limits cell proliferation, a remaining functional allele may still provide sufficient activity. In other circumstances the amount of gene product from the remaining allele may be inadequate. A loss of both functional copies can have a different effect from loss of one copy. The two-hit concept and the important exceptions are addressed in 4.E.04; for now, separate the number of physical copies from the number of functional copies.

Loss of heterozygosity describes disappearance of the distinction between two alleles at a locus. A normal cell carrying alleles A and B is heterozygous. A descendant carrying only A after deletion of B has lost heterozygosity. But a cell carrying two copies of A and no B has also lost heterozygosity, despite having two DNA copies in total. The latter possibility is called copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity. A copy-number measurement alone cannot distinguish A/B from A/A if both have two copies. Allele-sensitive evidence is needed. This distinction prevents a common examination mistake: equating every instance of loss of heterozygosity with chromosome deletion.

Gene amplification can increase the supply of transcription templates for a growth-promoting protein. An illustrative example is amplification of ERBB2 in a subset of breast cancers. More gene copies can contribute to elevated receptor abundance, with altered signaling consequences. However, transcriptional activity per copy, RNA stability, translation, protein turnover and the availability of interacting partners can all limit or reshape the relationship. A region containing multiple amplified genes may also include several candidates whose contribution is unequal. The entire amplified region must not automatically be interpreted as a single activated gene. Evidence of increased expression and functional dependence strengthens the assignment of a relevant driver.

Copy-number changes can involve a short region, a chromosome arm or an entire chromosome. A whole-chromosome gain increases the dosage of many unrelated genes simultaneously. Such a gain can be disadvantageous because it changes the balance of proteins assembled from multiple gene products. A local amplification may alter a narrower set of loci, although it too can include unrelated genes. The physical organization of additional DNA also varies. Extra copies can occur within chromosomes or, in some cancers, on extrachromosomal DNA. For examination purposes, the essential point is that the assay's reported copy number does not by itself identify the arrangement or establish which product causes a phenotype.

A diploid reference is useful but must not become a hidden assumption. A cancer cell that has undergone whole-genome doubling can have four copies of many autosomal regions. Three copies at one locus then represent a relative loss from its background ploidy, even though three exceeds the ordinary diploid number of two. Interpretation also changes when a tumor sample contains normal diploid cells. Measurements averaged across all cells can produce fractional copy-number estimates. Individual cells contain integer numbers of a locus, but a mixture of cells with distinct copy numbers need not have an integer mean. Consequently, copy-number interpretation requires a reference for both ploidy and sample composition.

Gene dosage as a molecular and experimental relationship

An extra copy of a gene can increase transcriptional capacity because it supplies an additional DNA template. However, transcription is a regulated process involving interactions between promoter DNA, transcription factors and chromatin. If the available transcriptional machinery is limited, additional templates may compete for it. If a regulatory feedback system reduces transcription when the protein product is abundant, the cell can partially compensate for increased dosage. In another cell type, where the promoter is inaccessible or inactive, extra copies may contribute little expression. These relationships explain why the number of DNA copies is the appropriate starting variable for an amplification question but not necessarily the final variable controlling phenotype.

A simple model clarifies the assumptions behind proportional dosage. Let C be the number of equivalent expressed gene copies, a the rate of transcript production per copy, and k the first-order RNA degradation constant. The RNA abundance M satisfies dM/dt = aC − kM in the simplest constant-rate model. At steady state, production balances degradation, giving M = aC/k. If a and k are unchanged, tripling C triples M. But if the promoter activity per copy declines or RNA degradation accelerates, the relationship changes. This equation describes a conditional quantitative prediction, not a universal law of cancer gene expression. It also distinguishes a measurement of steady-state abundance from the underlying rates that establish it.

The next stage is protein expression. If translation occurs at rate s per RNA molecule and protein turnover follows a first-order constant h, the analogous steady-state abundance is P = sM/h. Combining the idealized equations gives P = saC/(hk). All four rate parameters need not remain constant between a normal cell and an altered cell. Protein localization and the fraction in an active state add further variables. The value of the model is that it identifies where the simple gene-dosage hypothesis could fail. If a question gives copy number, RNA amount and protein amount separately, the pattern of departures from proportionality can help distinguish transcriptional, post-transcriptional or post-translational effects.

The classical report by Slamon and colleagues in 1987 connected amplification of HER-2/neu, now commonly designated ERBB2, with clinical outcomes in breast cancer [15]. The key lesson is that a DNA dosage alteration could be linked to meaningful differences among tumors of similar tissue origin. An association between amplification and outcome does not by itself establish a complete molecular mechanism or apply to every breast tumor. Subsequent work on receptor signaling and targeted interventions gave the locus particular biological significance. In a cancer genetics chapter, the distinction between increased genomic copies and increased receptor activity is more important than memorizing clinical percentages or treatment protocols. The next oncogene chapter will discuss why enhanced signaling can arise through several different routes.

A deletion also has consequences beyond the single locus used as a marker. If a chromosomal region carries a tumor-suppressive gene alongside genes required for normal metabolism, the deletion can reduce the dosage of all of them. The net effect on a lineage is the combined result, which may include harmful as well as beneficial changes. A recurrently deleted interval can therefore contain a likely driver locus and several neighboring genes removed incidentally. Comparing many independent deletions with different endpoints can help identify a minimal shared region, but the smallest common region is still a hypothesis about functional relevance. Evidence from gene expression and cellular behavior can strengthen or challenge that hypothesis.

Copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity is especially useful for understanding why chromosome copy measurements and allelic measurements complement each other. If the original cell carries a functional allele F and an altered allele f, loss of F followed by duplication of f can produce f/f without a reduction in the number of copies. The cell now has two physical copies but a changed functional composition. In a different lineage, duplication of F after loss of f could remove the altered allele instead. The same category of copy-neutral rearrangement can thus have opposite biological consequences depending on which allele survives. The direction of functional change cannot be inferred from copy number alone.

6. Chromosome rearrangements and their molecular consequences

A chromosome is an organized DNA molecule associated with proteins, with a centromere and chromosome ends that have specialized functions. A chromosome rearrangement changes the order, orientation or linkage of segments. A reciprocal translocation exchanges segments between nonhomologous chromosomes. A simplified representation begins with A–B and C–D and produces A–D and C–B after breaks and exchange. If no DNA is detectably lost or gained, the exchange may be described as balanced at the level of the assay. Balanced does not mean biologically neutral. A breakpoint can interrupt a gene, relocate a regulatory element or join portions of different genes.

An inversion reverses the orientation of a chromosome segment. Two breaks define a segment, which becomes rejoined in the opposite orientation. An inversion that includes the centromere is pericentric; one that excludes it is paracentric. These classifications are important for chromosome organization, but the cancer-relevant question is the effect of the new breakpoints and junctions. Inversions can disrupt genes or change the regulatory context without altering total DNA dosage. Some can generate a fusion when genes on the original chromosome are brought into a compatible orientation. It is not necessary to introduce a change in whole-chromosome number for a rearrangement to affect cellular behavior.

Gene fusion and gene rearrangement should not be used interchangeably. A rearrangement is a structural DNA event. A gene fusion is the joining of portions of genes into a new genetic arrangement that may produce a hybrid RNA or protein. Many rearrangements occur in regions that cannot create an expressed coding fusion. The two genes might face incompatible directions; the necessary promoter might be absent; the RNA may be unstable; the coding junction may disrupt the reading frame. In other cases a rearrangement activates a normal coding gene by placing it near a new regulatory element, with no hybrid protein. The functional categories are defined by the product and its expression, not just by the proximity of two labels on a chromosome image.

Imagine a gene X controlled by a weak promoter in a progenitor and a nearby gene Y with a strong regulatory region active in that lineage. If a rearrangement places the normal coding sequence of X under the influence of Y's regulatory element, the amino acid sequence of X can remain unchanged even while its abundance changes substantially. By contrast, a fusion that joins a regulatory domain from X to a catalytic domain from Y can create a protein with altered molecular control. The distinction is crucial because the first case is best tested by studying expression of the original protein, whereas the second requires evidence of a novel product and its activity.

Chromosome rearrangements can occur during physiological processes as well as in disease. Developing lymphocytes deliberately rearrange parts of antigen-receptor genes to generate immune diversity. The regulated developmental process is not itself cancer. Errors in DNA joining or unusual genomic juxtapositions can, however, have different consequences from the intended recombination products. This is a useful connection to Unit 4.C: the same general need to join DNA ends can occur in a normal developmental program and in a pathological event, but the biological interpretation depends on the locus, product, regulation and lineage. The details of antigen-receptor diversity remain the responsibility of the immunology chapters.

Chromosome topology, orientation and contrasting rearrangement examples

The representation of a chromosome as a row of labeled segments is a useful abstraction if its conventions are understood. A labeled block can indicate a physical interval and an arrow can indicate the direction of transcription through it. Reversing a segment changes its orientation relative to the flanking sequences. When a diagram lists A–B–C–D and gives A–C–B–D as the product of inversion, the orientations of B and C should also be reversed; otherwise the rearrangement has not been described completely. A chromosome diagram that omits arrows can identify order but cannot establish the compatibility of a proposed fusion. This is particularly important when two genes are transcribed from opposite DNA strands.

An expressed chimeric gene requires more than the new physical adjacency of two genomic intervals. A transcriptional start region must direct RNA synthesis through the relevant sequences in a compatible orientation, and processing must produce an RNA that can be translated into the proposed product. DNA breakpoints often lie within introns rather than precisely at the coding junction of the final RNA. RNA splicing can then join exons across the new genomic boundary. A breakpoint diagram and a mature transcript diagram therefore use different coordinates. The junction seen in the RNA is not necessarily the exact point at which the DNA was originally broken and rejoined. This connection to RNA processing is a frequent source of confusion in questions about cancer-associated fusions.

A reciprocal translocation can retain approximately the same DNA amount while changing the regulatory neighborhood of a gene. Burkitt lymphoma provides a representative example: studies in the early 1980s identified the involvement of MYC and immunoglobulin loci in characteristic rearrangements [16]. Regulatory elements normally active in B-cell development can influence the rearranged MYC locus. Unlike the classic BCR–ABL1 example, the central conceptual consequence is deregulated expression of a growth-regulatory gene, not an obligatory hybrid kinase. These examples are especially useful side by side because they show that the same broad structural category of rearrangement can yield different molecular outcomes. The significance of the event depends on the genes and the configuration produced.

Inversions can also create fusions, which demonstrates that joining genes does not require exchange between different chromosomes. A report by Soda and colleagues in 2007 identified the EML4–ALK fusion associated with an inversion within the short arm of chromosome 2 in a subset of non-small-cell lung cancers [17]. The original genes occupy positions on the same chromosome. The altered orientation and junction permit expression of a fusion product with kinase-related transforming activity. For the present chapter this example contrasts with the interchromosomal exchange producing BCR–ABL1. It is not necessary to memorize all fusion isoforms or detailed treatment regimens. The useful distinction is intrachromosomal versus interchromosomal rearrangement, followed by the common question of product identity and activity.

An unbalanced rearrangement may yield loss of one region and gain of another rather than a simple equal exchange. When analyzing a schematic, count the occurrences of each segment before and after the event instead of assuming the total chromosome number establishes balance. A cell can have the ordinary number of chromosomes but carry a segmental duplication and deletion. Conversely, changes in whole-chromosome number can occur without a new fusion junction. The structural categories overlap in real samples: a tumor may carry a fusion on one derivative chromosome together with later gains or losses of other chromosomes. Classify each observed change according to its structural consequence before attempting to infer its contribution to cancer.

Finally, a rearrangement can be biologically consequential even if it destroys a gene rather than creating a new product. A break within a coding region or separation from a necessary promoter may reduce expression or function. Such a rearrangement can contribute to loss of a growth restraint rather than increased signaling. The label translocation says nothing about whether the result is gain or loss of function. An examination question may specify a breakpoint within one allele of a gene that normally limits growth; the correct prediction depends on whether the remaining allele supplies enough functional product. This links rearrangement biology to the tumor-suppressor chapter without prematurely treating all chromosome exchange as oncogene activation.

7. The Philadelphia chromosome as a causal model

The history of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) offers a particularly clear example of how a visible chromosomal abnormality can lead to a molecular explanation of cancer. Nowell and Hungerford reported an unusually small chromosome in leukemic cells in 1960 [3]. At the time, the observation established a recurring cytogenetic feature rather than its exact molecular origin. With improved chromosome identification, Janet Rowley reported in 1973 that the abnormality arose through exchange involving chromosomes 9 and 22 [4]. This transformed the interpretation from an apparently shortened chromosome to a specific rearrangement. The discovery mattered because it offered a reproducible physical clue that could be followed to the genes affected by the exchange.

The classical notation t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) [8] identifies a translocation involving the long arms of chromosomes 9 and 22. The derivative chromosome 22 is called the Philadelphia chromosome. The ABL1 locus is normally on chromosome 9, and BCR is normally on chromosome 22. In the characteristic rearrangement, portions of BCR and ABL1 become joined on the derivative 22, forming BCR::ABL1. The fusion gene is distinct from the derivative chromosome that carries it. It is also distinct from the encoded protein. Those three levels correspond to different measurements: chromosome structure, DNA or RNA sequence, and molecular function.

Not every translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 can automatically be assumed to generate the same transcript. Breakpoint positions vary within genes, and transcript processing determines the final coding junction. In typical CML, the major BCR–ABL1 protein is commonly designated p210 according to its approximate molecular mass in kilodaltons. Other fusion forms occur in leukemia and the fusion is not exclusive to CML. The identity of a particular disease therefore requires biological and diagnostic context beyond the mere presence of an ABL1-related signal. At the NET level, the important relationship is the recurrent connection between the rearrangement, a characteristic fusion product and altered kinase regulation.

ABL1 is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase. Its normal activity is controlled through intramolecular interactions and regulated conformational changes. The BCR portion of the fusion contributes an oligomerization domain; joining the BCR and ABL1 portions changes the usual regulatory arrangement. Association of fusion molecules and altered autoinhibitory constraints contribute to persistent kinase activity. The catalytic domain uses ATP to transfer phosphate to tyrosine residues of suitable protein substrates. The fusion is therefore not an energy-independent growth signal. It still requires substrate molecules and ATP, but its regulation no longer follows the normal pattern of ABL1 activity.

Phosphorylated proteins can provide binding sites for signaling partners and change the activity of downstream pathways. In hematopoietic cells, BCR–ABL1 activity influences networks controlling proliferation and survival, including pathways previously studied in the cell-signaling subunit. Persistent input shifts the balance of phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, and cells can behave abnormally even when the normal initiating extracellular signals are absent or reduced. The fusion does not replace all signaling mechanisms. Instead, it alters the input to a network whose components still operate according to biochemical rules. The response therefore depends on the cell type and the state of the downstream network.

A molecular cause must be distinguished from a diagnostic association. The recurrent Philadelphia chromosome was first a correlation with leukemia. The identification of the hybrid gene and p210 protein established a more specific molecular connection. Ben-Neriah and colleagues reported the relationship between the hybrid gene and the characteristic protein in 1986 [5]. Daley, Van Etten and Baltimore reported in 1990 that the p210 fusion could induce a leukemia-like disease in mice [6]. The animal findings support a causal role in an appropriate biological context; they do not imply that a mouse model reproduces every feature of human leukemia. These observations together illustrate why evidence from chromosomes, molecular identity and functional models is more persuasive than any one of them alone.

Inhibiting the kinase activity of BCR–ABL1 provides a further type of evidence: the altered catalytic activity is a biologically meaningful dependency of many leukemia cells. The general success of targeted kinase inhibition illustrates why identifying a genetic cause can guide molecular therapy. The DNA rearrangement may still be present when phosphorylation falls, so blocking a protein is not the same as undoing the chromosome rearrangement. A surviving cell may retain the fusion even when its current proliferation is suppressed. Detailed therapeutic mechanisms belong to 4.E.12. Here the principle is that molecular activity, genotype and the abundance of a lineage represent distinct levels of explanation.

From a fusion transcript to the altered signaling state

The distinction between a gene and a protein domain becomes particularly useful for understanding BCR–ABL1. A domain is a region of a protein with a characteristic structural or functional contribution. The name of a protein may refer to its original gene, but many proteins contain several domains that participate in different processes. A fusion can bring a domain that controls association of molecules into the same polypeptide as a domain that catalyzes a reaction. The BCR contribution to the common fusion promotes association between fusion molecules, whereas the ABL1 contribution supplies tyrosine kinase activity. These domains do not acquire entirely new chemical properties merely by being joined. The rearrangement changes how their activities are organized and regulated within the same protein. This is a useful structural explanation of why the same catalytic reaction can become abnormal through a change in regulatory context.

Ordinary phosphorylation-dependent signaling is dynamic. A kinase adds phosphate to appropriate substrates, and phosphatases remove phosphate. In a simplified model with a fixed total pool of substrate, let x be the fraction phosphorylated, k₁ the effective first-order phosphorylation constant and k₂ the dephosphorylation constant. The rate equation is dx/dt = k₁(1−x) − k₂x. At steady state, x = k₁/(k₁+k₂). An increase in k₁ can raise the phosphorylated fraction even if phosphatase activity remains unchanged. The equation is not a complete model of the BCR–ABL1 signaling network, which has multiple substrates and feedback loops. It nevertheless explains why persistent kinase input can shift the biochemical state of a cell without requiring every downstream component to be genetically altered. It also predicts that the fraction is bounded between zero and one and that continued dephosphorylation prevents unlimited accumulation of phosphate on the fixed pool.

The formation of the fusion changes regulation, not the fundamental chemistry of phosphoryl transfer. An active kinase binds ATP and a compatible protein substrate and facilitates transfer of ATP's terminal phosphate group to an appropriate hydroxyl group on the substrate. The product can alter molecular interactions, localization or downstream enzyme activity. ATP consumption and opposing phosphatase reactions make the signaling network a nonequilibrium system sustained by metabolism. If the kinase is inhibited, pre-existing phosphorylation need not disappear immediately; its decline depends on opposing reactions and the time scale of the network. Distinguishing the initiation of activity from the persistence of its downstream products is important when interpreting a graph that records signaling after a perturbation. A molecular inhibitor can decrease signaling long before a change in the number of cells is visible.

Hematopoietic progenitors normally integrate signals that govern proliferation, differentiation and survival. A persistent kinase signal can affect several of these processes simultaneously, but each phenotype needs to be distinguished experimentally. For instance, an increased number of viable progenitors after a fixed interval could result from reduced loss even if the rate of division is unchanged. An increase in differentiated descendants could reflect altered progenitor output rather than indefinite self-renewal. The fusion's effect is therefore assessed in relation to defined phenotypes and appropriate comparison populations. The evidence from a leukemia-associated fusion is especially instructive because it connects a precise structural event to a defined biochemical activity without claiming that the same event produces an identical disease in every possible cell background.

The discovery sequence also illustrates how scientific interpretations become more specific. The early cytogenetic observation was not yet evidence of a particular enzyme activity. Chromosome banding assigned the exchanged regions, while molecular characterization identified a hybrid gene product. Functional studies in animal models then investigated whether the altered product could contribute causally to disease-like behavior. Studies of kinase regulation subsequently clarified the importance of the BCR oligomerization region and the loss of usual ABL1 constraints. The 2024 review of ABL kinases [9] integrates this mechanistic evidence. The older studies are useful for following the changing evidence, from chromosome structure to the gene product and its biological effects. The history is valuable because it demonstrates the progression from association to molecular explanation and then to tests of biological dependence.

8. Genomic instability and the changing chromosome population

Genome instability refers to an elevated tendency to acquire genomic changes. Chromosomal instability is a more specific dynamic property involving ongoing alterations of chromosome number or structure. Aneuploidy, by contrast, describes an abnormal number of individual chromosomes or chromosome segments in a cell. A stable trisomic lineage can remain aneuploid over many divisions without exhibiting a high ongoing rate of chromosome-number change. Conversely, a population with extensive chromosome missegregation can contain cells whose current chromosome counts happen to be close to diploid. The distinction between a state and a rate prevents erroneous reasoning from a single karyotype.

Normal mitosis depends on correct attachments of chromosomes to the spindle and proper separation of sister chromatids. Failures in attachment correction or segregation can produce daughters with unequal chromosome complements. A lagging chromosome can become enclosed in a micronucleus, a small compartment separate from the primary daughter nucleus. DNA in such compartments may experience replication and structural problems. These observations connect cell division to genome structure, but they do not imply every missegregation event creates a viable cancer clone. Many chromosome imbalances harm cells or cause cell-cycle arrest. The variants observed in an established tumor are a selected subset of those that arose.

Structural instability can generate deletions, duplications, inversions and translocations without any net change in chromosome count. Sequence instability can likewise increase nucleotide substitutions or short insertions and deletions while leaving the karyotype apparently normal. Mismatch repair deficiency is associated with instability of short repeated sequences, because replication slippage at such sites can change repeat length. Microsatellite instability is therefore not synonymous with chromosomal instability. A question may describe one tumor with numerous changes in repeat length and another with heterogeneous chromosome numbers; these observations support different forms of genome alteration.

Selection can favor an unstable genome indirectly when a lineage acquires advantageous changes from the increased variation. Yet instability has costs: loss of essential DNA, disturbed stoichiometry of protein complexes, impaired replication and cell death. The relationship between instability and tumor growth is therefore not necessarily monotonic. Excessive instability can be harmful even to a lineage whose earlier changes allowed proliferation. This makes a distinction between mutation supply and population expansion necessary. A process that produces many new genotypes can shrink the population if most of its consequences are damaging.

Changes in telomere integrity illustrate the relationship between chromosome-end structure and stability. Functional telomeres protect natural chromosome ends from inappropriate recognition as double-strand breaks. If end protection fails, chromosome ends may participate in abnormal joining events, potentially generating unstable structures. Nevertheless, the cell's damage response may arrest or eliminate such cells. A tumor lineage that survives these stresses can carry structural alterations, but no single telomere outcome is inevitable. The later chapter on senescence and survival will explain the role of replicative limits; the present concern is how chromosome-end integrity contributes to faithful inheritance.

The consequences of chromosome imbalance and error propagation

A chromosome gain changes the dosage of a coordinated collection of genes rather than simply adding another copy of one desired product. Suppose two subunits of a protein complex are encoded on different chromosomes and are normally produced in similar amounts. Gain of the chromosome carrying one subunit may increase its production without increasing the other subunit. Assembly of the complete complex can remain limited by the less abundant partner, leaving some excess molecules unused. Turnover of excess proteins consumes cellular resources. This example illustrates why whole-chromosome gains can impose physiological burdens and why increased dosage of many genes is not automatically advantageous. A particular chromosome gain can nevertheless be retained when its net effects favor the lineage under its current conditions.

Whole-genome doubling is conceptually distinct from gain of one chromosome. Doubling initially increases many copy numbers together and may preserve some relative dosage relationships better than an isolated gain. Subsequent chromosome losses can create a highly uneven genome. When comparing such a population with a diploid reference, one must distinguish absolute copy number from copy number relative to the tumor's background genome content. A chromosome complement observed at one point also does not reveal the sequence of events that produced it. Several different histories can result in similar present-day counts. This is another reason why a karyotype, while extremely informative, is not a complete record of cancer evolution.

The spindle assembly checkpoint normally delays anaphase until chromosome attachments are appropriately established. This mechanism reduces some segregation errors but is not a guarantee that every chromosome will be correctly distributed. Some erroneous attachments can persist despite the checkpoint, and failures in chromosome cohesion or centrosome organization can create further problems. Cells receiving an abnormal complement may stop dividing, die or continue proliferating depending on their remaining genetic and physiological capacities. A tumor with a damaged response to chromosome imbalance can therefore retain variants that normal tissues would eliminate. The detailed molecular checkpoint circuitry belongs to 4.E.05; the relevant point here is the separation between the production of chromosome errors and the transmission of their consequences.

Measurements of chromosome instability can examine changes across cells or across successive divisions. Heterogeneity in a single population is compatible with instability but is not, by itself, a direct measurement of the current error rate. The population could contain stable branches with distinct chromosome complements acquired long ago. Conversely, a rapidly changing population may appear relatively homogeneous if most new variants are quickly eliminated. An observational study that follows chromosome segregation or the emergence of new karyotypes over time addresses a different question from an assay of current DNA content. This distinction is emphasized in the review on chromosomal instability and aneuploidy [10]. It is a particularly useful example of the distinction between measuring a rate and measuring the accumulated outcome of a process.

9. Drivers, passengers and multistep clonal evolution

A driver alteration contributes to the selective success of a cell lineage in a particular biological context. It may increase division, reduce death, facilitate retention of proliferative descendants or enable survival under an otherwise restrictive condition. A passenger alteration does not contribute a relevant selective advantage in that context. The distinction concerns phenotype and population consequences, not the size of the alteration. A single nucleotide change may be a powerful driver, while a large rearrangement can be incidental. A driver may even be disadvantageous in a different tissue or when a different constraint becomes limiting.

Nowell's 1976 account of clonal evolution [7] provided an important conceptual framework for tumor heterogeneity. Heritable variation arises within a population of related cells, and differences in survival and reproduction change the relative abundance of those cells. The same principles that govern selection in populations of organisms can operate among somatic lineages. There is no need to assume that mutations arise because a cell needs them. Mutation generates variants; selection acts on the consequences of variants under particular conditions. The frequent observation of a driver may therefore reflect its beneficial effect in the tumor rather than an unusually high probability of generating that exact event.

Suppose a progenitor acquires alteration A and leaves a persistent group of descendants. A descendant then acquires B, while a different descendant acquires C. The two subclones inherit A but may differ in their additional changes. If B improves net population growth and C does not, the A+B branch may become more abundant. The A+C branch need not disappear immediately, particularly if it occupies a different location or has another advantage. Tumor evolution can consequently resemble a branching tree rather than a ladder with one universal sequence of events. The trunk contains changes shared by the sampled branches; the branches contain changes acquired in particular lineages.

The presence of a variant in all sampled tumor cells suggests it occurred before their most recent common ancestor or became fixed before the surviving lineages diverged. It does not prove that the variant was the first change in the original normal cell. Earlier variants may have been lost by deletion, or sampling may exclude lineages that lack the variant. A subclonal variant is also not necessarily late in an absolute chronological sense: a small branch may have persisted alongside a larger branch for a long time. Phylogenetic reconstruction depends on assumptions about mutation recurrence, copy-number changes and the representativeness of the samples.

Neutral variants can become common through genetic hitchhiking. When a cell gains a selective advantage, all genetic changes already carried by that cell can be inherited by the expanding descendants. A passenger tightly associated with the successful lineage rises in frequency without causing the expansion. Neutral variants can also change in frequency through chance, especially during small population bottlenecks. These mechanisms explain why recurrence across independent tumors and biological functional evidence are more informative than abundance in just one tumor. Even recurrence must be interpreted against background mutation rates: some genomic sites mutate frequently for reasons unrelated to selection.

A change that confers an advantage relative to neighboring clones may still be insufficient for malignancy. In a tissue, a clone can expand locally while remaining constrained by differentiation, spatial boundaries, immune interactions and limited resources. Additional events may be required for progression, invasion and sustained survival. Different tumors may solve similar biological problems using different genes. The 2013 cancer-genome review by Vogelstein and colleagues [2] provides an influential overview of the distinction between driver and passenger changes and the importance of recurrently altered pathways. Its estimates should not be memorized as a universal fixed number of required mutations.

Cancer evolution is sometimes described in terms of mutation order. An alteration that increases proliferation may expand the number of cells in which another event can occur. A defect in genome maintenance can increase the chance of later changes, while disruption of a checkpoint may alter survival after damage. The consequences are not interchangeable. In one lineage, an early growth advantage enlarges the population available for subsequent variation; in another, genome-maintenance failure changes the variation rate even before a measurable growth advantage appears. These pathways can interact, but neither supplies a predetermined itinerary for cancer progression.

Interpreting selection, competition and ancestry

Two alterations can interact because one changes the conditions under which the other has an effect. A mutation increasing a growth-promoting signal may have limited influence if an intact restraint blocks cell-cycle progression whenever that signal becomes excessive. A later alteration reducing the restraint can expose a larger effect of the earlier change. This dependence of one genetic effect on the background genotype is a form of epistasis. It means that selective advantages of alterations cannot always be added independently. The order in which two alterations become established can influence the observed sequence of clonal expansion even if the final genotype is the same. A mutation with a weak effect alone may be a meaningful contributor in a cooperating genetic background.

Tumor heterogeneity can also reflect selection on cellular properties other than division rate. In a compartment where cells frequently differentiate and leave, a change in lineage retention may improve the representation of a clone without changing the time taken for each individual division. In a region with increased physiological stress, a change that reduces the rate of loss can be more important than one increasing proliferation. When two branches compete under conditions that vary across space, neither branch necessarily has the higher fitness everywhere. The result can be coexistence rather than immediate replacement. Spatial differences and temporal changes are therefore biological reasons why a tumor does not always contain just one dominant genotype.

A branching tree is an inference about the order of ancestry, not a direct record of every historical cell division. For example, if three sampled regions carry changes A, A+B and A+B+C, a nested ancestry is compatible with the pattern. However, the observation does not establish that the sampled A-only cells are the actual individual ancestors of the sampled A+B cells. They may be surviving relatives of an earlier population. If one region has lost a chromosome carrying B, a descendant of A+B might also appear to carry only A. Thus a tree based on simple presence and absence becomes less certain when copy-number changes can erase evidence. The strongest inference combines multiple variants, genomic context and independent samples, while recognizing that extinct branches leave limited evidence.

The frequency of a mutation across independent tumors can provide a clue about selection, but it also depends on the underlying probability that the event is generated. A repeatedly altered base could be particularly susceptible to a mutational process, while a rare alteration could be a driver of a rare tumor subtype. Functional annotation must therefore distinguish a site where mutations arise often from a site where mutations produce a substantial selective effect. Cancer-genome studies such as the review by Vogelstein and colleagues [2] use patterns across many tumors alongside biological knowledge to identify candidate driver genes. A driver gene is a locus in which some changes contribute to cancer; this does not mean every observed variant within that locus has the same effect.

An evolutionary explanation of cancer does not imply that a tumor has intentions. Mutations do not appear because the clone anticipates a later requirement. Environmental change can alter which existing variants are favored, and additional variation can arise while selection is occurring. If a rare lineage is already present before a condition changes, its later enrichment can result from differential survival of that lineage. If an alteration is first detected afterward, it might have arisen later or been present below the earlier assay's detection limit. These alternatives matter when interpreting paired tumor samples. A conclusion that a new environment directly caused a particular mutation requires stronger evidence than the observation that the mutation is detected after the change.

10. Quantitative reasoning about altered cell populations

Population growth is easiest to understand when cell production and loss are considered separately. Let N denote the number of cells in a particular lineage, b the average per-cell production rate and d the average per-cell loss rate, both with units of inverse time. If these rates are constant and the population is large enough for a continuous approximation, the rate of change is dN/dt = (b − d)N. Writing r = b − d and integrating dN/N = r dt yields N(t) = N(0)e^(rt). The net growth rate is not the division rate. A lineage may proliferate rapidly yet show little expansion if losses are similarly rapid. The equation ignores spatial crowding, time-dependent conditions and fluctuations important in small clones.

When two clones have constant net rates rA and rB, their numbers after time t are NA(0)e^(rA t) and NB(0)e^(rB t). Dividing these expressions eliminates the common exponential factor and gives NA(t)/NB(t) = [NA(0)/NB(0)]e^((rA−rB)t). Thus relative abundance depends on the difference of net rates. If the rates are equal, the ratio remains constant in this deterministic model even when both populations grow. If one lineage has a higher net rate, its ratio increases. The faster relative clone may still decline in absolute number when both rates are negative. An increase in frequency is not by itself evidence of increasing total tumor burden.

Sequence data introduce a different measurement: the variant allele fraction, or VAF. It is the proportion of informative reads at a locus carrying a particular variant. In a simplified mixture where all cells are diploid and a heterozygous mutation occurs in a fraction f of all cells, one altered copy is present per mutated cell and two total copies per cell. The expected VAF is therefore f/2. This relationship does not hold unchanged when the variant has been amplified, the normal allele has been deleted, or the background ploidy differs. Nor is the observed read fraction exact: sampling variation and mapping biases can affect it. The formula is a model of the expected DNA representation.

A useful generalization considers a fraction p of tumor cells in a specimen, a fraction c of tumor cells carrying the mutation, m mutant copies per carrier cell, and a constant total tumor copy number C at the locus. If remaining cells are diploid and lack the mutation, the expected allele fraction is pcm/[pC+2(1−p)]. All four quantities except copy counts are dimensionless fractions; the denominator counts expected copies per sampled cell. If copy number differs between mutant and nonmutant tumor subclones, a weighted denominator is required. The equation shows why a low allele fraction can arise from low tumor purity, limited clonal representation or copy-number context. One number cannot identify all these parameters simultaneously.

A basic mutation-probability model is also informative. Suppose a defined alteration has an independent probability μ per relevant cell division, and D divisions occur. The probability that none produces the alteration is (1−μ)^D, and the probability that at least one does is 1−(1−μ)^D. When μ is small and Dμ is small, the latter is approximately Dμ; when Dμ is not small, the linear approximation becomes inappropriate. This is a model of occurrence, not of tumor formation. It does not account for whether an altered cell survives, divides again or encounters additional necessary events. Its educational use is to separate opportunity for variation from establishment of a successful lineage.

For copy-number data, a bulk sample containing proportion p of tumor cells with C copies of a locus and proportion 1−p of normal diploid cells has expected mean copy number pC+2(1−p). This expression follows from summing copies across the two populations and dividing by total cell count. An observed mean of four copies can arise from a pure population with four copies, a half-pure sample whose tumor cells have six copies, or other mixtures. Thus a fractional or integer bulk estimate is not an individual karyotype. Combining copy-number and allele-specific information helps constrain the possibilities, but the result remains conditional on the model assumptions.

Reading quantitative evidence with explicit denominators

In a finite sample, observed allele fractions fluctuate around their expected values. If reads were independent and unbiased and the expected mutant fraction were v, then the number of mutant reads among n informative reads would follow a binomial model with mean nv and variance nv(1−v). The standard deviation of the observed fraction would be the square root of v(1−v)/n. The result is dimensionless and decreases as the number of independent observations increases. Actual sequence reads may not be independent because of amplification, mapping and other technical effects, so the model is only an approximation. The principle nevertheless explains why a small difference in read fractions measured with few observations should not automatically be interpreted as a biological change.

Allele fraction can also change when the number of tumor cells is constant. Suppose a pure population carries a heterozygous mutation at a diploid locus. The expected allele fraction is one-half. If the wild-type allele is lost and the mutant allele is duplicated, the total locus copy number remains two but the expected allele fraction becomes one. The increase is due to allelic composition rather than an increase in the fraction of tumor cells. Alternatively, an increase in the number of normal cells included in a biopsy can reduce the observed fraction even if every tumor cell retains the mutation. These examples show why a comparison of two VAF values is insufficient to establish clonal expansion unless changes in sample composition and allele dosage are considered.

A useful distinction in population modeling is between a rate per unit time and a probability per division. The growth-rate model above uses parameters with units such as inverse days. The mutation probability model uses a dimensionless probability per defined division. A rapidly dividing population may experience more divisions in a fixed period than a slowly dividing population even if their probabilities of mutation per division are equal. However, increased cell production can be accompanied by increased loss, so the population observed at the end of the period may not reflect the number of divisions that actually occurred. Examination problems should state whether the number of divisions, the time interval or the final number of cells is supplied. Using final population size as a substitute for cumulative divisions can lead to incorrect conclusions.

Consider also a sample in which the tumor cell fraction p is known but the mutation is present only in a subclone. If all tumor cells have two copies of the locus and the mutant cells carry one altered copy, the expression for expected allele fraction simplifies to pc/2. Rearranging gives c = 2v/p for p greater than zero. If the calculation returns a value larger than one, the assumed diploid single-copy model is incompatible with the observations, subject to measurement uncertainty. This does not mean that a biological subclone can occupy more than the entire tumor. It means that some assumption about copy number, sample purity, mutant dosage or measurement needs to be reconsidered. This type of consistency check is more useful than treating an equation as an automatic answer generator.

Finally, simple models are valuable when they reveal a mechanism of interpretation. The proportional dosage model tests the relation between copies and expression; the net growth model tests the difference between production and loss; the relative frequency model tests competition; the allele-fraction model tests the composition of a mixed sample. Each uses different variables and assumptions. None provides a direct estimate of an individual's cancer risk. Students should identify the biological quantity being measured and the conditions under which the mathematical relation holds before inserting values. A result is meaningful only when it is interpreted in the context of the relevant cellular or molecular process.

11. How genetic evidence is interpreted

Chromosome visualization, DNA analysis, RNA analysis and protein measurements address different layers of a causal model. Karyotyping displays relatively large changes in chromosome structure and number. It can reveal a reciprocal translocation without specifying the precise nucleotide junction. A normal-looking karyotype does not rule out a point mutation or a rearrangement below its resolution. Conversely, a visibly abnormal chromosome does not identify the gene product responsible for a growth phenotype. The distinction between the spatial resolution of an assay and the biological importance of an event is essential.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization uses labeled nucleic-acid probes to identify selected chromosomal regions. Probes can be chosen to show whether normally separated sequences are near each other, or whether neighboring probes have become separated. Such observations help identify rearrangements involving a particular locus. Probe proximity is not the same as proof of an in-frame, expressed fusion transcript. The signal pattern may reflect a structural relationship even if the rearranged sequence is not transcribed. Signal counts are also affected by cell ploidy and the design of the assay. A conceptual FISH diagram must therefore be interpreted according to the probe positions specified in the question.

A DNA-level analysis may identify a junction between segments, while RNA analysis can show whether a chimeric transcript is expressed. Protein analysis can establish the presence of the corresponding product or provide evidence of altered phosphorylation. These observations form a chain, but each has its own alternative interpretations. A fusion RNA may not yield a stable protein; elevated phosphorylation can be produced by more than one upstream kinase; a stable protein may be inactive. A convincing mechanism requires evidence that connects the relevant levels rather than assuming a DNA label completely determines cell behavior.

Functional reasoning asks whether a genetic alteration is necessary or sufficient for a defined phenotype. If a model cell with a candidate alteration proliferates under a condition in which the corresponding comparison cell does not, the result can support sufficiency for that particular behavior in that background. It does not establish sufficiency for all properties of malignancy. If selectively blocking the implicated activity reduces the phenotype, the result supports dependence, provided other effects such as general toxicity are considered. The definition of the measured outcome matters: a decline in total viable cell count can reflect reduced division, increased death or both. Different measurements are needed to distinguish these possibilities.

Matched comparisons and suitable controls are fundamental. A tumor sample and a constitutional sample differ in cell composition as well as in potential genetic alterations. An expression comparison across unrelated tumors can be affected by tissue identity or differentiation state. A functional study may also be influenced by differences in starting cell numbers or background genotypes. Controls narrow the range of explanations; they do not make every alternative impossible. An advanced examination question often supplies evidence at several levels and asks which conclusion is justified. The strongest answer typically respects the exact distinction between observation, inference and demonstrated functional dependence.

The Philadelphia example connects all these levels historically. The small chromosome was visible, its rearrangement was identified, a hybrid product was characterized, and its biological relevance was investigated in models. Other cancers do not necessarily offer such a singular recurrent event. Some arise through multiple different combinations of alterations that affect related pathways. The transferable lesson is to build a causal explanation from structural and functional evidence, while recognizing the difference between a useful biomarker and a driver of cellular behavior.

Distinguishing alternative explanations in genetic experiments

A frequent experimental challenge is to separate an effect of a candidate gene from effects of other changes located nearby. In an amplified genomic region, several genes may be present at increased dosage. An association between amplification of the region and a cellular phenotype does not identify a single causal gene. Evidence that one gene's RNA and protein levels are increased narrows the hypothesis but does not establish dependence. A functional comparison of the relevant protein's activity with the phenotype can help evaluate whether the altered product contributes to the effect. The interpretation should account for general changes in viability and differences in the genetic backgrounds of the cells. Such reasoning is applicable to many forms of cancer-associated copy-number change.

The distinction between a biomarker and a functional driver is important in molecular diagnosis. A biomarker is a measurable feature associated with a biological state or useful for identifying it. A genetic driver contributes to a phenotype through its effect on cells. A recurrent chromosome marker can be useful even before the mechanism of disease is fully understood, and a functional driver need not be an easily measured diagnostic marker. The Philadelphia chromosome illustrates a case in which a recurrent marker led to discovery of a biologically important fusion. But a marker found in an established tumor could instead be a neutral feature of the lineage. Evidence from prevalence, molecular function and dependence must be interpreted together rather than requiring all biomarkers to be causal.

The meaning of a negative result also depends on the assay. A negative test for a known fusion RNA may indicate that the specified transcript is absent, but it may also reflect a transcript variant not recognized by that particular test or an insufficient amount of informative material. A negative chromosome-level result can coexist with a sequence-level alteration that lies below the resolution of the method. A protein measurement can be negative because the product is absent or because it is not detected under the measurement conditions. Accordingly, examination questions should specify the target of the assay and its relevant limitations. No general conclusion that a sample has no cancer-associated alteration can be derived from a single negative test directed at one class of change.

The use of matched samples and independent measurement methods provides complementary evidence rather than a guarantee of correctness. For example, a recurrent DNA rearrangement may be identified in one sample and an expressed hybrid RNA detected in the same population. These observations strengthen the link between structure and expression. If downstream phosphorylation is also increased, the proposed kinase mechanism becomes more plausible, although another active kinase could contribute to the measured signal. A comparison involving selective reduction of the implicated activity can help assess dependence. Each step restricts alternative explanations, and the conclusion should be limited to the phenotype and conditions actually measured. This is the reasoning expected in analytical NET questions about molecular genetics and cell signaling.

12. From genome change to abnormal tissue behavior

A genetic change can have different consequences in distinct developmental lineages because the available transcription factors, proteins, extracellular signals and survival conditions are different. A fusion kinase that modifies hematopoietic growth signaling may have a different phenotype in an epithelial cell. Likewise, a mutation that affects a receptor may have little immediate consequence in a cell that does not express that receptor. The phenotype belongs to the interaction between genotype and cellular context. This principle connects cancer genetics to developmental biology, where cells with broadly similar genomes differentiate through different patterns of gene expression.

At the tissue level, the fitness of a clone depends on more than its intrinsic proliferation rate. A cell must occupy space, obtain nutrients and maintain viable interactions with surrounding cells. A mutation that permits a lineage to persist during one environmental condition may be neutral or costly under another. Selection can change when the tissue changes. A driver is thus not a permanently beneficial property independent of environment. This is the same conceptual distinction encountered in evolutionary biology, although the selectable units here are competing somatic lineages rather than organisms passing alleles to offspring.

Plants and animals both possess mechanisms for regulating division and repairing DNA. Both may develop abnormal proliferative growth, but their tissue organization and developmental constraints differ. Rigid plant cell walls and the different organization of plant tissues make direct transfer of animal invasion and metastasis concepts inappropriate. The present chapter uses human and other animal cancer examples because the syllabus places cancer in the context of oncogenes, tumor suppressors and abnormal cell–cell interactions. A comparison with microbial mutation and selection can clarify population principles, but bacterial clonal expansion is not animal cancer.

The major molecular categories can now be separated. A rearrangement can create an altered gene product or change gene expression; a sequence substitution can alter catalytic activity or regulation; a deletion can reduce gene function; amplification can increase the number of gene templates. Defects in genome maintenance can supply additional variation, while defects in growth control can change the ability of altered cells to persist. These effects can interact within one lineage. The next chapter will concentrate on the gain-of-function side of this framework: how normal growth-promoting genes become oncogenes and why a change in abundance or regulation can have consequences comparable to a change in molecular structure.

Choosing the appropriate level of explanation

Cancer genetics is an integrative subject because the same observation can be described at several biological levels. A rearrangement is a change in the organization of DNA; an expressed hybrid RNA is a transcript-level consequence; a persistently active kinase is a biochemical state; expansion of an altered lineage is a population-level result. A complete explanation connects these levels without assuming that one measurement can substitute for all others. This approach is especially important when examining a tumor that contains both genetically altered and normal cells. The population-level phenotype may reflect the combined behavior of several cell types, even though a particular genomic alteration resides in only one of them. Later chapters will extend this reasoning to tumor microenvironment and invasion.

A similar distinction helps when comparing cancer genetics with Mendelian inheritance. In Unit 8, pedigrees trace the transmission of alleles between generations of organisms. In a developing tumor, somatic genealogies trace the transmission of alleles through mitotic divisions. Both involve inheritance, but the units being followed differ. A variant that is dominant in its cellular effect does not automatically show a dominant pattern in a family pedigree, because the particular alteration may not be present in the germ line at all. A predisposition transmitted through a family may likewise require additional somatic events before a tumor forms. The terminology should always be tied to the level of inheritance under discussion.

The practical importance of these distinctions is not limited to naming rearrangements. Molecular characterization can help classify diseases that appear similar by tissue origin but differ in their underlying genetic changes. It can also identify shared signaling dependencies in tumors with different genetic configurations. However, similarity of a gene label alone is insufficient to establish identical biology. One alteration can remove a functional gene product, another can increase its activity, and a third can be neutral. A student should connect the specific alteration to the expected molecular effect before drawing conclusions about proliferation, survival or the relevance of a potential molecular target. This prepares the conceptual distinction between proto-oncogene activation and tumor-suppressor inactivation, which structures the next two chapters.

Worked analytical examples

All numerical datasets below are hypothetical teaching models, not clinical measurements.

1. Allele identity after a structural change

Given: A constitutional locus is F/f. In an altered lineage two total copies remain, both f.

Reasoning and calculation: Compare allele identity and physical copy number independently. Before: two copies, two allele types. After: two copies, one allele type. The total dosage ratio is 2/2=1.

Biological interpretation and limitations: Copy-neutral LOH is supported. The biological outcome depends on whether f is functional; the copy count alone cannot answer that.

2. Copy number in a mixed specimen

Given: A hypothetical biopsy contains 40% normal diploid cells and 60% tumor cells. Mean locus copy number is 4.4.

Reasoning and calculation: Use 4.4=0.6C+0.4×2. Thus 4.4−0.8=3.6=0.6C, giving C=6 copies per tumor cell.

Biological interpretation and limitations: The result assumes uniform tumor dosage and correct purity. It does not establish which amplified gene is a driver.

3. Subclone fraction from sequencing

Given: A sample has 75% diploid tumor cells and a tumor-restricted heterozygous variant VAF of 0.15.

Reasoning and calculation: With one mutant copy per carrier, v=pc/2. Rearrange to c=2×0.15/0.75=0.40.

Biological interpretation and limitations: The inferred clone comprises 40% of tumor cells, or 30% of all cells. The result assumes equal locus dosage and unbiased read sampling.

4. Net growth and absolute loss

Given: Clone X has b=0.12 day⁻¹ and d=0.08 day⁻¹; Y has b=0.10 day⁻¹ and d=0.02 day⁻¹. Both start at 100 cells.

Reasoning and calculation: rX=0.04/day; rY=0.08/day. At 25 days, X=100e¹≈272 cells and Y=100e²≈739 cells. The ratio Y:X=e≈2.718.

Biological interpretation and limitations: The faster expansion of Y is due to lower loss despite lower production. Constant rates, no crowding and a deterministic approximation are assumed.

5. Mutation opportunity versus established disease

Given: A hypothetical event has probability μ=0.001 per division; D=100 independent divisions.

Reasoning and calculation: P(at least one)=1−(1−μ)^D=1−0.999^100≈0.09521. The linear approximation μD=0.10 overestimates it slightly.

Biological interpretation and limitations: The result is about 9.52% for occurrence under the model. It says nothing about the viability of the altered cells or the probability of developing cancer.

6. Steady-state gene expression

Given: An amplified locus changes from two to six copies. RNA abundance rises from 100 to 200 molecules per cell.

Reasoning and calculation: For M=aC/k, (a/k)old=100/2=50 molecules per copy; (a/k)new=200/6≈33.33 molecules per copy. The ratio is 2/3.

Biological interpretation and limitations: The per-copy effective expression is lower despite increased total RNA. These observations do not separate transcription from degradation.

7. Opposing phosphorylation rates

Given: A fixed pool of 200 substrate molecules follows the simple k₁/k₂ model, with k₁=3 min⁻¹ and k₂=1 min⁻¹.

Reasoning and calculation: At steady state, fraction x=3/(3+1)=0.75. Expected phosphorylated pool=200×0.75=150 molecules; unphosphorylated pool=50.

Biological interpretation and limitations: The model assumes fixed total substrate and effective first-order rates. The result is not a measured BCR–ABL1 activity or a full network simulation.

8. Interpret a fusion-evidence chain

Given: Hypothetical sample P contains a rearrangement and hybrid RNA; comparison sample Q lacks both. P and Q have the same level of pathway phosphorylation.

Reasoning and calculation: The chromosome and RNA evidence supports a specific structural/transcript event in P. Comparable phosphorylation can arise through shared or alternative mechanisms. A functional comparison of the implicated activity is needed to support specific dependence.

Biological interpretation and limitations: Do not infer that P lacks an active fusion, that Q has the fusion, or that the rearrangement alone is sufficient for malignancy.

9. Uncertainty in read sampling

Given: An earlier sample has 50 independent informative reads and a hypothetical true VAF of 0.02.

Reasoning and calculation: P(no variant read)=0.98^50≈0.3642. P(at least one)=1−0.3642≈0.6358.

Biological interpretation and limitations: About 36.4% of such samples would miss the variant under the ideal model. Real read biases can change this; absence of detection is not proof of absence.

Part B conceptual practice

Original Academy practice questions.

1. Which feature makes a normal stem cell distinct from a transit-amplifying progenitor?

  1. Presence of DNA
  2. Sustained self-renewal
  3. Ability to transcribe RNA
  4. Presence of a plasma membrane
Answer and explanation

B. Sustained self-renewal distinguishes the developmental role; the other features are shared by both cell types.

2. A change confined to the descendants of one postzygotic cell is most directly described as what?

  1. Physiological transcription
  2. Obligately inherited germline variant
  3. Whole-genome doubling
  4. Somatic alteration
Answer and explanation

D. Its restricted lineage distribution fits somatic acquisition; the other terms do not follow from distribution.

3. What does a DNA lesion describe?

  1. A physical or chemical DNA abnormality
  2. A mandatory chromosome gain
  3. A protein modification
  4. An invariably inherited sequence change
Answer and explanation

A. A lesion may be repaired without mutation; it need not affect chromosome count or protein directly.

4. What is a transition substitution?

  1. Purine-to-pyrimidine only
  2. Loss of one chromosome
  3. Purine-to-purine or pyrimidine-to-pyrimidine
  4. Reversal of a DNA segment
Answer and explanation

C. A transition preserves base chemical class; a transversion changes class, and the other choices are structural changes.

5. A one-base deletion inside a coding region ordinarily causes which direct sequence consequence?

  1. A downstream reading-frame change
  2. A whole-chromosome loss
  3. An unchanged codon grouping
  4. An obligatory receptor amplification
Answer and explanation

A. The codon grouping changes because one is not a multiple of three; none of the chromosome outcomes is implied.

6. Which condition is copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity?

  1. A/B remains A/B
  2. A/B becomes A/A
  3. A/B becomes A/−
  4. A/B becomes A/B/B
Answer and explanation

B. Two identical copies replace two different alleles. Deletion changes dosage; gain and unchanged heterozygosity do not satisfy the definition.

7. What defines a reciprocal translocation?

  1. Addition of one nucleotide
  2. Duplication of a whole genome
  3. Reverse orientation within a single segment
  4. Exchange of segments between chromosomes
Answer and explanation

D. Reciprocal exchange is distinct from inversion, insertion and genome doubling.

8. Which inversion includes the centromere?

  1. Paracentric
  2. Reciprocal
  3. Pericentric
  4. Heterozygous
Answer and explanation

C. Pericentric inversions encompass the centromere; paracentric ones do not.

9. Which chromosome is the classical Philadelphia chromosome?

  1. Normal chromosome 9
  2. Derivative 21
  3. Derivative 22
  4. Normal chromosome 22
Answer and explanation

C. The derivative 22 carries the characteristic BCR::ABL1 fusion; it is not either normal homolog.

10. What molecular class is ABL1?

  1. Nonreceptor tyrosine kinase
  2. Histone methyltransferase
  3. DNA polymerase
  4. Transmembrane ion channel
Answer and explanation

A. ABL1 catalyzes protein tyrosine phosphorylation; the other proteins have distinct functions.

11. Which term describes an abnormal chromosome number at an observation time?

  1. Mutation probability per division
  2. Genetic drift
  3. Chromosomal instability rate
  4. Aneuploidy
Answer and explanation

D. Aneuploidy is a state, whereas CIN describes dynamic changes; the remaining terms concern other processes.

12. Which is the most appropriate description of a passenger variant?

  1. Any mutation in a large gene
  2. An alteration without a relevant selective advantage in its context
  3. An alteration always present at low frequency
  4. A mutation that is necessarily lethal
Answer and explanation

B. Passenger status depends on functional selection, not gene size, frequency or lethality.

13. What is the most direct effect of a gene-amplification event?

  1. An increase in genomic copies of a region
  2. A guaranteed proportional increase in active protein
  3. A mandatory change in coding sequence
  4. A requirement for a reciprocal translocation
Answer and explanation

A. Amplification changes DNA dosage. Expression and activity depend on regulation; sequence changes and translocation are not required.

14. Which statement about an expressed gene fusion is correct?

  1. It cannot involve RNA processing
  2. It always requires two nonhomologous chromosomes
  3. It can arise through intrachromosomal rearrangement
  4. It always increases whole-genome ploidy
Answer and explanation

C. An inversion can create a fusion, as exemplified by EML4–ALK. Gene fusions can involve splicing, and do not require ploidy change.

15. Which finding most directly indicates ongoing chromosome instability?

  1. A large cell nucleus alone
  2. Repeated changes in chromosome complement across divisions
  3. A single heterozygous base substitution
  4. One stable abnormal karyotype
Answer and explanation

B. Instability concerns a dynamic process; the other observations are static or measure a different class of variation.

16. Which property of a BCR–ABL1 fusion directly connects it to altered phosphorylation?

  1. DNA ligation activity
  2. Sodium transport
  3. RNA polymerization
  4. Deregulated tyrosine kinase activity
Answer and explanation

D. ABL1 supplies the catalytic tyrosine kinase domain. Neither DNA repair, ion movement nor transcription catalysis is its defining activity.

17. What is the main conceptual distinction between a driver mutation and a mutator defect?

  1. A mutator is always a passenger
  2. A driver must increase every mutation rate
  3. Both terms mean chromosome amplification
  4. A driver contributes to clonal advantage; a mutator increases the supply of variation and may act indirectly
Answer and explanation

D. A genome maintenance defect can contribute indirectly to cancer; the terms describe differing effects, and some alterations may have overlapping consequences.

18. Which is a plausible consequence of a rearrangement without a chimeric coding product?

  1. Conversion of DNA into protein
  2. Mandatory loss of both homologs
  3. Relocation of a gene next to a different regulatory element
  4. Absence of all transcription
Answer and explanation

C. Regulatory relocation can change expression without a fusion protein. The other outcomes are not intrinsic to rearrangement.

19. What is the most precise description of a fusion transcript assay?

  1. It directly measures the number of all chromosomes
  2. It detects an expressed RNA junction
  3. It proves malignant behavior in every cell
  4. It necessarily measures catalytic rate
Answer and explanation

B. RNA junction evidence concerns the transcript; protein activity, chromosome complement and behavior require other measurements.

20. Which distinction is central when interpreting a germline cancer predisposition?

  1. Inheritance of a risk-associated allele does not equal inheritance of an established malignant clone
  2. The inherited allele is always limited to a tumor
  3. A predisposition must be a translocation
  4. A germline variant is always a passenger
Answer and explanation

A. A constitutional variant can be present before tumor formation; the other statements confuse risk, distribution and function.

Part C analytical practice

Original Academy questions. Numerical values and comparative datasets are hypothetical. For each, identify the relevant assumptions before calculating or interpreting the evidence.

1. Two progenitor clones each start with 100 cells. A has a per-cell production rate of 0.15/day and loss rate of 0.12/day; B has rates 0.10/day and 0.02/day. After 20 days, which joint prediction is correct?

  1. B:A = e⁻¹ because A divides faster
  2. B:A = 1 because both populations lose cells
  3. B:A = e¹ ≈ 2.72; B's higher net rate, not higher production, explains enrichment
  4. B:A = 5/3 from production rates alone
Answer and explanation

C. Net rates are 0.03 and 0.08 per day. The ratio after 20 days is exp[(0.08−0.03)×20]=e. Production-only comparisons ignore losses; equal starting populations do not imply equal later populations.

2. A diploid specimen has 75% tumor cells. A heterozygous variant V is carried by every tumor cell; another heterozygous variant W has VAF 0.15. Neither occurs in normal cells. Which conclusion follows under equal dosage and unbiased reads?

  1. Expected VAF(V)=0.375; W is present in 40% of tumor cells
  2. Expected VAF(V)=0.75; W is present in 20%
  3. Expected VAF(V)=0.50; W is present in 30%
  4. Expected VAF(V)=0.375; W must be present in every tumor cell
Answer and explanation

A. V=0.75/2=0.375. W=pc/2, so c=2×0.15/0.75=0.40. The distractors confuse tumor purity, read fractions, and cell fractions.

3. Constitutional genotype is F/f. A pure tumor has two total copies at the locus, only f reads, and a smaller functional protein signal. Which interpretation explains all three observations without requiring an increased total dosage?

  1. Hemizygous deletion of F with no duplication
  2. Triplication of F
  3. Retention of F/f with a higher tumor purity
  4. Copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity retaining f; reduced function is consistent but not proved solely by genotype
Answer and explanation

D. Two copies and only f rule out a simple hemizygous deletion or unchanged heterozygosity. Retaining f twice can reduce function if f is defective; the lower protein signal supports this but does not by itself establish the precise activity defect.

4. A tissue sample at time 1 contains 1,000 cells, 10% clone X. At time 2 it has 200 cells, 30% clone X. Which combined observation is correct?

  1. X rose from 100 to 300 cells
  2. X fell from 100 to 60 cells; other cells fell from 900 to 140; X has relative enrichment despite absolute loss
  3. X remained 100 cells because frequency and count are independent
  4. X fell to 20 cells and others remained unchanged
Answer and explanation

B. Multiply fractions by total populations. X: 100→60. Other: 900→140. X lost 40% of its count, while others lost 84.4%; the relative representation of X rose without absolute expansion.

5. A rearrangement-positive sample contains an expressed RNA fusion and elevated pathway phosphorylation. A second sample lacking the fusion has similar phosphorylation. Both lose phosphorylation after a broad kinase inhibitor. Which claim is warranted?

  1. Both samples must carry the same rearrangement
  2. The fusion is necessarily inactive
  3. The inhibitor has reversed the DNA rearrangement
  4. The fusion RNA is demonstrated, but the shared phosphorylation response is insufficient to show fusion-specific dependence
Answer and explanation

D. The structural/transcript evidence is specific to the first sample. The pathway response is not, because an alternative kinase may act on the same substrates. None of the observations establishes restoration of DNA sequence or proves inactivity.

6. Three sampled branches carry {A,B}, {A,C} and {A,B,D}, respectively. Assume variants do not recur independently or get lost. Which ancestry statement best fits the pattern?

  1. A is shared; B is ancestral to the first and third sampled branches; D marks a descendant of the B lineage
  2. D precedes A; C is present in all branches
  3. All three arose from independent normal cells with unrelated A
  4. The first sampled branch must be the actual individual parent of the third sampled cells
Answer and explanation

A. A is common to all. B is shared only by branches 1 and 3, so it predates their divergence; D is restricted to branch 3. A sampled cell need not be the literal ancestor; surviving relatives can share its genotype.

7. A specimen contains 40% normal diploid cells. The remaining cells all carry six copies of a locus; half of those tumor cells have two mutant copies. Which pair is expected?

  1. Mean copy number 6; VAF 50%
  2. Mean copy number 4.4; VAF ≈ 13.6%
  3. Mean copy number 4.4; VAF ≈ 27.3%
  4. Mean copy number 3; VAF 20%
Answer and explanation

B. Mean copies=0.4×2+0.6×6=4.4. Mutant copies=0.6×0.5×2=0.6, so VAF=0.6/4.4≈0.1364. The denominator includes copies from both tumor and normal cells.

8. Two populations each show 47 chromosomes in 80% of sampled cells. Longitudinal tracking finds that population P retains its karyotypes, whereas Q generates multiple new counts but most new cells disappear. Which statement is supported?

  1. P and Q necessarily have equal CIN because their endpoints match
  2. P must have higher CIN because it retains the abnormal state
  3. Q has evidence of ongoing numerical CIN despite the similar endpoint distribution; selection can remove new variants
  4. Q cannot show CIN unless every new cell survives
Answer and explanation

C. Endpoint prevalence is shaped by the production and survival of variants. Longitudinal formation of new counts in Q supports ongoing instability even if selection eliminates many of them.

9. Two tumors show rearrangements. Tumor P retains the original protein sequence but expresses four times as much protein; tumor Q has a hybrid RNA and a protein joining two functional regions. Which pair of hypotheses best fits?

  1. P: regulatory relocation or altered expression; Q: expressed coding fusion
  2. P: obligatory hybrid protein; Q: whole-chromosome deletion only
  3. P: absent transcription; Q: no altered coding product
  4. Both must have identical kinase-domain rearrangements
Answer and explanation

A. P has evidence of altered amount rather than sequence, consistent with regulatory relocation, but expression alone does not identify the exact mechanism. Q has direct evidence of a hybrid product. Neither observation requires identical loci or pathways.

10. A defined event has probability 0.001 per division. Population P experiences 100 divisions and Q experiences 200. Assuming independence, which comparison is correct?

  1. Q probability is exactly twice P because the division count doubles
  2. P probability=0.001^100; Q=0.001^200
  3. Both probabilities equal 0.001 regardless of division count
  4. P(at least one)=1−0.999^100; Q=1−0.999^200, with Q greater but not exactly twice P
Answer and explanation

D. The complement of zero events is appropriate. P≈0.0952 and Q≈0.1814; the second is not exactly twice the first because probabilities saturate toward one. Neither is the probability of malignant progression.

11. A specimen is 60% tumor, with four copies at a locus in every tumor cell and one mutant copy per carrier. Observed VAF is 9.375%. What fraction of tumor cells carry the mutation in the stipulated model?

  1. 9.375%
  2. 25%
  3. 50%
  4. 100%
Answer and explanation

C. Total copies per sampled cell=0.6×4+0.4×2=3.2. Mutant copies=0.6c. Setting 0.6c/3.2=0.09375 gives c=0.5. The distractors use read fraction or disregard tumor dosage.

12. A tumor specimen has purity 0.8 and VAF 0.50. A single-copy heterozygous diploid model gives c=1.25. Which revised model is compatible with the observed VAF?

  1. One mutant copy in 100% of tumor cells with unchanged purity
  2. Diploid total locus dosage, two mutant copies in 62.5% of tumor cells
  3. One mutant copy in 50% of tumor cells
  4. One mutant copy in 80% of tumor cells
Answer and explanation

B. Under the two-mutant-copy model v=0.8×0.625×2/2=0.50. Single-copy alternatives give 0.40, 0.20, and 0.32. The initial impossible estimate identifies a violated assumption rather than an impossible biological fraction.

13. Copy number rises from two to six and steady-state RNA doubles. Protein abundance remains unchanged. In M=aC/k and P=sM/h, what pair of changes is required in the effective ratios?

  1. (a/k)new is 2/3 of old; (s/h)new is 1/2 of old
  2. Both ratios triple
  3. a/k doubles and s/h doubles
  4. a/k is unchanged and s/h is unchanged
Answer and explanation

A. RNA fold change 2 = copy fold change 3 × per-copy ratio fold change, giving 2/3. Protein fold change 1 = RNA fold change 2 × translation/turnover ratio fold change, giving 1/2. The data do not specify which individual rate constants changed.

14. Equal populations have net rates −0.01 and −0.03/day. After 50 days, which prediction follows?

  1. First:second = e⁻¹; both increase
  2. First:second = 3 because rate magnitudes determine abundance directly
  3. First:second = e; both have fewer cells than at the start
  4. Both reach the same count because both rates are negative
Answer and explanation

C. Their relative ratio is exp[(−0.01+0.03)×50]=e. Absolute counts are N₀e⁻⁰·⁵ and N₀e⁻¹·⁵. A relative advantage does not require positive net growth.

15. Two branches of a tumor share variant V. Branch 1 has two locus copies, one V. Branch 2 has two locus copies, both V after loss of the wild-type allele. A biopsy is pure tumor and equally represents both branches. Which VAF and inference are correct?

  1. 50%; only half of cells carry V
  2. 75%; every cell carries V even though the VAF is not 50%
  3. 100%; V must have occurred in every normal tissue
  4. 25%; branch 2 lost the variant
Answer and explanation

B. Each cell has two copies; average mutant copies=(1+2)/2=1.5, giving 1.5/2=0.75. All cells carry V, while allelic dosage differs by branch. Constitutional origin cannot be inferred.

16. A simplified chromosome A→B→C→D is inverted between A/B and C/D. A promoter outside A remains unchanged. Which inference is justified about a proposed B–C hybrid transcript?

  1. The new order is A→ C→ B→ D→ and any adjacency proves an expressed fusion
  2. The entire chromosome must be duplicated
  3. The inversion must have deleted B and C
  4. The new order is A→ C← B← D→; expression of a specific hybrid requires strand orientation and promoter/splicing evidence
Answer and explanation

D. The inversion reverses order and orientation of the included segments without obligatory dosage loss. Physical juxtaposition alone does not establish a transcribed and correctly processed coding product.

17. In a hypothetical study, fusion-positive and fusion-negative cells start with equal counts. After selective reduction of the candidate kinase activity, the fusion-positive group loses pathway phosphorylation and viable cells, but the comparison group is unchanged. What is the strongest conclusion?

  1. The fusion is sufficient for every feature of cancer
  2. The rearrangement has been excised
  3. The data support a selective dependence under the tested conditions, but do not establish sufficiency for malignancy or distinguish reduced division from increased death
  4. Only the proliferation rate can have changed
Answer and explanation

C. The matched response supports a functional dependency if specificity and other controls are adequate. A viability endpoint alone cannot separate birth and loss, and blocking activity does not reverse the genome change.

18. In a fixed-substrate model, an altered kinase raises k₁ from 1 to 3 min⁻¹; k₂ remains 1 min⁻¹. A separate condition also raises k₂ to 3 min⁻¹. Which steady-state fractions are predicted?

  1. Reference 0.50; altered kinase 3; both increased 1
  2. Reference 0.50; altered kinase 0.75; both increased 0.50
  3. Reference 0.25; altered kinase 0.75; both increased 0.75
  4. Reference 1; altered kinase 1; both increased 0
Answer and explanation

B. Use x=k₁/(k₁+k₂). Results are 1/2, 3/4 and 3/6. Equal proportional increases in both rates preserve the steady-state fraction even though reaction turnover may increase.

19. Five tumors have overlapping deletions affecting genes X and Y. All have reduced X RNA; only two have reduced Y RNA. A sixth tumor has normal X copy number but a loss-of-function X variant. Which inference is most appropriate?

  1. Y is excluded as a contributor in every tumor
  2. All six tumors share the same chromosome deletion
  3. Reduced X expression proves it is sufficient for malignancy
  4. X is a stronger candidate for a recurrent functional target, but the observations do not independently prove causal dependence
Answer and explanation

D. Independent alterations affecting the same gene strengthen its candidacy. The sixth tumor has a different DNA alteration, and Y could still contribute in a subset. A causal function requires additional evidence.

20. At time 1 a test detects no variant X among 50 informative reads. At time 2, X is detected in a minority lineage. If the earlier true VAF had been 2%, what does the ideal binomial model imply?

  1. Probability of zero earlier mutant reads is 0.98^50≈36.4%; pre-existence remains plausible
  2. Probability is zero, proving newly acquired X
  3. Probability is 0.02^50, so X could not have existed
  4. Probability is 98%, proving X was constitutional
Answer and explanation

A. Under independent unbiased sampling, zero mutant reads has probability (1−0.02)^50≈0.364. A negative small sample does not establish absence. It neither proves earlier presence nor determines constitutional origin.

Mixed practice

Original Academy practice questions.

1. Assertion: A balanced rearrangement can alter gene function. Reason: Breakpoints can create new gene junctions despite retained dosage.

  1. Assertion true, reason false
  2. Assertion false, reason true
  3. Both true but unrelated
  4. Both true and reason explains assertion
Answer and explanation

D. A new junction can change product or regulation without net DNA loss.

2. Match I deletion, II inversion, III amplification with P dosage increase, Q orientation reversal, R dosage decrease.

  1. I–R, II–Q, III–P
  2. I–Q, II–P, III–R
  3. I–P, II–R, III–Q
  4. I–R, II–P, III–Q
Answer and explanation

A. Deletion reduces dosage, inversion reverses orientation, amplification increases dosage.

3. Which pair is conceptually mismatched?

  1. RNA junction — transcript identity
  2. Copy-number assay — locus dosage
  3. Karyotype — catalytic activity
  4. Protein phosphorylation — signaling state
Answer and explanation

C. Karyotyping measures chromosome organization, not enzyme activity.

4. Assertion: A cancer-associated fusion kinase can be constitutive. Reason: It no longer requires ATP for phosphate transfer.

  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Both true and reason explains
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

B. Constitutive regulation is compatible with continued ATP dependence.

5. Assertion: DNA copy number can remain unchanged after loss of heterozygosity. Reason: One allele may be lost and the other duplicated.

  1. Both true but reason does not explain
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Both true and reason explains assertion
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

C. The replacement preserves total copy count while eliminating allelic diversity.

6. Match I BCR::ABL1, II EML4::ALK, III ERBB2 amplification with P dosage increase, Q intrachromosomal inversion, R classical t(9;22).

  1. I–R, II–P, III–Q
  2. I–P, II–R, III–Q
  3. I–Q, II–P, III–R
  4. I–R, II–Q, III–P
Answer and explanation

D. The examples illustrate three distinguishable mechanisms: interchromosomal fusion, inversion-derived fusion and DNA dosage gain.

7. Which two statements are correct? I. A normal renewing clone can expand. II. Every expanded clone is malignant. III. Drivers may depend on context. IV. All drivers have high VAF.

  1. II and IV
  2. I and III
  3. III and IV
  4. I and II
Answer and explanation

B. Normal clonal expansion occurs and driver effects depend on context. Clonality alone does not define cancer and VAF depends on sampling and dosage.

8. In the schematic A→B→C→D, inversion of B and C yields which order and orientation?

  1. A→, C←, B←, D→
  2. A→, C→, B→, D→
  3. D→, C→, B→, A→
  4. A→, B←, C←, D→
Answer and explanation

A. An inversion reverses both order and orientation of the included segment; outside flanks stay unchanged.

9. Which measurement cannot alone establish the historical cell of origin?

  1. A pre-event lineage label followed through descendants
  2. Stem-cell marker expression in an established tumor
  3. A temporally defined ancestry-tracing observation
  4. A documented lineage history
Answer and explanation

B. The tumor marker may have been acquired later; ancestry data directly address lineage origin, though still subject to assay limitations.

10. Assertion: A synonymous substitution can sometimes affect phenotype. Reason: The DNA and RNA sequence can also participate in regulation or splicing.

  1. Both false
  2. Both true but reason unrelated
  3. Both true and reason explains assertion
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

C. Identical encoded amino acids do not guarantee identical transcript processing or regulation.

11. Which set correctly identifies a state, a rate, and a proportion respectively?

  1. Aneuploidy; chromosome missegregation events per division; VAF
  2. Chromosome count; VAF; mutation events per division
  3. CIN rate; copy number; aneuploidy
  4. VAF; aneuploidy; chromosome count
Answer and explanation

A. Aneuploidy describes a state; events per division quantify instability; VAF is a fraction of informative reads.

12. A driver and a neutral variant lie in the same expanding lineage. What can explain enrichment of both?

  1. Both variants necessarily have equal catalytic activity
  2. Neutral variants cannot increase in frequency
  3. Both must cause chromosome instability
  4. Genetic hitchhiking of the neutral variant
Answer and explanation

D. The neutral allele can be inherited by descendants of the advantaged cell without providing its own selective advantage.

Examination traps and misconceptions

1. All genetic cancer is inherited

Why it seems plausible: The phrase genetic disease often refers to constitutional variants, so the confusion is understandable.

Correct interpretation: Somatic alterations also change DNA and are transmitted to daughter cells through mitosis.

Exam reasoning: In a tumor-normal comparison, ask whether the variant is present outside the tumor lineage before classifying its origin.

2. Every cancer begins in a normal stem cell

Why it seems plausible: Tumor self-renewal resembles normal stem-cell behavior.

Correct interpretation: A committed progenitor or more differentiated cell may acquire persistent proliferative behavior; present phenotype does not identify historical ancestry.

Exam reasoning: Distinguish pre-event lineage-tracing evidence from stem markers measured after transformation.

3. A lesion is already a mutation

Why it seems plausible: Both terms concern DNA abnormalities.

Correct interpretation: A lesion can be accurately repaired; a fixed sequence alteration persists through copying.

Exam reasoning: When a question specifies damage and repair, identify the actual inherited sequence before counting mutations.

4. Balanced translocation means harmless

Why it seems plausible: The absence of a detectable net gain or loss appears to preserve the genome.

Correct interpretation: New junctions can disrupt genes, create fusions or relocate regulatory elements.

Exam reasoning: Count DNA segments, then independently inspect the breakpoints and transcriptional consequences.

5. Every rearrangement creates a fusion protein

Why it seems plausible: Two genes close together on a chromosome diagram appear connected.

Correct interpretation: A compatible orientation, expression, RNA processing, reading frame and product stability are required for an expressed coding fusion.

Exam reasoning: Do not infer protein function from a karyotype or FISH pattern alone.

6. Philadelphia chromosome is chromosome 9

Why it seems plausible: ABL1, the kinase component, normally resides on chromosome 9.

Correct interpretation: The classical Philadelphia chromosome is derivative chromosome 22 carrying BCR::ABL1.

Exam reasoning: Distinguish the normal gene location from the derivative carrying the fusion.

7. BCR–ABL1 is a membrane receptor

Why it seems plausible: Many familiar growth-promoting kinases are receptor tyrosine kinases.

Correct interpretation: BCR–ABL1 is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase with altered intracellular regulation.

Exam reasoning: Identify protein class before predicting membrane topology or extracellular ligand dependence.

8. Constitutive activity requires no ATP

Why it seems plausible: Continuous signaling can be mistaken for spontaneous catalysis.

Correct interpretation: Constitutive refers to the regulatory state; phosphoryl transfer still uses ATP.

Exam reasoning: Keep energy requirements separate from dependence on an initiating signal.

9. Amplification gives proportional active protein

Why it seems plausible: An extra template can increase expression.

Correct interpretation: Transcription, RNA decay, translation, protein turnover and active-state occupancy can modify the response.

Exam reasoning: State constant-rate assumptions before applying a linear dosage model.

10. Loss of heterozygosity always means deletion

Why it seems plausible: Losing one allele sounds like losing DNA copies.

Correct interpretation: An allele can be replaced by another copy of the remaining allele without a net dosage change.

Exam reasoning: A copy-number assay cannot distinguish A/B and A/A when both have two copies.

11. Aneuploidy and CIN are identical

Why it seems plausible: Chromosome instability frequently produces abnormal chromosome complements.

Correct interpretation: Aneuploidy is a state; CIN refers to the ongoing generation of abnormal complements or structures.

Exam reasoning: A stable abnormal karyotype is not a direct estimate of the current segregation-error rate.

12. A frequent variant is necessarily a driver

Why it seems plausible: A successful clone contains many highly represented variants.

Correct interpretation: Neutral variants can hitchhike with an advantageous alteration or become common by drift.

Exam reasoning: Evaluate recurrence across independent tumors and functional evidence rather than relying on one VAF.

13. A driver guarantees malignancy

Why it seems plausible: The word driver suggests a complete explanation.

Correct interpretation: A driver contributes to selection in a particular context and may need cooperating events.

Exam reasoning: Separate sufficiency for a measured phenotype from sufficiency for all malignant properties.

14. Rising clone frequency means rising cell count

Why it seems plausible: Percentages are often read as absolute quantities.

Correct interpretation: A lineage can become relatively enriched while declining more slowly than its competitors.

Exam reasoning: Multiply the frequency by total cell count at both time points.

15. VAF directly measures tumor fraction

Why it seems plausible: The variant is associated with the tumor population.

Correct interpretation: VAF is a read fraction affected by purity, copy number and mutant allele multiplicity.

Exam reasoning: Specify the expected copy denominator before solving for the cell fraction.

16. A fusion transcript proves active protein

Why it seems plausible: RNA is an intermediate in gene expression.

Correct interpretation: Translation, stability and catalytic regulation intervene between RNA detection and activity.

Exam reasoning: Identify what the specific assay measures and what additional evidence would be required.

17. All tumors follow one mutation order

Why it seems plausible: Simplified multistep diagrams often look like a linear pathway.

Correct interpretation: Different branches and cooperating alterations can produce similar phenotypes through different histories.

Exam reasoning: Use supplied ancestry assumptions and do not turn one illustrative progression into a universal chronology.
