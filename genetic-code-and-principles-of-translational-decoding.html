1. From a nucleotide message to an amino-acid sequence

A mature messenger RNA is not a small protein waiting to be unfolded. It is a chemically different polymer. Its backbone contains ribose and phosphate, and its variable groups are the bases adenine, guanine, cytosine and uracil. A polypeptide, by contrast, is a succession of amino-acid residues joined through peptide bonds. The variable side chains of those residues determine important properties of the eventual protein. A mechanism that merely copies complementary nucleotides could reproduce a nucleic acid sequence, but it could not directly produce the chemically diverse side chains of a protein. Translation solves this problem by linking the information in one kind of polymer to the construction of another.

The distinction between transcription and translation is therefore fundamental. In transcription, the sequence relationship between the DNA template and newly synthesized RNA follows complementary base pairing. In translation, the relationship between an RNA sequence and the primary sequence of its product follows the genetic code. There is no universal chemical attraction that makes a free molecule of phenylalanine bind specifically to three uracils on messenger RNA. The cell establishes this relationship through an adaptor and the proteins that attach amino acids to those adaptors. Ribosomes organize the repeated use of the adaptors along the message. It is the coordinated system that implements the code, not the RNA sequence acting in isolation.

Consider the difference between the information required to describe a protein and the information required to produce it at the right time. The first problem involves specifying which residues are arranged in what order. The second involves determining whether a message is accessible, where translation starts, how rapidly elongation proceeds and whether the resulting product persists. The genetic code chiefly addresses the residue assignment problem. Many synonymous changes that preserve the encoded sequence can nevertheless influence production. A student should therefore avoid equating a preserved amino-acid sequence with a guarantee of identical expression or biological function.

The messenger is read in the direction from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end. The resulting polypeptide is synthesized from its amino terminus toward its carboxyl terminus. These two directions describe different chemical structures: a nucleic acid backbone and a chain of residues. They become related because the codon encountered first in the selected reading frame directs the first incorporated residue, and successive codons normally direct successive additions to the carboxyl end of the chain. A peptide sequence presented from the carboxyl terminus must first be reoriented before it is compared with a conventional messenger RNA coding sequence.

A particular sequence of bases may lie within a protein-coding region, an untranslated region or a noncoding RNA. Its location matters. The triplet AUG appearing in a transfer RNA sequence is not automatically an instruction to insert methionine. The same three letters occurring in the untranslated tail of an mRNA are not automatically decoded. The genetic code specifies a correspondence only when the translation system presents a triplet as a codon in a particular reading frame. This observation also explains why searching a genome for every occurrence of a codon associated with a stop signal does not reveal every protein boundary.

The correct product depends on more than the order of bases. If the message is read from the wrong position, perfectly normal nucleotide letters will direct an abnormal sequence of residues. If an adaptor is attached to the wrong amino acid, the correct RNA triplet may be recognized while the wrong residue is incorporated. If the release mechanism fails, a valid termination signal may not result in efficient release. These are distinct categories of decoding failure. Their separation is useful in designing experiments because different molecular defects can generate superficially similar decreases in functional protein.

2. Why the basic code is a triplet code

Before individual codon assignments were experimentally established, researchers could examine the arithmetic requirements of a coding system. Suppose a fixed-length word contains exactly k positions, and every position can independently contain one of four nucleotide types. The first position has four possible choices. For each of those choices, the second position again has four possibilities. Continuing this multiplication yields 4^k possible words. Repeated bases are allowed: AAA is a possible word just as ACG is. The order of the letters matters, so ACG and GCA are different words.

For single-base words, this calculation gives four alternatives, insufficient to distinguish twenty standard amino acids. For two-base words, there are sixteen alternatives, still insufficient under the assumption that each residue must have at least one distinct word. For three-base words, there are sixty-four alternatives. Triplets are thus the shortest fixed-length words that can accommodate twenty separate residue assignments using a four-letter alphabet. This reasoning does not itself establish that nature uses triplets. It establishes a constraint conditional on the model of equal-length words, direct residue assignment and unrestricted use of the four letters. An alternative hypothetical system with variable-length words or additional information would not obey the same requirement.

The experimental code is indeed organized around triplets. A codon consists of three consecutive nucleotides of a message in its chosen reading frame. In the standard code, sixty-one triplets are sense codons and three are conventional stop codons. The sense triplets distribute their assignments across twenty standard amino acids. A stop codon is a signal to end normal decoding rather than the name of another amino acid. A conventional initiation codon is not a sixty-fifth triplet; it is a triplet used in a particular functional context. AUG, for instance, normally specifies methionine within a standard coding sequence and can also establish its beginning.

It is instructive to distinguish capacity from actual assignment. The arithmetic tells us that 64 words exist but does not tell us that GGU specifies glycine. That assignment is empirical. The arithmetic does not determine how many words will specify each amino acid. Nature could in principle have distributed the excess words differently. The actual distribution has unequal degeneracy, with some residues specified by six triplets and others by only one. Memorizing the count of sixty-four without understanding these limitations risks treating a mathematical possibility as an experimental discovery.

For an RNA fragment of length L nucleotides, the number of distinct strings of exactly that length is 4^L, under the same unconstrained alphabet assumption. For a chain of n residues selected independently from twenty standard amino acids, the number of ordered sequences is 20^n. Neither expression is the number of functional genes or viable proteins. Many RNA strings contain stop signals in unsuitable positions, and many hypothetical proteins would not fold into useful structures. Combinatorial capacity measures possibilities before biological constraints are applied. It is an upper count in a specified abstract sequence space.

An alternative way to express the triplet argument is to consider binary information capacity. With four equally probable and independent letters, one nucleotide carries two bits of maximum information, because four equals two squared. A three-base word has six bits of capacity. Distinguishing twenty equally likely residue labels requires log base two of twenty, approximately 4.32 bits. These numbers illustrate why two nucleotides, providing at most four bits, cannot encode all twenty labels directly under the fixed-word model. In a real transcript, however, bases are neither always equally frequent nor statistically independent. Six bits is the maximum capacity of an unconstrained triplet alphabet, not a measured information content for every biological codon.

A code with more words than residue types creates opportunities for redundancy, but it does not make a message unnecessarily long in the ordinary sense. Three nucleotides are the minimum fixed word length under the assumptions already discussed. Once the word length is three, several unused words remain unless the assignments include alternative words for the same amino acid, punctuation-like signals or other contextual meanings. The genetic code uses all of its standard triplets through sense or termination assignments. No standard triplet should be assumed to have no meaning simply because it is absent from a particular transcript.

3. Establishing the reading frame

A nucleotide sequence has no visible spaces between codons. Its phosphodiester bonds connect neighboring nucleotides in the same general fashion whether they lie inside one codon or at the boundary between codons. The grouping therefore has to be imposed by a decoding mechanism. In an ordinary coding region, the initiation process fixes the first triplet, and subsequent translocation usually advances the ribosome by one codon at a time. The boundaries are maintained by that coordinated molecular cycle. The absence of literal punctuation between consecutive triplets is described by saying the standard code is read without commas.

Take the sequence 5′-AUGGCUAAACCGUAA-3′. With the first nucleotide as the beginning of the frame, the triplets are AUG, GCU, AAA, CCG and UAA. The encoded nascent chain is Met-Ala-Lys-Pro, followed by termination. Starting instead at the second nucleotide produces UGG, CUA, AAC and CGU, with two ungrouped nucleotides at the end. The potential residue string is Trp-Leu-Asn-Arg, but this is a reading-frame calculation rather than evidence that the second frame would be biologically initiated. Starting at the third nucleotide produces GGC, UAA, ACC and GUA, with one nucleotide left over. An early stop signal in that grouping interrupts ordinary translation before the later potential assignments are used.

The offset of a reading frame can be represented by zero, one or two relative to a specified reference position. A zero offset begins with the first position of that reference sequence. A one-base offset begins with its second position, and a two-base offset begins with its third. A shift by three positions returns to the same grouping phase, although some initial nucleotides have now been skipped. A reading frame is therefore a phase, not a particular absolute genomic position. Exam questions sometimes label the three frames as +1, +2 and +3, but the underlying grouping is identical. Check the numbering convention before applying a formula.

If the first base of the first codon is at coordinate s in one-based messenger RNA coordinates, the first base of codon number j is s + 3(j − 1). Its third base is s + 3(j − 1) + 2. For an RNA nucleotide at coordinate x downstream of s, the codon number is floor((x − s)/3) + 1, and the position within the codon is ((x − s) mod 3) + 1. These expressions are valid when the frame is continuous between the chosen start and x. They do not guarantee that the codon is actually translated, because an earlier termination signal or a programmed change in frame might intervene.

For example, if the first translated nucleotide is at position 17, codon number 8 occupies positions 38, 39 and 40: 17 + 3 times 7 equals 38. For position 46, the distance from the starting position is 29: 46 − 17 = 29, giving quotient 9 and remainder 2. Position 46 is therefore the third base of codon 10. Position 47 starts codon 11. The calculation illustrates why subtracting the actual start coordinate before dividing by three is essential. A coordinate numbered from the transcript is not necessarily numbered from the coding sequence.

The conventional non-overlapping nature of translation means that, within one uninterrupted reading event, the first and second adjacent codons do not share nucleotides. A code using overlapping triplets at every step would treat nucleotide positions one through three as the first word, two through four as the second and three through five as the third. Under such a scheme, a mutation at an interior position would generally affect three words at once, and the identity of one word would constrain the next two. Observed protein sequence diversity and mutational behavior are consistent instead with the ordinary non-overlapping triplet scheme.

Non-overlapping decoding must not be confused with non-overlapping genes. Two distinct open reading frames can overlap in the genome or even on the same RNA. Each translation event may use its own frame. One mutation can then alter codons in two different protein products, even though neither ribosome reads overlapping triplets in its own ordinary elongation cycle. The compact genomes of many viruses provide familiar examples of overlapping coding regions, but the detailed regulation of such expression is outside the present topic. The relevant lesson is that overlap at the level of genes and overlap at the level of neighboring codons are different concepts.

A double-stranded DNA fragment has two possible strand orientations. If both strands are considered as possible coding strands and each is written five-prime to three-prime, each gives three possible reading frames. There are therefore six conventional genomic reading frames to examine in an unspliced double-stranded fragment. A specific mature mRNA, however, already has a defined direction and has only three ordinary forward grouping phases. The three reverse-strand frames describe potential products of different transcripts, not a ribosome moving backward on the original message. Introns and other processing events introduce further requirements in a eukaryotic genome; a long unbroken DNA frame is not sufficient to establish the exact mature coding sequence.

4. Distinguishing transcription initiation and translation initiation

A transcription start site is the first nucleotide incorporated into an RNA by RNA polymerase at the relevant transcription unit. The site is frequently designated +1 on a transcription map. A translation start site is the first nucleotide of the initiation codon used to begin a polypeptide. Although both positions are beginnings, they are beginnings of different chemical processes. An RNA can contain an untranslated segment between its first nucleotide and its first translated codon. This segment is termed the five-prime untranslated region, or 5′ UTR.

Consider the conceptual RNA sequence 5′-GGCAAC-AUG-GAA-UUC-UGA-CAC-3′, with the hyphens introduced solely for readability. The first six bases constitute the specified upstream untranslated segment. AUG begins the coding sequence, GAA directs glutamate, UUC directs phenylalanine and UGA signals termination in the standard code. CAC lies downstream of that stop and is not translated in this event. The transcript contains twenty-one nucleotides, but its product has only three residues. Neither the initial GGC in the UTR nor the final CAC should be counted as an incorporated amino acid simply because they are valid triplets in the codon table.

In bacterial messages, different coding regions on a polycistronic RNA may have distinct initiation sites. One upstream transcription start can thus produce a transcript that supports several independently initiated polypeptides. In a typical eukaryotic nuclear mRNA, a major translated region is selected by the initiation machinery acting on a processed transcript; an untranslated segment precedes it. These general comparisons explain why a promoter mutation and a start-codon mutation can have very different outcomes. The former may change the supply of RNA, whereas the latter can change where or whether a protein starts even when a normal amount of RNA is present. Exact initiation-factor mechanisms will be developed in the corresponding later chapters.

The initiator codon must be distinguished from all chemically identical occurrences inside a coding sequence. An AUG encountered midway through an established reading frame usually directs incorporation of methionine without restarting the protein. An AUG at the initiation position is recognized by a specialized initiation process and is associated with the initiator transfer RNA. In a typical bacterial example the first incorporated residue is N-formylmethionine; in eukaryotic cytosolic translation it is ordinarily methionine without the formyl group. Subsequent removal of the initiating residue may yield a mature protein whose first residue differs from the first residue originally inserted. A protein's mature amino terminus is consequently not an infallible direct record of the initiating codon.

Not every AUG is a start, and not every genuine start is AUG. In bacteria, GUG and UUG are familiar alternative start codons, although their internal standard-code assignments are valine and leucine respectively. The initiator tRNA supplies the initiating methionine derivative rather than the amino acid normally associated with an internal GUG or UUG. Eukaryotic systems also have context-dependent non-AUG initiation in particular circumstances. When a question gives a sequence and explicitly states that a particular GUG is used for bacterial initiation, assigning valine as the first residue would be an error even if the remainder of the sequence is translated correctly. If GUG occurs later in the frame, valine is the appropriate ordinary assignment.

An open reading frame is an operational sequence concept. Depending on the search method, it may denote a stretch without an in-frame stop or a candidate region beginning with an allowed start and ending at an in-frame stop. These computational criteria help identify potential coding sequences, but do not alone establish biological translation. A short random sequence can contain an AUG followed by several sense triplets and a stop. An experimentally supported coding region requires additional evidence such as conservation, transcript context, ribosome association in the relevant frame, or detection of the encoded protein. A predicted ORF and a verified protein-coding gene should therefore not be treated as interchangeable terms.

5. Understanding the code table rather than memorizing a list

The standard genetic code is best examined as a systematic arrangement of the four possibilities at each of three positions. A conventional table organizes codons by the first base, then the second base, then the third. The reader starts with the written mRNA codon in the five-prime to three-prime direction. When given a template DNA strand, it must first be converted to the appropriate complementary RNA sequence. When given the coding DNA strand in the correct direction, replacing thymine by uracil produces the corresponding coding RNA letters, provided that any necessary RNA processing is already accounted for.

For the codon GCU, select the first-base group G, then the second-base subgroup C, and finally the third base U. The table assigns alanine. Changing the final base yields GCC, GCA and GCG; all four also specify alanine. In this four-codon family the identity of the first two positions determines the residue assignment, while the third position can vary among all four bases without changing the encoded residue. This pattern does not apply to every first-two-base combination. The UG subgroup contains cysteine codons UGU and UGC, the stop codon UGA and the tryptophan codon UGG. Merely knowing that a substitution occurs at the third position is insufficient to predict its effect.

A useful exercise is to compare codons related by a single base substitution. UUU and UUC both specify phenylalanine; UUA and UUG specify leucine. A change from UUU to UUA thus crosses a residue-assignment boundary, whereas UUU to UUC remains within the same assignment. The four triplets all share their first two bases. The difference lies in the codon table's partitioning of the third-position possibilities. Biological conclusions about a substitution must follow the actual grouping rather than the oversimplified rule that third-position substitutions are always harmless.

Another exercise is to locate the single-codon residues. Under the standard code, methionine has the sense codon AUG and tryptophan has UGG. Changing any one position in either of those two triplets necessarily changes the ordinary standard-code residue assignment or produces a stop. This result is conditional on using the standard table and examining internal decoding. It does not say that a change at AUG cannot leave bacterial initiation possible; a non-AUG start might still be used in a suitable context, with a different efficiency. Nor does it describe mitochondria whose code assignments differ. The same question can therefore have distinct answers when it refers to an internal methionine codon, a bacterial initiation site or an alternative genetic code.

The genetic code is degenerate, not ordinarily ambiguous. Degeneracy means that more than one distinct codon may specify the same residue. Unambiguity means that, within a specified code and the ordinary internal translation context, a codon has a particular assignment rather than arbitrarily directing different residues. The two properties describe opposite directions of a mapping. Leucine has six synonymous codons, but each of these normally specifies leucine rather than a mixture of unrelated amino acids. The apparent paradox disappears when one distinguishes many-to-one mapping from one-to-many assignment.

Degeneracy can also be counted across the entire table. Methionine and tryptophan each have one codon. Nine amino acids each have two: phenylalanine, tyrosine, cysteine, histidine, glutamine, asparagine, lysine, aspartate and glutamate. Isoleucine has three. Alanine, glycine, proline, threonine and valine each have four. Leucine, serine and arginine each have six. The sum is 2 times 1 plus 9 times 2 plus 1 times 3 plus 5 times 4 plus 3 times 6, equal to 61. Adding the three stop signals accounts for the full 64-triplet alphabet. The number of different amino acids represented by this grouping is 2 + 9 + 1 + 5 + 3 = 20.

The six-codon families reveal why a simple rule involving only the last position cannot describe every synonymous relationship. Leucine is assigned both the CUN four-codon family and UUA/UUG. Serine is assigned the UCN family as well as AGU and AGC. Arginine is assigned the CGN family as well as AGA and AGG. The first or second base may therefore differ between synonymous codons. In particular, some pairs of synonymous codons differ in two positions; not every synonymous change can be reached in one substitution without passing through an intermediate sequence with a different assignment. The distribution of synonymous words is not a uniform pattern repeated identically throughout the table.

6. Discovering the triplet mechanism: the power of frameshift genetics

The triplet hypothesis can be examined by asking what happens when an extra nucleotide is inserted into an otherwise functional sequence. A single substitution leaves the sequence length unchanged, but an insertion potentially alters the grouping of all downstream letters. If the message is read in fixed groups of three from a particular beginning, the insertion changes the downstream grouping phase by one. A second insertion changes it by another position, whereas a third insertion restores the original phase beyond the third insertion. The original stretch between mutations may have become different, but sufficiently far downstream the original grouping can resume.

In 1961, Francis Crick, Leslie Barnett, Sydney Brenner and Richard Watts-Tobin investigated this issue with mutations in the rII region of bacteriophage T4. Their approach used the behavior of mutant phages on bacterial hosts as a genetic readout. An important class of mutations generated with acridine treatment could be studied in combinations. The researchers inferred that mutations of two opposite classes caused opposite changes in reading phase; these were conventionally described as plus and minus mutations. A plus-type mutation could be compensated by a minus-type mutation at another nearby position, whereas combinations of three changes of the same sign could also restore a measure of function. The experimental evidence linked the number and signs of mutations to restoration of functional reading.

A critical feature of the experiment was that it did not merely count viable and nonviable phages after an arbitrary mutagen treatment. The arrangement and combination of defined genetic changes were central. If a fixed-length code had words of two nucleotides, two equivalent one-base shifts would restore phase. If the words had length four, four equivalent shifts would be needed. Restoration after particular combinations of three same-sign mutations, together with opposite-sign compensation, is what supported a triplet reading scheme. The results were obtained using functional genetic tests before individual RNA codons had all been biochemically identified.

This interpretation is more subtle than claiming that three insertions always repair a gene. Consider an original coding sequence AUG-AAA-GGC-UUC-CCG-GAA-UAA. A nucleotide insertion shortly after the start changes the grouping. Another insertion in a later region changes it again, and a third restores the grouping downstream of that position, provided translation has not already stopped. The interval between the first and last mutations can encode many altered residues. A gene may tolerate changes in a relatively dispensable stretch yet fail if an essential catalytic residue lies in that interval. Restoration of reading phase is therefore a necessary condition for one particular kind of compensation, not a general sufficient condition for restoration of protein activity.

It is also important to distinguish a deletion from an insertion without relying on the visual spacing of letters. The sequence after a deletion is shorter by one nucleotide, so positions originally downstream of that site move one coordinate toward the start. The new triplets may consist of the second and third positions of one original codon followed by the first position of the next. An insertion changes the relationship in the other direction. Both are frameshifting events when their length is not divisible by three and they occur inside an ordinary uninterrupted coding region. Their exact peptide products must be obtained by regrouping actual altered nucleotide sequences, not merely by giving the event a plus or minus label.

The mathematical generalization is straightforward. Let delta denote the cumulative number of inserted nucleotides minus the cumulative number of deleted nucleotides upstream of a reference location, assuming unchanged initiation. The downstream grouping aligns again with the original grouping when delta is divisible by three. A value of zero may represent cancellation of an insertion and deletion. A value of three may represent a net gain of one codon's worth of bases. A value of minus three may represent a net loss of that amount. These possibilities are distinct at the sequence level even though all have the same phase modulo three.

An experiment can demonstrate the distinction through an original educational example. Suppose a reporter has an essential downstream enzyme domain that functions only when its original reading frame is retained. The wild-type construct gives normalized signal 100. A one-base insertion gives signal 2, a two-base insertion gives signal 3, and a three-base insertion gives signal 70. A one-base insertion followed by a nearby one-base deletion gives signal 65. These are hypothetical values, not historical measurements. Their pattern is consistent with a triplet mechanism because the two frame-restored variants regain much of their activity. The restoration is incomplete, which may reflect altered residues, changes in expression or effects of the inserted or deleted sequence on folding.

For a meaningful test, the constructs must be compared with appropriate controls. The wild-type sequence establishes the assay's reference activity. A construct without the reporter establishes the assay background. Sequencing the variants verifies that the observed mutations really are the expected lengths and locations. Measuring transcript abundance helps distinguish altered RNA production from changed protein function. Measuring protein size and abundance provides another check on whether the loss of activity arises because translation ends prematurely or because a full-length but inactive protein is produced. A functional phenotype alone does not identify all of these intermediate molecular events.

The historical frameshift evidence and the arithmetic argument answer related but different questions. The capacity argument asks how long a fixed code word must be to accommodate the known amino-acid alphabet. The frameshift results ask how the actual translating machinery groups sequence information. Neither establishes that a particular triplet corresponds to a particular residue. For that, experimental biochemistry was needed. A complete interpretation of the genetic code therefore required investigators to combine genetic evidence about grouping with biochemical evidence about assignment.

7. Deciphering individual assignments through synthetic RNA

The decisive insight of early cell-free protein synthesis experiments was to remove the need to interpret an entire living cell at once. A cell extract can provide ribosomes, soluble factors, transfer RNAs, enzymes and the chemical substrates needed for translation. If the experimenter supplies a defined RNA template, changes in newly synthesized polypeptide can then be attributed to that input more directly than in an intact cell containing many endogenous messages. This does not make the system chemically simple, but it makes the relationship between template and product experimentally accessible.

In their 1961 work, Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei used an Escherichia coli cell-free translation system and tested synthetic polyribonucleotides. An RNA composed of repeated uracils caused a strong stimulation of phenylalanine incorporation into material with the properties of polyphenylalanine. The result was especially informative because any uninterrupted three-base grouping of a homopolymer of uracil yields UUU. Once the triplet organization was understood, the assignment of UUU to phenylalanine followed naturally from this biochemical observation. Their paper presented the experimental result before all features of the complete code had been settled, and it should not be described as having independently solved the entire triplet question.

The assay required a way to distinguish newly incorporated amino acids from the large pool of free amino acids supplied to the extract. Radioactively labelled amino acids gave a measurable signal when incorporated into the product fraction. A set of parallel reactions could differ in which amino acid was labelled while the other amino acids remained available. A strong increase in incorporation of the labelled phenylalanine in the presence of poly(U) indicated a template-directed relationship rather than merely a general stimulation of protein production. The comparison with background synthesis in the absence of added template was essential.

A homopolymer is a powerful but limited probe. Poly(U) cannot distinguish UUU from a hypothetical repeated two-base word UU solely on the basis of its product, because every overlapping or non-overlapping grouping contains uracils. Poly(A) and poly(C) can similarly provide evidence for assignments associated with repeated adenine and repeated cytosine, yielding lysine and proline respectively under standard-code decoding. Other homopolymers may be difficult to use in the same way because of RNA structural properties and assay conditions. Absence of an incorporation signal is not automatically evidence that a triplet has no assignment.

The limitation of a random mixed polymer is different. Suppose a synthetic RNA is prepared with a known average proportion of A and U. A translation system may incorporate a mixture of residues. If the bases are independently distributed, the experimenter can estimate the frequencies of triplets containing two U bases and one A base, for example. However, AAU, AUA and UAA have the same base composition but different orders and different standard assignments. A random polymer with only known composition cannot by itself identify which particular ordered sequence produces a particular amino acid. It can narrow the possibilities and support statistical associations, but sequence-defined probes are needed to resolve the order.

The probability reasoning behind such experiments is worth understanding. If the fractions of A and U are 0.25 and 0.75 and neighboring bases are independent, the expected proportion of the specific ordered triplet UUA is 0.75 times 0.75 times 0.25, equal to 0.140625. The ordered triplets UAU and AUU have the same expected frequency. The combined proportion containing two U bases and one A base is therefore three times 0.140625, equal to 0.421875. A researcher who measured only the total frequency of products associated with that composition could not distinguish those three triplets without further information. The hypothetical calculation is a model of the inferential difficulty, not a reproduction of a particular historical incorporation experiment.

Real incorporation measurements also depend on properties not represented by base-composition probabilities. A message with abundant stop codons might yield shorter products. RNA secondary structure can affect access by the translation machinery. Different tRNAs and amino acids can be supplied or recognized at different efficiencies. The incorporation signal reflects the entire biochemical process, not simply the arithmetic frequency of a triplet in a template. The usefulness of the synthetic-polymer approach depended on comparing multiple experiments rather than treating one incorporation ratio as a direct universal codon census.

Har Gobind Khorana and colleagues advanced the problem by preparing defined repeating nucleotide sequences. An alternating RNA with a repeated UC pattern, for instance, contains UCU and CUC as successive triplets when read in one phase. These specify serine and leucine. Reading the same alternating polymer beginning at the other position reverses which of those codons appears first, but the repetitive character of the resulting sequence is unchanged. The observed association of repeating templates with particular peptide compositions supplied information that composition-only methods could not provide.

A repeated trinucleotide template behaves differently from a repeated dinucleotide template. In an infinite repetition of AUG, starting at the A yields consecutive AUG codons, starting at the U yields consecutive UGA codons, and starting at the G yields consecutive GAU codons. These have methionine, stop and aspartate assignments in the standard code. The example illustrates the effect of the phase of translation on a periodic template; it is not a claim that a real cell-free assay would initiate efficiently in each phase. Initiation and termination impose additional constraints. Precisely because phase and sequence order matter, well-defined polymers provided complementary evidence about the reading mechanism.

A general periodicity argument can be made without advanced mathematics. Suppose a template repeats a sequence of length m. Every time a new ordinary codon begins, the position within that repeating pattern advances by three modulo m. The same position is reached again after the smallest positive number of codons q for which 3q is divisible by m. That number equals m divided by the greatest common divisor of m and three. A repeat of length two therefore has a two-codon cycle; a repeat of length three has a one-codon cycle for each fixed phase; a repeat of length four has a four-codon cycle. This is a statement about grouping a periodic nucleotide string, not about the biological ability to translate the complete cycle when a stop signal occurs.

For a hypothetical repeat of GUAC, the sequence written in groups of three beginning with G is GUA-CGU-ACG-UAC, and then the cycle returns to GUA. Using the standard table, the cycle specifies valine, arginine, threonine and tyrosine. A sample of peptide containing all four residues in that order would be consistent with the specified phase. An observation of the four residue types without information about order is less decisive: it could support a set of candidate codons without establishing the precise phase or direction. The example teaches why the design of a template determines what kind of inference can be made from a product.

The breakthrough in individual ordered triplet assignment came through a method that did not require a long chain to be synthesized. Nirenberg and Philip Leder described a ribosome-binding assay in 1964 in which defined trinucleotides stimulated binding of corresponding aminoacyl transfer RNAs to ribosomes. The radioactive label could identify which amino acid was associated with the retained tRNA. Free tRNA and ribosome-associated tRNA behaved differently in the separation step. The resulting signal linked an ordered triplet to an aminoacyl-tRNA recognition event. This approach enabled direct testing of synthetic codons in a way that random mixed polymers could not.

The binding assay and the cell-free peptide-synthesis experiment address different stages. A binding assay demonstrates an association of a triplet, ribosome and particular aminoacyl-tRNA under specified conditions. It does not alone establish the rate of elongation, the efficiency of initiation on a full mRNA or the final functionality of a protein. A complete synthesis assay can establish incorporation into a chain but may make individual assignments difficult when the template has mixed sequences. Their combined use made the inferences stronger. The presence of a cognate binding signal and the corresponding synthesis product connect recognition to incorporation.

In a hypothetical binding assay, a radiolabelled alanyl-tRNA is supplied with ribosomes and synthetic GCU. Retained radioactivity is 850 units compared with 30 without added triplet and 35 with UUU. A labelled phenylalanyl-tRNA instead gives 810 units with UUU and 25 with GCU. The values are invented for reasoning practice. The reciprocal specificity is more informative than a single positive sample, because it argues against nonspecific retention caused by any RNA triplet. The no-triplet sample provides a background estimate, and a no-ribosome sample would help exclude retention of free tRNA by the filter. Equal input radioactivity and validated aminoacylation would strengthen the comparison.

A lack of ordinary aminoacyl-tRNA binding in response to a stop triplet must be interpreted cautiously. Stop signals normally recruit release machinery rather than a cognate ordinary tRNA. The absence of a particular tRNA signal is compatible with this mechanism, but a poorly functioning assay can also yield low signals for genuine sense codons. A direct termination experiment using a ribosome carrying a nascent chain provides complementary evidence. The important general lesson is that a negative result needs both a positive control establishing that the assay works and independent evidence that distinguishes the plausible explanations for the absence of signal.

The discoveries recognized by the 1968 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine represent complementary contributions. Nirenberg and Khorana were central to determining the code's assignments through biochemical and synthetic-nucleic-acid approaches, while Robert W. Holley's work on tRNA helped reveal the molecular adaptor that gives the code a physical basis. The complete modern understanding did not emerge from a single demonstration. It emerged from convergence among sequence chemistry, functional genetics, biochemical recognition and the study of the molecules that connect codons to amino acids.

8. The adaptor principle and molecular orientation

Transfer RNA solves the central chemical problem of translation by combining two functional regions on one molecule. One region contains an anticodon capable of recognizing a complementary triplet in mRNA. Another region carries an amino acid attached to the acceptor end. Because these regions are separated in the folded tRNA, the nucleotide recognition event can be linked to a residue that has no direct chemical resemblance to its codon. A molecular mechanism can therefore associate UUU with phenylalanine, GCU with alanine and many other triplets with their assigned amino acids.

The anticodon occupies a defined region of the tRNA anticodon loop. Conventional numbering labels its three bases 34, 35 and 36 when tRNA is read in its own five-prime to three-prime direction. The first base of the mRNA codon pairs with anticodon base 36, the second with base 35 and the third with base 34. This reversed correspondence follows directly from the antiparallel organization of paired RNA segments. It is a common source of examination errors because authors may write an anticodon in either orientation while describing the same molecular interaction.

For a strictly complementary example, the codon 5′-AUG-3′ pairs with the anticodon 3′-UAC-5′. Written in the conventional five-prime to three-prime direction, that same anticodon is 5′-CAU-3′. The letters UAC and CAU are not contradictory when their orientations are specified. To obtain the anticodon in the five-prime to three-prime convention from a given codon, first complement each base, then reverse the order. To align a codon and anticodon as they pair, write the two sequences in opposite directions and complement position by position. Confusing an alignment notation with a written sequence notation can turn a correct base-pairing concept into a wrong numerical answer.

Consider a codon 5′-GAC-3′, which specifies aspartate. Its exact complement as an aligned anticodon is 3′-CUG-5′. Its reversed sequence is 5′-GUC-3′. If an examination gives an anticodon sequence GUC with no indicated direction, the conventional assumption is five-prime to three-prime; under strict complementary pairing it recognizes GAC. However, a chemically modified or wobble-capable anticodon can recognize additional synonymous codons. The initial reverse-complement operation identifies exact pairing, not necessarily the complete range of codons recognized by that tRNA in vivo.

Anticodon recognition is only one part of the specificity of translation. Before a tRNA is used in normal decoding, it must carry the correct amino acid. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases establish the chemical association by recognizing appropriate tRNAs and amino acids. They are not a set of enzymes that simply read each messenger codon. The tRNA itself is the intermediary. A correct anticodon combined with an incorrectly attached amino acid can deliver the wrong residue at a correctly read codon. Consequently, one cannot infer aminoacylation accuracy solely from evidence of correct codon–anticodon pairing.

This separation is demonstrated conceptually by considering a tRNA whose anticodon normally recognizes a glycine codon but whose acceptor end has been experimentally supplied with alanine. If that tRNA is admitted to the translating ribosome and participates in peptide-bond formation, the residue delivered at the glycine codon is alanine. This statement is conditional: altered tRNAs can be affected by other recognition steps, and their efficiency may vary. It does not mean that ordinary cells routinely tolerate incorrect charging. It explains why the ribosome's reading of the anticodon and the synthetase's selection of the amino acid are complementary requirements for fidelity. The full charging mechanism belongs to the next chapter.

The energetic requirements of translation should also be located correctly. Pairing of a codon and an anticodon is a reversible molecular recognition interaction; it is not itself a chemical reaction that consumes an ATP for every base pair. Charging the tRNA requires energy and creates an activated linkage, while the translation machinery uses additional energy-dependent steps to select, move and recycle molecules. The detailed ATP and GTP bookkeeping belongs to the later lessons on charging and elongation. For the present chapter, the key concept is that correct decoding requires both sequence recognition and a supply of activated aminoacyl-tRNAs. The genetic code table alone does not describe the machinery or its energetic cost.

The adaptor interpretation was tested experimentally by Chapeville and colleagues in 1962. They used a tRNA originally charged with cysteine and chemically converted its attached residue to alanine without replacing the tRNA adaptor. In a suitable translation system, the altered adaptor directed incorporation of alanine at positions normally specified for cysteine. The experiment separates two possible explanations for amino acid choice. If the ribosome recognized the chemical identity of the free residue as the decisive code signal, changing the residue while retaining the tRNA should have prevented that specific readout. Instead, the altered product supported the adaptor's role in linking the codon to the carried amino acid. This classical evidence does not imply that all mischarged molecules are accepted equally efficiently; it establishes that codon recognition and attachment of a particular residue are distinct molecular operations.

9. The wobble hypothesis: pairing flexibility without losing specificity

If each of the sixty-one standard sense codons required an entirely separate anticodon with exact Watson–Crick complementarity, a cell would need a distinct matching anticodon type for each codon. But many translation systems possess fewer distinct anticodon sequences than the number of sense codons they decode. This is possible because particular base-pairing interactions at one end of the codon–anticodon duplex allow a tRNA to recognize more than one synonymous triplet. Crick formulated the wobble hypothesis in 1966 to account for the relationship between flexible recognition and code degeneracy.

The hypothesis concerns a specific position. The third nucleotide of the codon, counted from its five-prime end, faces the first nucleotide of the anticodon, counted from the anticodon's five-prime end. The latter is conventionally position 34 of tRNA. The first two codon positions normally have stricter geometric recognition requirements in the ribosomal decoding site, whereas the third pair can accommodate certain noncanonical geometries. The term wobble does not imply that the ribosome permits random misalignment of all three letters. It describes a limited form of flexibility that operates within an otherwise ordered reading process.

Ordinary Watson–Crick RNA pairs are A with U and G with C. A and U have the complementary donor and acceptor arrangement of the standard pair, as do G and C. G and U can form a distinct hydrogen-bonded pair with a geometry different from the ordinary G-C or A-U pairing. Whether a noncanonical pair is tolerated depends on its location and on molecular constraints. A free RNA duplex in solution and a codon–anticodon duplex held within a ribosome are not identical experimental environments. Wobble refers to those noncanonical interactions that are accommodated in the decoding context, not all possible associations between RNA bases.

The classical wobble model is usually represented by five rows based on the identity of the anticodon's five-prime base. An anticodon ending in the written sequence convention at its three-prime side is not the relevant position; one must examine its very first letter when it is written five-prime to three-prime. In the simple rules, C at that position pairs with codon G, A pairs with codon U, G pairs with codon C or U, and U pairs with codon A or G. Inosine, represented by I, can recognize U, C and A. These relationships are predictions for the simplified classical model and are not a complete catalogue of all modified tRNA behavior in all species.

Take phenylalanine codons UUU and UUC. Both begin UU. An anticodon 5′-GAA-3′ has A at positions 35 and 36, each of which can pair with the corresponding U in the first two codon positions. Its G at position 34 pairs canonically with the third-position C of UUC and can form a wobble pair with the third-position U of UUU. The same tRNA can thereby recognize both phenylalanine codons in the simplified model. The amino acid assignment remains coherent because the codons recognized by that adaptor belong to the same residue family.

For lysine, AAA and AAG are the two standard sense codons. An anticodon 5′-UUU-3′ has U at the wobble position, allowing recognition of terminal A or G under the classical rule. The anticodon's remaining U residues match the first two A residues of the codon. This example looks symmetrical with the phenylalanine example, but biological tRNAs may have chemical modifications at position 34 that affect actual recognition efficiency and specificity. A formal pairing prediction is therefore not the same as a complete description of a particular organism's decoding repertoire.

Inosine is an informative example because it introduces an RNA base that is not one of the four ordinary letters in the messenger RNA codon table. Inosine is produced in certain tRNAs by modification of adenosine. It contains hypoxanthine as its base, whose hydrogen-bonding properties differ from adenine. In the classical wobble framework, an inosine in the first anticodon position can pair with codon U, C or A. This broadens recognition without adding a fifth ordinary letter to the mRNA genetic code. The distinction between the codon alphabet and the modified nucleotide repertoire of the adaptor must be maintained.

Consider an anticodon 5′-IGC-3′. The reverse pairing of its last two bases predicts codons beginning GC. The terminal mRNA base can be U, C or A in the simplified inosine model, so GCU, GCC and GCA are potential targets. All specify alanine in the standard code. GCG is not included by the stated inosine pairing rule, even though it is also an alanine codon. A second compatible anticodon or an additional decoding mechanism is therefore required to cover it in that simplified system. The genetic code's degeneracy and the recognition range of an individual tRNA are related but not identical properties.

An anticodon 5′-IAU-3′ similarly predicts recognition of AUU, AUC and AUA. All three correspond to isoleucine in the standard code. It does not predict recognition of AUG, the ordinary methionine codon. The AU family illustrates why recognition boundaries matter. A tRNA that could pair indiscriminately with all four codons of this first-two-base family would confuse isoleucine with methionine. Actual translation systems use a combination of available tRNAs and base modifications to preserve the required discrimination. Bacteria and eukaryotes do not necessarily achieve the same result with identical anticodon chemistry.

The relationship between chemical modification and specificity can be explored using an examination scenario. Suppose a tRNA normally possesses I at position 34 and recognizes three synonymous codons ending U, C and A. A mutation in a modification enzyme prevents conversion of the precursor adenosine at this position. Under the simplified model the unmodified A recognizes U, not C or A. One therefore predicts that this tRNA's direct decoding range has narrowed. Whether total cellular translation of the affected codons declines depends on the presence of other tRNAs capable of decoding them. The prediction about the individual molecule is stronger than a prediction that the entire organism will necessarily stop producing every protein containing those codons.

This kind of distinction is essential in interpreting mutant phenotypes. Redundancy exists at several levels: multiple synonymous codons can encode the same residue, multiple tRNA genes may encode the same anticodon, and different anticodon types may sometimes recognize overlapping codon sets. Removal of one tRNA gene does not necessarily eliminate the corresponding tRNA species if another gene produces an equivalent molecule. Removal of a modification enzyme can affect several tRNA types simultaneously. The phenotype depends on the collective repertoire, not just the genetic code table.

It is sometimes useful to calculate a minimum number of anticodon types needed in a deliberately simplified decoding model. If a fourfold synonymous family contains codons ending U, C, A and G, a tRNA with G at the wobble position can cover the U/C pair, and one with U at that position can cover the A/G pair. Thus two anticodon types suffice for this family under the classical pairing assumptions. Four exactly complementary types would suffice but would not be minimal. An inosine-bearing anticodon could instead cover three endings, with an additional suitable anticodon covering G. The model does not establish the number of distinct tRNAs actually expressed by a cell.

For a twofold family ending U and C, a single G-bearing anticodon can theoretically cover both. For a family ending A and G, the simple U-bearing anticodon covers both. For the threefold isoleucine family, an inosine-bearing anticodon can in principle cover U, C and A without recognizing G. A single-codon methionine or tryptophan assignment requires its own compatible anticodon in ordinary decoding. Sixfold families must be examined as two separate first-two-base boxes: one fourfold box and one twofold box. The two boxes cannot be combined into one wobble recognition set if their first or second positions differ.

Under the strict first-two-position assumptions, eight fourfold boxes occur in the standard table: UC, CU, CC, CG, AC, GU, GC and GG. Two anticodon types per box give sixteen. There are twelve twofold sense groups distributed across the remaining boxes, giving twelve more. The threefold isoleucine group adds one, and the single methionine and tryptophan groups add two. The total is thirty-one anticodon recognition types for sense decoding under this idealized set of rules. This familiar theoretical result assumes the classical pairing sets are available where needed, ignores specialized initiation requirements and does not count stop-recognizing factors as tRNAs. To avoid misusing the result, students should check each subgroup in the actual table rather than treating thirty-one as a universal count of tRNA genes.

A distinct way to count would assume that some highly modified wobble bases can support decoding of all four codons in a synonymous box, a phenomenon encountered in certain reduced translation systems. In that model one type per fourfold box could sometimes suffice. Such superwobble behavior illustrates that the classical set-cover calculation is conditional on the allowed pairing rules. It does not establish that every fourfold family in every organism is decoded by a single tRNA. Differences in tRNA modification, organelle translation systems and specific codon contexts can all affect which decoding solutions actually operate.

There is an important conceptual distinction between the existence of synonymous codons and the evolutionary origin of the particular code table. Wobble explains how multiple synonymous codons can be read efficiently by a limited repertoire of adaptors and helps explain the relationship between degeneracy patterns and tRNA recognition. It is not a proof that wobble alone historically caused every assignment in the genetic code. The evolutionary origin of the assignments is a larger question involving the history of translation and the constraints imposed by existing protein sequences. For examinations, the molecular consequence of wobble is the firm and testable inference: certain tRNAs decode multiple codons without necessarily changing the amino-acid identity they deliver.

10. When the standard code is not the whole story

The genetic code is often described as universal because the same ordinary assignments are shared across a vast range of cellular life. The extensive correspondence among bacteria, archaea and eukaryotic nuclear translation makes sense in the context of common evolutionary ancestry. A gene from one organism can often be expressed in another using a compatible expression system, and the ordinary codon assignments will produce the same predicted primary sequence. The conservation of the code is biologically remarkable because a global reassignment of a frequently used triplet could alter residues in many proteins simultaneously.

However, near universality is more accurate than absolute universality. Organellar translation systems and particular microbial or protist lineages have documented alternative assignments. A sequence should therefore be translated according to the appropriate organism and cellular compartment. Genome annotation systems use explicitly numbered translation tables for this reason. The standard table is a default for many routine sequence questions, not a rule that overrides an examination's stated mitochondrial or organism-specific code.

An especially familiar comparison concerns vertebrate mitochondria. In the standard code UGA normally signals termination, whereas in the vertebrate mitochondrial translation table it specifies tryptophan. AUA normally specifies isoleucine in the standard code but methionine in this mitochondrial table. AGA and AGG are listed as termination assignments in the vertebrate mitochondrial annotation table, though actual termination at particular mammalian mitochondrial gene boundaries can involve additional context and mechanisms. For an ordinary codon-table exercise, the assigned translation table determines the expected answer; it would be incorrect to generalize every vertebrate mitochondrial assignment to every mitochondrial genome.

For example, consider 5′-AUG-AUA-UGA-GCU-UAA-3′. With standard internal assignments and the initial AUG stipulated as a start, the product before the first stop is Met-Ile. With the vertebrate mitochondrial table and the same stipulated start, it is Met-Met-Trp-Ala before UAA. The nucleotide sequence itself has not changed. The difference arises because the decoding system supplies a different correspondence for some triplets. This exercise isolates code variation from changes in reading frame. The positions of all codons are identical in both interpretations.

Certain ciliates provide other well-known alternative codes in which UAA and UAG can have glutamine assignments. Some bacterial lineages, including examples of Mycoplasma, decode UGA as tryptophan. These examples are not interchangeable descriptions of every ciliate or every bacterium. Their value lies in demonstrating that the shared code is highly conserved but capable of lineage-specific departures. A taxonomic name or organelle label in a problem can be as important as the written sequence.

A global change in a codon assignment is biologically different from the use of a special context to interpret a particular codon within a particular message. In the first case the translation repertoire for a lineage or cellular compartment changes the ordinary assignment across its coding sequences. In the second, most occurrences may retain their standard meaning, while a selected occurrence behaves differently because extra molecular signals are present. A conventional standard-code table cannot express all of these context-dependent events with a single unconditional entry.

Selenocysteine provides the best-known natural example of such selective recoding. A UGA triplet can direct incorporation of selenocysteine when the required specialized tRNA, protein factors and RNA signals are present. The selenocysteine insertion sequence, abbreviated SECIS, is an RNA structural element that participates in specifying this alternative readout. In bacteria the relevant structural signal typically lies downstream of the recoded UGA within the coding region; in eukaryotic selenoprotein mRNAs the essential SECIS element is in the three-prime untranslated region. These differences matter because a signal need not lie immediately adjacent to the codon in every translation system.

The presence of selenium in a medium does not by itself convert every UGA into a selenocysteine codon. The special decoding pathway must be present and directed to the appropriate message. Ordinary termination remains necessary for numerous other proteins. Selenocysteine is often called the twenty-first genetically encoded amino acid because it is incorporated through a specialized translational mechanism rather than being merely a chemical modification applied indiscriminately to proteins after their synthesis. Its biological significance includes functions in selenoproteins involved in redox chemistry. The key distinction for this chapter is between selective recoding of UGA and replacement of UGA's usual meaning in an entire translation table.

Pyrrolysine is another naturally encoded amino acid, often called the twenty-second. In certain archaea and bacteria, a specialized tRNA and its corresponding aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase can support incorporation of pyrrolysine in response to UAG. Its decoding system is not simply an identical copy of the selenocysteine system using another stop codon. The machinery and contextual requirements differ; a universal requirement for an RNA element analogous to SECIS should not be assumed. Both examples expand the familiar twenty-residue model, yet neither invalidates the sixty-four possible three-letter combinations of ordinary RNA nucleotides.

A further distinction concerns stop readthrough. Sometimes a ribosome continues translation beyond a stop codon because an aminoacyl-tRNA is accepted in competition with termination. This may be a low-frequency error or a regulated, biologically meaningful event in a particular message. In either case a longer chain can result. It is not necessary to posit a new genome-wide genetic code merely because a small fraction of ribosomes continue beyond a stop. The relevant question is whether the altered meaning is ordinary throughout a decoding system, selectively supported by a specific mechanism, or an occasional failure of normal fidelity.

Programmed ribosomal frameshifting provides another departure from the simplest uninterrupted decoding model. In particular sequences, the translation machinery can switch its grouping phase in a controlled manner, so the residues downstream follow a different frame. This differs from a DNA insertion or deletion: the RNA sequence itself may remain unchanged. A genetically introduced indel changes the sequence that reaches the ribosome, whereas a programmed frameshift changes the interpretation of an existing sequence. The same downstream grouping could result in some circumstances, but the causal mechanisms are different. Detailed viral regulatory examples belong to the later gene expression chapter.

One can design a hypothetical experiment to distinguish general reassignment from selective recoding. Prepare two otherwise comparable reporter messages containing the same internal UGA. Only one contains a functional context element required for recoding. If both yield a similar amount of full-length protein in a system where UGA has an ordinary sense assignment, selective context is not necessary for that observation. If full-length product depends strongly on the intact element and on the specialized translation machinery, selective recoding becomes a plausible explanation. The protein's residue identity must still be measured; full-length product alone does not distinguish incorporation of selenocysteine from other forms of readthrough.

This experiment also highlights why an observation of a short or long protein is not enough to determine a code table. A premature stop can lead to a short product. Failure of initiation can eliminate detectable product entirely. A release defect can change the distribution of ribosome-associated chains. A context-sensitive recoding mechanism can produce a mixture of short and long chains from a single transcript. In a mutation or variant-code question, the investigator needs to compare the actual mRNA sequence, the relevant decoding system and the composition of the protein products before concluding that a triplet has acquired an alternative assignment.

11. Reading termination signals accurately

The ordinary standard stop codons are UAA, UAG and UGA, traditionally called ochre, amber and opal or umber, respectively. The names are useful historical terminology but the nucleotide sequences are more important for solving a problem. Unlike standard sense triplets, the ordinary stop signals do not specify addition of a residue by a cognate standard aminoacyl-tRNA. A release factor recognizes the termination situation and promotes release of the nascent polypeptide. Consequently, a stop is not represented by an amino acid in the primary sequence.

Consider the coding RNA AUG-GGU-CUA-UAG. It contains four complete codons, of which three are sense codons. Assuming normal initiation and termination, the nascent chain has three residues: methionine, glycine and leucine. It has two peptide bonds connecting them. The termination signal is counted as part of the complete coding sequence in conventions that include the terminal stop, but not as a fourth residue. A problem that gives the number of amino acids and requests a nucleotide length must therefore be read carefully to determine whether the requested region includes the stop codon.

For a chain of n incorporated residues synthesized in one ordinary reading frame, the corresponding sense region contains 3n nucleotides. Including the immediately following stop triplet gives 3(n + 1). The chain contains n − 1 peptide bonds before any further cleavage or modification. A sequence of 903 nucleotides that includes precisely one complete terminal stop and otherwise consists of an uninterrupted translated region therefore contains 301 triplets, of which 300 specify residues. It yields a chain of 300 residues with 299 peptide bonds. A mature product could be shorter if an initiating residue or a signal peptide is subsequently removed.

An out-of-frame occurrence of the letters UAA is not a termination signal for an elongating ribosome in a different frame. To identify the first true stop in an ordinary sequence, the reader must begin at the specified start and examine consecutive non-overlapping triplets in that grouping. Searching for the first substring UAA without regard to its starting coordinate can truncate a predicted protein incorrectly. Similarly, a stop-like triplet in an untranslated region or in the wrong strand of a DNA fragment has no obligatory termination effect on the chosen translation event.

A stop-loss mutation is conceptually the opposite of a premature stop-gain mutation. If a normal stop is changed to a sense codon, the ribosome may continue into previously untranslated downstream sequence until it encounters another in-frame termination signal. The eventual product can be longer than normal, with extra residues at its carboxyl end. If no suitable downstream stop is available, other cellular quality-control mechanisms may become relevant. The standard-code assignment predicts the local readout, but one needs the downstream sequence to predict exactly where the extended chain would end.

The full mechanism of termination, release factors and ribosome recycling is assigned to 3.C.07. For present purposes, the essential comparison is between an initiation context establishing grouping, a sense triplet directing addition of a residue, and a stop triplet directing termination in the usual system. All three functions operate through specific molecular machinery. They do not arise from visible delimiters between codons. Understanding this separation provides the foundation for later lessons on translation mechanisms.

12. Translating DNA and RNA sequences systematically

Sequence questions become much easier when each operation is performed separately. First establish the identity and orientation of the supplied strand. Second establish which RNA sequence is actually present in the mature message. Third identify the specified initiation point or reading phase. Fourth partition the sequence into consecutive triplets, using the correct genetic code for the biological system. Fifth stop at the first relevant in-frame termination signal unless the question expressly describes a recoding event. This order prevents errors in strand conversion from being hidden by an otherwise correct use of the codon table.

Suppose the coding DNA strand of a continuous, already specified coding region is 5′-ATG GAC TTT CCA TAA-3′. It corresponds to the RNA sequence 5′-AUG GAC UUU CCA UAA-3′, and that RNA encodes Met-Asp-Phe-Pro before termination. The DNA template is complementary and antiparallel to the coding DNA, so in aligned notation it is 3′-TAC CTG AAA GGT ATT-5′. Given this template, complementary RNA synthesis generates the same five-prime to three-prime RNA sequence. These two routes must converge if strand identity and direction have been handled correctly.

A more challenging question might present the template DNA in its conventional five-prime to three-prime form, as 5′-TTA TGG AAA GTC CAT-3′. Transcription reads this template from the other end. Simply complementing the displayed letters without reversing their order would give the wrong RNA orientation. Reversing the order and taking the RNA complement produces 5′-AUG GAC UUU CCA UAA-3′. Once a sequence has been converted, the amino acid assignments remain the same regardless of which form of the DNA was originally supplied. The molecular sequence is not altered merely by changing how the strand is printed.

RNA processing may complicate this relationship. A eukaryotic genomic sequence can include introns that will not appear in the mature coding RNA. Suppose two coding exons contribute the sequences 5′-AUGGC and 5′-UAAAUAA when considered in their spliced RNA orientation. After removal of the intervening intron, their direct join is AUGGCUAAAUAA. The triplet GCU crosses the exon junction: its first two bases derive from the first exon and its final base derives from the second. The complete product is Met-Ala-Lys, followed by UAA. One cannot translate the two exons independently as separate complete groups and then concatenate their amino-acid strings. Exon boundaries and codon boundaries need not coincide.

This issue is particularly important when an examination problem describes alternative splicing. If an exon containing a multiple of three coding nucleotides is skipped, the downstream phase can be preserved, provided that the same start is used and the exon is entirely within the uninterrupted translated region. The peptide sequence can still change substantially because residues disappear and new junction codons may form. If the removed coding length is not a multiple of three, the downstream phase generally changes. An intron removed during normal splicing does not cause such a change relative to the normal mature message; it was never part of that reference coding sequence. The effect of exon skipping must be compared with the correctly spliced reference transcript rather than with raw genomic length.

The first and last complete codons are equally important when a fragment does not contain the whole coding sequence. Imagine that the question supplies 5′-GCUUAACG-3′ and states that the first letter is the first position of a codon in a larger transcript. The complete codons are GCU and UAA, and the remaining CG lacks its third base in the supplied fragment. If this fragment is translated as part of a longer ordinary reading frame, alanine is incorporated before termination. There is no reason to search for an AUG within the fragment, because the problem has already specified the reading frame and the fragment may begin internally. If the question instead asks whether the isolated fragment can autonomously initiate translation, the available information is insufficient to establish a normal initiation event.

An analogous issue occurs when a nucleotide sequence ends without a stop. If the end of the supplied sequence is merely the boundary of an excerpt, the final protein length is unknown. It is legitimate to report the amino acid string encoded by the complete triplets shown, but not to claim that the protein terminates after the last displayed codon. The absence of a depicted stop is not a stop signal. A full mRNA lacking a usable termination signal presents a different biological problem involving stalled ribosomes or quality control; that cannot be inferred simply from an abbreviated examination sequence.

When counting lengths, distinguish nucleotide coordinates from base-pair counts and residue numbers. A coding region of 600 nucleotides on one strand corresponds to 600 base pairs of double-stranded DNA if the relevant region is continuous and no extra bases are counted. It does not correspond to 1,200 base pairs merely because both strands contain 600 nucleotides. The double-stranded region contains 1,200 nucleotide residues in total but 600 paired positions. If the 600-nucleotide coding segment includes a terminal stop, its nascent peptide has 199 residues; if it consists exclusively of sense triplets and excludes the stop, it encodes 200. The difference of one triplet is small numerically but conceptually important.

The molecular mass of a peptide can sometimes be estimated from its length using a mean residue mass, often about 110 daltons per incorporated amino-acid residue for a rough protein-level estimate. A 200-residue chain would then be estimated at approximately 22,000 daltons, or 22 kilodaltons, before additional modifications. This is an approximation, not an exact relationship. Exact mass depends on the identities of the residues, terminal groups and modifications. Likewise, the mass of the full RNA cannot be obtained exactly from its length without knowing its composition and chemical state. A length-based estimate should be clearly distinguished from a sequence-specific calculation.

13. Degeneracy as a quantitative and biological property

The relation from a known RNA coding sequence to an ordinary peptide sequence is much less underdetermined than the reverse relation. Given a standard-code reading frame with no recoding, each codon has a specified assignment. Given only a peptide sequence, however, there are usually many possible RNA sequences that could encode it. Reverse translation is a problem of choosing among synonymous words. This difference is important in interpreting a protein sequence obtained from an experiment. It can constrain possible genes without uniquely determining every nucleotide in them.

Let d(a) be the number of standard sense codons assigned to amino acid a. For a specified sequence of n residues a1 through an, and independent choice of a synonymous codon at every position, the number of possible coding RNA sense sequences is the product d(a1) × d(a2) × ... × d(an). The independence assumption means that any synonymous codon is allowed at each position without additional constraints on initiation, RNA structure, splicing or expression. If the first residue is methionine and AUG is fixed as the only allowed initiator in the model, its multiplicative factor is one. If an ordinary terminal stop is also required and any of the three stop triplets is allowed, a further factor of three appears.

For the peptide Met-Ala-Gly-Lys-Trp, the degeneracies are 1, 4, 4, 2 and 1. The product is 32 possible sense-region strings. Allowing any of the three conventional stop codons gives 96 strings for the complete specified start-to-stop region. If a problem additionally fixes the lysine codon as AAG, the two alternatives at that position become one, reducing the total to 48 with a freely chosen stop. The calculation demonstrates how explicit biological or experimental restrictions change a combinatorial answer.

The possible coding sequences for a peptide of fixed length should not be confused with the total number of standard sense-codon strings of that length. If all sixty-one sense triplets may be independently chosen for each of n positions, the total number of sense-only nucleotide strings is 61^n. The number of possible amino-acid strings of the same length is 20^n. The mapping from the former set to the latter is uneven because different peptides have different products of degeneracy. A peptide containing many leucine residues has more synonymous coding possibilities than one of the same length containing only methionine and tryptophan.

For example, two-residue chains made from standard residues have 400 possible ordered amino-acid sequences. There are 61 squared, or 3,721, possible ordered pairs of sense codons. Dividing 3,721 by 400 gives 9.3025 possible encoding strings per peptide on average if peptide sequences are given equal weight. This average does not imply that any particular peptide has nine or ten encodings. Met-Trp has one, while Leu-Arg has thirty-six. The average describes the entire abstract sequence space. Examination questions often ask for a count for one specified peptide, in which case using the average would be incorrect.

A related probability question assumes that each of the 64 triplets is equally probable in a random nucleotide string with equal base frequencies. Under the standard code the probability of a stop in a given independent codon is 3/64, and the probability of a sense triplet is 61/64. The probability that n consecutive codons in a fixed frame are all sense is (61/64)^n. For ten codons, this value is approximately 0.619. The calculation assumes independent nucleotide choices and a frame already selected. It does not estimate the chance that a real genomic region is translated or that a random peptide would function.

The distribution of the number of sense codons preceding the first stop under that random model follows a geometric pattern. Let p = 3/64. The probability that exactly n sense codons precede the first stop is (1 − p)^n p. The expectation of this count is (1 − p)/p = 61/3, approximately 20.33 sense codons. If counting the stop-containing triplet as well, the expected total is 1/p = 64/3, approximately 21.33. Neither mean is a prediction for actual protein lengths, since natural coding sequences are subject to strong constraints on premature termination, biological function and initiation. The model instead provides a simple null expectation for randomly grouped sequence.

Real genomes also differ in base composition. If pA, pC, pG and pU denote independent base frequencies, the probability of a standard stop in a randomly grouped triplet is pU pA pA + pU pA pG + pU pG pA. In a toy example with A and U each at 0.30 and C and G each at 0.20, the three terms are 0.027, 0.018 and 0.018, giving a stop probability of 0.063. With a different base composition, the expected frequency can change even when the code table itself is unchanged. Statistical properties of random sequences are therefore not identical to properties of the genetic code.

The assumptions deserve as much attention as the arithmetic. Neighboring nucleotides in actual DNA or RNA may be correlated; initiation is not random; the distribution of synonymous codons reflects mutation, selection and other biological processes; and coding regions are not random samples drawn from the whole genome. A probability formula using independent bases is useful for reasoning in an explicitly artificial problem. It should not be used as empirical evidence about a particular species without sequence data and a model that accounts for those dependencies.

Degeneracy can reduce the amino-acid consequences of some nucleotide substitutions. A third-position U-to-C change in many twofold families preserves the original assignment, and any third-base change within a fourfold box preserves the assignment. This property permits variation in coding sequences while maintaining a protein's primary sequence. Nevertheless, the table is not a universal error-correcting code. Many single-nucleotide changes alter an amino acid, and some introduce premature stops. Even a synonymous change can influence other features of the mRNA. The code's organization supplies a pattern of robustness for particular classes of changes, not protection against all mutations.

14. Mutational effects as tests of decoding logic

A synonymous substitution changes the nucleotide sequence while preserving the amino-acid assignment at the affected codon under the applicable code. A missense substitution changes a sense codon into another sense codon that specifies a different residue. A nonsense or stop-gain substitution changes a sense codon to an ordinary stop. These terms describe consequences relative to a particular coding sequence and reading context. The same base substitution may have a different effect if it occurs in another frame or in a different translation system.

Take the ordinary internal glutamate codon GAA. A change to GAG is synonymous. A change to GUA produces a valine assignment and is missense. A change to UAA produces a conventional stop and is nonsense. The last substitution changes only the first base but can have a much larger consequence for peptide length than a substitution at another position. The code table, rather than the mere physical position of a mutation in the codon, determines its immediate classification.

The nine one-base substitution neighbors of a triplet can be enumerated because each of its three positions can change to any of the other three bases. For GAA, the first-position neighbors are AAA, CAA and UAA; the second-position neighbors are GCA, GGA and GUA; the third-position neighbors are GAC, GAG and GAU. There is one synonymous neighbor, GAG, and one stop, UAA. The remaining seven neighbors specify other amino acids. If a hypothetical problem assumes every single-position substitution alternative is equally likely, the probabilities of synonymous, nonsense and missense outcomes from this codon are 1/9, 1/9 and 7/9, respectively. Actual mutation probabilities may be unequal, so the stated assumption is necessary.

A fourfold codon such as GCU has three synonymous neighbors at its third position. Its other six single-base neighbors can be examined individually. The first-position variants UCU, CCU and ACU specify serine, proline and threonine. The second-position variants GAU, GGU and GUU specify aspartate, glycine and valine. All six are missense. The hypothetical equal-neighbor probabilities are 1/3 synonymous and 2/3 missense, with no single-substitution stop neighbor. A degeneracy of four guarantees three synonymous third-base alternatives in this box, but it is not enough by itself to determine whether other positions can also change without changing the assignment in more complex families.

Transition and transversion distinctions can sharpen a question. Transitions replace a purine with the other purine or a pyrimidine with the other pyrimidine, whereas transversions exchange bases between the two classes. In UUU, changing the third U to C is a transition and preserves phenylalanine. Changing the third U to A or G is a transversion and produces leucine. These observations do not establish that every transition is synonymous or every transversion is missense. They illustrate that mutation spectra can interact with the organization of the code. A probability model that weights transitions more heavily than transversions will give different outcomes from the equal-neighbor model.

In sickle hemoglobin, the familiar glutamate-to-valine change in the beta-globin chain is often represented by the coding RNA change GAG to GUG. The example connects a single base change to a change in residue chemistry: glutamate normally has an acidic side chain, while valine has a nonpolar side chain. The physical consequences of the substitution depend on its structural location within hemoglobin and the interactions of the altered proteins. The code table can establish the substitution in primary sequence, but understanding the full disease mechanism also requires protein structure and physiology. The same Glu-to-Val change in an unrelated protein would not necessarily produce the same phenotype.

A stop-gain mutation can shorten a nascent chain, but the effect on the detectable protein depends on several biological events. In an appropriate eukaryotic transcript context, premature termination may trigger RNA surveillance and reduce transcript abundance. Some truncated products are unstable or rapidly degraded. Others may persist and retain partial function. A student should not automatically equate a premature stop with a stable protein of the length predicted by the codon table. That prediction describes the possible nascent chain before additional regulation and degradation are considered.

Insertions and deletions require careful grouping. In the reference coding sequence AUG-GCU-AAA-CCG-UAA, the product is Met-Ala-Lys-Pro. If the first G of GCU is deleted, the resulting sequence is AUG-CUA-AAC-CGU-AA, whose displayed complete codons specify Met-Leu-Asn-Arg. The terminal AA is incomplete in this excerpt; the original UAA is no longer in the same phase. The mutation changes sequence information after the deletion, not merely the single alanine assignment. The exact new termination point cannot be predicted without more downstream sequence. The example contrasts sharply with a one-base substitution, which does not shift grouping in an ordinary reading event.

If instead the complete GCU triplet is deleted, the sequence becomes AUG-AAA-CCG-UAA. The new nascent chain is Met-Lys-Pro. The downstream grouping and terminal stop remain aligned because three nucleotides were removed at a codon boundary. A three-base deletion that begins within a codon can affect the identities of adjacent residues by forming a new junction codon, even though the downstream phase is preserved. Thus the general rule for multiples of three is preservation of phase, not necessarily deletion of exactly one intact original amino acid for every three bases removed.

Compensatory changes can occur at different molecular levels. An insertion followed by a deletion may restore a reading phase at the sequence level. A suppressor tRNA may instead recognize a triplet that normally terminates translation, allowing some ribosomes to continue without changing the original mutant RNA sequence. The latter case does not repair the DNA sequence. It changes the decoding environment. A nonsense suppressor may insert a residue different from the one originally encoded, so full-length protein production does not prove complete restoration of the original primary sequence.

Consider a reporter with a normal UAU tyrosine codon changed to UAG. A suppressor tRNA able to recognize UAG could restore synthesis of a full-length product in an appropriate system. If the inserted residue happens to be tyrosine, the original residue may be restored at that site. If the suppressor delivers another amino acid, the product is full length but altered. A comparison of reporter activity, product size and sequence identity is therefore more informative than product size alone. This kind of reasoning links the code to genetic suppression without needing to introduce the detailed inheritance patterns reserved for the genetics unit.

15. Integrating decoding with experimental interpretation

The availability of a complete codon table makes sequence translation appear deterministic, yet experiments measure outputs of real biological systems. A reduction in a reporter's protein abundance may be caused by fewer mRNA molecules, less frequent initiation, slower or failed elongation, changes in amino-acid incorporation, premature termination, or increased degradation. Identifying a synonymous or nonsense mutation is a valuable initial clue, but the observed phenotype must be interpreted by considering the intervening steps. The code specifies a local assignment under given conditions; the experiment tests which biological process actually occurred.

Suppose a synonymous substitution converts GCU to GCC in a reporter. A researcher measures the same mRNA abundance in wild-type and mutant cells, but the mutant protein accumulates to only half the reference amount. Since both codons specify alanine, the result cannot be explained by a conventional missense substitution at that position. Possible explanations include altered translation efficiency, changes in mRNA folding that affect access by the translation machinery, or a change in the stability of the protein product mediated indirectly through synthesis. Equal steady-state mRNA abundance does not establish equal translational initiation rates or protein turnover. Further controls are needed to separate these explanations.

A comparison of newly synthesized protein over a short measurement interval with steady-state protein abundance can help distinguish reduced synthesis from increased degradation. If newly synthesized product accumulates normally while steady-state protein is reduced, altered turnover is a plausible contributor. If newly synthesized product is also lower, reduced synthesis is supported, although the exact translation step is not yet identified. These are broad experimental inferences suitable for the genetic-code chapter. The detailed analysis of translational regulation and protein degradation will be developed in subsequent nodes.

Another experiment may compare reporters differing only in which synonymous codons occur within an otherwise identical coding sequence. If supplying a relevant tRNA improves the expression of one variant preferentially, the result supports a role for that tRNA's decoding availability. A nonmatching tRNA and empty-vector controls help establish specificity. Measurements of mRNA and peptide identity help ensure that the apparent improvement is not caused by unrelated changes in transcript supply or production of an abnormal product. Such experiments illustrate how wobble and tRNA repertoire can matter biologically without treating the standard amino acid assignments as variable.

Ribosome-associated RNA fragments provide another way to infer which reading phase is being used. In a typical ribosome-profiling interpretation, protected fragments from translated coding regions may exhibit three-nucleotide periodicity after suitable alignment and assignment of ribosomal positions. A strong periodicity in one frame supports its use, whereas a shift in downstream periodicity may indicate a change in decoding phase. However, library preparation, mapping ambiguity and the method of assigning the relevant ribosome position can influence observed patterns. A local signal must be considered alongside independent protein and sequence evidence. The idea is useful here because it relates modern observations back to the original question of fixed triplet grouping.

In a hypothetical educational experiment, 90% of appropriately mapped ribosome positions upstream of a candidate shift fall in the original frame, whereas 80% of positions downstream fall in a different phase. A variant that disrupts a candidate RNA signal restores the original downstream periodicity. This pattern is consistent with a sequence-dependent change in reading phase, but it does not by itself establish the exact molecular mechanism. An alternative overlapping coding region could contribute to the signal. A reporter designed to isolate the two phases, combined with peptide evidence and the appropriate sequence controls, would distinguish these interpretations more effectively.

The connection between decoding and protein identity is also important in biotechnology. A standard coding sequence can often be expressed in a different organism because the ordinary code is shared, but promoters, RNA-processing requirements, initiation signals, tRNA abundance and protein maturation may differ. A eukaryotic genomic fragment containing introns may not be decoded correctly by a bacterial expression system simply because both use the standard genetic code. A suitable processed coding sequence is needed, and expression signals must be compatible. The broad conservation of codon assignments is a necessary advantage for recombinant expression, not a guarantee that every native gene can function unchanged in every host.

Finally, the code provides a link between evolutionary comparison and molecular mechanism. Two homologous coding sequences can differ at many nucleotide positions yet encode identical or closely related proteins. Synonymous differences help explain why DNA divergence may exceed amino-acid divergence. Conversely, conserved residues across organisms may indicate functional or structural constraints, although conservation alone is not proof of a specific role. The later genetics and evolution units examine these patterns more fully. At this stage the student should be able to explain the underlying mapping: multiple nucleotide words may produce the same residue, whereas changes crossing assignment boundaries may alter protein sequence.

The concepts of this chapter converge on a single causal picture. The nucleotide message must have a defined direction and a chosen grouping. Its triplets must be interpreted through the applicable code. Adaptors must recognize those triplets and carry the correct amino acids. Initiation and termination define the boundaries of an ordinary translation event, while limited wobble helps a finite adaptor repertoire cover synonymous assignments. Exceptions show why the biological context must always accompany the table. With this framework in place, the next chapter can examine how a tRNA becomes correctly associated with its amino acid and how synthetase proofreading protects the correspondence that the genetic code requires.

The smallest q satisfying 3q divisible by 5 is 5. The result describes grouping; a real stop codon could prevent completion of the cycle.

Question 42

A chain has 230 residues and an assumed mean residue mass of 110 Da. Estimate its mass and minimum sense coding length.

Worked answer

25.3 kDa; 690 nt

230×110=25,300 Da; 230×3=690 nt. Including an ordinary stop gives 693 nt. The mass is approximate and excludes added modifications.

Question 43

Assume classical wobble coverage for one fourfold, one threefold and one twofold synonymous group in separate first-two-base boxes. Minimum anticodon types?

Worked answer

Four

Two types cover the fourfold box, one inosine type covers U/C/A in the threefold box, and one compatible type covers the twofold group. The calculation assumes the groups have the appropriate endings.

Question 44

Translate the template DNA written 5′-TTA TGG AAA GTC CAT-3′ assuming a complete standard coding region.

Worked answer

Met-Asp-Phe-Pro

Reverse-complement into mRNA 5′-AUG GAC UUU CCA UAA-3′. Read the four sense codons, then stop.

Question 45

A sequence is 5′-AUG GCU AAA CCG UAA-3′. Remove the initial G of GCU. Translate every complete displayed codon.

Worked answer

Met-Leu-Asn-Arg; termination not established

The altered sequence groups AUG-CUA-AAC-CGU-AA. The remaining AA is incomplete. The old stop is no longer in phase; downstream sequence is needed to predict eventual termination.

Question 46

Two coding exons contribute AUGGC and UAAAUAA in their RNA order. What is the spliced coding product?

Worked answer

Met-Ala-Lys

Join to AUGGCUAAAUAA, regroup AUG-GCU-AAA-UAA. GCU crosses the junction; independent exon translation would fail.

Question 47

Compare standard and vertebrate mitochondrial translation of AUG-AUA-UGA-GCU-UAA, with AUG stipulated as the start.

Worked answer

Standard: Met-Ile; vertebrate mitochondrial: Met-Met-Trp-Ala

In standard decoding AUA is Ile and UGA stops. In table 2 AUA is Met and UGA is Trp; termination is at UAA.

Question 48

Hypothetical binding assay: labelled Phe-tRNA retention is 30 without triplet, 850 with UUU, and 35 with GCU. What is the strongest inference and one critical control?

Worked answer

UUU specifically promotes Phe-tRNA association with ribosomes; include a no-ribosome control

The large specific increase supports recognition rather than general nucleotide binding. No-ribosome retention assesses nonspecific filter binding. A full synthesis assay would additionally test incorporation.

Question 49

Hypothetical reporter signals for wild type, +1, +2 and +3 insertions are 100, 3, 2 and 72. What does the pattern support, and what does 72 not prove?

Worked answer

Triplet-phase restoration; not complete restoration of original protein

Three added bases preserve downstream phase. Activity recovery can coexist with altered junction residues, changes in abundance or partial loss of function.

Question 50

A 5′-IGC-3′ anticodon loses its inosine modification and now contains A at position 34. Predict the simple-model targets before and after.

Worked answer

GCU/GCC/GCA before; GCU after

I34 permits U/C/A; A34 pairs with U. Other tRNAs may compensate in a cell, so reduced recognition range is more certain than the whole-cell phenotype.

Question 51

A reporter has a premature UAG. A suppressor tRNA restores full-length product but activity remains 20% of wild type. Explain.

Worked answer

Decoding may continue with a wrong residue or inefficient suppression; length alone does not establish normal function

Sequence the product at the suppressed site and quantify full-length abundance. A changed residue or lower yield can account for reduced activity. The DNA mutation need not be repaired.

Question 52

Hypothetical reporters contain an identical internal UGA; only one has a functional SECIS. Full-length protein occurs chiefly with SECIS. What conclusion is supported, and what needs testing?

Worked answer

Context-dependent readthrough/recoding is supported; verify Sec identity and dependence on the specialized machinery

The contextual signal argues against a universal sense assignment in that system. Product length alone cannot identify the inserted residue.

Question 53

A synonymous GCU→GCC variant has unchanged mRNA abundance but half the steady-state protein level. Is a missense mechanism supported? Which measurements help?

Worked answer

No conventional missense effect; measure newly synthesized protein and protein turnover

Both encode Ala. Altered initiation/elongation or degradation can change accumulation. Equal steady-state RNA does not prove equal protein production.

Question 54

Ribosome footprints show phase 0 upstream, phase +1 downstream. Mutation of a candidate signal removes the shift. Is programmed frameshifting proved?

Worked answer

Supported, not conclusively proved

Check mapping, potential overlapping ORFs and actual peptide sequence. The signal-sensitive phase change is compatible with programmed frameshifting but is not uniquely diagnostic.

Question 55

A bacterial expression construct containing a eukaryotic genomic gene produces an aberrant peptide. Why does standard-code conservation not rule this out?

Worked answer

RNA processing and expression contexts differ between organisms

Introns may be retained in a bacterial transcript; promoters, initiation signals and tRNA availability can differ. The shared code only ensures ordinary assignment given the proper message and context.

Question 56

A UAU codon is changed to UAG in a vertebrate nuclear gene. A second mutation converts UAG to UAC. Classify both changes relative to their immediate precursor and compare final product with original.

Worked answer

First stop gain; second stop loss; final Tyr restored

UAU and UAC both specify Tyr. UAG is stop. The final local assignment matches wild type, despite a changed nucleotide sequence; expression effects remain possible.

Examination traps

  1. Codons are read 5′→3′, not from the written left edge of a reverse-oriented template.
  2. The first transcribed nucleotide is not necessarily the first translated nucleotide.
  3. Do not count an ordinary stop as an amino acid.
  4. An internal AUG is not necessarily an initiation event.
  5. A bacterial GUG start can specify fMet even though internal GUG specifies valine.
  6. Degeneracy is not ordinary ambiguity.
  7. Wobble affects the third codon base and first written anticodon base.
  8. Inosine does not recognize all four bases under the classical model.
  9. The third codon position is not always synonymous.
  10. An out-of-frame UAA does not terminate the selected frame.
  11. A three-base indel preserves phase but can alter junction residues.
  12. A full-length suppressor product need not have the original residue.
  13. Alternative mitochondrial assignments are not universal across organelles.
  14. An ORF prediction is not proof of expression.
  15. Synonymous does not imply no change in expression.
