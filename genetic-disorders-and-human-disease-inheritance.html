From genetic variation to disease

A genetic disorder is best understood by following a causal chain, rather than by memorising a list of names. A DNA sequence or chromosome configuration changes; that change modifies the quantity, structure, location or regulation of a biological product; a cell or tissue can no longer perform some function normally; the resulting disturbance contributes to a phenotype. The phenotype may be a laboratory measurement, an anatomical difference, a physiological impairment or a developmental characteristic. Each link in this chain is a separate biological question. A pedigree can tell us that a characteristic travels with a particular allele without telling us how that allele alters protein function. Conversely, a biochemical defect measured in a tissue can suggest a mechanism without identifying the route by which the defect was inherited.

It is important to distinguish a genetic condition from an inherited condition. A genetic alteration may arise for the first time in a parental gamete, during early embryogenesis or in a later somatic lineage. Only a change present in the cells that contribute genetic material to offspring can be transmitted through that individual. A child with a newly arisen constitutional variant can therefore have a genetic condition without a history of that condition in either parent's phenotype. A mosaic person contains cell populations with different genotypes derived from the same zygote; the distribution of these populations can matter as much as the identity of the variant. A blood sample may not represent the genetic composition of every organ.

The term pathogenic variant refers to a variant supported by evidence as a cause of disease in an appropriate genetic context. It does not mean that every sequence difference is harmful. Many variants have no detectable adverse effect. Some affect disease probability only in combination with other variants or conditions. Others produce a molecular change but have no readily detectable phenotype because an organism has redundant pathways, excess enzymatic capacity or compensatory regulation. Thus genetic causality should not be inferred simply from an unusual sequence or from an observation made in one individual.

Four broad classes are useful for organising human disease inheritance. In monogenic disorders, variation at one locus is the principal genetic cause in a family. A chromosome disorder involves a change in chromosome number or the organisation of a segment, often altering dosage across multiple genes. Mitochondrial disorders may result from nuclear or mitochondrial variants, but only those caused by mitochondrial DNA generally follow maternal transmission. Complex disorders involve multiple genetic contributions and non-genetic influences. The categories are mechanistic descriptions rather than mutually exclusive boxes: a mitochondrial enzyme encoded in the nucleus may be involved in a monogenic autosomal condition, and a single-gene disease may show substantial variation owing to genetic modifiers.

For an examination problem, identify the level at which the observation was made. A karyogram showing an extra chromosome supplies cytological information. A protein band on an electrophoretic gel supplies molecular information. Affected siblings with unaffected parents supply segregation information. A stronger explanation combines independent lines of evidence and asks whether any alternative model makes the same predictions. This approach also prevents a common error: assuming a recognisable clinical phenotype uniquely identifies a specific mutation.

Mendelian disease models and probability

Consider a diploid autosomal locus with alleles A and a. In a simplified recessive model AA and Aa have no disease phenotype, whereas aa is affected. The heterozygote produces A-bearing and a-bearing gametes with equal probability. Two heterozygotes therefore produce AA, Aa, aA and aa zygotes in equal expected proportions, giving genotype probabilities one quarter, one half and one quarter after combining the two heterozygous outcomes. A common biological basis for this model is that a single functional allele produces sufficient activity for normal tissue performance. This is not a universal explanation for every recessive disorder, nor does it mean heterozygotes have exactly normal enzyme activity.

Suppose an unaffected sibling of a child with a fully penetrant recessive disorder has two known heterozygous parents. Conditioning on being unaffected removes the aa outcome. Among the remaining outcomes, two of three are heterozygous. The probability of carrying the allele is therefore two thirds, not one half. If an unrelated partner is known to carry the same disease-associated allele, the probability of an affected child in the simplified model is two thirds multiplied by one quarter, or one sixth. These numbers describe a hypothetical pedigree with specified assumptions, not a general clinical recurrence estimate.

In an autosomal dominant model a single altered allele can produce the defined disease phenotype. A heterozygote and an individual lacking the variant transmit the variant to one half of offspring on average. Dominance describes a relationship between alleles with respect to the phenotype being measured. It does not imply that a variant is frequent, evolutionarily advantageous or necessarily biochemically active. A loss-of-function variant can be dominant when half the usual gene product is insufficient. A protein that interferes with the wild-type product may also produce dominance, as may a novel or excessive activity.

Penetrance is the probability of expressing a specified phenotype given a specified genotype in a defined context. If a heterozygous parent transmits a dominant variant with probability one half and the penetrance in offspring under the conditions of the problem is 0.8, the probability of an offspring expressing the condition through that variant is 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4. This calculation assumes no background phenocopies and that penetrance is applicable to the offspring's age and environment. Expressivity, in contrast, concerns the nature or severity of the phenotype among those who express it. A pedigree that appears to skip a generation need not exclude a dominant model if penetrance is incomplete or if disease manifests later in life.

For an X-linked locus outside the pseudoautosomal regions, a father typically transmits his X chromosome to daughters and his Y chromosome to sons. Under the conventional XX/XY inheritance model, a heterozygous mother and a father lacking the variant have a one-half probability of an affected son conditional on the child being a son, if the variant is fully penetrant in hemizygous males. With equal probabilities for the two chromosomal sexes, the joint probability of an affected son among all births is one quarter. The distinction between a conditional and a joint probability is essential. Heterozygous females can also show manifestations, depending on the gene, X-inactivation pattern and other factors. The simplified carrier category is useful for solving a stated genetic model but must not be confused with a universal absence of symptoms.

Independent gamete unions explain why an earlier birth does not alter the next birth's segregation probability when parental genotypes and assumptions remain fixed. On the other hand, new family information can change our uncertainty about parental genotypes, so the probability calculated by an observer can change. This distinguishes biological independence from statistical learning.

Functional reserve and the relationship between allele dosage and phenotype

A familiar way to introduce recessive inheritance is to say that one healthy copy of a gene is sufficient. That statement is a useful starting point, but an explanation should show how a cell can function with less product. Consider an enzyme catalysing a reaction in a pathway. Its maximum possible catalytic rate depends partly on the amount of active enzyme. If normal cells produce substantially more catalytic capacity than is required to maintain the relevant metabolic flux, a heterozygous loss of activity may leave adequate reserve. A tissue may therefore behave normally across a considerable range of enzyme concentrations even though the biochemical activity measured in a laboratory is reduced.

The relationship between enzyme activity and pathway flux is rarely a universally linear function. A metabolic pathway includes several reactions, substrate supply, transport and regulatory feedback. Reducing one enzyme to half its original concentration might cause only a small change in the total flux if another reaction limits the pathway under the tested conditions. Conversely, a reduction in the same enzyme may become important when physiological demand rises. This conceptual distinction explains why a recessive allele can have a measurable biochemical effect in a heterozygote without causing the same phenotype as two pathogenic alleles.

Construct an illustrative, deliberately simplified example. Let a normal diploid genotype produce 100 units of active protein, a heterozygous null produce 50 units, and a homozygous null produce zero. If a particular tissue needs at least 35 units for its ordinary function, the heterozygote retains sufficient capacity. A recessive phenotype is predicted. If a different protein is required at a threshold of 75 units and has no compensation, the corresponding heterozygote falls below that threshold. In this second example, a heterozygous loss-of-function variant is sufficient to disturb the phenotype. The thresholds are invented teaching values, not measurements from a human disorder. The central principle is the interaction of gene dosage with the functional demand of a tissue.

A dominant-negative model makes a different prediction. Suppose a protein functions as a dimer, two alleles produce equal amounts of monomer, and monomers associate at random. The heterozygote produces a population in which half of monomers are wild type and half are altered. The expected fractions of dimers are one quarter wild-type homodimers, one half mixed dimers and one quarter altered homodimers. If any altered subunit destroys the activity of a dimer, only one quarter of the assembled dimers are functional. A simple heterozygous null, in which all produced monomers are normal, does not generate mixed inactive dimers. Distinguishing a reduction in production from interference with activity matters for the interpretation of structural protein disorders.

The dimer calculation is conditional on equal expression, equal assembly probabilities and complete disruption by one altered subunit. If the mutant monomer fails to assemble, the random-association model is inappropriate. If mixed dimers retain partial activity, the predicted fraction of functional activity changes. These conditions illustrate how molecular assumptions enter a genetic explanation. A familiar Mendelian ratio can remain unchanged while the underlying biochemistry differs greatly.

Penetrance can be represented as a population of individuals with the same defined genotype but a distribution of functional reserves. The phenotype may be present only among those whose physiological demands exceed their reserve. Expressivity concerns variation in which tissues are affected or how strongly they are affected once the phenotype is manifest. Modifiers can alter expression of the causal gene, stability of its product or activity of interacting pathways. Environmental conditions can alter the demand placed on the tissue. This framework gives students a mechanistic way to interpret why a genotype is not always a precise prediction of an individual's phenotype.

For example, a hypothetical dominant genotype has 80 percent penetrance in the age range under consideration. Among 100 carriers we expect roughly 80 expressing individuals, not necessarily exactly 80. If a particular sample contains 75, sampling variation is one possible explanation. If a sample includes a much younger group, age-dependent expression may also change the estimate. Penetrance is conditional on the defined phenotype, age and sampling context. A report that simply states a gene is 80 percent penetrant without those qualifications can invite mistaken comparisons.

Family ascertainment introduces another source of bias. A study that recruits families because several relatives express a phenotype may overrepresent families with strong expression. In contrast, sampling unselected carriers can reveal apparently unaffected individuals who would not have been noticed in a phenotype-based survey. This does not change the molecular defect, but it changes the population from which a penetrance estimate is calculated. It is useful to distinguish the probability of observing a genotype among affected individuals from the probability of being affected given that genotype. These two conditional probabilities answer different questions and cannot generally be interchanged.

Sickle cell disease: a structural protein defect

Adult haemoglobin A is a tetramer formed from two alpha and two beta globin chains. Each chain contains a haem group, enabling reversible oxygen binding. Red cells require a high concentration of soluble haemoglobin to transport oxygen while remaining flexible enough to move through narrow vessels. Sickle cell disease illustrates what can happen when a sequence change leaves much of a protein's normal function intact but gives the molecule an abnormal interaction with other copies of itself.

The classic sickle allele of HBB changes a glutamate residue to valine at position six of the mature beta globin chain, conventionally called beta 6 Glu to Val. The numbering is seven when the initiator methionine is included in the reference protein sequence. Glutamate has a negatively charged side chain at physiological pH, while valine has a nonpolar side chain. This substitution creates a hydrophobic interaction site on haemoglobin S. In the deoxygenated state, complementary surfaces on neighbouring HbS molecules can associate, and repeated intermolecular contacts create long polymers. The primary abnormality is not simply that haemoglobin cannot bind oxygen.

Polymer formation changes the mechanics of the red cell. A flexible membrane is distorted around rigid intracellular fibres. Repeated deformation and associated cellular damage shorten red-cell survival; the resulting haemolysis can contribute to anaemia. Reduced deformability and interactions among blood and vascular cells also contribute to impaired blood flow. This explains why a molecular event at the surface of one protein has consequences at the level of a tissue. The processes interact, but haemolysis and vascular obstruction are not interchangeable terms.

Pauling, Itano and colleagues in 1949 investigated whether haemoglobin from individuals with sickle cell anaemia differed in physical properties from ordinary haemoglobin. Electrophoretic comparisons established a difference in mobility, and heterozygote samples showed components corresponding to the two haemoglobin forms. The experiment connected an inherited phenotype with a measurable molecular property. It did not by itself establish the exact amino acid substitution. Ingram's later peptide comparisons identified a specific chemical difference between globins; subsequent sequence work established the substitution. This sequence of findings is historically significant because it shows how progressively more resolved measurements can narrow a causal model.

Consider a hypothetical separation with an HbA reference in one lane, HbS in a second and an unknown sample showing both bands in a third. With no additional haemoglobin variants and appropriate controls, the mixed pattern supports production of both protein forms. It does not demonstrate a simple dominant pattern for the disease itself. At the molecular level the two alleles can both be expressed, whereas clinically important disease in the classical HbAA, HbAS and HbSS comparison is generally recessive. A heterozygote can nevertheless have circumstances in which the altered protein matters. Molecular codominance and recessive disease expression are not contradictory because they refer to different phenotype definitions.

A person who inherits HbS and a beta-thalassaemia allele can also have sickle cell disease. The second allele may provide reduced or absent beta globin production rather than HbS. The outcome depends on the particular alleles and their effects on the mixture of globin chains. Sickle cell disease therefore includes more than homozygosity for the HbS allele. The comparison sets up the next question: how does producing an abnormal globin differ from producing too little of an otherwise necessary globin?

Interpreting haemoglobin evidence and developmental switches

A hemoglobin separation experiment is an opportunity to distinguish molecular identity from abundance. Electrophoretic migration depends on the physical properties of the molecular species and the experimental conditions, including buffer pH. A comparison should therefore include known reference preparations examined under the same conditions. A migration difference between two samples can support a difference in molecular properties but does not reveal the exact sequence automatically. Two distinct proteins may sometimes migrate similarly, and quantitative abundance cannot be inferred reliably from the mere presence of a visible band.

Consider a hypothetical data table with a reference HbA band and an HbS band. Sample P contains only a band matching HbA, sample Q shows both A and S, and sample R contains a band matching HbS with no detectable HbA. In a deliberately limited model of the A and S alleles, P is consistent with HbAA, Q with HbAS and R with HbSS. The assumptions are essential. For example, absence of HbA in R is also compatible with HbS paired with an allele producing no beta globin. Other haemoglobin variants and recent changes in the cellular composition of a sample can complicate an interpretation. A band pattern should be described as compatible with a model, not as unconditional proof of a specific genotype.

The detection of two haemoglobin forms in a heterozygote reveals a molecular phenotype of codominance. To see why this does not require a dominant disease phenotype, distinguish product expression from polymerisation. A cell containing both proteins can have a lower concentration of HbS than a cell whose beta chain output consists predominantly of the sickle form. Formation of a polymer depends on intermolecular encounters and the availability of compatible interacting surfaces. The relationship between the fraction of sickle globin and the rate or extent of polymerisation is therefore not required to be linear. The cell's hydration state and oxygenation conditions also influence the environment in which these molecules interact.

In an illustrative reasoning experiment, compare three samples containing an identical total haemoglobin concentration. One contains only ordinary HbA, a second contains a mixture with a substantial fraction of HbS, and a third contains predominantly HbS. Examine each at high and low oxygen availability. A prediction from the polymerisation model is that formation of fibres is strongly favoured in samples containing HbS under deoxygenated conditions. A suitable control is the HbA preparation exposed to the same conditions. If all preparations instead became equally rigid in the absence of oxygen, the explanation would need to account for an effect independent of the specific HbS interaction. Designing a control that isolates the proposed causal difference is more informative than merely describing the result expected for one sample.

The developmental change in haemoglobin composition supplies a second independent line of biological reasoning. Before birth, haemoglobin F, which contains gamma rather than beta globin chains, has an important role in oxygen transfer. Following birth, beta globin expression increases while gamma globin expression normally declines. A defect in the adult beta chain may thus become more conspicuous following the developmental transition even though the genotype was already present. This is an example of an age-dependent molecular context, not a newly occurring mutation. Genotype and onset need not coincide in time.

An increase in gamma globin expression can change the mixture of haemoglobin molecules available in red cells. In sickle cell disease, HbF can inhibit HbS polymerisation; variation in fetal haemoglobin contributes to phenotypic variation. In beta-thalassaemia, production of gamma chains can also provide an alternative partner for alpha globin. The relevant effects are different: one concerns the molecular interactions involved in polymerisation and the other concerns chain assembly and balance. Similar developmental modifiers can therefore affect distinct disorders through different biochemical mechanisms. The example is appropriate for inheritance biology because it makes the relation between a major allele and modifier loci explicit.

A comparison of alpha and beta globin synthesis further illustrates the limits of counting gene copies. In normal cells, the expression of the two linked alpha globin genes is regulated; their contributions are not necessarily identical. A deletion can alter regulatory context as well as remove a coding sequence. A count of functional alpha loci is a strong introductory predictor for typical deletional alpha-thalassaemia, but equal counts do not guarantee identical output. When a question asks whether cis and trans arrangements with two functional copies are identical, the appropriate answer depends on the measured phenotype: they can be similar for some dosage-related traits but give different gametes and therefore different inheritance predictions.

A further phase problem illustrates the point. Cross a parent with cis loss --/αα to a parent with trans loss -α/-α. The first produces -- or αα gametes, while the second produces -α gametes in the simplified model. The offspring genotypes are --/-α and αα/-α in equal probabilities. They contain one and three functional alpha loci, respectively. No --/-- offspring are predicted because the trans parent does not transmit a chromosome lacking both alpha genes under the stated assumptions. The example uses chromosome organisation rather than independent assortment of four freely segregating loci.

These arguments also make it clear why structural and quantitative haemoglobin disorders can overlap. An individual may inherit a structural allele from one parent and a reduced-production allele from the other. Because both alleles contribute to the total chain pool, their combined phenotype cannot always be predicted by averaging the names of the two disorders. At a genetic level the individual is a compound heterozygote at HBB. At a molecular level, the products and their relative abundance determine the biochemical context. Students should move between these levels deliberately rather than treat a genetic label as the mechanism.

Thalassaemia: globin dosage and chain balance

Haemoglobin assembly depends on coordinated globin production. A decrease in the production of one class of chain reduces the availability of correctly assembled tetramers and can leave an excess of its partner. Excess unpaired chains can damage developing erythroid cells. Thalassaemias illustrate this quantitative and stoichiometric mechanism; their defining defect is reduced synthesis of alpha or beta globin rather than the classic sickle polymerisation mechanism.

Beta-thalassaemia is associated with HBB variants that decrease beta globin production. In traditional notation a beta-zero allele yields no beta globin from that allele, while a beta-plus allele retains reduced output. Regulatory variants can lower transcription, and certain splice-site variants reduce the amount of correctly processed RNA. Other changes can interfere with translation or stability. Different DNA changes in the same locus can thus converge on reduced protein production, and residual output helps explain phenotypic differences. Excess alpha globin damages developing erythroid cells, causing ineffective erythropoiesis as well as contributing to haemolysis. The full severity is modified by other loci and the distribution of globin types.

Alpha-thalassaemia introduces a different dosage arrangement. Normally a person has two alpha globin genes on each chromosome 16, HBA1 and HBA2, giving four alpha globin gene copies in a typical diploid genome. Deletions are a frequent cause of alpha-thalassaemia. Losing one copy commonly produces a silent carrier state; losing two produces a trait phenotype; loss or inactivation of three is associated with HbH disease; and loss of all four is associated with Hb Bart syndrome. This is a useful progression, but non-deletion alleles and differences between individual genes mean that a simple count is not a complete severity model.

The two-copy loss can occur in cis, with both losses on the same homolog, or in trans, with one loss on each. The total number of functional copies is identical in the two arrangements, but gamete composition differs. If both parents have a cis deletion represented as --/αα, each produces -- and αα gametes in equal proportions under the basic model. One quarter of their offspring are expected to inherit --/--, one half --/αα and one quarter αα/αα. If both parents instead have the trans arrangement -α/-α, each transmits a chromosome carrying one remaining alpha gene in the simplified model without recombination. All offspring then inherit -α/-α. Comparing the two families demonstrates why phase matters even when a total copy number measurement is identical.

Developmental regulation further changes the consequences of globin imbalance. Fetal haemoglobin contains alpha and gamma chains; adult haemoglobin A contains alpha and beta chains. A disturbance of alpha chain supply can therefore affect haemoglobin assembly during fetal as well as adult life, while severe beta chain shortage becomes particularly evident as the developmental shift to beta globin takes place. Excess gamma chains can assemble into Hb Bart (γ4), and excess beta chains can assemble into HbH (β4). Their properties differ from normal oxygen transport tetramers. These examples tie gene dosage to developmental gene expression rather than treating haemoglobin as a static molecular species.

Cystic fibrosis and haemophilia: transport versus cascade defects

Epithelial cells maintain fluid composition across a selectively permeable barrier. CFTR encodes a membrane protein that functions as a regulated anion channel, transporting chloride and contributing to bicarbonate transport. When CFTR function is substantially reduced, epithelial ion movement and associated water balance are altered. In the airway, disruption of the surface environment can impair mucus clearance; in other organs the consequences depend on the local transport physiology. The molecular deficit therefore affects several organs, an illustration of pleiotropy.

The common F508del variant removes a phenylalanine residue, disrupting normal CFTR folding and processing so that much of the protein fails to reach the cell surface. Residual molecules can also have altered channel behaviour. Other CFTR variants can primarily reduce channel opening, reduce conductance or decrease expression. It follows that a sequence variant need not eliminate protein synthesis to impair function. A cell with abundant protein at the wrong location can have lower membrane transport than a cell with fewer molecules correctly positioned.

In a conceptual comparison of cultured epithelial samples, an assay of total CFTR protein and an assay of cell-surface CFTR protein answer different questions. If total protein is detectable but membrane protein is greatly reduced, defective processing or trafficking is plausible. If surface abundance is preserved but ion current remains low, a defect in activity becomes more plausible. Neither observation alone identifies the DNA change. Experiments must control for cell number, sample loading and conditions of stimulation. Such inference is the bridge between a recessive family pattern and the molecular processes of the affected cells.

Haemophilia illustrates a distinct mechanism. The coagulation network amplifies signals leading to thrombin generation and fibrin formation. Factor VIII supports factor IXa in the intrinsic tenase complex, which facilitates activation of factor X. Loss or reduction of either factor can impair the generation of sufficient downstream activity even though many other components are present. Haemophilia A is classically associated with F8 variants and haemophilia B with F9 variants. Both genes are X-linked, and their disorders can resemble one another phenotypically despite different molecular causes.

This similarity is an example of locus heterogeneity: variants at more than one genetic locus can produce related phenotypes through a shared pathway. It also illustrates why a cascade should be analysed as a sequence of dependent activities. Increasing a downstream substrate does not necessarily restore the missing catalytic or cofactor step. In a hypothetical activity assay, low factor VIII activity with preserved factor IX activity supports a different functional deficit from preserved factor VIII with low factor IX activity. The assays do not, by themselves, prove the inheritance route or distinguish inherited defects from all acquired causes.

An X-linked pedigree can be consistent with either F8 or F9 variation. A molecular or functional observation is needed to distinguish them. The exam-relevant point is that pedigree geometry specifies a class of inheritance, not a unique gene.

Experimental reasoning across channel, matrix and coagulation disorders

Several monogenic diseases appear to share a simple loss-of-function pattern in pedigrees but differ in the experiments required to understand them. A membrane transporter, an extracellular matrix component and a soluble coagulation protein have different physical roles. It is therefore useful to select functional measurements that correspond to the normal job of each product rather than rely on a generic test of protein abundance.

For a membrane channel, a conceptual model of current is I = N × i × Popen, where N is the number of channels in the membrane, i is the average current through a single open channel at the specified driving force, and Popen is the probability of a channel being open. The variables describe different biological processes. A trafficking defect can reduce N without reducing the conductance of the few channels that arrive. A gating defect can lower Popen while leaving membrane abundance comparatively intact. A change in the pore can alter i. The expression is a simplified expectation for a collection of similar independently behaving channels measured under fixed conditions; actual epithelial transport also depends on other membrane proteins and driving forces.

Suppose wild-type cells have 100 arbitrary units of surface channel abundance and an observed current of 100 units under fixed conditions. Sample A has 20 units of surface abundance and 20 units of current. Sample B has 100 units of surface abundance and 20 units of current. One plausible interpretation is that sample A has reduced delivery or retention of the channel at the membrane, while sample B has impaired activity per surface channel. If total protein in A is low, reduced synthesis or increased degradation might contribute; if total protein is near normal, intracellular retention becomes more plausible. The observations are illustrative and do not establish the exact variant. The experimental variables and their relation to the mechanism are the educational purpose of the example.

A barrier measurement can provide another perspective. An epithelial layer consists of cells with apical and basolateral membranes and intercellular junctions. Changes in transepithelial transport can result from disruption of any of these components. When interpreting CFTR-related measurements, the location of the affected channel and the tissue's normal transport direction matter. The sweat duct, for example, normally reabsorbs chloride and sodium, whereas secretory epithelia have different transport arrangements. A defect can therefore produce different physiological consequences in different tissues without invoking unrelated causal genes.

For haemophilia, measuring the abundance of a factor is not always equivalent to measuring its activity. An inactive protein can be present at normal antigen concentration but fail to perform its biological function. A quantitative activity assay asks how much of a defined reaction the sample supports; an antigen assay asks how much recognisable protein is present. A hypothetical sample has factor VIII antigen at 90 percent of a reference value but factor VIII functional activity at 10 percent. This suggests a qualitative functional defect or inhibition rather than a simple tenfold reduction in the amount of detectable protein. Another sample with both values near 10 percent is more compatible with a quantitative deficit. Neither alone distinguishes all possible causes.

The coagulation system also demonstrates pathway dependence. Imagine a reaction sequence in which active factor IX and its cofactor VIII together promote formation of active factor X. A low output at this stage can result from insufficient IX activity, insufficient VIII activity or conditions that prevent their productive association. A measurement confined to the final product is not enough to identify the affected component. Separate measurements of each activity provide more discriminating evidence. In examination settings, this kind of comparison can be presented as a table of normal and reduced activities rather than as a memorised list of factor numbers.

For a structural matrix protein such as fibrillin, another distinction is whether a protein has been secreted and whether it contributes normally to organised extracellular fibres. Detecting a mutant product outside the cell does not prove that the matrix has acquired normal mechanical properties. A matrix assembled from abnormal components may have altered architecture or interactions. Likewise, the observation that a tissue is mechanically weak does not by itself tell us whether the cause is reduced synthesis, altered assembly, increased degradation or changes in supporting components. Each model predicts a different combination of product abundance and structural organisation.

A simple experimental comparison can distinguish two broad mechanisms. In one hypothetical group, cells expressing only one functional allele generate less matrix protein but the fibres that form are organised normally. In a second group, cells produce a similar amount of total detectable protein as reference cells but deposit disorganised fibres. The first pattern is compatible with reduced supply, whereas the second suggests that the product or its interactions are abnormal. Additional evidence is needed to rule out indirect regulatory effects. A strong answer avoids calling every dominant structural disorder dominant-negative without testing the assumptions.

For dystrophin, a relevant structural observation is the continuity and localisation of the protein around muscle fibres. An assay that measures total protein may miss abnormal localisation or differences between cells. Comparison of protein size and localisation can help explain why distinct variants of DMD produce different functional capacities. A partial protein may retain regions required for mechanical support; another partial product may lack an essential region. A reading-frame calculation is useful for predicting whether translation proceeds in register, but it is not a full functional assessment.

These examples emphasise a general principle: a test must correspond to a well-defined question. DNA sequence addresses the identity of a variant; RNA measurements address transcript processing and abundance; protein assays address quantity, form or localisation; functional measurements address biological activity. None automatically substitutes for all the others. In a Part C problem, select the observation that separates competing explanations rather than the one that merely confirms that an affected sample differs from a reference.

Dominant and X-linked structural disorders

Marfan syndrome provides a contrast with recessive enzyme and channel deficiencies. FBN1 encodes fibrillin-1, an extracellular matrix protein involved in microfibril structure. These structures contribute to tissue organisation and influence signalling contexts. A pathogenic change in a single FBN1 allele can be associated with a dominant multisystem phenotype, involving such tissues as the skeleton, ocular structures and cardiovascular system. The broad distribution of extracellular matrix structures helps explain how one locus can affect several apparently unrelated organs.

Dominance may reflect insufficient normal fibrillin or interference by altered protein, depending on the allele. It is not necessary to assume that all pathogenic FBN1 variants operate through one identical molecular mechanism. This matters in an examination asking for a distinction between a null allele and a dominant-negative allele. If mutant products are absent and reducing the amount of wild-type product reproduces the phenotype, dosage insufficiency is plausible. If a mutant protein actively disrupts a complex even in the presence of sufficient wild-type product, dominant-negative interference is more plausible. A dominant pedigree alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms.

Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy are related dystrophinopathies caused by pathogenic variation in the X-linked DMD gene. Dystrophin connects the intracellular actin-associated cytoskeleton to a membrane-associated protein complex that helps transmit mechanical force. Its loss makes the muscle cell membrane more susceptible to damage during contraction. Repeated injury and degeneration help explain progressive muscle dysfunction. Dystrophin has a structural role rather than serving simply as an enzyme in a metabolic reaction.

A useful, though not absolute, genotype-phenotype rule is that many variants disrupting the reading frame cause little functional dystrophin and a Duchenne phenotype, while many deletions preserving the reading frame permit a partly functional protein and a Becker phenotype. An in-frame deletion in a critical functional region can nevertheless cause severe disease, and some apparently frame-disrupting variants can have unexpected RNA-processing consequences. Reading-frame predictions are therefore mechanistic hypotheses rather than guarantees.

Consider a hypothetical coding sequence with three adjacent exons containing 90, 91 and 92 coding nucleotides. Deleting the exon of 90 nucleotides preserves the reading frame if the adjoining splice boundaries permit the expected joining; deleting the exon of 91 nucleotides shifts the frame. The exercise tests divisibility by three, not the clinical severity of a real variant. Protein domain loss, splicing and residual expression must also be considered. This is an example of allelic heterogeneity in which multiple variants of one gene produce a spectrum of phenotypes.

Chromosome dosage and representative aneuploidies

A chromosome contains many genes. Changing the number of copies of a chromosome changes the dosage of multiple loci simultaneously, even if each individual gene sequence is intact. In a simplified non-compensated expression model, three copies of a gene produce 1.5 times the expression expected from two copies. Real cells can compensate partially or change regulatory networks, and not all transcripts track copy number equally. Nevertheless the comparison illustrates why aneuploidy can affect diverse developmental processes at once.

Down syndrome commonly involves three copies of chromosome 21, represented for a typical full trisomy in an XY complement as 47,XY,+21. Extra chromosome 21 material can also result from a Robertsonian translocation, and a mosaic individual may have both disomic and trisomic cell populations. The important genetic feature is the increased dosage of relevant chromosome 21 material, not simply the total chromosome count. An individual with translocation Down syndrome may have 46 chromosomes while carrying excess chromosome 21 long-arm material. The origin and recurrence patterns of free trisomy and translocation-associated forms can consequently differ. Detailed meiotic segregation of rearranged chromosomes is treated in subunit 8.F.

Turner syndrome is associated with complete or partial absence of a second sex chromosome in individuals typically developing a female phenotype. The classic karyotype is 45,X, but mosaics and structural X abnormalities occur. In typical XX cells, one X is largely inactivated; some genes escape inactivation, including genes in the pseudoautosomal regions. Reduced dosage of such genes helps explain why a single X is not generally equivalent to two X chromosomes with one inactive. SHOX dosage contributes to the growth phenotype, without accounting for all manifestations.

Klinefelter syndrome commonly involves a 47,XXY complement in an individual typically developing a male phenotype. One X is largely inactivated, so extra expression is not a uniform doubling of every X-linked gene. Genes escaping X-inactivation and broader developmental effects contribute to differences in growth and gonadal function. The textbook Barr body rule predicts approximately one inactive X in 47,XXY and none in 45,X, in cell types where conventional X-inactivation applies. The rule counts inactive X chromosomes, not the number of Y chromosomes, and does not imply that an inactive X contributes no transcription.

A schematic dataset can test the distinction between mosaicism and ordinary aneuploidy. If 16 of 40 scored cells from a specimen show 47,XX,+21 and the remainder show 46,XX, the observed trisomic fraction in the specimen is 40 percent. It is not legitimate to conclude that 40 percent of every organ is trisomic. Sampling, culture selection and tissue-specific lineage history can influence the measured fraction. Cytogenetic methods estimate particular features in particular samples, not an entire organism's complete genotype.

Tracing chromosome origin, dosage and cytogenetic evidence

Chromosome number is normally established by the reduction of chromosome sets during meiosis and their restoration at fertilisation. Homologous chromosomes normally separate in the first meiotic division, and sister chromatids separate during the second. If segregation of a particular pair fails in meiosis I, the resulting gametes in a simple four-product representation can include two with an extra homolog and two lacking that homolog. If the failure occurs in meiosis II in one of the two cells, the four products may instead include two with the ordinary number, one with an extra chromatid-derived chromosome and one lacking it. This idealised comparison is useful for understanding the origins of aneuploid conceptions, though human oogenesis has asymmetric divisions rather than four equivalent eggs.

Combining a gamete containing two copies of chromosome 21 with a gamete containing one yields a conception with three copies. The corresponding loss can yield monosomy. A postzygotic segregation error is different: the embryo may have started with an ordinary complement, and only descendants of the cell in which the error occurs have an altered complement. The timing and subsequent proliferation of cell lineages then influence mosaic composition. A simple karyogram showing trisomy does not necessarily distinguish all possible timing mechanisms.

Genetic marker information can sometimes provide evidence about meiotic origin. Consider a parent heterozygous at a marker near the centromere of a chromosome, with distinguishable alleles A and B on the two homologs. If an abnormal gamete contains both A and B at the centromeric region, failure to segregate the two homologs at the first division is a plausible explanation. If the two copies carry the same marker allele, failure of sister chromatid segregation at the second division is plausible under a simplified model. Recombination, marker informativeness and other mechanisms can complicate this inference. It is essential not to generalise the centromere-based logic to all distal markers without considering crossing over.

A balanced Robertsonian translocation can join the long arms of two acrocentric chromosomes into a derivative chromosome. The number of separately counted chromosomes is then reduced, often to 45 in a balanced carrier, while the dosage of the essential long-arm genes can remain approximately balanced. During meiosis, segregation may yield a gamete with an unbalanced arrangement. The resulting offspring can have additional chromosome 21 long-arm material without the usual count of 47 chromosomes. This mechanism is conceptually distinct from a free extra chromosome, and the physical organisation of the chromosomes is relevant to transmission.

Consider three hypothetical cytogenetic descriptions. Case A has 47 chromosomes, including three separate chromosome 21 homologs. Case B has 46 chromosomes with a derivative containing extra chromosome 21 long-arm material in addition to two ordinary copies of that relevant material. Case C has 45 chromosomes and a balanced fusion that retains the relevant long-arm dosage of chromosomes 14 and 21. Case A and B can show dosage imbalance related to Down syndrome despite different chromosome counts, while C illustrates that a reduced chromosome count is not sufficient evidence of a corresponding loss of essential gene dosage. These descriptions are schematic rather than complete clinical ISCN notation.

X-chromosome inactivation adds another regulatory level to the relation between chromosomes and phenotype. In most somatic cells of typical XX individuals, one of the two X chromosomes becomes largely transcriptionally inactive early in development. Descendant cells generally maintain their choice of active X, producing a mosaic of expression in heterozygotes. This is functional mosaicism even when the sequence genotype of every cell is the same. It differs from a karyotypic mosaic containing cells with different chromosome complements.

Some genes escape inactivation, and the fraction of escape varies by gene and tissue. In the pseudoautosomal regions, homologous sequences are present on X and Y chromosomes. Such genes can be expressed from both copies in typical XY as well as typical XX complements. The loss of a second sex chromosome in 45,X can thus reduce expression of genes normally supplied by both chromosomes. In 47,XXY, an inactive X still contributes expression of escape genes, and the extra dosage can have biological effects. The presence of a Barr body is not a statement that all genes on the condensed chromosome are permanently silent.

The most useful way to compare these cases is to define the question before choosing the measurement. Chromosome counts help identify numerical changes. Banding helps reveal large structural rearrangements. Locus-specific fluorescence signals can indicate the number and position of selected sequences. An array can estimate copy number across regions but does not necessarily establish the physical arrangement of those sequences or detect balanced rearrangements. Each technique has resolution limits, which were introduced in the preceding cytogenetics chapter. A normal result from one method cannot exclude every genetic cause of a phenotype.

Mitochondrial disorders and variable transmission

Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation requires proteins encoded by both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. A variant in a nuclear gene involved in mitochondrial function therefore follows the relevant nuclear inheritance pattern. It is the physical location of the causal allele, rather than the location of the affected organelle, that determines the usual route of transmission.

Human mitochondrial DNA is typically inherited maternally. An egg contributes a population of mitochondria to the zygote, whereas paternal mitochondrial contribution does not normally persist. Mitochondrial DNA is present in many copies per cell, so a cell can contain a mixture of different mitochondrial genotypes, termed heteroplasmy. Homoplasmy refers to a state in which the mitochondrial DNA copies at a particular site are of the same type. Neither term alone establishes whether a tissue functions normally.

During oocyte development and subsequent cell divisions, sampling and replication of mitochondrial DNA can lead to markedly different heteroplasmy fractions between offspring and tissues. This phenomenon is connected to the mitochondrial genetic bottleneck. The fraction of altered mitochondrial genomes is therefore not generally transmitted as a fixed nuclear Mendelian ratio. The same family can show a broad distribution of severity, and both male and female offspring may express an mtDNA-associated condition, though maternal transmission is the expected route to the next generation.

MELAS, a mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, illustrates dysfunction of energy-demanding tissues. A common associated variant is m.3243A>G in the mitochondrial tRNA gene MT-TL1. Disturbance of mitochondrial translation can affect respiratory chain function. An impairment of oxidative energy metabolism is particularly relevant to tissues with sustained ATP demands. The mutation is not the only cause of the syndrome, and a heteroplasmy measurement in one tissue does not fix the phenotype in every other tissue.

Suppose a cell contains 300 copies of mitochondrial DNA at a particular locus and 180 carry an altered allele. Its heteroplasmy fraction at that locus is 180/300 = 0.60. This is a genome-copy fraction, not the probability that the person's next child has disease, and not necessarily the fraction of mitochondria containing a variant because individual mitochondria can contain several genomes. A threshold model describes the possibility that substantial dysfunction appears only when the altered genome fraction exceeds the functional reserve of a cell. Thresholds vary with allele and tissue. The model helps explain non-linear phenotype changes without suggesting a universal percentage cutoff.

Dynamic repeat expansion and anticipation

Many short DNA motifs occur consecutively in the human genome. A repeat length can sometimes change during transmission, rather than behaving as an allele of permanently fixed size. The probability and direction of change depend on the repeat sequence, genomic context and parental transmission. The concept of genetic anticipation describes a tendency toward earlier onset or greater severity in successive generations in some inherited disorders. It is a statistical and mechanistic tendency, not a rule that every descendant will have more severe disease.

Huntington disease is caused by expansion of a CAG repeat in the coding region of HTT. CAG encodes glutamine, so the expansion lengthens a polyglutamine region of the huntingtin protein. The altered protein contributes to neuronal dysfunction through multiple effects on cellular processes. The condition classically follows autosomal dominant transmission. Longer expansions are generally associated with earlier onset, and large intergenerational increases are particularly associated with paternal transmission. GeneReviews distinguishes 36–39 repeats as reduced-penetrance disease-causing alleles and 40 or more as the full-penetrance range, while age of manifestation varies. Repeat length is a predictor, not an exact clock.

Fragile X syndrome instead usually involves a CGG expansion in a noncoding region of FMR1. Full expansion is commonly accompanied by methylation and silencing, decreasing expression of the RNA-binding protein FMRP. This is fundamentally different from the coding polyglutamine expansion in Huntington disease. A premutation has distinct biological implications from a silenced full mutation. Expansion from a premutation into the full-mutation range is principally associated with maternal transmission. Both molecular mechanism and parent-of-origin transmission patterns matter.

Suppose a hypothetical sequence analysis shows an increasing number of CAG triplets within an open reading frame. An increase of ten triplets adds 30 nucleotides and, if translated, ten glutamine residues without shifting the downstream reading frame. The same arithmetic applied to a noncoding CGG expansion does not justify a prediction of ten extra amino acids. The genomic position of a repeat is indispensable information. A question presenting a repeat size alone is incomplete if it asks for an exact protein-level consequence without specifying where the repeat lies.

Genomic imprinting and parent-of-origin effects

For most autosomal loci, the same allele is expected to have broadly comparable potential for expression regardless of which parent transmitted it. Imprinting is a regulated exception in which expression of particular genes depends on parental origin, through epigenetic marks established in the germline and maintained in relevant tissues. The DNA sequence need not differ between two copies for their activity to differ. This makes chromosome origin a biologically important variable.

Prader–Willi syndrome results from loss of expression of paternally active genes in a region of chromosome 15. A paternal deletion can remove those genes; maternal uniparental disomy can leave two maternal-origin chromosomes but no appropriately active paternal copy; and certain imprinting defects can disrupt the normal expression program. The same general phenotype can therefore arise from distinct genomic mechanisms. A copy number assay alone may not detect uniparental disomy or an imprinting defect.

Angelman syndrome involves loss of appropriate maternal UBE3A function in the nervous system, where the paternal copy is largely silenced in relevant neurons. A maternal deletion can remove the active copy, while paternal uniparental disomy can supply two paternal copies without the appropriate maternal expression. Pathogenic variants in the active maternal UBE3A allele can also produce the condition. The comparison with Prader–Willi syndrome demonstrates that the same general chromosomal neighbourhood can have contrasting consequences depending on parental origin and the particular expressed genes.

The contrast should not be reduced to a statement that all chromosome 15 genes are either maternally or paternally active. Imprinting is gene-specific and may depend on tissue and developmental stage. It also differs from maternal cytoplasmic inheritance: the genes in this example lie in the nuclear genome and imprint marks are normally reset in the germline according to the sex of the transmitting individual. Maternal mitochondrial inheritance instead reflects transmission of organellar genetic material. A parent-of-origin pattern can arise through either mechanism, but the mechanistic predictions are different.

Heterogeneity, multifactorial risk and the evidence hierarchy

Allelic heterogeneity means that distinct variants at a single locus can produce related phenotypes. Locus heterogeneity means that variants in different genes can produce a similar phenotype, as when separate components of a biological pathway are impaired. Pleiotropy is the converse direction of description: variation at one locus contributes to multiple phenotypic effects. These terms answer different questions and need not be mutually exclusive. For instance, a gene can have many pathogenic alleles and also influence several tissues.

Multifactorial disorders are not explained by a single necessary and sufficient disease allele in the usual case. Variants at multiple loci can influence biological processes such as insulin secretion, metabolic regulation or vascular physiology. Environmental exposures, developmental history and chance also affect outcomes. A family may cluster for a trait because relatives share both alleles and environments, so clustering is not by itself proof of a simple inheritance mode.

One useful mathematical representation is a liability model. Imagine an underlying continuous variable L built from genetic contributions G and environmental or other contributions E. In the simplest additive representation L = G + E. If the phenotype is classified as present when L exceeds a chosen threshold T, many different combinations can result in the same category. This is a modelling device; it does not assert that a disorder has a single directly measurable liability or a sharp biological threshold in every individual. It explains why a binary phenotype may emerge from continuous variation.

Consider an illustrative dataset in which a condition is observed in 20 of 100 individuals with a particular marker allele and 10 of 100 individuals without it. The observed risk ratio is 0.20/0.10 = 2. The risk difference is 0.20 − 0.10 = 0.10, or ten percentage points. Neither statistic establishes that the marker causes the disorder. The marker may track a nearby causal variant, or groups may differ in an environmental exposure. Sample uncertainty also matters. Moreover, a doubling of risk does not mean that the outcome is inevitable: in the hypothetical marker group, 80 of the 100 people do not show the phenotype.

This distinction prepares the study of quantitative genetics. The next subunit develops population allele frequencies, variance, heritability and mapping. Human disease examples are useful because they show why the inference must respect biological mechanism, segregation and population context simultaneously. A pedigree, sequence, chromosome image and molecular assay each give a partial view. A convincing interpretation integrates them, identifies assumptions and avoids claiming certainty from evidence that cannot discriminate alternative explanations.
