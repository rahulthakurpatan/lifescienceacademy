From genetic variation to disease

A genetic disorder is best understood by following a causal chain, rather than by memorising a list of names. A DNA sequence or chromosome configuration changes; that change modifies the quantity, structure, location or regulation of a biological product; a cell or tissue can no longer perform some function normally; the resulting disturbance contributes to a phenotype. The phenotype may be a laboratory measurement, an anatomical difference, a physiological impairment or a developmental characteristic. Each link in this chain is a separate biological question. A pedigree can tell us that a characteristic travels with a particular allele without telling us how that allele alters protein function. Conversely, a biochemical defect measured in a tissue can suggest a mechanism without identifying the route by which the defect was inherited.

It is important to distinguish a genetic condition from an inherited condition. A genetic alteration may arise for the first time in a parental gamete, during early embryogenesis or in a later somatic lineage. Only a change present in the cells that contribute genetic material to offspring can be transmitted through that individual. A child with a newly arisen constitutional variant can therefore have a genetic condition without a history of that condition in either parent's phenotype. A mosaic person contains cell populations with different genotypes derived from the same zygote; the distribution of these populations can matter as much as the identity of the variant. A blood sample may not represent the genetic composition of every organ.

The term pathogenic variant refers to a variant supported by evidence as a cause of disease in an appropriate genetic context. It does not mean that every sequence difference is harmful. Many variants have no detectable adverse effect. Some affect disease probability only in combination with other variants or conditions. Others produce a molecular change but have no readily detectable phenotype because an organism has redundant pathways, excess enzymatic capacity or compensatory regulation. Thus genetic causality should not be inferred simply from an unusual sequence or from an observation made in one individual.

Four broad classes are useful for organising human disease inheritance. In monogenic disorders, variation at one locus is the principal genetic cause in a family. A chromosome disorder involves a change in chromosome number or the organisation of a segment, often altering dosage across multiple genes. Mitochondrial disorders may result from nuclear or mitochondrial variants, but only those caused by mitochondrial DNA generally follow maternal transmission. Complex disorders involve multiple genetic contributions and non-genetic influences. The categories are mechanistic descriptions rather than mutually exclusive boxes: a mitochondrial enzyme encoded in the nucleus may be involved in a monogenic autosomal condition, and a single-gene disease may show substantial variation owing to genetic modifiers.

For an examination problem, identify the level at which the observation was made. A karyogram showing an extra chromosome supplies cytological information. A protein band on an electrophoretic gel supplies molecular information. Affected siblings with unaffected parents supply segregation information. A stronger explanation combines independent lines of evidence and asks whether any alternative model makes the same predictions. This approach also prevents a common error: assuming a recognisable clinical phenotype uniquely identifies a specific mutation.

Mendelian disease models and probability

Consider a diploid autosomal locus with alleles A and a. In a simplified recessive model AA and Aa have no disease phenotype, whereas aa is affected. The heterozygote produces A-bearing and a-bearing gametes with equal probability. Two heterozygotes therefore produce AA, Aa, aA and aa zygotes in equal expected proportions, giving genotype probabilities one quarter, one half and one quarter after combining the two heterozygous outcomes. A common biological basis for this model is that a single functional allele produces sufficient activity for normal tissue performance. This is not a universal explanation for every recessive disorder, nor does it mean heterozygotes have exactly normal enzyme activity.

Suppose an unaffected sibling of a child with a fully penetrant recessive disorder has two known heterozygous parents. Conditioning on being unaffected removes the aa outcome. Among the remaining outcomes, two of three are heterozygous. The probability of carrying the allele is therefore two thirds, not one half. If an unrelated partner is known to carry the same disease-associated allele, the probability of an affected child in the simplified model is two thirds multiplied by one quarter, or one sixth. These numbers describe a hypothetical pedigree with specified assumptions, not a general clinical recurrence estimate.

In an autosomal dominant model a single altered allele can produce the defined disease phenotype. A heterozygote and an individual lacking the variant transmit the variant to one half of offspring on average. Dominance describes a relationship between alleles with respect to the phenotype being measured. It does not imply that a variant is frequent, evolutionarily advantageous or necessarily biochemically active. A loss-of-function variant can be dominant when half the usual gene product is insufficient. A protein that interferes with the wild-type product may also produce dominance, as may a novel or excessive activity.

Penetrance is the probability of expressing a specified phenotype given a specified genotype in a defined context. If a heterozygous parent transmits a dominant variant with probability one half and the penetrance in offspring under the conditions of the problem is 0.8, the probability of an offspring expressing the condition through that variant is 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.4. This calculation assumes no background phenocopies and that penetrance is applicable to the offspring's age and environment. Expressivity, in contrast, concerns the nature or severity of the phenotype among those who express it. A pedigree that appears to skip a generation need not exclude a dominant model if penetrance is incomplete or if disease manifests later in life.

For an X-linked locus outside the pseudoautosomal regions, a father typically transmits his X chromosome to daughters and his Y chromosome to sons. Under the conventional XX/XY inheritance model, a heterozygous mother and a father lacking the variant have a one-half probability of an affected son conditional on the child being a son, if the variant is fully penetrant in hemizygous males. With equal probabilities for the two chromosomal sexes, the joint probability of an affected son among all births is one quarter. The distinction between a conditional and a joint probability is essential. Heterozygous females can also show manifestations, depending on the gene, X-inactivation pattern and other factors. The simplified carrier category is useful for solving a stated genetic model but must not be confused with a universal absence of symptoms.

Independent gamete unions explain why an earlier birth does not alter the next birth's segregation probability when parental genotypes and assumptions remain fixed. On the other hand, new family information can change our uncertainty about parental genotypes, so the probability calculated by an observer can change. This distinguishes biological independence from statistical learning.

Functional reserve and the relationship between allele dosage and phenotype

A familiar way to introduce recessive inheritance is to say that one healthy copy of a gene is sufficient. That statement is a useful starting point, but an explanation should show how a cell can function with less product. Consider an enzyme catalysing a reaction in a pathway. Its maximum possible catalytic rate depends partly on the amount of active enzyme. If normal cells produce substantially more catalytic capacity than is required to maintain the relevant metabolic flux, a heterozygous loss of activity may leave adequate reserve. A tissue may therefore behave normally across a considerable range of enzyme concentrations even though the biochemical activity measured in a laboratory is reduced.

The relationship between enzyme activity and pathway flux is rarely a universally linear function. A metabolic pathway includes several reactions, substrate supply, transport and regulatory feedback. Reducing one enzyme to half its original concentration might cause only a small change in the total flux if another reaction limits the pathway under the tested conditions. Conversely, a reduction in the same enzyme may become important when physiological demand rises. This conceptual distinction explains why a recessive allele can have a measurable biochemical effect in a heterozygote without causing the same phenotype as two pathogenic alleles.

Construct an illustrative, deliberately simplified example. Let a normal diploid genotype produce 100 units of active protein, a heterozygous null produce 50 units, and a homozygous null produce zero. If a particular tissue needs at least 35 units for its ordinary function, the heterozygote retains sufficient capacity. A recessive phenotype is predicted. If a different protein is required at a threshold of 75 units and has no compensation, the corresponding heterozygote falls below that threshold. In this second example, a heterozygous loss-of-function variant is sufficient to disturb the phenotype. The thresholds are invented teaching values, not measurements from a human disorder. The central principle is the interaction of gene dosage with the functional demand of a tissue.

A dominant-negative model makes a different prediction. Suppose a protein functions as a dimer, two alleles produce equal amounts of monomer, and monomers associate at random. The heterozygote produces a population in which half of monomers are wild type and half are altered. The expected fractions of dimers are one quarter wild-type homodimers, one half mixed dimers and one quarter altered homodimers. If any altered subunit destroys the activity of a dimer, only one quarter of the assembled dimers are functional. A simple heterozygous null, in which all produced monomers are normal, does not generate mixed inactive dimers. Distinguishing a reduction in production from interference with activity matters for the interpretation of structural protein disorders.

The dimer calculation is conditional on equal expression, equal assembly probabilities and complete disruption by one altered subunit. If the mutant monomer fails to assemble, the random-association model is inappropriate. If mixed dimers retain partial activity, the predicted fraction of functional activity changes. These conditions illustrate how molecular assumptions enter a genetic explanation. A familiar Mendelian ratio can remain unchanged while the underlying biochemistry differs greatly.

Penetrance can be represented as a population of individuals with the same defined genotype but a distribution of functional reserves. The phenotype may be present only among those whose physiological demands exceed their reserve. Expressivity concerns variation in which tissues are affected or how strongly they are affected once the phenotype is manifest. Modifiers can alter expression of the causal gene, stability of its product or activity of interacting pathways. Environmental conditions can alter the demand placed on the tissue. This framework gives students a mechanistic way to interpret why a genotype is not always a precise prediction of an individual's phenotype.

For example, a hypothetical dominant genotype has 80 percent penetrance in the age range under consideration. Among 100 carriers we expect roughly 80 expressing individuals, not necessarily exactly 80. If a particular sample contains 75, sampling variation is one possible explanation. If a sample includes a much younger group, age-dependent expression may also change the estimate. Penetrance is conditional on the defined phenotype, age and sampling context. A report that simply states a gene is 80 percent penetrant without those qualifications can invite mistaken comparisons.

Family ascertainment introduces another source of bias. A study that recruits families because several relatives express a phenotype may overrepresent families with strong expression. In contrast, sampling unselected carriers can reveal apparently unaffected individuals who would not have been noticed in a phenotype-based survey. This does not change the molecular defect, but it changes the population from which a penetrance estimate is calculated. It is useful to distinguish the probability of observing a genotype among affected individuals from the probability of being affected given that genotype. These two conditional probabilities answer different questions and cannot generally be interchanged.

Sickle cell disease: a structural protein defect

Adult haemoglobin A is a tetramer formed from two alpha and two beta globin chains. Each chain contains a haem group, enabling reversible oxygen binding. Red cells require a high concentration of soluble haemoglobin to transport oxygen while remaining flexible enough to move through narrow vessels. Sickle cell disease illustrates what can happen when a sequence change leaves much of a protein's normal function intact but gives the molecule an abnormal interaction with other copies of itself.

The classic sickle allele of HBB changes a glutamate residue to valine at position six of the mature beta globin chain, conventionally called beta 6 Glu to Val. The numbering is seven when the initiator methionine is included in the reference protein sequence. Glutamate has a negatively charged side chain at physiological pH, while valine has a nonpolar side chain. This substitution creates a hydrophobic interaction site on haemoglobin S. In the deoxygenated state, complementary surfaces on neighbouring HbS molecules can associate, and repeated intermolecular contacts create long polymers. The primary abnormality is not simply that haemoglobin cannot bind oxygen.

Polymer formation changes the mechanics of the red cell. A flexible membrane is distorted around rigid intracellular fibres. Repeated deformation and associated cellular damage shorten red-cell survival; the resulting haemolysis can contribute to anaemia. Reduced deformability and interactions among blood and vascular cells also contribute to impaired blood flow. This explains why a molecular event at the surface of one protein has consequences at the level of a tissue. The processes interact, but haemolysis and vascular obstruction are not interchangeable terms.

Pauling, Itano and colleagues in 1949 investigated whether haemoglobin from individuals with sickle cell anaemia differed in physical properties from ordinary haemoglobin. Electrophoretic comparisons established a difference in mobility, and heterozygote samples showed components corresponding to the two haemoglobin forms. The experiment connected an inherited phenotype with a measurable molecular property. It did not by itself establish the exact amino acid substitution. Ingram's later peptide comparisons identified a specific chemical difference between globins; subsequent sequence work established the substitution. This sequence of findings is historically significant because it shows how progressively more resolved measurements can narrow a causal model.

Consider a hypothetical separation with an HbA reference in one lane, HbS in a second and an unknown sample showing both bands in a third. With no additional haemoglobin variants and appropriate controls, the mixed pattern supports production of both protein forms. It does not demonstrate a simple dominant pattern for the disease itself. At the molecular level the two alleles can both be expressed, whereas clinically important disease in the classical HbAA, HbAS and HbSS comparison is generally recessive. A heterozygote can nevertheless have circumstances in which the altered protein matters. Molecular codominance and recessive disease expression are not contradictory because they refer to different phenotype definitions.

A person who inherits HbS and a beta-thalassaemia allele can also have sickle cell disease. The second allele may provide reduced or absent beta globin production rather than HbS. The outcome depends on the particular alleles and their effects on the mixture of globin chains. Sickle cell disease therefore includes more than homozygosity for the HbS allele. The comparison sets up the next question: how does producing an abnormal globin differ from producing too little of an otherwise necessary globin?

Interpreting haemoglobin evidence and developmental switches

A haemoglobin separation experiment is an opportunity to distinguish molecular identity from abundance. Electrophoretic migration depends on the physical properties of the molecular species and the experimental conditions, including buffer pH. A comparison should therefore include known reference preparations examined under the same conditions. A migration difference between two samples can support a difference in molecular properties but does not reveal the exact sequence automatically. Two distinct proteins may sometimes migrate similarly, and quantitative abundance cannot be inferred reliably from the mere presence of a visible band.

Consider a hypothetical data table with a reference HbA band and an HbS band. Sample P contains only a band matching HbA, sample Q shows both A and S, and sample R contains a band matching HbS with no detectable HbA. In a deliberately limited model of the A and S alleles, P is consistent with HbAA, Q with HbAS and R with HbSS. The assumptions are essential. For example, absence of HbA in R is also compatible with HbS paired with an allele producing no beta globin. Other haemoglobin variants and recent changes in the cellular composition of a sample can complicate an interpretation. A band pattern should be described as compatible with a model, not as unconditional proof of a specific genotype.

The detection of two haemoglobin forms in a heterozygote reveals a molecular phenotype of codominance. To see why this does not require a dominant disease phenotype, distinguish product expression from polymerisation. A cell containing both proteins can have a lower concentration of HbS than a cell whose beta chain output consists predominantly of the sickle form. Formation of a polymer depends on intermolecular encounters and the availability of compatible interacting surfaces. The relationship between the fraction of sickle globin and the rate or extent of polymerisation is therefore not required to be linear. The cell's hydration state and oxygenation conditions also influence the environment in which these molecules interact.

In an illustrative reasoning experiment, compare three samples containing an identical total haemoglobin concentration. One contains only ordinary HbA, a second contains a mixture with a substantial fraction of HbS, and a third contains predominantly HbS. Examine each at high and low oxygen availability. A prediction from the polymerisation model is that formation of fibres is strongly favoured in samples containing HbS under deoxygenated conditions. A suitable control is the HbA preparation exposed to the same conditions. If all preparations instead became equally rigid in the absence of oxygen, the explanation would need to account for an effect independent of the specific HbS interaction. Designing a control that isolates the proposed causal difference is more informative than merely describing the result expected for one sample.

The developmental change in haemoglobin composition supplies a second independent line of biological reasoning. Before birth, haemoglobin F, which contains gamma rather than beta globin chains, has an important role in oxygen transfer. Following birth, beta globin expression increases while gamma globin expression normally declines. A defect in the adult beta chain may thus become more conspicuous following the developmental transition even though the genotype was already present. This is an example of an age-dependent molecular context, not a newly occurring mutation. Genotype and onset need not coincide in time.

An increase in gamma globin expression can change the mixture of haemoglobin molecules available in red cells. In sickle cell disease, HbF can inhibit HbS polymerisation; variation in fetal haemoglobin contributes to phenotypic variation. In beta-thalassaemia, production of gamma chains can also provide an alternative partner for alpha globin. The relevant effects are different: one concerns the molecular interactions involved in polymerisation and the other concerns chain assembly and balance. Similar developmental modifiers can therefore affect distinct disorders through different biochemical mechanisms. The example is appropriate for inheritance biology because it makes the relation between a major allele and modifier loci explicit.

A comparison of alpha and beta globin synthesis further illustrates the limits of counting gene copies. In normal cells, the expression of the two linked alpha globin genes is regulated; their contributions are not necessarily identical. A deletion can alter regulatory context as well as remove a coding sequence. A count of functional alpha loci is a strong introductory predictor for typical deletional alpha-thalassaemia, but equal counts do not guarantee identical output. When a question asks whether cis and trans arrangements with two functional copies are identical, the appropriate answer depends on the measured phenotype: they can be similar for some dosage-related traits but give different gametes and therefore different inheritance predictions.

A further phase problem illustrates the point. Cross a parent with cis loss --/αα to a parent with trans loss -α/-α. The first produces -- or αα gametes, while the second produces -α gametes in the simplified model. The offspring genotypes are --/-α and αα/-α in equal probabilities. They contain one and three functional alpha loci, respectively. No --/-- offspring are predicted because the trans parent does not transmit a chromosome lacking both alpha genes under the stated assumptions. The example uses chromosome organisation rather than independent assortment of four freely segregating loci.

These arguments also make it clear why structural and quantitative haemoglobin disorders can overlap. An individual may inherit a structural allele from one parent and a reduced-production allele from the other. Because both alleles contribute to the total chain pool, their combined phenotype cannot always be predicted by averaging the names of the two disorders. At a genetic level the individual is a compound heterozygote at HBB. At a molecular level, the products and their relative abundance determine the biochemical context. Students should move between these levels deliberately rather than treat a genetic label as the mechanism.

Thalassaemia: globin dosage and chain balance

Haemoglobin assembly depends on coordinated globin production. A decrease in the production of one class of chain reduces the availability of correctly assembled tetramers and can leave an excess of its partner. Excess unpaired chains can damage developing erythroid cells. Thalassaemias illustrate this quantitative and stoichiometric mechanism; their defining defect is reduced synthesis of alpha or beta globin rather than the classic sickle polymerisation mechanism.

Beta-thalassaemia is associated with HBB variants that decrease beta globin production. In traditional notation a beta-zero allele yields no beta globin from that allele, while a beta-plus allele retains reduced output. Regulatory variants can lower transcription, and certain splice-site variants reduce the amount of correctly processed RNA. Other changes can interfere with translation or stability. Different DNA changes in the same locus can thus converge on reduced protein production, and residual output helps explain phenotypic differences. Excess alpha globin damages developing erythroid cells, causing ineffective erythropoiesis as well as contributing to haemolysis. The full severity is modified by other loci and the distribution of globin types.

Alpha-thalassaemia introduces a different dosage arrangement. Normally a person has two alpha globin genes on each chromosome 16, HBA1 and HBA2, giving four alpha globin gene copies in a typical diploid genome. Deletions are a frequent cause of alpha-thalassaemia. Losing one copy commonly produces a silent carrier state; losing two produces a trait phenotype; loss or inactivation of three is associated with HbH disease; and loss of all four is associated with Hb Bart syndrome. This is a useful progression, but non-deletion alleles and differences between individual genes mean that a simple count is not a complete severity model.

The two-copy loss can occur in cis, with both losses on the same homolog, or in trans, with one loss on each. The total number of functional copies is identical in the two arrangements, but gamete composition differs. If both parents have a cis deletion represented as --/αα, each produces -- and αα gametes in equal proportions under the basic model. One quarter of their offspring are expected to inherit --/--, one half --/αα and one quarter αα/αα. If both parents instead have the trans arrangement -α/-α, each transmits a chromosome carrying one remaining alpha gene in the simplified model without recombination. All offspring then inherit -α/-α. Comparing the two families demonstrates why phase matters even when a total copy number measurement is identical.

Developmental regulation further changes the consequences of globin imbalance. Fetal haemoglobin contains alpha and gamma chains; adult haemoglobin A contains alpha and beta chains. A disturbance of alpha chain supply can therefore affect haemoglobin assembly during fetal as well as adult life, while severe beta chain shortage becomes particularly evident as the developmental shift to beta globin takes place. Excess gamma chains can assemble into Hb Bart (γ4), and excess beta chains can assemble into HbH (β4). Their properties differ from normal oxygen transport tetramers. These examples tie gene dosage to developmental gene expression rather than treating haemoglobin as a static molecular species.

Cystic fibrosis and haemophilia: transport versus cascade defects

Epithelial cells maintain fluid composition across a selectively permeable barrier. CFTR encodes a membrane protein that functions as a regulated anion channel, transporting chloride and contributing to bicarbonate transport. When CFTR function is substantially reduced, epithelial ion movement and associated water balance are altered. In the airway, disruption of the surface environment can impair mucus clearance; in other organs the consequences depend on the local transport physiology. The molecular deficit therefore affects several organs, an illustration of pleiotropy.

The common F508del variant removes a phenylalanine residue, disrupting normal CFTR folding and processing so that much of the protein fails to reach the cell surface. Residual molecules can also have altered channel behaviour. Other CFTR variants can primarily reduce channel opening, reduce conductance or decrease expression. It follows that a sequence variant need not eliminate protein synthesis to impair function. A cell with abundant protein at the wrong location can have lower membrane transport than a cell with fewer molecules correctly positioned.

In a conceptual comparison of cultured epithelial samples, an assay of total CFTR protein and an assay of cell-surface CFTR protein answer different questions. If total protein is detectable but membrane protein is greatly reduced, defective processing or trafficking is plausible. If surface abundance is preserved but ion current remains low, a defect in activity becomes more plausible. Neither observation alone identifies the DNA change. Experiments must control for cell number, sample loading and conditions of stimulation. Such inference is the bridge between a recessive family pattern and the molecular processes of the affected cells.

Haemophilia illustrates a distinct mechanism. The coagulation network amplifies signals leading to thrombin generation and fibrin formation. Factor VIII supports factor IXa in the intrinsic tenase complex, which facilitates activation of factor X. Loss or reduction of either factor can impair the generation of sufficient downstream activity even though many other components are present. Haemophilia A is classically associated with F8 variants and haemophilia B with F9 variants. Both genes are X-linked, and their disorders can resemble one another phenotypically despite different molecular causes.

This similarity is an example of locus heterogeneity: variants at more than one genetic locus can produce related phenotypes through a shared pathway. It also illustrates why a cascade should be analysed as a sequence of dependent activities. Increasing a downstream substrate does not necessarily restore the missing catalytic or cofactor step. In a hypothetical activity assay, low factor VIII activity with preserved factor IX activity supports a different functional deficit from preserved factor VIII with low factor IX activity. The assays do not, by themselves, prove the inheritance route or distinguish inherited defects from all acquired causes.

An X-linked pedigree can be consistent with either F8 or F9 variation. A molecular or functional observation is needed to distinguish them. The exam-relevant point is that pedigree geometry specifies a class of inheritance, not a unique gene.

Experimental reasoning across channel, matrix and coagulation disorders

Several monogenic diseases appear to share a simple loss-of-function pattern in pedigrees but differ in the experiments required to understand them. A membrane transporter, an extracellular matrix component and a soluble coagulation protein have different physical roles. It is therefore useful to select functional measurements that correspond to the normal job of each product rather than rely on a generic test of protein abundance.

For a membrane channel, a conceptual model of current is I = N × i × Popen, where N is the number of channels in the membrane, i is the average current through a single open channel at the specified driving force, and Popen is the probability of a channel being open. The variables describe different biological processes. A trafficking defect can reduce N without reducing the conductance of the few channels that arrive. A gating defect can lower Popen while leaving membrane abundance comparatively intact. A change in the pore can alter i. The expression is a simplified expectation for a collection of similar independently behaving channels measured under fixed conditions; actual epithelial transport also depends on other membrane proteins and driving forces.

Suppose wild-type cells have 100 arbitrary units of surface channel abundance and an observed current of 100 units under fixed conditions. Sample A has 20 units of surface abundance and 20 units of current. Sample B has 100 units of surface abundance and 20 units of current. One plausible interpretation is that sample A has reduced delivery or retention of the channel at the membrane, while sample B has impaired activity per surface channel. If total protein in A is low, reduced synthesis or increased degradation might contribute; if total protein is near normal, intracellular retention becomes more plausible. The observations are illustrative and do not establish the exact variant. The experimental variables and their relation to the mechanism are the educational purpose of the example.

A barrier measurement can provide another perspective. An epithelial layer consists of cells with apical and basolateral membranes and intercellular junctions. Changes in transepithelial transport can result from disruption of any of these components. When interpreting CFTR-related measurements, the location of the affected channel and the tissue's normal transport direction matter. The sweat duct, for example, normally reabsorbs chloride and sodium, whereas secretory epithelia have different transport arrangements. A defect can therefore produce different physiological consequences in different tissues without invoking unrelated causal genes.

For haemophilia, measuring the abundance of a factor is not always equivalent to measuring its activity. An inactive protein can be present at normal antigen concentration but fail to perform its biological function. A quantitative activity assay asks how much of a defined reaction the sample supports; an antigen assay asks how much recognisable protein is present. A hypothetical sample has factor VIII antigen at 90 percent of a reference value but factor VIII functional activity at 10 percent. This suggests a qualitative functional defect or inhibition rather than a simple tenfold reduction in the amount of detectable protein. Another sample with both values near 10 percent is more compatible with a quantitative deficit. Neither alone distinguishes all possible causes.

The coagulation system also demonstrates pathway dependence. Imagine a reaction sequence in which active factor IX and its cofactor VIII together promote formation of active factor X. A low output at this stage can result from insufficient IX activity, insufficient VIII activity or conditions that prevent their productive association. A measurement confined to the final product is not enough to identify the affected component. Separate measurements of each activity provide more discriminating evidence. In examination settings, this kind of comparison can be presented as a table of normal and reduced activities rather than as a memorised list of factor numbers.

For a structural matrix protein such as fibrillin, another distinction is whether a protein has been secreted and whether it contributes normally to organised extracellular fibres. Detecting a mutant product outside the cell does not prove that the matrix has acquired normal mechanical properties. A matrix assembled from abnormal components may have altered architecture or interactions. Likewise, the observation that a tissue is mechanically weak does not by itself tell us whether the cause is reduced synthesis, altered assembly, increased degradation or changes in supporting components. Each model predicts a different combination of product abundance and structural organisation.

A simple experimental comparison can distinguish two broad mechanisms. In one hypothetical group, cells expressing only one functional allele generate less matrix protein but the fibres that form are organised normally. In a second group, cells produce a similar amount of total detectable protein as reference cells but deposit disorganised fibres. The first pattern is compatible with reduced supply, whereas the second suggests that the product or its interactions are abnormal. Additional evidence is needed to rule out indirect regulatory effects. A strong answer avoids calling every dominant structural disorder dominant-negative without testing the assumptions.

For dystrophin, a relevant structural observation is the continuity and localisation of the protein around muscle fibres. An assay that measures total protein may miss abnormal localisation or differences between cells. Comparison of protein size and localisation can help explain why distinct variants of DMD produce different functional capacities. A partial protein may retain regions required for mechanical support; another partial product may lack an essential region. A reading-frame calculation is useful for predicting whether translation proceeds in register, but it is not a full functional assessment.

These examples emphasise a general principle: a test must correspond to a well-defined question. DNA sequence addresses the identity of a variant; RNA measurements address transcript processing and abundance; protein assays address quantity, form or localisation; functional measurements address biological activity. None automatically substitutes for all the others. In a Part C problem, select the observation that separates competing explanations rather than the one that merely confirms that an affected sample differs from a reference.

Dominant and X-linked structural disorders

Marfan syndrome provides a contrast with recessive enzyme and channel deficiencies. FBN1 encodes fibrillin-1, an extracellular matrix protein involved in microfibril structure. These structures contribute to tissue organisation and influence signalling contexts. A pathogenic change in a single FBN1 allele can be associated with a dominant multisystem phenotype, involving such tissues as the skeleton, ocular structures and cardiovascular system. The broad distribution of extracellular matrix structures helps explain how one locus can affect several apparently unrelated organs.

Dominance may reflect insufficient normal fibrillin or interference by altered protein, depending on the allele. It is not necessary to assume that all pathogenic FBN1 variants operate through one identical molecular mechanism. This matters in an examination asking for a distinction between a null allele and a dominant-negative allele. If mutant products are absent and reducing the amount of wild-type product reproduces the phenotype, dosage insufficiency is plausible. If a mutant protein actively disrupts a complex even in the presence of sufficient wild-type product, dominant-negative interference is more plausible. A dominant pedigree alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms.

Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy are related dystrophinopathies caused by pathogenic variation in the X-linked DMD gene. Dystrophin connects the intracellular actin-associated cytoskeleton to a membrane-associated protein complex that helps transmit mechanical force. Its loss makes the muscle cell membrane more susceptible to damage during contraction. Repeated injury and degeneration help explain progressive muscle dysfunction. Dystrophin has a structural role rather than serving simply as an enzyme in a metabolic reaction.

A useful, though not absolute, genotype-phenotype rule is that many variants disrupting the reading frame cause little functional dystrophin and a Duchenne phenotype, while many deletions preserving the reading frame permit a partly functional protein and a Becker phenotype. An in-frame deletion in a critical functional region can nevertheless cause severe disease, and some apparently frame-disrupting variants can have unexpected RNA-processing consequences. Reading-frame predictions are therefore mechanistic hypotheses rather than guarantees.

Consider a hypothetical coding sequence with three adjacent exons containing 90, 91 and 92 coding nucleotides. Deleting the exon of 90 nucleotides preserves the reading frame if the adjoining splice boundaries permit the expected joining; deleting the exon of 91 nucleotides shifts the frame. The exercise tests divisibility by three, not the clinical severity of a real variant. Protein domain loss, splicing and residual expression must also be considered. This is an example of allelic heterogeneity in which multiple variants of one gene produce a spectrum of phenotypes.

Chromosome dosage and representative aneuploidies

A chromosome contains many genes. Changing the number of copies of a chromosome changes the dosage of multiple loci simultaneously, even if each individual gene sequence is intact. In a simplified non-compensated expression model, three copies of a gene produce 1.5 times the expression expected from two copies. Real cells can compensate partially or change regulatory networks, and not all transcripts track copy number equally. Nevertheless the comparison illustrates why aneuploidy can affect diverse developmental processes at once.

Down syndrome commonly involves three copies of chromosome 21, represented for a typical full trisomy in an XY complement as 47,XY,+21. Extra chromosome 21 material can also result from a Robertsonian translocation, and a mosaic individual may have both disomic and trisomic cell populations. The important genetic feature is the increased dosage of relevant chromosome 21 material, not simply the total chromosome count. An individual with translocation Down syndrome may have 46 chromosomes while carrying excess chromosome 21 long-arm material. The origin and recurrence patterns of free trisomy and translocation-associated forms can consequently differ. Detailed meiotic segregation of rearranged chromosomes is treated in subunit 8.F.

Turner syndrome is associated with complete or partial absence of a second sex chromosome in individuals typically developing a female phenotype. The classic karyotype is 45,X, but mosaics and structural X abnormalities occur. In typical XX cells, one X is largely inactivated; some genes escape inactivation, including genes in the pseudoautosomal regions. Reduced dosage of such genes helps explain why a single X is not generally equivalent to two X chromosomes with one inactive. SHOX dosage contributes to the growth phenotype, without accounting for all manifestations.

Klinefelter syndrome commonly involves a 47,XXY complement in an individual typically developing a male phenotype. One X is largely inactivated, so extra expression is not a uniform doubling of every X-linked gene. Genes escaping X-inactivation and broader developmental effects contribute to differences in growth and gonadal function. The textbook Barr body rule predicts approximately one inactive X in 47,XXY and none in 45,X, in cell types where conventional X-inactivation applies. The rule counts inactive X chromosomes, not the number of Y chromosomes, and does not imply that an inactive X contributes no transcription.

A schematic dataset can test the distinction between mosaicism and ordinary aneuploidy. If 16 of 40 scored cells from a specimen show 47,XX,+21 and the remainder show 46,XX, the observed trisomic fraction in the specimen is 40 percent. It is not legitimate to conclude that 40 percent of every organ is trisomic. Sampling, culture selection and tissue-specific lineage history can influence the measured fraction. Cytogenetic methods estimate particular features in particular samples, not an entire organism's complete genotype.

Tracing chromosome origin, dosage and cytogenetic evidence

Chromosome number is normally established by the reduction of chromosome sets during meiosis and their restoration at fertilisation. Homologous chromosomes normally separate in the first meiotic division, and sister chromatids separate during the second. If segregation of a particular pair fails in meiosis I, the resulting gametes in a simple four-product representation can include two with an extra homolog and two lacking that homolog. If the failure occurs in meiosis II in one of the two cells, the four products may instead include two with the ordinary number, one with an extra chromatid-derived chromosome and one lacking it. This idealised comparison is useful for understanding the origins of aneuploid conceptions, though human oogenesis has asymmetric divisions rather than four equivalent eggs.

Combining a gamete containing two copies of chromosome 21 with a gamete containing one yields a conception with three copies. The corresponding loss can yield monosomy. A postzygotic segregation error is different: the embryo may have started with an ordinary complement, and only descendants of the cell in which the error occurs have an altered complement. The timing and subsequent proliferation of cell lineages then influence mosaic composition. A simple karyogram showing trisomy does not necessarily distinguish all possible timing mechanisms.

Genetic marker information can sometimes provide evidence about meiotic origin. Consider a parent heterozygous at a marker near the centromere of a chromosome, with distinguishable alleles A and B on the two homologs. If an abnormal gamete contains both A and B at the centromeric region, failure to segregate the two homologs at the first division is a plausible explanation. If the two copies carry the same marker allele, failure of sister chromatid segregation at the second division is plausible under a simplified model. Recombination, marker informativeness and other mechanisms can complicate this inference. It is essential not to generalise the centromere-based logic to all distal markers without considering crossing over.

A balanced Robertsonian translocation can join the long arms of two acrocentric chromosomes into a derivative chromosome. The number of separately counted chromosomes is then reduced, often to 45 in a balanced carrier, while the dosage of the essential long-arm genes can remain approximately balanced. During meiosis, segregation may yield a gamete with an unbalanced arrangement. The resulting offspring can have additional chromosome 21 long-arm material without the usual count of 47 chromosomes. This mechanism is conceptually distinct from a free extra chromosome, and the physical organisation of the chromosomes is relevant to transmission.

Consider three hypothetical cytogenetic descriptions. Case A has 47 chromosomes, including three separate chromosome 21 homologs. Case B has 46 chromosomes with a derivative containing extra chromosome 21 long-arm material in addition to two ordinary copies of that relevant material. Case C has 45 chromosomes and a balanced fusion that retains the relevant long-arm dosage of chromosomes 14 and 21. Case A and B can show dosage imbalance related to Down syndrome despite different chromosome counts, while C illustrates that a reduced chromosome count is not sufficient evidence of a corresponding loss of essential gene dosage. These descriptions are schematic rather than complete clinical ISCN notation.

X-chromosome inactivation adds another regulatory level to the relation between chromosomes and phenotype. In most somatic cells of typical XX individuals, one of the two X chromosomes becomes largely transcriptionally inactive early in development. Descendant cells generally maintain their choice of active X, producing a mosaic of expression in heterozygotes. This is functional mosaicism even when the sequence genotype of every cell is the same. It differs from a karyotypic mosaic containing cells with different chromosome complements.

Some genes escape inactivation, and the fraction of escape varies by gene and tissue. In the pseudoautosomal regions, homologous sequences are present on X and Y chromosomes. Such genes can be expressed from both copies in typical XY as well as typical XX complements. The loss of a second sex chromosome in 45,X can thus reduce expression of genes normally supplied by both chromosomes. In 47,XXY, an inactive X still contributes expression of escape genes, and the extra dosage can have biological effects. The presence of a Barr body is not a statement that all genes on the condensed chromosome are permanently silent.

The most useful way to compare these cases is to define the question before choosing the measurement. Chromosome counts help identify numerical changes. Banding helps reveal large structural rearrangements. Locus-specific fluorescence signals can indicate the number and position of selected sequences. An array can estimate copy number across regions but does not necessarily establish the physical arrangement of those sequences or detect balanced rearrangements. Each technique has resolution limits, which were introduced in the preceding cytogenetics chapter. A normal result from one method cannot exclude every genetic cause of a phenotype.

Mitochondrial disorders and variable transmission

Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation requires proteins encoded by both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes. A variant in a nuclear gene involved in mitochondrial function therefore follows the relevant nuclear inheritance pattern. It is the physical location of the causal allele, rather than the location of the affected organelle, that determines the usual route of transmission.

Human mitochondrial DNA is typically inherited maternally. An egg contributes a population of mitochondria to the zygote, whereas paternal mitochondrial contribution does not normally persist. Mitochondrial DNA is present in many copies per cell, so a cell can contain a mixture of different mitochondrial genotypes, termed heteroplasmy. Homoplasmy refers to a state in which the mitochondrial DNA copies at a particular site are of the same type. Neither term alone establishes whether a tissue functions normally.

During oocyte development and subsequent cell divisions, sampling and replication of mitochondrial DNA can lead to markedly different heteroplasmy fractions between offspring and tissues. This phenomenon is connected to the mitochondrial genetic bottleneck. The fraction of altered mitochondrial genomes is therefore not generally transmitted as a fixed nuclear Mendelian ratio. The same family can show a broad distribution of severity, and both male and female offspring may express an mtDNA-associated condition, though maternal transmission is the expected route to the next generation.

MELAS, a mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, illustrates dysfunction of energy-demanding tissues. A common associated variant is m.3243A>G in the mitochondrial tRNA gene MT-TL1. Disturbance of mitochondrial translation can affect respiratory chain function. An impairment of oxidative energy metabolism is particularly relevant to tissues with sustained ATP demands. The mutation is not the only cause of the syndrome, and a heteroplasmy measurement in one tissue does not fix the phenotype in every other tissue.

Suppose a cell contains 300 copies of mitochondrial DNA at a particular locus and 180 carry an altered allele. Its heteroplasmy fraction at that locus is 180/300 = 0.60. This is a genome-copy fraction, not the probability that the person's next child has disease, and not necessarily the fraction of mitochondria containing a variant because individual mitochondria can contain several genomes. A threshold model describes the possibility that substantial dysfunction appears only when the altered genome fraction exceeds the functional reserve of a cell. Thresholds vary with allele and tissue. The model helps explain non-linear phenotype changes without suggesting a universal percentage cutoff.

Mitochondrial segregation, respiratory reserve and tissue evidence

To understand why mitochondrial inheritance differs from ordinary diploid segregation, consider how the genetic material is packaged. A nuclear autosomal locus is ordinarily represented by two homologous copies in a somatic cell. Mitochondrial genetic material, in contrast, is distributed among many DNA molecules in a mitochondrial network. Mitochondria divide, fuse and exchange components, and mitochondrial genomes replicate in a context distinct from nuclear chromosome replication. A particular cell can therefore contain a large population of genomes rather than a pair of alleles. It is not appropriate to assign that cell the ordinary diploid genotypes AA, Aa and aa at a mitochondrial DNA locus.

If a heteroplasmic cell divides, daughter cells can receive different fractions of the two genome types. Replication and turnover can subsequently shift those fractions further. A conceptual model in which a small effective sample of genomes is transmitted and then amplified explains why substantial heteroplasmy differences can appear in relatives. In a teaching example, suppose the source population has 50 percent altered genomes and each daughter lineage initially samples only four genomes independently. The probability of obtaining exactly two altered copies is six times one half to the fourth power, equal to six sixteenths or 37.5 percent. The probability of sampling no altered copy is one sixteenth, and that of sampling all four is also one sixteenth. Real mitochondrial inheritance is not literally a four-copy sampling process; the example demonstrates why small effective population sizes permit widely varying outcomes.

Amplifying each sampled population does not necessarily restore its original fraction. If every genome in a sample has an equal opportunity to replicate, a sample containing three altered genomes and one reference genome tends to remain near 75 percent altered on average as it expands. Selection among mitochondrial genomes or among cells can modify the outcome. It is this combination of stochastic partitioning and biological regulation, rather than simple segregation of a pair of chromosomes, that accounts for many maternal pedigree patterns.

A tissue needs a certain respiratory capacity relative to its workload. The consequences of a mitochondrial variant depend on the affected molecular process and the fraction of functional products available. Electron transfer, maintenance of an electrochemical gradient and ATP synthesis form linked processes. A variant that interferes with mitochondrial translation may affect several respiratory complexes at once because multiple components depend on proteins encoded by mitochondrial DNA. The phenotype can therefore have an apparently broad biochemical origin even when the primary sequence alteration is localised to one gene.

An important conceptual distinction is that a high percentage of altered mitochondrial DNA need not correspond to exactly the same percentage loss of respiratory capacity. Different products can be shared within mitochondrial compartments; complexes differ in their sensitivity to incomplete assembly; cellular pathways can compensate in varying degrees. Once reserve becomes inadequate, functional output may fall steeply. A threshold diagram can illustrate this principle but cannot determine the threshold for a particular mutation or organ without empirical evidence.

Imagine two hypothetical tissues from a model organism, one with modest energy demand and the other with sustained high energy demand. Suppose they contain similar proportions of altered mitochondrial DNA but differ in respiratory reserve. If the higher-demand tissue shows dysfunction before the lower-demand tissue, the difference does not prove that the former carries more altered DNA. Conversely, two tissues with comparable metabolic demand might differ because their heteroplasmy fractions diverged during development. Both fraction and demand need to be considered. This form of reasoning explains why the distribution of an organellar genome change matters.

MELAS provides an example of a mitochondrial tRNA defect. Transfer RNA molecules must participate in decoding messenger RNA during translation. A change affecting a mitochondrial tRNA can interfere with its stability or function, limiting synthesis of mitochondrial proteins. The respiratory chain depends partly on such proteins, so inefficient translation can have consequences beyond one reaction. As ATP production through oxidative pathways becomes impaired, cells can rely more heavily on other routes of ATP generation. Lactic acidosis is consistent with disturbed energy metabolism, but lactate alone is not a specific marker of a single mitochondrial gene or disorder.

A useful examination experiment compares respiratory measurements and DNA data. Suppose two cell cultures contain comparable mitochondrial mass, but one has lower respiratory capacity and a higher fraction of an altered mitochondrial allele. That association supports the idea that the variant contributes to dysfunction, but other nuclear genetic differences or culture conditions might also contribute. A stronger comparison uses samples with a matched nuclear background or follows changes in respiratory capacity across a series of samples with varying heteroplasmy. The goal is to discriminate competing explanations, not to infer causality from any one measurement.

The same logic applies to maternal pedigrees. Imagine an affected mother and children of both chromosomal sexes with different levels of dysfunction. An affected son has children who lack the phenotype, while an affected daughter has both affected and unaffected children. A mitochondrial DNA model is plausible because transmission is concentrated through the maternal line and expression varies. But a small pedigree is not conclusive: an autosomal model with incomplete penetrance could generate some similar observations. Genetic localisation and appropriate molecular evidence strengthen the mitochondrial interpretation.

There is a second distinction between maternal mitochondrial inheritance and maternal effect. In a maternal-effect trait, the offspring's early phenotype can depend on products supplied by the mother's nuclear genotype, even though nuclear alleles themselves segregate normally. In mitochondrial inheritance, genetic material carried in the maternal cytoplasm is itself transmitted and replicated. A maternal-effect allele can pass through a father and subsequently exert its effect when inherited by a female offspring who produces eggs. A pathogenic mitochondrial DNA allele typically does not follow this route through the father. A question stating only that maternal phenotype predicts offspring phenotype does not necessarily distinguish the two mechanisms.

Mitochondrial disorders also provide a counterexample to the assumption that the gene responsible for dysfunction must lie in the affected organelle. The majority of proteins required for mitochondrial structure, maintenance and metabolism are encoded by nuclear genes and imported into mitochondria. A recessive nuclear defect affecting mitochondrial function can produce affected siblings born to unaffected heterozygous parents. If the pedigree shows ordinary biparental transmission, a mitochondrial location of the physiological defect is not evidence against a nuclear causal gene. The molecular location of the allele is the relevant distinction.

One can quantify the difference between a genome-copy measurement and a phenotypic frequency without confusing their denominators. If a laboratory sample contains 400 altered copies among 1,000 mitochondrial DNA molecules, the altered fraction is 0.4. If 4 of 10 offspring in a hypothetical family show a phenotype, the observed phenotype frequency is also 0.4. These numerically equal values describe different quantities. There is no general equation that converts the first into the second. This distinction becomes important when students encounter examination statements suggesting that 40 percent heteroplasmy implies a 40 percent risk for each offspring.

Finally, compare an organellar genotype with a karyotypic mosaic. Both can produce tissue variation, but their units differ. In a karyotypic mosaic, the proportion of cells with a particular chromosome complement is measured. In a heteroplasmic sample, the fraction of mitochondrial DNA molecules carrying a variant is measured; a single cell may contribute many copies of both types. A bulk sample with 50 percent altered mitochondrial DNA need not contain half fully altered and half fully reference cells. It could contain a more evenly mixed population across cells or many intermediate arrangements. Without additional data, one should not infer the cellular distribution from the bulk genome-copy fraction.

Dynamic repeat expansion and anticipation

Many short DNA motifs occur consecutively in the human genome. A repeat length can sometimes change during transmission, rather than behaving as an allele of permanently fixed size. The probability and direction of change depend on the repeat sequence, genomic context and parental transmission. The concept of genetic anticipation describes a tendency toward earlier onset or greater severity in successive generations in some inherited disorders. It is a statistical and mechanistic tendency, not a rule that every descendant will have more severe disease.

Huntington disease is caused by expansion of a CAG repeat in the coding region of HTT. CAG encodes glutamine, so the expansion lengthens a polyglutamine region of the huntingtin protein. The altered protein contributes to neuronal dysfunction through multiple effects on cellular processes. The condition classically follows autosomal dominant transmission. Longer expansions are generally associated with earlier onset, and large intergenerational increases are particularly associated with paternal transmission. GeneReviews distinguishes 36–39 repeats as reduced-penetrance disease-causing alleles and 40 or more as the full-penetrance range, while age of manifestation varies. Repeat length is a predictor, not an exact clock.

Fragile X syndrome instead usually involves a CGG expansion in a noncoding region of FMR1. Full expansion is commonly accompanied by methylation and silencing, decreasing expression of the RNA-binding protein FMRP. This is fundamentally different from the coding polyglutamine expansion in Huntington disease. A premutation has distinct biological implications from a silenced full mutation. Expansion from a premutation into the full-mutation range is principally associated with maternal transmission. Both molecular mechanism and parent-of-origin transmission patterns matter.

Suppose a hypothetical sequence analysis shows an increasing number of CAG triplets within an open reading frame. An increase of ten triplets adds 30 nucleotides and, if translated, ten glutamine residues without shifting the downstream reading frame. The same arithmetic applied to a noncoding CGG expansion does not justify a prediction of ten extra amino acids. The genomic position of a repeat is indispensable information. A question presenting a repeat size alone is incomplete if it asks for an exact protein-level consequence without specifying where the repeat lies.

Repeat length, molecular consequence and family evidence

The genetic instability of a repeated sequence illustrates a limitation of the simplest allele notation. In a Mendelian cross we often assign an allele a symbol such as A and assume that it is copied faithfully between generations. That approximation is appropriate for many sequence variants, but the number of copies of a short repeat may increase or decrease during transmission. Repeat structures can influence how DNA is processed during replication and repair. The precise mechanism differs among repeat loci, and a single generic slippage explanation should not be treated as a complete account of every disease-associated expansion.

What matters at the level of inheritance is that an allele can have a variable state in addition to its identity at a locus. A parent carrying a repeat-bearing chromosome may transmit that chromosome according to the usual segregation rules, yet the offspring's repeat length may differ from the parent's. The probability of transmitting the chromosome and the conditional probability of a repeat change are different. If a heterozygous parent transmits the repeat-bearing chromosome with probability one half and, in a hypothetical model, that repeat expands on 20 percent of such transmissions, the joint probability of transmitting an expanded repeat is 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1. The numbers are invented to demonstrate probability; actual repeat dynamics depend on the locus and the particular allele.

Huntington disease offers a clear case of a coding repeat. Translation of an mRNA containing successive CAG codons produces successive glutamine residues. Increasing the length of this sequence alters the huntingtin protein without necessarily shifting the reading frame, since each additional complete triplet adds three nucleotides. A polyglutamine expansion can change protein interactions and cellular handling of the protein. This is commonly described as a toxic gain of function, though that phrase should not imply that every normal huntingtin activity is preserved or that only one downstream molecular pathway is involved.

A useful classification for HTT repeats separates repeat counts that are not disease-causing from alleles with instability and from disease-associated ranges. In the conventional classification, alleles up to 26 repeats are in the normal range, 27–35 are termed intermediate, 36–39 have reduced penetrance, and at least 40 are in the full-penetrance range. The intermediate category is important because instability of transmission and expression of disease are different properties. Likewise, reduced penetrance means that the presence of an allele does not uniquely establish whether or when the phenotype will occur. These classifications should be interpreted with age and other biological modifiers in mind.

The relation between repeat length and onset is a statistical relationship rather than a deterministic schedule. If a group of individuals with longer repeats tends to have earlier onset than a group with shorter repeats, that pattern supports an effect of repeat length. Consider two hypothetical groups with median onsets of 42 and 55 years and overlapping distributions. It would be erroneous to assign every individual in the first group an onset of 42 years. A median describes the distribution, not a fixed prediction for a carrier. Genetic background, interruptions in repeat sequences and other factors contribute to variation. An observation of earlier onset in a single child compared with a parent is compatible with anticipation but is not sufficient by itself to prove repeat expansion.

Fragile X syndrome makes a contrasting example because the CGG repeat occurs in the 5′ untranslated region of FMR1. This region can be transcribed into RNA but does not encode a stretch of the normal protein. Thus the addition of complete triplets does not imply a longer poly-amino-acid tract. A large full expansion is commonly associated with abnormal methylation and transcriptional silencing. FMRP normally contributes to regulation of RNA metabolism and translation in cells including neurons. Reduced expression of FMRP is therefore a fundamentally different molecular consequence from the expanded coding product of HTT.

FMR1 repeat alleles are commonly distinguished as normal, intermediate, premutation and full mutation categories. A premutation generally contains approximately 55–200 CGG repeats and a full mutation more than 200. These categories are not interchangeable with the HTT classification; they apply to a different motif and locus. Premutation alleles usually have a distinct expression state and can be associated with other biological outcomes, including disorders different from the classical fragile X phenotype. Full expansions commonly show silencing, although mosaic repeat sizes and methylation states can complicate the relationship. For examination purposes, the key is to distinguish expansion length, genomic position, epigenetic state and phenotype.

A family in which an individual with an FMR1 premutation transmits an allele to a child does not necessarily transmit a full mutation. The repeat may remain in the premutation range or change, and the transition depends on the initial allele and transmission context. Full expansion from premutation is predominantly a maternal-transmission phenomenon. An X-linked pedigree must therefore be interpreted together with the repeat's instability. A father generally passes his X chromosome to daughters, not sons, but that fact does not establish the probability of an expansion into every repeat category. Transmission of the chromosome and transition of repeat state remain separate questions.

Compare two hypothetical molecular experiments. In the first, a coding repeat-bearing allele produces RNA and a protein with an expanded region. In the second, a noncoding repeat-bearing allele shows markedly increased methylation, low RNA abundance and reduced protein abundance. Both involve expansion, but the first supports an altered protein model while the second supports an expression-loss model. An experiment measuring RNA alone cannot distinguish every possible downstream effect in the first case, and a measurement of DNA repeat length alone cannot fully characterise methylation mosaicism in the second. The joint interpretation of sequence position and expression is essential.

A useful distinction is between anticipation and imprinting. Both can produce differences associated with parental transmission, but anticipation concerns changes in the severity or age of onset across generations and often relates to changing repeat length. Imprinting concerns differential expression according to the parental origin of a nuclear allele. An imprinted gene does not have to undergo repeat expansion. Similarly, an expanded repeat may have a parental bias in transmission without being an imprinted gene. The mere observation of parent-of-origin differences does not uniquely identify the mechanism.

A final statistical issue is ascertainment. If a family is recruited because a younger member is affected while older relatives are still being examined retrospectively, it may be easier to document severe early-onset cases than mild later-onset cases. Such selection can create an apparent difference between generations even without a demonstrable DNA repeat change. The strongest evidence for expansion-mediated anticipation connects repeat measurements in family members with phenotypic trends while considering the ages and selection of participants. The objective is not to reject the established phenomenon but to understand what counts as convincing evidence for it in a particular dataset.

Genomic imprinting and parent-of-origin effects

For most autosomal loci, the same allele is expected to have broadly comparable potential for expression regardless of which parent transmitted it. Imprinting is a regulated exception in which expression of particular genes depends on parental origin, through epigenetic marks established in the germline and maintained in relevant tissues. The DNA sequence need not differ between two copies for their activity to differ. This makes chromosome origin a biologically important variable.

Prader–Willi syndrome results from loss of expression of paternally active genes in a region of chromosome 15. A paternal deletion can remove those genes; maternal uniparental disomy can leave two maternal-origin chromosomes but no appropriately active paternal copy; and certain imprinting defects can disrupt the normal expression program. The same general phenotype can therefore arise from distinct genomic mechanisms. A copy number assay alone may not detect uniparental disomy or an imprinting defect.

Angelman syndrome involves loss of appropriate maternal UBE3A function in the nervous system, where the paternal copy is largely silenced in relevant neurons. A maternal deletion can remove the active copy, while paternal uniparental disomy can supply two paternal copies without the appropriate maternal expression. Pathogenic variants in the active maternal UBE3A allele can also produce the condition. The comparison with Prader–Willi syndrome demonstrates that the same general chromosomal neighbourhood can have contrasting consequences depending on parental origin and the particular expressed genes.

The contrast should not be reduced to a statement that all chromosome 15 genes are either maternally or paternally active. Imprinting is gene-specific and may depend on tissue and developmental stage. It also differs from maternal cytoplasmic inheritance: the genes in this example lie in the nuclear genome and imprint marks are normally reset in the germline according to the sex of the transmitting individual. Maternal mitochondrial inheritance instead reflects transmission of organellar genetic material. A parent-of-origin pattern can arise through either mechanism, but the mechanistic predictions are different.

Imprinting, uniparental disomy and discriminating evidence

A gene can be present in two copies but functionally expressed from only one in a particular tissue. This fact changes the interpretation of dosage. A deletion of one copy of an ordinary biallelically expressed gene reduces the amount of potentially active genetic material. A deletion of the expressed copy of an imprinted gene can eliminate the relevant expression entirely, even if the other homolog still contains an intact DNA sequence. Deleting the normally silent copy may have little direct effect on that gene's expression under the same conditions. The consequences of a structurally similar change can therefore depend on which parent supplied the affected chromosome.

To make the reasoning explicit, designate the maternal homolog M and the paternal homolog P. In a simplified imprinted region, gene group A is expressed from P and gene B is expressed from M in the relevant tissue. The reference M/P combination contains one expressed copy of each. If the paternal region is deleted, expression of A is lost, while expression of B can remain. If the maternal region is deleted, expression of B is lost, while A can remain. If a cell has two intact maternal homologs but no paternal homolog, it can have a normal copy number yet lack the expression normally associated with P. The model deliberately omits other genes and tissue-dependent details; its purpose is to illustrate the distinction between physical copy number and active allele number.

Uniparental disomy means that both copies of a chromosome or a chromosomal region originate from one parent. Heterodisomy refers to inheritance of both homologs of that parent, whereas isodisomy refers to two copies of the same parental homolog. Recombination can produce more complicated mixtures of these states along a chromosome. Uniparental disomy is particularly relevant to disease mechanisms when imprinted loci require contributions with both parental expression programs, or when isodisomy makes a recessive allele homozygous. It does not necessarily entail an abnormal total chromosome count.

One possible origin of uniparental disomy is trisomy rescue. Imagine an early conception containing two maternal copies of chromosome 15 and one paternal copy. If a subsequent cell loses the paternal chromosome, its chromosome count can return to the usual number while leaving two maternal copies of chromosome 15. If instead it loses one of the maternal chromosomes, it retains one of each origin. In a deliberately simplified equal-probability model, one of the three possible chromosome losses produces maternal uniparental disomy. This is not a clinical frequency; the model ignores differences in viability, segregation and other possible developmental mechanisms. Its purpose is to show how correction of chromosome number does not necessarily restore parental origin.

Prader–Willi and Angelman syndromes make the distinction experimentally relevant. Both are associated with alterations involving a region of chromosome 15, but the critical expressed products differ. Loss of the paternal expression program in the Prader–Willi region and loss of appropriate maternal UBE3A activity in neurons lead to distinct developmental phenotypes. The correct interpretation does not require memorising all the imprinted genes in the region. Instead, it requires linking the missing expressed allele to the expected molecular deficit and recognising that a deletion is not the only way to disrupt expression.

A hypothetical laboratory comparison illustrates how multiple evidence types can be combined. In sample A, the relevant chromosome region has reduced physical copy number and markers identify the remaining copy as maternal. This is compatible with a paternal deletion. In sample B, the region has two physical copies and informative markers indicate both are maternally derived. That supports maternal uniparental disomy. In sample C, two copies of biparental origin are detected, yet methylation at a relevant imprint-associated region resembles a maternal-only pattern. An imprinting defect becomes a plausible explanation. These three samples can converge on loss of the paternal expression program even though the genetic changes differ.

A methylation measurement provides a different kind of information from sequencing. A conventional sequence analysis can identify the nucleotide sequence, but the same sequence may carry different epigenetic states. A methylation-associated observation can establish whether an imprint-related expression program is abnormal without necessarily identifying the underlying structural or parental-origin mechanism. A copy-number measurement can identify a deletion but may not detect two intact copies from the same parent. Informative parental markers can distinguish parental contributions but do not alone measure gene expression in neurons. The exam-relevant point is that the different techniques are complementary.

An imprinting mark is not necessarily retained unchanged across the entire life cycle of an allele. During germline development, many imprint-associated marks are erased and re-established according to the germline context. Consider a nuclear allele transmitted through a father into a daughter, who later transmits it to her own offspring. Its origin in her somatic cells is paternal, but in her offspring it is a maternally transmitted allele. This distinction is essential for understanding why a variant in an imprinted gene can behave differently depending on whether a male or female transmits it. It should not be confused with permanent inheritance of the grandparental expression state.

A comparison with X-inactivation is also informative. Both mechanisms can cause unequal expression of alleles, but conventional random X-inactivation creates a mosaic in which different cells may use different parental X chromosomes. Imprinting of a particular gene instead specifies a parental-origin bias in relevant cells. Tissue-specific imprinting and skewed X-inactivation complicate these simple pictures, yet the fundamental distinction remains. The observation of one predominantly active allele is not sufficient by itself to decide which mechanism produced the bias.

Finally, distinguish uniparental disomy from ordinary homozygosity. A person can be homozygous for a common marker allele while inheriting one copy from each parent. Conversely, heterodisomy can retain two different marker alleles from one parent. A study must use informative markers and parental genotypes to infer origin, rather than assuming that any homozygous region proves uniparental inheritance. These principles connect genomic imprinting to the linkage and cytogenetic reasoning developed earlier in Human Genetics.

Heterogeneity, multifactorial risk and the evidence hierarchy

Allelic heterogeneity means that distinct variants at a single locus can produce related phenotypes. Locus heterogeneity means that variants in different genes can produce a similar phenotype, as when separate components of a biological pathway are impaired. Pleiotropy is the converse direction of description: variation at one locus contributes to multiple phenotypic effects. These terms answer different questions and need not be mutually exclusive. For instance, a gene can have many pathogenic alleles and also influence several tissues.

Multifactorial disorders are not explained by a single necessary and sufficient disease allele in the usual case. Variants at multiple loci can influence biological processes such as insulin secretion, metabolic regulation or vascular physiology. Environmental exposures, developmental history and chance also affect outcomes. A family may cluster for a trait because relatives share both alleles and environments, so clustering is not by itself proof of a simple inheritance mode.

One useful mathematical representation is a liability model. Imagine an underlying continuous variable L built from genetic contributions G and environmental or other contributions E. In the simplest additive representation L = G + E. If the phenotype is classified as present when L exceeds a chosen threshold T, many different combinations can result in the same category. This is a modelling device; it does not assert that a disorder has a single directly measurable liability or a sharp biological threshold in every individual. It explains why a binary phenotype may emerge from continuous variation.

Consider an illustrative dataset in which a condition is observed in 20 of 100 individuals with a particular marker allele and 10 of 100 individuals without it. The observed risk ratio is 0.20/0.10 = 2. The risk difference is 0.20 − 0.10 = 0.10, or ten percentage points. Neither statistic establishes that the marker causes the disorder. The marker may track a nearby causal variant, or groups may differ in an environmental exposure. Sample uncertainty also matters. Moreover, a doubling of risk does not mean that the outcome is inevitable: in the hypothetical marker group, 80 of the 100 people do not show the phenotype.

This distinction prepares the study of quantitative genetics. The next subunit develops population allele frequencies, variance, heritability and mapping. Human disease examples are useful because they show why the inference must respect biological mechanism, segregation and population context simultaneously. A pedigree, sequence, chromosome image and molecular assay each give a partial view. A convincing interpretation integrates them, identifies assumptions and avoids claiming certainty from evidence that cannot discriminate alternative explanations.

Complex traits, conditional risk and genetic evidence

When one major locus explains a familial disorder, a useful starting point is to enumerate parental gametes and calculate genotype probabilities. That method becomes insufficient when the phenotype depends on contributions from many loci and non-genetic factors. This does not mean that the rules of segregation stop operating. Each autosomal allele still follows the usual chromosome transmission rules under the relevant conditions. What becomes complex is the mapping from the combined genotype and environment to the measured phenotype.

A simple additive illustration uses three independent loci. At each locus, suppose a particular allele contributes one arbitrary unit to a liability and the alternative contributes none. A diploid genotype can then carry zero to six contributing copies across the three loci. If all contributing alleles have a frequency of one half and the population is at the assumed equilibrium with independent loci, the number of contributing copies follows the familiar binomial distribution for six draws with probability one half. The expected number is three, but individuals can have different totals. The frequency of exactly three copies is the number of ways to choose three from six, divided by 64, or 20/64. This is a mathematical toy model, not a description of the genetics of a specific human condition.

If the phenotype is present whenever the liability exceeds four units and no environmental contribution is present in this model, individuals with five or six contributing copies meet the threshold. There are six combinations with five and one with six, so the predicted frequency is 7/64. If an environmental contribution of one unit applies equally to a second group, individuals with four, five or six genetic units now meet the same criterion. The expected frequency becomes (15 + 6 + 1)/64 = 22/64. The allele frequencies did not change between groups. The difference arises because the relationship between genotype and threshold crossing changed. Such examples illustrate why population disease prevalence can change without a corresponding change in the frequency of every causal allele.

Real multifactorial models are more complicated. Alleles have different effect sizes, effects can depend on other loci, environmental variables may be correlated with genetic background, and the phenotype definition may be imperfect. A liability scale is an abstract representation designed to summarise these contributions. It is not generally possible to estimate a person's complete liability by assigning one point to each associated allele. Many statistical models estimate an average effect in a population, and the meaning of the effect depends on the population and measurement context.

It is useful to distinguish a genetic main effect, an environmental main effect and a gene–environment interaction. Suppose an illustrative exposure raises the phenotype frequency by ten percentage points in individuals with either of two genotypes. On a risk-difference scale, this pattern is consistent with the same exposure effect in the two genotype groups. If the exposure raises the frequency by two percentage points in one genotype and by thirty in the other, the association between exposure and phenotype differs according to genotype on that scale. That is an example of interaction. A statistical interaction depends on the chosen scale, so the same data can have different descriptions under additive and multiplicative models. One should specify the comparison rather than assume that any combined genetic and environmental influence is an interaction.

Consider a four-cell hypothetical dataset with 100 individuals in each group. Without exposure, 5 individuals of genotype G0 and 10 of genotype G1 show a phenotype. With exposure, 10 of genotype G0 and 40 of genotype G1 show it. The exposure-associated risk difference is 5 percentage points for G0 and 30 for G1. The difference of those differences is 25 percentage points. The genotype-associated risk ratio is 10/5 = 2 among unexposed individuals and 40/10 = 4 among exposed individuals. These calculations reveal different patterns of association across groups. They do not prove that exposure caused the outcome or that the genotype directly modifies a particular biochemical pathway. Confounding and measurement error must still be considered.

A common confusion concerns relative and absolute probability. A genetic marker may be reported to increase the relative frequency of a phenotype by a factor of two. If a reference probability is one percent, doubling corresponds to two percent, a difference of one percentage point. If a reference probability is twenty percent, doubling corresponds to forty percent, a difference of twenty percentage points. The same ratio can therefore represent very different differences in observed frequency. For a probability comparison to be meaningful, the reference group, outcome definition and observation period should be specified.

The distinction between an odds ratio and a risk ratio is another useful Part C skill. In an illustrative group of 100 carriers with 20 affected, the odds of the phenotype are 20/80 = 0.25. In a reference group of 100 with 10 affected, the odds are 10/90, approximately 0.111. Their odds ratio is about 2.25, whereas the risk ratio is exactly 2. At low frequencies, odds and risks may be numerically similar, but they are not identical definitions. An odds ratio is particularly common when the study design compares selected affected and unaffected groups. Such a design does not generally estimate population prevalence directly without additional sampling information.

A marker can be associated with a phenotype without being the molecular cause. If a marker allele tends to be inherited together with a nearby causal allele, the marker can show a statistical association through linkage disequilibrium. If population subgroups differ both in allele frequency and in phenotype prevalence for other reasons, an association can also arise through population structure. This is why replication, appropriate comparisons and functional evidence matter. Linkage studies and association studies are complementary but ask different questions. A family linkage study follows cosegregation over informative transmissions; an association study compares the distribution of variants among individuals or phenotypic categories.

Familial aggregation is useful evidence but must also be interpreted with care. Relatives share parts of their genome and may share diet, geographic environment, exposures or socioeconomic conditions. Similarity among relatives cannot be attributed entirely to inherited DNA without addressing shared environment. Conversely, relatives may share many alleles yet experience different conditions because their environments or other genetic contributions differ. A multifactorial model allows both possibilities without requiring a single dominant or recessive allele.

Twin comparisons provide a conceptual way to investigate the relative contributions of inherited variation and environment. Monozygotic twins generally share nearly all their inherited nuclear sequence, whereas dizygotic twins share, on average, half their segregating autosomal variation as siblings do. Greater phenotypic similarity among monozygotic pairs than among dizygotic pairs can support a genetic contribution, provided assumptions about comparable environments and sampling are considered. Incomplete concordance among monozygotic twins demonstrates that shared inherited genotype alone need not determine the outcome. It does not establish that every difference is caused by one specific environmental exposure.

Heritability measures how much of phenotypic variation in a defined population and context is associated with genetic variation under a specified model. It is not the percentage of an individual phenotype caused by genes. A high heritability estimate can coexist with environmental influence or with changes in population mean following an environmental change. This distinction will receive mathematical treatment in subunit 8.E. For the present chapter, it matters because genetic contribution to susceptibility should not be equated with inevitability.

There is also a distinction between a common complex disorder and a rare monogenic form of a broadly similar phenotype. For example, a disease category defined by an abnormal physiological measurement can include cases caused by a high-effect variant in one locus and many other cases caused by interacting small-effect loci and environmental conditions. The broad phenotype alone does not uniquely specify inheritance. The existence of a monogenic form does not imply that every person with the phenotype has the same genetic cause. Conversely, identifying common environmental associations does not exclude a major inherited cause in a particular family.

To compare alternative explanations in an examination, consider what new observation would alter the conclusion. A multigeneration pedigree showing consistent cosegregation with a particular rare allele may support a major-locus model. A population-wide distribution of small marker effects with substantial environmental association supports a more complex model. A measured defect in the corresponding protein strengthens a molecular causal explanation. A failure of expected segregation, if confirmed with appropriate data, may motivate a model with incomplete penetrance, heterogeneity or an incorrect initial locus assignment. The correct approach is to update the model in response to evidence rather than force every observation into a memorised pattern.

Integrated genetic casework: choosing among explanations

The strongest questions in inheritance biology combine molecular observations, family transmission and quantitative data. An effective reasoning sequence begins by stating the phenotype and the unit of measurement. Next identify whether the suspected alteration is in a nuclear locus, an organellar genome or chromosome organisation. Then determine the expected transmission under the proposed model and ask whether the molecular mechanism could explain the phenotype. Finally, consider alternative explanations and identify the observation that would separate them.

Case one concerns two unaffected hypothetical parents who have an affected child with two distinct variants in the same autosomal gene. One variant was inherited from each parent. If both variants substantially reduce function, the observation is consistent with a compound heterozygous recessive model. Calling the child a simple homozygote would be inaccurate because the alleles are not identical. If both variants were instead found on the same chromosome, the other homolog might still contain a functional allele. Establishing phase therefore changes the inference. The important evidence is not merely the number of reported sequence differences but their distribution between homologs.

Case two concerns two unrelated families with similar X-linked bleeding phenotypes. In one family, functional activity of factor VIII is reduced; in the other, factor IX activity is reduced. The pedigrees alone could have led to the same proposed mode of inheritance, yet the molecular observations support distinct affected components of the same biological pathway. This demonstrates locus heterogeneity. If two different mutations in F8 were instead observed in separate families, the corresponding term would be allelic heterogeneity. These distinctions can be applied without memorising an exhaustive disease catalogue.

Case three concerns a hypothetical chromosome count of 46 in each of two samples. In sample one, chromosome organisation and dosage are unremarkable at the resolution of the test. In sample two, a derivative chromosome carries extra chromosome 21 material. The equal counts do not imply equal dosage. To interpret the second sample, identify the material carried by the derivative and the number of copies of the relevant region. A chromosome number is a useful first observation but cannot replace structural analysis. The same reasoning explains why a balanced rearrangement may alter the count without creating the same expression imbalance as a full trisomy.

Case four concerns an apparent maternally transmitted neurological phenotype. A nuclear imprinting mechanism and a mitochondrial DNA mechanism are both proposed. Detection of abnormal expression associated with the parental origin of a nuclear chromosome 15 region supports the former. Detection of a heteroplasmic mitochondrial variant associated with reduced respiratory capacity supports the latter. Neither the affected organ nor the word maternal is enough to discriminate the alternatives. A parent-of-origin effect must be traced to the location and regulation of genetic material.

Case five compares two families with earlier disease onset in younger generations. In one, repeat-length measurements demonstrate enlargement of a coding CAG tract. In the other, no molecular measurements are supplied, and older relatives' onset ages are uncertain. The first provides evidence consistent with expansion-mediated anticipation. The second is an observation that warrants alternative explanations, including ascertainment and variation among relatives. The absence of molecular evidence is not proof that expansion did not occur, but a scientifically appropriate conclusion remains conditional.

Case six concerns a complex disease association. A variant is more frequent among affected individuals than among reference individuals, but an experiment shows no detectable effect of the variant on the tested protein function. The association may still be meaningful: the variant may affect expression not captured by the assay, be associated with another causal variant, or reflect a difference between sampled groups. A negative functional result excludes only the specific effect tested under the stated conditions. The correct reasoning is to describe what the experiment does and does not address.

Together these cases reinforce a hierarchy of evidence. Segregation suggests transmission, localisation identifies a region, molecular measurements identify changes in products or activity, and functional comparisons evaluate the proposed mechanism. The relevant questions are causal and conditional. They require an ability to move between genetic levels while preserving the assumptions of each model. This is the final objective of the Human Genetics subunit and the foundation for the next discussion of genetic variation in populations.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Genetic means inherited from an affected parent
A new variant or recessive segregation can cause a condition in a child of unaffected parents. Separate cause, transmission and parental phenotype. Students often identify disease by family history alone. An item may deliberately show two unaffected parents; ask whether a new alteration or recessive model predicts the observation.
Dominant means common
Dominance is phenotype expression in a heterozygote, not allele frequency. A rare dominant allele can be highly penetrant. Students confuse the everyday meaning of dominant with statistical abundance. A question may provide a rare but strongly expressed heterozygous phenotype; evaluate genotype-specific expression rather than population frequency.
An unaffected sibling of an affected recessive child has carrier probability one half
For two known carrier parents and complete penetrance, condition on unaffected status to obtain two thirds. The unconditional genotype ratio is memorised as 1:2:1. Examiners remove one genotype by stating that the sibling is unaffected; renormalise the remaining probabilities.
Penetrance is severity
Penetrance asks whether a phenotype appears; expressivity asks how it appears. Do not substitute one for the other. The words are both used to describe variable phenotypes. If two carriers show different severity, ask about expressivity; if one has no defined phenotype, ask about penetrance.
HbS cannot carry oxygen
The central sickling mechanism is deoxy-HbS polymerisation. Oxygen binding and polymer formation are different properties. Sickle shape and oxygen transport are tightly linked, which invites oversimplification. If a question distinguishes oxygen binding from polymer formation, identify the condition favouring intermolecular association.
Codominant HbA/HbS bands imply dominant disease
The molecular phenotype and clinical disease phenotype use different definitions. Both proteins can be detected in a heterozygote. The same genotype is often assigned one dominance label without specifying the trait. If an item shows two protein bands but no severe disease phenotype, distinguish the measured molecular trait from the clinical phenotype.
All sickle cell disease is HbSS
Compound heterozygotes such as HbS/beta-thalassaemia can also express sickle cell disease. Textbook crosses often use only A and S alleles. A question may introduce a beta-zero allele on the other homolog; consider the product mixture and the phase of the two alleles.
Two alpha-gene deletions always imply identical transmission
Cis and trans arrangements have different gamete composition. Infer phase before calculating the cross. Dosage counts conceal which homolog contains the deleted loci. A pedigree question may compare two parents with the same copy count but different phase; enumerate their actual gametes.
All CFTR variants prevent protein production
Some impair trafficking, gating or conductance despite detectable protein. Distinguish total protein, surface amount and current. Students equate loss of biological function with absence of protein. An item may give normal total protein but low membrane abundance or normal membrane abundance with low current; discriminate the affected step.
All X-linked heterozygous females are asymptomatic
X-inactivation and other factors can alter expression. Apply the simplified carrier model only when stated. The textbook carrier symbol is usually unshaded. An item may explicitly describe skewed X-inactivation or variable factor activity; do not reject the X-linked model simply because a heterozygous female has manifestations.
A chromosome count of 46 excludes Down syndrome
A derivative chromosome can carry extra relevant chromosome 21 material despite the count of 46. The classic trisomy karyotype is easier to remember than dosage. A question may describe a derivative chromosome in a 46-chromosome complement; count relevant long-arm material rather than chromosomes alone.
An inactive X is entirely silent
Some genes escape X-inactivation. Consider escape-gene dosage when comparing 45,X and 47,XXY. The phrase inactive chromosome suggests complete suppression. An item may ask why 45,X differs biologically from 46,XX despite one active X in both; identify genes that escape inactivation.
Any mitochondrial disease has maternal inheritance
Only mtDNA-associated variants usually do; nuclear genes affecting mitochondria can follow ordinary Mendelian inheritance. Students classify disorders by the location of the physiological defect. A pedigree with affected offspring from carrier parents can reflect a nuclear mitochondrial protein gene; localise the gene before assigning transmission.
Heteroplasmy percentage equals offspring disease risk
The percentage measures genome copies. Bottleneck, tissue distribution and expression thresholds prevent direct conversion. Both heteroplasmy and recurrence are expressed as percentages. An item may offer the measured mtDNA fraction as a tempting recurrence prediction; identify the denominator and distinguish molecule fraction from offspring outcomes.
Every trinucleotide expansion produces a longer protein
The genomic location matters. HTT has a coding expansion; the FMR1 repeat is noncoding. The genetic code is introduced as triplets, making all repeats appear like codons. A question may put the repeat in a 5′ untranslated region; determine whether the sequence is translated before predicting protein length.
Anticipation guarantees earlier onset in every child
Repeat dynamics and onset variation are probabilistic. Compare actual repeat measurements and cohort context. Students turn a group-level trend into an individual certainty. If onset distributions overlap or a transmitted repeat contracts, anticipation can still be a general tendency; inspect actual repeat lengths and conditional data.
A normal chromosome copy number rules out imprinting disorders
Uniparental disomy or altered imprint marks can disrupt expression while retaining two physical copies. Copy-number changes are common causes of chromosome-region disorders, so a normal count may be misleading. An item may supply maternal-only parental markers or an abnormal methylation pattern despite two copies; use origin and expression information.
A statistically associated allele must cause the disease
Linkage disequilibrium, confounding or population structure can explain association. Seek independent functional evidence. Association tables often appear to identify a risk gene directly. A Part C problem may present a marker near a causal locus or groups of different ancestry; ask whether association survives appropriate controls and functional tests.

Revision and mastery

Chapter synthesis

Start with the genomic level, identify the product or dosage change, follow the cellular consequence, and then test the inheritance prediction. Distinguish molecule-level phenotypes from tissue-level outcomes, physical copies from active copies, and genotype transmission from conditional phenotype expression. Use independent pedigree, cytogenetic and functional evidence to evaluate the model.

Original examination practice

These are original NET/SET-style questions. All numerical and assay datasets are hypothetical teaching examples.

1. Part B — Which distinction best separates genetic from inherited disease?

  1. A new constitutional variant can cause disease without prior parental transmission of that variant.
  2. Every genetic disease is transmitted by a parent.
  3. Only chromosome disorders are genetic.
  4. Somatic variants always pass to offspring.
Answer and explanation

A. A new constitutional variant can cause disease without prior parental transmission of that variant.

A newly arising variant may cause a genetic condition in the individual even if neither parent carried it constitutionally. Genetic describes cause; inherited describes transmission. Somatic changes generally do not pass through gametes.

2. Part B — Two heterozygotes at a fully penetrant autosomal recessive locus produce what expected proportion of affected offspring?

  1. 0
  2. 1/2
  3. 1/4
  4. 3/4
Answer and explanation

C. 1/4

Each parent transmits the recessive allele with probability 1/2. Independent union gives 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 for aa; heterozygotes are unaffected in the stated model.

3. Part B — What does penetrance measure?

  1. Severity among affected individuals
  2. Probability of expressing a defined phenotype given a genotype
  3. Frequency of an allele in all gametes
  4. Number of tissues expressing a gene
Answer and explanation

B. Probability of expressing a defined phenotype given a genotype

Penetrance concerns whether a defined phenotype is expressed in carriers under specified conditions. Expressivity describes the extent or pattern of manifestations, not the probability of expression.

4. Part B — Which molecular change characterises the classical HbS allele?

  1. Loss of both alpha globin genes
  2. CGG expansion in HBB
  3. Deletion of chromosome 11
  4. Glutamate to valine in mature beta globin at position 6
Answer and explanation

D. Glutamate to valine in mature beta globin at position 6

The classical HbS substitution replaces a charged glutamate with nonpolar valine at mature beta-chain residue 6. This changes interactions between deoxygenated HbS molecules; alpha deletions cause a different disorder.

5. Part B — What favours the formation of HbS polymers?

  1. Inactivation of chromosome X
  2. Deoxygenation
  3. Loss of FMRP
  4. Increased factor VIII activity
Answer and explanation

B. Deoxygenation

Deoxy-HbS has intermolecular contacts conducive to polymer growth. The other processes are unrelated to this structural mechanism.

6. Part B — The principal molecular deficit in beta-thalassaemia is:

  1. Increased HBB copy number
  2. Defective chloride conductance
  3. Paternal imprinting of chromosome 15
  4. Reduced beta globin production
Answer and explanation

D. Reduced beta globin production

Beta-thalassaemia involves reduced beta chain supply. This can reflect transcriptional, splicing or other defects. HbS polymer formation and CFTR dysfunction are distinct mechanisms.

7. Part B — How many alpha globin gene copies are typically present in a diploid genome?

  1. 4
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 3
Answer and explanation

A. 4

Each homolog of chromosome 16 normally carries HBA1 and HBA2, giving four copies in total.

8. Part B — The primary product of CFTR acts as a:

  1. Nuclear transcription factor
  2. Mitochondrial tRNA
  3. Regulated anion channel
  4. Coagulation protease
Answer and explanation

C. Regulated anion channel

CFTR mediates epithelial anion transport, including chloride and bicarbonate. Its reduction affects ion and water balance rather than globin production or clotting.

9. Part B — Haemophilia A and B classically involve which respective factors?

  1. IX and VIII
  2. X and XI
  3. II and VII
  4. VIII and IX
Answer and explanation

D. VIII and IX

Factor VIII deficiency or dysfunction is associated with haemophilia A; factor IX deficiency or dysfunction with B. Both participate in the intrinsic tenase system.

10. Part B — Marfan syndrome is classically associated with pathogenic variation in:

  1. FBN1
  2. HBA1
  3. FMR1
  4. MT-TL1
Answer and explanation

A. FBN1

FBN1 encodes fibrillin-1, a component of extracellular microfibrils. The other loci are relevant to alpha-thalassaemia, fragile X and mitochondrial disease.

11. Part B — Dystrophin helps connect the muscle-cell cytoskeleton with:

  1. Nuclear ribosomes
  2. Circulating haemoglobin
  3. A membrane-associated protein complex
  4. Mitochondrial DNA replication origins
Answer and explanation

C. A membrane-associated protein complex

Dystrophin supports mechanical coupling between intracellular actin-associated structures and membrane complexes. Its loss contributes to vulnerability of muscle cells during contraction.

12. Part B — Which chromosome material is increased in Down syndrome?

  1. Chromosome 18
  2. Chromosome 21
  3. Chromosome X only
  4. Chromosome 15 maternal homolog only
Answer and explanation

B. Chromosome 21

Extra relevant chromosome 21 material is the central dosage alteration. It can result from free trisomy, translocation or mosaic forms.

13. Part B — The classic nonmosaic Turner karyotype is:

  1. 45,X
  2. 47,XXY
  3. 47,XXX
  4. 47,XY,+21
Answer and explanation

A. 45,X

45,X represents a single X chromosome with no second sex chromosome. Turner syndrome also encompasses other mosaic and structural arrangements.

14. Part B — The typical 47,XXY complement contains how many largely inactive X chromosomes in cells following ordinary X-inactivation?

  1. 0
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

D. 1

One of two X chromosomes is largely inactive in relevant somatic cells. The Y is not counted by this rule, and some genes escape X-inactivation.

15. Part B — Heteroplasmy describes:

  1. Different alleles at two autosomal loci
  2. A mixture of mitochondrial genome variants within a sample or cell
  3. An extra paternal chromosome 21
  4. A mosaic of random X-inactivation only
Answer and explanation

B. A mixture of mitochondrial genome variants within a sample or cell

Heteroplasmy refers to variation among mitochondrial DNA copies. It is distinct from diploid heterozygosity or a mixture of nuclear karyotypes.

16. Part B — The classical transmission route for pathogenic mitochondrial DNA variants is:

  1. Paternal only
  2. Y-linked
  3. Maternal
  4. Autosomal dominant
Answer and explanation

C. Maternal

The egg normally supplies the persistent mitochondrial genome population. Nuclear variants affecting mitochondria can follow other inheritance patterns.

17. Part B — The expanded repeat in classical Huntington disease is:

  1. CGG in FMR1
  2. A deletion in HBA2
  3. CAG in the coding region of HTT
  4. GAA in HBB
Answer and explanation

C. CAG in the coding region of HTT

HTT coding CAG expansion produces an expanded polyglutamine region. FMR1 CGG expansion occupies a different genomic context and generally acts through a different mechanism.

18. Part B — The usual full mutation mechanism in fragile X syndrome involves:

  1. Expansion of a coding CAG repeat
  2. Noncoding CGG expansion with abnormal methylation and FMR1 silencing
  3. Loss of all mitochondrial DNA
  4. Extra chromosome 21
Answer and explanation

B. Noncoding CGG expansion with abnormal methylation and FMR1 silencing

The FMR1 CGG repeat lies in the 5′ untranslated region. Large expansions commonly cause abnormal methylation and reduced FMRP rather than a polyglutamine protein.

19. Part B — Maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 15 can cause:

  1. Haemophilia B
  2. Klinefelter syndrome
  3. HbSS disease
  4. Prader–Willi syndrome
Answer and explanation

D. Prader–Willi syndrome

Two maternal-origin copies can leave the paternally expressed gene program absent in the relevant region. Paternal UPD may instead disrupt maternal neuronal UBE3A expression.

20. Part B — What is locus heterogeneity?

  1. Alteration of different genes can yield a similar phenotype.
  2. One locus influences many traits.
  3. Different alleles within one gene cause related traits.
  4. A chromosome contains two identical homologs.
Answer and explanation

A. Alteration of different genes can yield a similar phenotype.

Locus heterogeneity concerns multiple causal genes for a related phenotype. Allelic heterogeneity concerns variants at one locus, while pleiotropy concerns multiple effects of one gene.

21. Part C — An unaffected sibling has an affected sibling with a fully penetrant recessive disorder. Both parents are known Aa. What is the unaffected sibling's carrier probability?

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/2
  3. 3/4
  4. 2/3
Answer and explanation

D. 2/3

Condition on the unaffected phenotype: AA, Aa and aA remain equally probable outcomes, and two contain a. The unconditional carrier probability of 1/2 is not applicable after excluding aa.

The 1/4 and 3/4 alternatives are unconditional probabilities of particular phenotype classes; neither is the probability of carriage among unaffected children.

22. Part C — The unaffected sibling in the previous hypothetical model has a known heterozygous partner at the same locus. What is the probability of an affected child?

  1. 1/12
  2. 1/6
  3. 1/4
  4. 1/2
Answer and explanation

B. 1/6

The sibling has probability 2/3 of being a carrier. Conditional on both partners being carriers, risk is 1/4, so the joint probability is 2/3 × 1/4 = 1/6.

The answer is lower than 1/4 because the first parent is not certainly a carrier. A 1/2 result confuses transmission from one carrier with inheritance of two recessive alleles.

23. Part C — A heterozygote transmits a dominant allele with probability 1/2. Its penetrance at a specified age is 70 percent. Ignoring phenocopies, the child's probability of the phenotype at that age is:

  1. 0.35
  2. 0.5
  3. 0.7
  4. 0.85
Answer and explanation

A. 0.35

Transmission and conditional expression must both occur. Multiply 0.5 by 0.7 to obtain 0.35. The 0.5 option is the probability of receiving the allele, not expressing the phenotype.

The 0.7 option ignores the chance that the allele is transmitted; a sum of probabilities would also be inappropriate for an intersection of conditional events.

24. Part C — A heterozygous mother carries a fully penetrant X-linked recessive variant in males. The father lacks the variant. Assuming equal sex probabilities, what is the joint probability of an affected son?

  1. 1/8
  2. 1/2
  3. 1/4
  4. 3/4
Answer and explanation

C. 1/4

A son receives his X from his mother. Conditional on a son, the probability is 1/2; the probability of a son is 1/2. Their product is 1/4.

The 1/2 option would answer a different question, namely the probability among sons. The question asks for the joint event among all offspring.

25. Part C — A heterozygote produces equal numbers of normal and altered monomers. Dimers assemble randomly, and any altered monomer inactivates a dimer. What fraction of dimers retain activity?

  1. 1/2
  2. 1/4
  3. 3/4
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

B. 1/4

Both subunits must be normal. Under independent random pairing the probability is (1/2)² = 1/4. Mixed dimers are not functional under the explicit premise.

The 1/2 option is the fraction of mixed dimers, and 3/4 is the fraction containing at least one altered subunit. Neither is the fraction of fully normal complexes.

26. Part C — A heterozygous null yields 50 units of active enzyme, with 35 required for ordinary tissue function. Which prediction is supported under this simplified model?

  1. Heterozygote fails the threshold.
  2. The variant must be a gain of function.
  3. The gene must be mitochondrial.
  4. Heterozygote meets the threshold.
Answer and explanation

D. Heterozygote meets the threshold.

The reserve of 50 units exceeds the stated demand of 35. A molecular activity reduction can coexist with a recessive tissue phenotype. The numbers do not establish genomic location.

27. Part C — A haemoglobin sample has both A and S bands under appropriate reference conditions. Which inference is strongest?

  1. The disease phenotype must be dominant.
  2. The exact genotype is proven regardless of other variants.
  3. Both protein forms are present.
  4. Beta globin synthesis is absent.
Answer and explanation

C. Both protein forms are present.

The direct observation is the presence of components with the expected band properties. Molecular coexpression does not establish clinical dominance or exclude every other explanation.

28. Part C — HbS-containing and HbA-only samples are compared at high and low oxygen availability. Which comparison best evaluates HbS-specific polymer formation?

  1. HbS low oxygen versus HbA low oxygen, with oxygenated controls
  2. HbS low oxygen versus a different tissue at unknown oxygen
  3. HbA only at high oxygen
  4. Two samples with unknown globin compositions
Answer and explanation

A. HbS low oxygen versus HbA low oxygen, with oxygenated controls

The proposed mechanism depends on both HbS identity and deoxygenation. Matched samples and oxygenated controls distinguish those variables rather than confounding them.

29. Part C — In the simplified alpha globin model, two parents have --/αα. What proportion of offspring have no intact alpha globin genes?

  1. 0
  2. 1/4
  3. 1/2
  4. 3/4
Answer and explanation

B. 1/4

Each parent transmits -- with probability 1/2. Combining those gametes gives --/-- with probability 1/4.

The 1/2 value counts offspring with one cis deletion chromosome; 3/4 is the combined probability of at least one such chromosome.

30. Part C — One parent has --/αα and the other -α/-α, ignoring recombination. Which offspring genotypes are expected?

  1. --/-- and αα/αα
  2. Only -α/-α
  3. --/-α and αα/-α, each 1/2
  4. Only αα/αα
Answer and explanation

C. --/-α and αα/-α, each 1/2

The cis parent contributes either -- or αα; the trans parent contributes -α. The two possible unions each occur with probability one half.

The trans parent always contributes -α in this model, so neither a fully deleted chromosome from that parent nor an intact αα chromosome is available.

31. Part C — Surface CFTR abundance is normal but current is 20 percent of reference under matched driving force. Which class of mechanism is supported?

  1. Complete absence of all surface channels
  2. Extra chromosome 21 necessarily
  3. A globin-chain imbalance
  4. Reduced channel function per surface channel
Answer and explanation

D. Reduced channel function per surface channel

With comparable surface abundance, reduced current suggests altered conductance or gating rather than simple loss of surface channel number. Additional measurements are needed to distinguish mechanisms.

A zero-abundance model predicts absence of the protein at the surface and conflicts with the given observation. Current alone, without surface measurement, would not discriminate these mechanisms.

32. Part C — A factor VIII antigen assay is near normal but factor VIII activity is severely reduced. Which interpretation is appropriate?

  1. A functional defect or inhibition is plausible despite detectable protein.
  2. The protein must be absent.
  3. Factor IX is proven defective.
  4. The finding establishes a specific inherited variant.
Answer and explanation

A. A functional defect or inhibition is plausible despite detectable protein.

Antigen and function measure different properties. A detectable but inactive product or inhibitor can yield this pattern; the assay alone does not establish inheritance or an exact allele.

33. Part C — Two patients show similar bleeding phenotypes, one with F8 variation and another with F9 variation. This exemplifies:

  1. Allelic heterogeneity only
  2. Mitochondrial heteroplasmy
  3. Locus heterogeneity
  4. Uniparental disomy
Answer and explanation

C. Locus heterogeneity

Different genes affect the same coagulation pathway and yield related phenotypes. If both patients instead had distinct F8 variants, allelic heterogeneity would apply.

34. Part C — A hypothetical coding exon contains 91 nucleotides. If exactly those coding nucleotides are deleted and the expected exon junction forms, what is predicted?

  1. A frameshift downstream
  2. A preserved reading frame
  3. Exactly 91 missing amino acids
  4. No RNA sequence change
Answer and explanation

A. A frameshift downstream

91 is not divisible by 3, so deleting 91 coding bases changes the downstream reading frame under the stated splice assumptions. Protein severity cannot be concluded from this calculation alone.

A deletion of 91 bases cannot remove 91 amino acids because coding triplets contain three bases. In-frame deletion would require a multiple of three, subject to actual exon junctions.

35. Part C — An extra noncompensated chromosome copy raises a gene's expression from a two-copy baseline. If expression scales with copy number, what is the fold increase?

  1. 1.25
  2. 1.5
  3. 2
  4. 3
Answer and explanation

B. 1.5

Three copies divided by two copies equals 1.5. The conclusion is conditional on proportional expression; regulatory compensation may alter actual expression.

A twofold value would correspond to doubling the copy count from two to four, not adding one copy. A threefold value confuses the absolute count with its ratio to the baseline.

36. Part C — A sample contains 18 trisomic and 42 disomic cells. What is the observed trisomic proportion?

  1. 18%
  2. 42%
  3. 70%
  4. 30%
Answer and explanation

D. 30%

The total is 60 cells; 18/60 = 0.30. This is the specimen's observed fraction, not a whole-body mosaic estimate.

The denominator is the number of all scored cells. The raw count of 18 is not a percentage, and 42/60 describes the complementary fraction.

37. Part C — Two extra chromosome copies in a gamete carry different centromere-near maternal marker alleles. Under a simplified nonrecombinant centromere model, which origin is most compatible?

  1. Failure of homolog separation in meiosis I
  2. Failure of sister separation in meiosis II only
  3. Random X-inactivation
  4. An FMR1 full mutation
Answer and explanation

A. Failure of homolog separation in meiosis I

Different homolog markers near the centromere support retention of both homologs. Recombination and more complicated segregation can limit inference, which is why the question states its assumptions.

38. Part C — A chromosome count is 46 but a derivative chromosome contains extra 21q material. Which conclusion follows?

  1. A dosage disorder is impossible with 46 chromosomes.
  2. The individual must be balanced.
  3. The chromosome count determines all gene expression.
  4. The extra material can cause dosage imbalance despite an ordinary total count.
Answer and explanation

D. The extra material can cause dosage imbalance despite an ordinary total count.

The relevant quantity is the copy number of chromosome 21 material. A derivative can alter dosage without changing the total number to 47.

39. Part C — A sample contains 180 altered mitochondrial genome copies among 300 total. Which statement is correct?

  1. 60% of offspring must be affected.
  2. 60% of mitochondria are necessarily homoplasmic mutant.
  3. Heteroplasmy is 60%, but offspring risk is not thereby fixed at 60%.
  4. 40% of cells must be completely normal.
Answer and explanation

C. Heteroplasmy is 60%, but offspring risk is not thereby fixed at 60%.

180/300 = 0.6. This measures copies of mitochondrial DNA, not cells, organelles or future offspring. Transmission and phenotype also depend on segregation and functional thresholds.

40. Part C — In a toy bottleneck model four copies are independently sampled from a 50% altered population. What is the probability of exactly two altered copies?

  1. 1/16
  2. 6/16
  3. 4/16
  4. 8/16
Answer and explanation

B. 6/16

There are six ways to choose two altered copies among four, each with probability (1/2)^4. This is an illustrative binomial model, not a literal estimate of human mitochondrial transmission.

The 1/16 value describes one specified arrangement, not all six arrangements with two altered copies. The 4/16 value can describe exactly one altered copy or exactly three.

41. Part C — A child receives a repeat-bearing autosomal chromosome with probability 1/2. Conditional on transmission, a hypothetical repeat expands with probability 0.3. What is the joint probability?

  1. 0.3
  2. 0.15
  3. 0.5
  4. 0.8
Answer and explanation

B. 0.15

The joint event is transmission followed by expansion. Multiply 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.15; expansion conditional on receiving the allele is a different quantity.

The 0.3 value is conditional on receiving the chromosome, and 0.5 is the chance of receiving it. Neither alone answers the two-event question.

42. Part C — Ten additional complete CAG triplets occur within an open reading frame. Which direct sequence-level prediction is correct?

  1. A 10-base frameshift
  2. 30 extra glutamine residues
  3. 30 additional nucleotides and ten extra glutamine residues
  4. Transcriptional silencing necessarily occurs
Answer and explanation

C. 30 additional nucleotides and ten extra glutamine residues

Each codon contains three bases and CAG encodes glutamine. Complete triplets preserve reading frame, while expansion in a noncoding region would not produce this translation consequence.

43. Part C — A region of chromosome 15 has two intact copies, both maternally derived, and an absent paternal expression program. Which model fits?

  1. Maternal uniparental disomy
  2. Paternal deletion with only one copy
  3. A balanced X-autosome translocation necessarily
  4. A coding CAG expansion
Answer and explanation

A. Maternal uniparental disomy

Two copies from one parent support uniparental disomy. The expression deficit can occur without physical copy-number loss.

44. Part C — A region has two copies of biparental origin but an abnormal maternal-only methylation signature in a relevant imprint-associated region. Which explanation is favoured?

  1. Simple paternal deletion
  2. Loss of mitochondrial DNA
  3. Trisomy 21
  4. An imprinting defect
Answer and explanation

D. An imprinting defect

Ordinary copy number and biparental marker origin argue against simple deletion or UPD. An abnormal epigenetic program remains plausible.

45. Part C — In a toy model of six independent allele copies each with frequency 1/2, liability equals the number of contributing alleles. Phenotype occurs when liability exceeds 4. What is the expected fraction?

  1. 6/64
  2. 15/64
  3. 7/64
  4. 22/64
Answer and explanation

C. 7/64

Only five and six contributing copies satisfy the strict threshold. There are six five-copy combinations and one six-copy combination among 64 equally likely outcomes.

The 22/64 alternative would include four-copy genotypes; that would apply if the threshold were at least four rather than strictly greater than four, or if the toy liability had an extra unit.

46. SET — Which comparison correctly matches a disorder to its primary mechanism?

  1. Fragile X — usual noncoding repeat-associated silencing
  2. HbS — chromosome trisomy
  3. Turner — polyglutamine expansion
  4. Haemophilia A — mitochondrial tRNA defect
Answer and explanation

A. Fragile X — usual noncoding repeat-associated silencing

Fragile X commonly follows expanded CGG repeats and methylation-associated FMR1 silencing. The other pairs mismatch the established mechanisms.

47. SET — Assertion: A genetic condition can occur without affected parents. Reason: De novo variants and recessive inheritance can produce affected offspring of unaffected parents.

  1. Both true; reason unrelated
  2. Assertion true, reason false
  3. Assertion false, reason true
  4. Both true; reason explains assertion
Answer and explanation

D. Both true; reason explains assertion

A genetic condition need not have a visibly affected parent. New variants or recessive segregation from unaffected carriers explain that observation.

48. SET — A study observes disease in 20 of 100 marker carriers and 10 of 100 noncarriers. Which statement is justified?

  1. The marker is conclusively causal.
  2. The observed risk ratio is 2.
  3. The risk difference is 2 percentage points.
  4. All carriers will develop disease.
Answer and explanation

B. The observed risk ratio is 2.

0.20/0.10 = 2. The difference is 0.10 or ten percentage points. An observational association does not establish causality or inevitability.

49. SET — Which distinction between imprinting and mitochondrial inheritance is correct?

  1. Both require a mutation in mtDNA.
  2. Both are ordinary X-linked inheritance.
  3. Imprinting concerns parental-origin-dependent nuclear gene expression; mitochondrial inheritance concerns organellar DNA transmission.
  4. Imprinting requires a coding repeat expansion.
Answer and explanation

C. Imprinting concerns parental-origin-dependent nuclear gene expression; mitochondrial inheritance concerns organellar DNA transmission.

The mechanisms operate at different genetic levels. Parent-of-origin patterns alone cannot establish which is involved.

50. SET — A marker is more frequent in cases than controls. Which additional evidence would strengthen a molecular causal interpretation?

  1. A plausible experimentally demonstrated effect on the relevant gene function with appropriate controls
  2. A larger title for the marker
  3. A different disease name
  4. An assumption that all associations are causal
Answer and explanation

A. A plausible experimentally demonstrated effect on the relevant gene function with appropriate controls

An experimentally supported functional effect links the variant to biological mechanism. Population structure and linked variants can otherwise explain association without direct causation.
