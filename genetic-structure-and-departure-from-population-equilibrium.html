What equilibrium does and does not predict

The Hardy–Weinberg model provides two predictions that must be separated before investigating a real population. First, if the two gametes forming a diploid zygote are independent samples from the same allele pool, the expected proportions of AA, Aa and aa among zygotes are p², 2pq and q². Second, if transmission and reproduction do not systematically change the representation of alleles and sampling is effectively infinite, the allele frequencies themselves remain constant across generations. The first claim concerns the combination of alleles within individuals at a particular stage of the life cycle. The second concerns changes in the gene pool across time. A population may satisfy one prediction while violating the other.

Consider a population with 100 diploid plants, including 40 AA, 20 Aa and 40 aa. There are 200 allele copies, of which 100 are A. Thus p = 0.5 and q = 0.5. Random mating at these frequencies would predict 25 AA, 50 Aa and 25 aa in a sample of 100 newly formed offspring. The actual plants have fewer heterozygotes and more homozygotes, but nothing in these counts alone establishes that the frequency of A has changed from an earlier generation. To establish that change, we would need information about the previous generation or a model of how the alleles were transmitted.

This separation provides a useful investigation strategy. Record the developmental stage, geographical origin and sampling design before calculating expected genotype frequencies. Estimate allele frequencies from the observed genotypes, then compare observed and expected genotype proportions within a biologically meaningful mating population. If a discrepancy occurs, ask whether it arises from how mates pair, from differences in the allele frequencies of groups that were pooled, from survival between zygote formation and sampling, from sampling noise, or from technical mistakes. A significant test is evidence against the specified statistical model, not direct proof of a particular biological process.

An additional distinction will become important when considering two loci. Hardy–Weinberg proportions describe the pairing of alleles at one locus within individuals; linkage equilibrium describes how alleles at separate loci are associated on chromosomes or gametes. Even a population that mates randomly can carry nonrandom associations between loci. To see why, remember that random fertilization combines chromosomes but does not instantly exchange the alleles carried along each chromosome.

Non-random mating and the inbreeding coefficient

A mating system describes how individuals become reproductive partners. Positive assortative mating means that similar individuals pair more often than predicted by a random-mating model, whereas negative assortative mating means that dissimilar individuals pair preferentially. Similarity can refer to flowering time, body size or another characteristic. If similarity is influenced by a particular genotype, positive assortment may increase homozygosity at relevant loci. But resemblance between partners is not equivalent to close genealogical relationship. Two unrelated plants may flower at the same time because both respond to the same temperature. Conversely, relatives may have very different phenotypes.

Inbreeding refers to mating between individuals related by ancestry, relative to a defined reference population. The two homologous gene copies in a diploid offspring may trace back to the same ancestral gene copy. They are then identical by descent, abbreviated IBD. Identity by state is a broader observation: two copies carry the same allelic state, whether or not they trace to the same ancestral copy within the specified genealogy. Two A copies are always identical by state under a two-allele classification, but the observed genotype cannot by itself tell us whether they are identical by descent. The distinction explains why the inbreeding coefficient is a probability defined in relation to ancestry, not merely a count of matching alleles.

Let F be the probability that the two alleles at an autosomal locus are identical by descent relative to a chosen base population. Let p and q denote the frequencies of A and a in that base model. With probability F, the offspring has two copies of one ancestral allele. That allele is A with probability p and a with probability q. With probability 1 − F, the two copies are modeled as independent draws from the base allele pool. These two mutually exclusive routes give the genotype probabilities P(AA) = Fp + (1 − F)p² = p² + Fpq, P(Aa) = (1 − F)2pq, and P(aa) = Fq + (1 − F)q² = q² + Fpq. The expressions sum to one. They are valid under the stated model of the base frequencies and independent sampling in the non-IBD component.

We can derive the frequency of A from these adjusted genotype probabilities rather than assuming it stays constant. Add the probability of AA and half the probability of Aa. The result is p² + Fpq + pq(1 − F), which simplifies to p² + pq = p. The added homozygotes balance the missing heterozygotes in their contributions to the allele pool. Inbreeding in this idealized setting redistributes alleles among genotypes without changing p or q. If the resulting homozygotes experience different survival or reproduction, selection can subsequently alter the allele frequencies. In that case the observed evolutionary change cannot be attributed solely to the rearrangement of mating partners.

For example, with p = 0.7, q = 0.3 and F = 0.2, Hardy–Weinberg proportions would be 0.49, 0.42 and 0.09. The correction Fpq is 0.042, so the inbreeding-adjusted probabilities are 0.532, 0.336 and 0.132. The heterozygote deficit is 0.084, exactly twice the correction applied to each homozygote. The A frequency calculated from these proportions is 0.532 + 0.168 = 0.7. The frequency of a recessive phenotype with complete penetrance would rise from 0.09 to 0.132 even though the frequency of its allele remained unchanged.

The relationship can also be written H_O = (1 − F)H_E, where H_O is observed heterozygosity and H_E = 2pq under a two-allele random-mating reference. Rearranging gives an empirical deficit statistic 1 − H_O/H_E. This estimate is informative only when H_E is positive. A negative value means heterozygote excess relative to the reference, not a negative probability of identity by descent. A positive value may be due to ancestry, subdivision, selection or technical error. Therefore it is important to distinguish an estimated fixation or deficit statistic from a pedigree-defined probability.

Self-fertilization is a particularly transparent example. An Aa plant produces AA, Aa and aa offspring in the ratio 1:2:1 when it self-fertilizes. AA and aa plants remain homozygous at this locus in the absence of mutation. If the entire population reproduces by selfing, only half of the heterozygotes in one generation generate heterozygous offspring in the next, so H_t = H_0(1/2)^t in the ideal deterministic model. When the initial population is outbred with H_0 = 2pq, this gives F_t = 1 − (1/2)^t. After three generations, seven-eighths of the starting heterozygosity has been lost. Yet there is no built-in preference for A or a in Mendelian segregation. Individual lines become increasingly homozygous while the aggregate allele frequencies can remain stable.

Inbreeding depression is a reduction in average biological performance associated with increased inbreeding. One explanation is that partially recessive deleterious variants become homozygous and therefore express stronger harmful effects. Loss of advantages associated with heterozygosity may also contribute. The severity depends on which variants are present, the environment and the history of selection. Inbreeding is not a synonym for reduced fitness, and increased homozygosity does not logically imply that every homozygous genotype is harmful. A breeding experiment should compare performance of selfed or related crosses against appropriate unrelated crosses under comparable environmental conditions.

Population subdivision and the Wahlund effect

A population is not simply a collection of all members of a species. For genetic inference, the boundaries of the group should reflect the processes of reproduction and gene exchange relevant to the question. Fish in separated lakes, plants on different mountain slopes and insects with different breeding seasons can have distinct allele frequencies even when they belong to the same species. The groups need not be completely isolated. Restricted gene exchange can be sufficient for differences to persist.

Consider two equally represented demes, each internally at Hardy–Weinberg proportions. In deme one, A has frequency 0.8 and a has frequency 0.2, giving AA = 0.64, Aa = 0.32 and aa = 0.04. In deme two, p = 0.2 and q = 0.8, giving AA = 0.04, Aa = 0.32 and aa = 0.64. When the records are pooled, the genotype frequencies are 0.34, 0.32 and 0.34, and the pooled allele frequency is 0.5. If the pooled sample were wrongly assumed to come from a single randomly mating population, the expected heterozygosity would be 0.50 rather than the observed 0.32. The difference of 0.18 occurs despite random mating within each deme. This is the Wahlund effect.

The calculation generalizes. Let w_i be the proportion of sampled individuals from group i, with weights summing to one, and let p_i be the group's A frequency. Define the weighted mean p̄ = Σw_i p_i. The mean expected heterozygosity within demes is H_S = Σw_i 2p_i(1 − p_i). The heterozygosity predicted by treating the pooled sample as a single random-mating population is H_T = 2p̄(1 − p̄). Expanding both terms and subtracting gives H_T − H_S = 2[Σw_i p_i² − p̄²] = 2 Var_w(p_i). The variance is nonnegative, so heterogeneity in allele frequency produces a deficit relative to the pooled reference. If the groups have the same allele frequencies, the variance and this particular effect are zero.

The weights should reflect the sample or population whose heterozygosity is being described. Suppose a small deme contributes 10% of the records and has p = 0.1, while a larger deme contributes 90% and has p = 0.7. The pooled p is 0.64, not 0.4. The within-deme expected heterozygosity is 0.1(0.18) + 0.9(0.42) = 0.396, and H_T = 2(0.64)(0.36) = 0.4608. The deficit is 0.0648. Equal weighting would answer a different question, namely the expectation when each deme is sampled with the same probability.

An introductory measure of subdivision is F_ST = (H_T − H_S)/H_T, provided H_T is nonzero. In the equally represented two-deme example, it equals 0.18/0.50 = 0.36. This quantity describes the relative reduction in expected heterozygosity associated with allele-frequency differences among demes; it does not mean that 36% of individuals are inbred. Practical estimators from finite samples account for sampling and population design, so a simple calculation from exact frequencies is an instructional model rather than a universal estimation procedure.

There is a useful distinction between H_I, observed heterozygosity within individuals averaged across groups, H_S, expected heterozygosity within groups, and H_T, expected heterozygosity from pooled frequencies. Define F_IS = 1 − H_I/H_S and F_IT = 1 − H_I/H_T. Algebra gives (1 − F_IT) = (1 − F_IS)(1 − F_ST). In the first example H_I = H_S, so F_IS = 0 even though F_IT is positive. If heterozygote deficits also occur within each deme, both components can contribute. The hierarchy is an organizing principle for separating different levels of genetic structure, not a guarantee that every source of deficit has been measured.

To discriminate subdivision from inbreeding, retain location and sampling metadata and analyze within-group genotype proportions before pooling. If the deficit largely disappears within independently justified breeding groups, subdivision is a plausible explanation. If it persists across many loci within groups, relatedness, nonrandom mating or technical problems deserve closer investigation. A single locus can show a deficit because an assay fails to detect one allele in heterozygotes. Repeating the assay with an independent marker or method is therefore an important control. The same numerical pattern is not necessarily the same biological mechanism.

Haplotypes and linkage disequilibrium

A diploid individual heterozygous at two loci, A/a and B/b, does not necessarily have the same chromosome arrangement as every other double heterozygote. Its two homologues could carry AB and ab in coupling phase, or Ab and aB in repulsion phase. A haplotype specifies allelic states carried together along one chromosome or gamete. Population data describing only unphased genotypes do not directly reveal every individual's haplotype arrangement. Family transmission or suitable molecular evidence can resolve phase, while population statistical methods may estimate it with uncertainty.

Let x_AB, x_Ab, x_aB and x_ab be the frequencies of the four possible haplotypes at two biallelic loci. Their sum is one. The marginal frequency of A is p_A = x_AB + x_Ab, while p_B = x_AB + x_aB. In linkage equilibrium, the occurrence of B in an A-bearing chromosome is no different from its occurrence in all chromosomes. The expected AB frequency is therefore p_A p_B. The signed linkage disequilibrium coefficient is D = x_AB − p_A p_B. By substituting the marginal frequencies and the identity that all four haplotypes sum to one, this can also be written D = x_AB x_ab − x_Ab x_aB.

A positive value means AB and ab are overrepresented relative to independence, with Ab and aB underrepresented. A negative value reverses the pattern for these allele labels. Relabeling one locus flips the sign without changing the biological strength of association. D cannot take every value between −1 and 1: all four haplotype frequencies must remain nonnegative. For fixed p_A and p_B, the largest possible positive value is the smaller of p_A(1 − p_B) and (1 − p_A)p_B. The largest magnitude of a negative value is the smaller of p_Ap_B and (1 − p_A)(1 − p_B). This frequency dependence explains why raw D cannot be compared naively across markers with very different allele frequencies.

The squared correlation measure r² is defined as D²/[p_A(1 − p_A)p_B(1 − p_B)] when both loci are polymorphic. To understand the denominator, assign an indicator value of one to an A-bearing chromosome and zero otherwise, and define a second indicator for B. The mean of the product of the indicators is x_AB, so their covariance is D. The variance of the first indicator is p_A(1 − p_A), and the second has variance p_B(1 − p_B). Dividing covariance squared by the product of variances yields the squared correlation. Thus r² ranges between zero and one and does not preserve the sign of D. It is undefined when either locus is fixed.

For example, suppose AB = 0.40, Ab = 0.10, aB = 0.10 and ab = 0.40 in an illustrative sample of chromosomes. Both marginal frequencies are 0.50. Independence predicts AB = 0.25, but the observed value is 0.40, giving D = 0.15. The denominator for r² is 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.0625. Hence r² = 0.0225/0.0625 = 0.36. If the two loci lie on different chromosomes, the association is still mathematically possible. LD is a description of the population's haplotypes, whereas physical linkage concerns inheritance and meiotic recombination.

A marker in LD with a causal allele can help predict the latter even if the marker itself has no effect on phenotype. Conversely, a strong association between two variants does not establish that either causes a trait. Population structure, common ancestry and shared selection history can produce correlated allele distributions. This distinction will be important when interpreting QTL mapping and association studies in node 8.E.05. For now, keep the statistical measurement separate from the biological interpretation.

Recombination and the decay of allele association

Recombination changes the combination of alleles carried on chromosomes during meiosis. In a random-mating diploid population, an AB chromosome can pair with an ab chromosome in a double heterozygote. Recombination between the two loci can generate Ab and aB gametes. A repulsion-phase individual can produce the opposite recombinant combinations. The net effect depends on the relative frequencies of the two double-heterozygote phases. With excess coupling haplotypes, recombination reduces that excess in expectation; with excess repulsion haplotypes, it moves the signed disequilibrium toward zero from the other direction.

Denote the recombination fraction by c, which ranges from zero to one-half under the standard two-locus genetic model. In a closed, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation, migration or drift, the haplotype recursion for AB is x'_AB = x_AB − cD. Because recombination changes combinations rather than marginal allele counts, p_A and p_B remain unchanged. Subtract their product from the recursion to get D' = (1 − c)D. Iterating yields D_t = D_0(1 − c)^t. The haplotypes Ab and aB each gain cD when D is positive, and AB and ab each lose cD. The net change in each allele frequency is therefore zero.

Suppose D_0 = 0.15 and c = 0.10. After one generation D = 0.135, and after three generations it is 0.15 × 0.9³ = 0.10935. Since the marginal allele frequencies are held constant in this model, r² declines by a factor of (1 − c)² each generation. For the previous example with initial r² = 0.36, after three generations the value is 0.36 × 0.9⁶ ≈ 0.1913. The decline of r² is faster than the proportional decline of D because the association is squared.

Even with independent assortment, c = 0.5, an initially nonzero D is halved in one generation rather than necessarily eliminated. Independence of transmission at meiosis does not imply that an entire population's historical associations vanish instantly. By contrast, if c = 0 and no other process intervenes, there is no recombination-driven change in D. These extreme cases are valuable checks on the meaning of the equation.

The decay model is not a rule that real populations must obey without exception. A pulse of immigration may introduce a new set of haplotypes. Selection may favor one haplotype relative to another, and finite populations experience random shifts in haplotype counts. Observed LD can therefore persist or even increase in a recombining population. Comparing the association of multiple marker pairs at different distances can reveal patterns consistent with recombination, but the inference requires attention to demography and allele frequencies. The mathematical expectation under the simplified model is a benchmark against which deviations can be interpreted.

Processes that change allele frequencies

Natural selection changes allele frequencies when heritable differences cause unequal contributions to subsequent generations. To calculate a simple viability-selection model, let the starting zygote frequencies be p², 2pq and q² and assign relative survival values w_AA, w_Aa and w_aa. The mean relative fitness is w̄ = p²w_AA + 2pqw_Aa + q²w_aa. Multiplying each initial frequency by its survival value and dividing by w̄ gives the genotype proportions among survivors. The normalization is essential: without it the surviving proportions sum to the mean fitness rather than one.

For p = 0.6 and q = 0.4, suppose the relative viability of AA and Aa is one and that of aa is 0.5. The starting frequencies are 0.36, 0.48 and 0.16. The unnormalized survivor weights are 0.36, 0.48 and 0.08, so the mean fitness is 0.92. The survivor A frequency is (0.36 + 0.24)/0.92 ≈ 0.6522. If these survivors produce gametes in equal proportions according to Mendelian segregation and mate randomly, the next zygote generation has Hardy–Weinberg proportions at the new allele frequency. This demonstrates that random mating and evolutionary change can coexist.

Mutation changes allelic state. If A mutates to a with probability u per transmission and a mutates to A with probability v, the next A frequency in a mutation-only model is p' = p(1 − u) + qv. Thus Δp = qv − pu. At a deterministic mutation-only equilibrium with u + v positive, the two flows balance, giving p* = v/(u + v). For very small mutation probabilities, the frequency change in one generation may be small, but mutation creates allelic variation upon which selection and drift can act. The equilibrium expression cannot be used unchanged if fitness differences or immigration are present.

Migration changes a local allele pool when the migrants that contribute to reproduction differ genetically from the recipient population. If m is the fraction of the new reproductive gene pool contributed by immigrants with A frequency p_m, then p' = (1 − m)p + mp_m. For p = 0.2, p_m = 0.8 and m = 0.1, p' = 0.26. The direction depends on the frequency contrast, not on whether A is dominant. Immigration that does not contribute to reproduction need not have this effect. The model describes gene flow, rather than simply physical movement.

Genetic drift is the random change in allele frequencies caused by finite sampling of gene copies across generations. In an ideal diploid Wright–Fisher population of N reproducing individuals, the count of A copies in the next generation is binomial with 2N trials and probability p. The mean next frequency is p and the variance is pq/(2N). A population with p = 0.5 and N = 50 has a one-generation standard deviation of 0.05; with N = 5000 it is 0.005. Drift is random in direction but stronger, on average, in smaller populations. The mean across independent replicate populations may remain unchanged while individual populations move toward fixation or loss.

Effective population size N_e is the size of an idealized population that would show the same rate of a specified genetic process, such as drift or inbreeding, as the real population. It is not automatically the census number. Unequal reproductive success, variation in population size over time and skewed reproductive sex ratios can reduce effective size relative to the census. Under an idealized separate-sex model with N_m breeding males and N_f breeding females, the sex-ratio approximation is N_e = 4N_mN_f/(N_m + N_f). For ten breeding males and ninety breeding females, the result is 36 rather than 100. This expression assumes the appropriate idealized reproductive conditions; it is not a general estimator for all populations.

A bottleneck can intensify genetic drift by reducing the number of individuals contributing to later generations. A founder event occurs when a new population begins from a subset of a source population. In either case, the surviving or founding individuals may not represent the original allele frequencies, and the genetic consequences can persist after census size recovers. These processes are not necessarily adaptive. Replicate populations under the same environment can change differently through drift, whereas consistent directional changes associated with experimentally measured fitness support selection as an explanation. Neither pattern alone rules out the other process.

Reasoning from genetic observations

The central task of population-genetic analysis is to connect observations with predictions that can distinguish mechanisms. A single snapshot of genotype frequencies gives a description of current structure but limited information about its history. To test a mating-system explanation, collect information about breeding partners or offspring from defined crosses. To test subdivision, retain group identity and compare within-group results with pooled data. To test viability selection, compare genotype frequencies before and after a defined developmental interval, controlling for movement and assay error. To investigate drift, compare temporal variation and independent replicate populations while accounting for the number of reproducing individuals.

An illustrative experiment begins with several populations of a short-generation organism at the same allele frequency. Some replicate populations are maintained at a small breeding size and others at a large breeding size, with environmental conditions held as similar as possible. If the small replicates display a wider distribution of frequency changes, including changes in opposite directions, the result supports the predicted size dependence of drift. One replicate alone could change strongly because of an unrecognized selective difference; replication provides information about the distribution of outcomes rather than merely its average.

In a different design, organisms are genotyped at the zygote stage and again after a developmental transition. Suppose the initial genotypes are near the expected Hardy–Weinberg proportions but one homozygote becomes rarer among survivors. This pattern is consistent with genotype-dependent viability. A reasonable alternative explanation is that the genotyping procedure behaves differently on samples from the two stages. Independent genotyping controls and documented survival of known genotypes strengthen the inference. The experiment should distinguish changes in relative genotype frequencies from absolute numbers of survivors.

In multilocus studies, compare both single-locus genotype proportions and phased haplotype frequencies. A population may be close to Hardy–Weinberg proportions at each locus and nevertheless have substantial D. Conversely, a single-locus heterozygote deficit does not directly quantify the correlation between loci. The equations used for these two problems require different data. A student who first identifies the observational unit—individual, gene copy, haplotype or deme—can avoid many common errors before beginning any arithmetic.

The next chapter considers traits affected by several genes and environmental variables. Population structure matters for such traits because the frequencies and correlations of contributing alleles vary among groups. However, differences between group phenotype averages do not prove a genetic cause; environmental variation and differences in sampling can produce similar patterns. The tools developed here are a foundation for examining those alternatives.

Original examination practice

1. Part B

What does pedigree-defined F measure?

Answer and explanation

Probability of identity by descent at the locus relative to the specified base population. Identical allelic states are not sufficient to establish identity by descent.

2. Part B

Does pure inbreeding necessarily change allele frequencies?

Answer and explanation

No. In the idealized model p² + Fpq + pq(1 − F) = p. Selection acting subsequently can change frequencies.

3. Part C

Two equally sampled demes have p = 0.9 and 0.1, each in equilibrium. Calculate H_S, H_T and F_ST.

Answer and explanation

H_S = 0.18, H_T = 0.50, F_ST = 0.64. The deficit reflects subdivision despite local random mating.

4. Part C

AB = 0.30, Ab = 0.20, aB = 0.10 and ab = 0.40. Find D and r².

Answer and explanation

p_A = 0.50, p_B = 0.40, D = 0.10, r² = 0.01/(0.25 × 0.24) = 1/6.

5. Part C

If D = −0.12 and c = 0.25, predict D after two generations under recombination alone.

Answer and explanation

D₂ = −0.12 × 0.75² = −0.0675. Its magnitude falls; its sign is unchanged.

6. Part C

A population is in equilibrium at the zygote stage, but aa adults are rarer than predicted. What comparison tests viability selection?

Answer and explanation

Measure survival of known genotypes from the same cohort under common conditions, accounting for migration and genotyping errors. Adult frequencies alone do not establish selection.
