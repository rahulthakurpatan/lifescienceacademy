What equilibrium does and does not predict

The Hardy–Weinberg model provides two predictions that must be separated before investigating a real population. First, if the two gametes forming a diploid zygote are independent samples from the same allele pool, the expected proportions of AA, Aa and aa among zygotes are p², 2pq and q². Second, if transmission and reproduction do not systematically change the representation of alleles and sampling is effectively infinite, the allele frequencies themselves remain constant across generations. The first claim concerns the combination of alleles within individuals at a particular stage of the life cycle. The second concerns changes in the gene pool across time. A population may satisfy one prediction while violating the other.

Consider a population with 100 diploid plants, including 40 AA, 20 Aa and 40 aa. There are 200 allele copies, of which 100 are A. Thus p = 0.5 and q = 0.5. Random mating at these frequencies would predict 25 AA, 50 Aa and 25 aa in a sample of 100 newly formed offspring. The actual plants have fewer heterozygotes and more homozygotes, but nothing in these counts alone establishes that the frequency of A has changed from an earlier generation. To establish that change, we would need information about the previous generation or a model of how the alleles were transmitted.

This separation provides a useful investigation strategy. Record the developmental stage, geographical origin and sampling design before calculating expected genotype frequencies. Estimate allele frequencies from the observed genotypes, then compare observed and expected genotype proportions within a biologically meaningful mating population. If a discrepancy occurs, ask whether it arises from how mates pair, from differences in the allele frequencies of groups that were pooled, from survival between zygote formation and sampling, from sampling noise, or from technical mistakes. A significant test is evidence against the specified statistical model, not direct proof of a particular biological process.

An additional distinction will become important when considering two loci. Hardy–Weinberg proportions describe the pairing of alleles at one locus within individuals; linkage equilibrium describes how alleles at separate loci are associated on chromosomes or gametes. Even a population that mates randomly can carry nonrandom associations between loci. To see why, remember that random fertilization combines chromosomes but does not instantly exchange the alleles carried along each chromosome.

Choosing the population and equilibrium baseline

The biological unit to which Hardy–Weinberg predictions apply is a pool of reproductive individuals that exchange alleles through the relevant mating process. This idea is easy to overlook because a genotype table looks the same whether it describes individuals from a single breeding pond, several ponds, or a collection accumulated over twenty years. The arithmetic always permits the calculation of an allele frequency. What changes is the meaning of the probability model used to combine that frequency with itself. Before using p² as the expected probability of AA, we must have a reason to believe that the two copies entering a zygote are independent draws from the same distribution. Defining the gene pool is therefore a biological problem preceding the calculation.

Consider the distinction between a census population and a reproductive population. Many individuals counted in a census may be immature, sterile, or outside the breeding range of the sampled population at the time of reproduction. A census of adults may include individuals from several cohorts that experienced distinct environments during development. If the purpose is to predict the genotype distribution of offspring, the relevant allele frequencies are those among gametes contributed by the parents, not necessarily those among all visible adults. A population of 100 individuals in which ten produce nearly all the offspring has a very different genetic transmission structure from a population in which each contributes equally. Both share a census size, but their reproductive distributions are not identical.

The simple two-allele model also assumes that sperm and egg pools have the same marginal allele frequency. Suppose that allele A occurs with frequency p_m in male gametes and p_f in female gametes, with complementary frequencies q_m and q_f. Random union of those gametes gives AA = p_mp_f, Aa = p_mq_f + q_mp_f, and aa = q_mq_f. The allele frequency in the resulting zygotes is the average (p_m + p_f)/2. If p_m and p_f differ, the zygote distribution cannot in general be represented by squaring this average. An excess of heterozygotes can arise despite independent pairing of the two gamete pools. The issue is not failure of independence but failure of the identical-distribution assumption.

For example, if p_m = 0.8 and p_f = 0.2, random fertilization produces AA = 0.16, Aa = 0.68 and aa = 0.16. The overall p is 0.5, giving an expectation of 0.50 heterozygotes if the two gamete pools were wrongly treated as identical. The observed excess of 0.18 is real relative to that latter model, but it is entirely compatible with random pairing between the two specified pools. To understand why, write p_m = p̄ + d and p_f = p̄ − d. The product p_mp_f is p̄² − d², while q_mq_f is q̄² − d². Both homozygotes are reduced by d² and the heterozygotes increase by 2d². This result is a useful contrast to the Wahlund effect, in which pooling separate groups with unequal allele frequencies produces a heterozygote deficit.

The two examples may initially appear contradictory: different source allele frequencies lead to a deficit in one case and an excess in another. The difference is whether the two allele copies within an individual are drawn together from the same source or from different source distributions. In a pooled sample of two demes, an individual's copies both tend to originate in its own deme. Common alleles in that deme therefore pair disproportionately with copies of the same type. In the unequal male and female gamete example, one copy originates in the male distribution and the other in the female distribution, so differences between those distributions increase the opportunity to form heterozygotes. The appropriate answer depends on the causal sampling structure, not on the mere existence of different frequencies.

The genetic structure of a population may also vary over time. A locus might be at random-mating proportions in each annual offspring cohort, yet a combined sample of cohorts show a deficit because the allele frequencies changed between years. This is a temporal version of the same mathematical mixture problem. Likewise, a sample that includes a mixture of early and late developmental stages can show deviations reflecting genotype-specific survival. For this reason, recording age and collection date can be as important as recording site. An apparently simple genetic calculation may require ecological context to establish what distribution should be expected.

The diploid autosomal model used throughout this chapter is a deliberately chosen starting point. At sex-linked loci, haploid stages, organelle genomes and polyploid loci, allele transmission differs. A male carrying one X-linked copy in a typical XY system should not be coded as a diploid homozygote for the purpose of calculating heterozygosity. If the locus is mitochondrial, an individual's transmitted organelle copies and their segregation may require a different model altogether. The first step in any application is thus identifying the unit of inheritance. A useful equation becomes misleading when applied to observations that violate the biological assumptions from which it was derived.

In the remainder of the chapter the terms p and q refer to frequencies at a biallelic autosomal locus unless another convention is explicitly introduced. A generation refers to the model's reproductive cycle, and all frequencies and coefficients are dimensionless probabilities or ratios. The assumptions will be varied one at a time before combined examples are considered. This method allows the effect of a particular departure to be identified without claiming that real populations experience only one process.

From population samples to genetic inference

A population geneticist usually observes a sample rather than the complete set of organisms capable of contributing to the next generation. This is a practical limitation with conceptual consequences. A collection of 200 insects from one tree may contain many siblings, whereas a collection of 200 insects drawn from scattered sites may contain members of several breeding groups. The count of organisms is the same in both cases, but the independence of the observations and the biological interpretation differ. The first collection may exaggerate the representation of a few parental genotypes; the second may introduce a population-mixture effect. A sampling frame is the set of individuals or locations from which the observations could have been drawn. Describing the sampling frame is therefore part of a scientific analysis, not an administrative detail.

In a diploid sample of size n, the observed counts n_AA, n_Aa and n_aa must add to n. Estimate p as (2n_AA + n_Aa)/(2n). Then compute expected counts np², 2npq and nq² using the estimated frequencies, and keep the distinction between counts and proportions. If there are 42 AA, 36 Aa and 22 aa organisms, the A count is 120, so p = 0.60. The expected counts are 36, 48 and 16. The three deviations from expectation are +6, −12 and +6. The pattern of equal absolute deviations for both homozygotes and an opposite deviation of twice their size follows from the fact that the observed and expected tables were constructed to have the same estimated allele count and the same total number of individuals.

A simple Pearson chi-square statistic adds the squared differences between observed and expected counts divided by their respective expectations. In this example, it is 36/36 + 144/48 + 36/16 = 1 + 3 + 2.25 = 6.25. For a large independently sampled biallelic autosomal population, with p estimated from the observations, the usual asymptotic reference distribution has one degree of freedom. The number of independent genotype categories is reduced by the total-count constraint and by estimating one allele-frequency parameter. For this instructional dataset the statistic exceeds the conventional 5% critical value of approximately 3.84. The conclusion is that the random-mating genotype model is inconsistent with these counts under the assumptions of the test. It is not that the organisms are definitively inbred.

The same dataset has observed heterozygosity 0.36 and estimated random-mating heterozygosity 0.48, giving a deficit index of 0.25. A test statistic and a deficit index answer different questions. The deficit index describes the size of the departure relative to the reference heterozygosity; the test statistic also depends on the number of observations. If every count in the example were divided by two, the allele-frequency estimate and deficit index would stay the same, but the chi-square statistic would be halved. Therefore a small sample can show a substantial deficit that is not statistically distinguishable from sampling variation, while a large sample can detect a much smaller departure. Both biological magnitude and statistical precision should be considered.

For rare alleles, expected homozygote counts may be extremely small. The asymptotic chi-square approximation can then be poor. For example, at q = 0.01 a sample of 1000 individuals has an expected aa count of only 0.1, even though 1000 may sound large. An exact test based on genotype configurations conditional on allele counts is one alternative. The point for examination reasoning is that sample adequacy depends on the expected frequencies of the categories rather than the total number alone. A rare allele with zero observed homozygotes has not necessarily been removed by selection. Such absence may be entirely compatible with a random-mating population.

A genotyping error can produce a particularly misleading pattern when one allele fails to be amplified or detected. If an Aa organism is recorded as AA because the a copy fails to register, the observed heterozygote count falls and the inferred allele frequency can shift as well. If a missing signal is more common in a particular location or type of sample, it can also create artificial population differentiation. An independent assay, replicate measurements, known positive controls and explicit recording of missing data are useful ways of examining such explanations. A population genetic model can be correct while the observations supplied to it are wrong.

When a locus is out of equilibrium in every sampled location, it is tempting to look for a shared biological mechanism. However, a consistent pattern across loci and a consistent pattern across populations provide different information. A mating system may influence many loci, whereas selection acting strongly on one region may produce a more localized departure. A widespread assay artifact could also affect many loci if they were measured by a common defective procedure. Conversely, population structure does not generate the same deficit at every marker because allele-frequency differentiation varies among markers. The sound inference is comparative rather than diagnostic from a single signature.

Non-random mating and the inbreeding coefficient

A mating system describes how individuals become reproductive partners. Positive assortative mating means that similar individuals pair more often than predicted by a random-mating model, whereas negative assortative mating means that dissimilar individuals pair preferentially. Similarity can refer to flowering time, body size or another characteristic. If similarity is influenced by a particular genotype, positive assortment may increase homozygosity at relevant loci. But resemblance between partners is not equivalent to close genealogical relationship. Two unrelated plants may flower at the same time because both respond to the same temperature. Conversely, relatives may have very different phenotypes.

Inbreeding refers to mating between individuals related by ancestry, relative to a defined reference population. The two homologous gene copies in a diploid offspring may trace back to the same ancestral gene copy. They are then identical by descent, abbreviated IBD. Identity by state is a broader observation: two copies carry the same allelic state, whether or not they trace to the same ancestral copy within the specified genealogy. Two A copies are always identical by state under a two-allele classification, but the observed genotype cannot by itself tell us whether they are identical by descent. The distinction explains why the inbreeding coefficient is a probability defined in relation to ancestry, not merely a count of matching alleles.

Let F be the probability that the two alleles at an autosomal locus are identical by descent relative to a chosen base population. Let p and q denote the frequencies of A and a in that base model. With probability F, the offspring has two copies of one ancestral allele. That allele is A with probability p and a with probability q. With probability 1 − F, the two copies are modeled as independent draws from the base allele pool. These two mutually exclusive routes give the genotype probabilities P(AA) = Fp + (1 − F)p² = p² + Fpq, P(Aa) = (1 − F)2pq, and P(aa) = Fq + (1 − F)q² = q² + Fpq. The expressions sum to one. They are valid under the stated model of the base frequencies and independent sampling in the non-IBD component.

We can derive the frequency of A from these adjusted genotype probabilities rather than assuming it stays constant. Add the probability of AA and half the probability of Aa. The result is p² + Fpq + pq(1 − F), which simplifies to p² + pq = p. The added homozygotes balance the missing heterozygotes in their contributions to the allele pool. Inbreeding in this idealized setting redistributes alleles among genotypes without changing p or q. If the resulting homozygotes experience different survival or reproduction, selection can subsequently alter the allele frequencies. In that case the observed evolutionary change cannot be attributed solely to the rearrangement of mating partners.

For example, with p = 0.7, q = 0.3 and F = 0.2, Hardy–Weinberg proportions would be 0.49, 0.42 and 0.09. The correction Fpq is 0.042, so the inbreeding-adjusted probabilities are 0.532, 0.336 and 0.132. The heterozygote deficit is 0.084, exactly twice the correction applied to each homozygote. The A frequency calculated from these proportions is 0.532 + 0.168 = 0.7. The frequency of a recessive phenotype with complete penetrance would rise from 0.09 to 0.132 even though the frequency of its allele remained unchanged.

The relationship can also be written H_O = (1 − F)H_E, where H_O is observed heterozygosity and H_E = 2pq under a two-allele random-mating reference. Rearranging gives an empirical deficit statistic 1 − H_O/H_E. This estimate is informative only when H_E is positive. A negative value means heterozygote excess relative to the reference, not a negative probability of identity by descent. A positive value may be due to ancestry, subdivision, selection or technical error. Therefore it is important to distinguish an estimated fixation or deficit statistic from a pedigree-defined probability.

Self-fertilization is a particularly transparent example. An Aa plant produces AA, Aa and aa offspring in the ratio 1:2:1 when it self-fertilizes. AA and aa plants remain homozygous at this locus in the absence of mutation. If the entire population reproduces by selfing, only half of the heterozygotes in one generation generate heterozygous offspring in the next, so H_t = H_0(1/2)^t in the ideal deterministic model. When the initial population is outbred with H_0 = 2pq, this gives F_t = 1 − (1/2)^t. After three generations, seven-eighths of the starting heterozygosity has been lost. Yet there is no built-in preference for A or a in Mendelian segregation. Individual lines become increasingly homozygous while the aggregate allele frequencies can remain stable.

Inbreeding depression is a reduction in average biological performance associated with increased inbreeding. One explanation is that partially recessive deleterious variants become homozygous and therefore express stronger harmful effects. Loss of advantages associated with heterozygosity may also contribute. The severity depends on which variants are present, the environment and the history of selection. Inbreeding is not a synonym for reduced fitness, and increased homozygosity does not logically imply that every homozygous genotype is harmful. A breeding experiment should compare performance of selfed or related crosses against appropriate unrelated crosses under comparable environmental conditions.

Ancestral paths, partial selfing and the reference population

The probability of identity by descent can be illustrated by tracing parental copies through a simple genealogy. Suppose an individual inherits one autosomal copy from its mother and one from its father. If the parents share an ancestor, both copies may descend from the same copy carried by that ancestor. A path of descent requires a particular allele to be transmitted at each meiosis. Under Mendelian segregation, each relevant transmission has probability one-half. For a non-inbred shared ancestor and independent non-overlapping descent paths, the contribution from one loop is (1/2) raised to the power n₁ + n₂ + 1, where n₁ and n₂ are the number of generations from the respective parents back to the shared ancestor. The extra factor reflects the probability that both paths trace to the same one of the ancestor's two homologous copies.

Consider offspring of two first cousins whose common grandparents are unrelated and non-inbred. Each parent is two generations from each shared grandparent, so each grandparent contributes (1/2)^5 = 1/32. There are two such grandparents, giving a total F of 1/16 under the stated pedigree model. If a common ancestor is itself inbred, the simple contribution is multiplied by 1 + F_A, where F_A denotes that ancestor's inbreeding coefficient relative to the same reference. Overlapping genealogies require care to avoid counting the same path more than once. The purpose of a basic pedigree calculation is to understand how shared ancestry increases the probability of autozygosity rather than to memorize isolated relationship coefficients.

The reference population is essential. If a population was founded by a small number of individuals centuries ago and all descendants share some ancestry, tracing every ancestral relationship indefinitely is not a meaningful practical baseline. A pedigree or population model specifies a reference at which identity by descent is treated as absent or is otherwise defined. Two estimates based on different reference populations can therefore differ without contradiction. A genomic estimate based on observed homozygous regions and a pedigree expectation based on known ancestry also measure related but not identical quantities. The pedigree gives an expected probability across transmissions; actual chromosome segments inherited by a particular individual are affected by segregation and recombination.

Partial self-fertilization helps connect a reproductive process to a stable pattern of inbreeding. Let s be the fraction of offspring produced by selfing, and let the remaining fraction 1 − s be produced by random outcrossing between individuals independently drawn from a very large reference population. In this idealization an outcrossed offspring has F = 0 relative to the current reference. For a selfed offspring, the two gametes may carry copies descended from the same parental allele with probability one-half. If they descend from different parental alleles, which also occurs with probability one-half, those parental alleles are identical by descent with probability F_t. The recursion is consequently F_(t+1) = s(1 + F_t)/2.

At equilibrium in this particular mating model, F* satisfies F* = s(1 + F*)/2. Rearrangement gives F* = s/(2 − s). If 40% of the offspring are produced through self-fertilization, the equilibrium coefficient is 0.4/1.6 = 0.25. If s = 0, the equilibrium is zero; if s = 1, the coefficient approaches one. The expression assumes that the outcrossing component does not itself increase relatedness, that the selfing rate is stable and that the specified population-frequency baseline remains appropriate. A finite isolated population with substantial relatedness among outcrossing individuals does not satisfy the same assumptions.

If the selfing rate is suddenly changed, the equilibrium coefficient does not generally appear in one generation. For instance, a population initially with F = 0 that shifts to s = 0.4 has F = 0.2 after the first generation, then 0.24 after the second, then 0.248 after the third. It approaches 0.25 rather than jumping immediately to that value. This sequence demonstrates that observed genetic structure can reflect historical as well as present reproductive conditions. Measuring today's mating system is valuable, but interpretation of today's genotype frequencies may also require a model of how long that system has operated.

Positive assortative mating and inbreeding can both increase homozygosity but make different predictions across the genome. If mating is based on a particular heritable flowering-time phenotype and individuals otherwise have little shared ancestry, the strongest effects may occur at loci contributing to that trait or correlated with it. Mating among relatives, on the other hand, increases the probability of shared ancestral copies at many autosomal loci, although the realized identity-by-descent pattern varies by region. A comparison of neutral markers, trait-associated markers and observed partner resemblance can help distinguish the two processes.

Negative assortative mating can instead favor heterozygous combinations at particular loci. Imagine a system in which two genetically determined morphs mate preferentially with each other. If the morphs correspond to different homozygotes, a higher proportion of their offspring will be heterozygous than expected from random pairing. This observation does not mean that the pedigree probability of identity by descent has become negative. It means that the observed heterozygosity exceeds the value predicted by the specified random-mating reference. Negative values of a fitted deficit index are valid descriptions of excess, while the ancestry probability remains bounded between zero and one.

A useful limiting example is an ideal population consisting of equal numbers of AA and aa individuals, each having equal reproductive success. If every AA mates with an aa, all zygotes are Aa. If each instead mates only with its own genotype, all zygotes are homozygotes, half AA and half aa. In both cases the gene pool has p = 0.5. Random pairing would produce the familiar intermediate pattern of one-quarter AA, one-half Aa and one-quarter aa. These extreme models clarify that the same allele frequencies support several genotype structures when the way gametes combine changes. Real mating preferences can also affect the number of offspring produced by different genotypes, in which case genotype reshuffling and changes in allele frequency are no longer cleanly separable.

Generalizing the inbreeding model and recognizing its limits

The inbreeding model is sometimes introduced with two alleles because the three genotype categories can be written in a compact equation. The underlying idea is not restricted to a biallelic locus. Suppose there are three alleles A₁, A₂ and A₃, with frequencies p₁, p₂ and p₃ whose sum is one. In the identity-by-descent component of probability F, a zygote carries two copies of one ancestral allelic state. Thus the probability of A₁A₁ from this component is Fp₁, and similarly for the other alleles. In the remaining component, each of the two copies is an independent draw, producing homozygote probability (1 − F)p_i² and heterozygote probability 2(1 − F)p_ip_j for i different from j.

Combining the components, the probability of the homozygote A_iA_i is p_i² + Fp_i(1 − p_i). The probability of A_iA_j is 2p_ip_j(1 − F). Summing over all heterozygous categories gives H = (1 − F)[1 − Σp_i²]. The expression in brackets is the probability that two independent draws differ in allelic state. Its complement is the sum of the squared frequencies. This provides a convenient generalization for markers with several observed alleles. It also helps explain why heterozygosity is not simply the number of alleles present. A marker with several alleles all at low frequency except one common allele may have low expected heterozygosity.

As an illustrative example, let p₁ = 0.5, p₂ = 0.3 and p₃ = 0.2, and F = 0.1. Random mating predicts H_E = 1 − (0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04) = 0.62. Under the inbreeding model H_O = 0.558. The A₁A₁ frequency is 0.25 + 0.1(0.5)(0.5) = 0.275, A₂A₂ is 0.111, and A₃A₃ is 0.056. The heterozygotes A₁A₂, A₁A₃ and A₂A₃ have frequencies 0.27, 0.18 and 0.108. Adding these categories produces one. Calculating the contribution of A₂ from its homozygote and half its two heterozygotes gives 0.111 + 0.135 + 0.054 = 0.30, preserving the marginal frequency. This calculation demonstrates how the identity model applies consistently beyond a two-allele example.

A useful biological consequence of increased homozygosity is the expression of recessive alleles that were previously concealed by dominant or otherwise compensating functional alleles. The magnitude of this change is easiest to see when the allele is rare. For a recessive allele with q = 0.01, the random-mating aa frequency is 0.0001. With F = 0.05, the inbreeding correction Fpq = 0.000495 raises the model homozygote frequency to 0.000595, nearly six times the random-mating value. Although the absolute change is small, it can be large in relative terms. This example refers to population-model expectations for an assumed single locus; it is not a prediction about any particular person's health or offspring.

The heterozygote deficit in that rare-allele example is 2Fpq, or 0.00099, which is only about one tenth of one percentage point in the total population. A large relative change in a rare homozygous genotype can therefore accompany a small change in total heterozygosity. The examination implication is that percentage increases, absolute probability differences and allele-frequency changes must not be conflated. If asked how many additional homozygotes are expected in a population of 100,000 under this model, multiply the absolute correction 0.000495 by 100,000 to obtain 49.5 expected additional individuals. This is an expectation across repeated populations, not a requirement that a real count contain half an individual.

What happens if an empirical inbreeding index differs across loci? The first possibility is sampling variation, because finite samples produce fluctuating genotype counts. The second is that the probability of identity by descent inferred from a particular marker depends on the information it provides. A marker fixed for one state cannot distinguish autozygosity from identity by state. The third is a genuine difference in how selection, mutation or mating preference affects different loci. For example, a locus related to mating preference might depart from the genome-wide pattern. The ideal F model gives a common baseline for comparing loci, not a guarantee that every marker must have exactly the same estimated value.

When breeding researchers compare offspring from selfing and outcrossing, heterozygosity and performance should be kept conceptually separate. A marker with no measurable effect on the trait can reveal increased homozygosity, while the performance difference may be due to other genes. Suppose the selfed group is shorter on average than the outcrossed group in a common environment. The result supports inbreeding depression if the experimental design makes relatedness the relevant difference. It does not establish that the measured marker is the causal locus. Conversely, a neutral marker can show a substantial deficit even if the measured phenotype is unaffected. A scientific account distinguishes evidence of genetic structure from evidence of its functional consequences.

A general limitation arises when the allele frequencies used as the base distribution have already been altered by the process being modeled. In an isolated small population, drift can change allele frequencies while inbreeding increases. Using a coefficient relative to an ancestral population alongside current allele frequencies without specifying the model can produce a confused interpretation. The elementary formula with p, q and F is best treated as a model for genotype proportions relative to a stated allele-frequency distribution. Pedigree-based identity, empirical heterozygosity and temporal changes in diversity are linked but not interchangeable quantities. A well-posed question will either specify the necessary baseline or require the student to identify why the answer cannot be uniquely determined.

Population subdivision and the Wahlund effect

A population is not simply a collection of all members of a species. For genetic inference, the boundaries of the group should reflect the processes of reproduction and gene exchange relevant to the question. Fish in separated lakes, plants on different mountain slopes and insects with different breeding seasons can have distinct allele frequencies even when they belong to the same species. The groups need not be completely isolated. Restricted gene exchange can be sufficient for differences to persist.

Consider two equally represented demes, each internally at Hardy–Weinberg proportions. In deme one, A has frequency 0.8 and a has frequency 0.2, giving AA = 0.64, Aa = 0.32 and aa = 0.04. In deme two, p = 0.2 and q = 0.8, giving AA = 0.04, Aa = 0.32 and aa = 0.64. When the records are pooled, the genotype frequencies are 0.34, 0.32 and 0.34, and the pooled allele frequency is 0.5. If the pooled sample were wrongly assumed to come from a single randomly mating population, the expected heterozygosity would be 0.50 rather than the observed 0.32. The difference of 0.18 occurs despite random mating within each deme. This is the Wahlund effect.

The calculation generalizes. Let w_i be the proportion of sampled individuals from group i, with weights summing to one, and let p_i be the group's A frequency. Define the weighted mean p̄ = Σw_i p_i. The mean expected heterozygosity within demes is H_S = Σw_i 2p_i(1 − p_i). The heterozygosity predicted by treating the pooled sample as a single random-mating population is H_T = 2p̄(1 − p̄). Expanding both terms and subtracting gives H_T − H_S = 2[Σw_i p_i² − p̄²] = 2 Var_w(p_i). The variance is nonnegative, so heterogeneity in allele frequency produces a deficit relative to the pooled reference. If the groups have the same allele frequencies, the variance and this particular effect are zero.

The weights should reflect the sample or population whose heterozygosity is being described. Suppose a small deme contributes 10% of the records and has p = 0.1, while a larger deme contributes 90% and has p = 0.7. The pooled p is 0.64, not 0.4. The within-deme expected heterozygosity is 0.1(0.18) + 0.9(0.42) = 0.396, and H_T = 2(0.64)(0.36) = 0.4608. The deficit is 0.0648. Equal weighting would answer a different question, namely the expectation when each deme is sampled with the same probability.

An introductory measure of subdivision is F_ST = (H_T − H_S)/H_T, provided H_T is nonzero. In the equally represented two-deme example, it equals 0.18/0.50 = 0.36. This quantity describes the relative reduction in expected heterozygosity associated with allele-frequency differences among demes; it does not mean that 36% of individuals are inbred. Practical estimators from finite samples account for sampling and population design, so a simple calculation from exact frequencies is an instructional model rather than a universal estimation procedure.

There is a useful distinction between H_I, observed heterozygosity within individuals averaged across groups, H_S, expected heterozygosity within groups, and H_T, expected heterozygosity from pooled frequencies. Define F_IS = 1 − H_I/H_S and F_IT = 1 − H_I/H_T. Algebra gives (1 − F_IT) = (1 − F_IS)(1 − F_ST). In the first example H_I = H_S, so F_IS = 0 even though F_IT is positive. If heterozygote deficits also occur within each deme, both components can contribute. The hierarchy is an organizing principle for separating different levels of genetic structure, not a guarantee that every source of deficit has been measured.

To discriminate subdivision from inbreeding, retain location and sampling metadata and analyze within-group genotype proportions before pooling. If the deficit largely disappears within independently justified breeding groups, subdivision is a plausible explanation. If it persists across many loci within groups, relatedness, nonrandom mating or technical problems deserve closer investigation. A single locus can show a deficit because an assay fails to detect one allele in heterozygotes. Repeating the assay with an independent marker or method is therefore an important control. The same numerical pattern is not necessarily the same biological mechanism.

Haplotypes and linkage disequilibrium

A diploid individual heterozygous at two loci, A/a and B/b, does not necessarily have the same chromosome arrangement as every other double heterozygote. Its two homologues could carry AB and ab in coupling phase, or Ab and aB in repulsion phase. A haplotype specifies allelic states carried together along one chromosome or gamete. Population data describing only unphased genotypes do not directly reveal every individual's haplotype arrangement. Family transmission or suitable molecular evidence can resolve phase, while population statistical methods may estimate it with uncertainty.

Let x_AB, x_Ab, x_aB and x_ab be the frequencies of the four possible haplotypes at two biallelic loci. Their sum is one. The marginal frequency of A is p_A = x_AB + x_Ab, while p_B = x_AB + x_aB. In linkage equilibrium, the occurrence of B in an A-bearing chromosome is no different from its occurrence in all chromosomes. The expected AB frequency is therefore p_A p_B. The signed linkage disequilibrium coefficient is D = x_AB − p_A p_B. By substituting the marginal frequencies and the identity that all four haplotypes sum to one, this can also be written D = x_AB x_ab − x_Ab x_aB.

A positive value means AB and ab are overrepresented relative to independence, with Ab and aB underrepresented. A negative value reverses the pattern for these allele labels. Relabeling one locus flips the sign without changing the biological strength of association. D cannot take every value between −1 and 1: all four haplotype frequencies must remain nonnegative. For fixed p_A and p_B, the largest possible positive value is the smaller of p_A(1 − p_B) and (1 − p_A)p_B. The largest magnitude of a negative value is the smaller of p_Ap_B and (1 − p_A)(1 − p_B). This frequency dependence explains why raw D cannot be compared naively across markers with very different allele frequencies.

The squared correlation measure r² is defined as D²/[p_A(1 − p_A)p_B(1 − p_B)] when both loci are polymorphic. To understand the denominator, assign an indicator value of one to an A-bearing chromosome and zero otherwise, and define a second indicator for B. The mean of the product of the indicators is x_AB, so their covariance is D. The variance of the first indicator is p_A(1 − p_A), and the second has variance p_B(1 − p_B). Dividing covariance squared by the product of variances yields the squared correlation. Thus r² ranges between zero and one and does not preserve the sign of D. It is undefined when either locus is fixed.

For example, suppose AB = 0.40, Ab = 0.10, aB = 0.10 and ab = 0.40 in an illustrative sample of chromosomes. Both marginal frequencies are 0.50. Independence predicts AB = 0.25, but the observed value is 0.40, giving D = 0.15. The denominator for r² is 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.0625. Hence r² = 0.0225/0.0625 = 0.36. If the two loci lie on different chromosomes, the association is still mathematically possible. LD is a description of the population's haplotypes, whereas physical linkage concerns inheritance and meiotic recombination.

A marker in LD with a causal allele can help predict the latter even if the marker itself has no effect on phenotype. Conversely, a strong association between two variants does not establish that either causes a trait. Population structure, common ancestry and shared selection history can produce correlated allele distributions. This distinction will be important when interpreting QTL mapping and association studies in node 8.E.05. For now, keep the statistical measurement separate from the biological interpretation.

Working with phased and unphased marker data

The difference between a genotype and a haplotype can be made concrete with two markers typed in four diploid individuals. Suppose that at the first locus two individuals are Aa and at the second locus those same two are Bb. Their unphased two-locus genotypes are identical. If one individual carries AB on one homologue and ab on the other, it contributes one AB and one ab chromosome to a phased dataset. If the other individual carries Ab and aB, it contributes the two opposite combinations. Merely counting the number of A and B alleles does not determine which of these chromosome arrangements is present. Phase is additional information about associations across loci.

For some genotypes, phase follows directly from the allele states. An individual with AA and Bb must have haplotypes AB and Ab, because both copies at the first locus are A. An individual with Aa and bb must carry Ab and ab. For a double heterozygote, both phases are possible. A pedigree in which one parental chromosome's alleles can be identified may resolve the phase through transmission. Molecular reads that connect markers on the same DNA molecule can provide another type of evidence. If the markers lie too far apart to be connected directly by the available data, the arrangement may be uncertain. The examination skill is to determine which genotypes yield unambiguous haplotypes and which require additional information.

Imagine a teaching dataset containing four AA BB individuals, four aa bb individuals and eight Aa Bb double heterozygotes. There are 32 chromosomes, with 16 A copies and 16 B copies, giving p_A = p_B = 0.5. If all eight double heterozygotes are in coupling phase, the phased haplotype counts are 16 AB and 16 ab, so D = 0.25 and r² = 1. If all are in repulsion phase, the homozygotes contribute eight AB and eight ab chromosomes, and the double heterozygotes contribute eight Ab and eight aB. The four classes then each have frequency 0.25 and D = 0. Both scenarios have precisely the same unphased genotype table. The examples are intentionally extreme, but they prove that identical unphased observations can admit distinct haplotype associations.

One must be especially careful when comparing an individual's phase to a population-level LD measurement. An AB/ab individual is in coupling phase for its two homologues even in a population where AB, Ab, aB and ab are all equally frequent. Conversely, the presence of an Ab/aB individual does not refute positive LD in the population, because a positive association need not make opposite-phase haplotypes absent. The population statistic summarizes frequencies across many chromosomes, not a label assigned to every member. The relationship resembles the distinction between an individual's homozygous genotype and the population's degree of inbreeding.

A useful check on the calculation of D is to reconstruct all four haplotype frequencies from the marginals and D. The four expressions are x_AB = p_Ap_B + D, x_Ab = p_Aq_B − D, x_aB = q_Ap_B − D and x_ab = q_Aq_B + D. The sum is one because the D terms cancel. Each marginal is preserved when the appropriate pair is added. This form is helpful for examination questions in which the allele frequencies and the association coefficient are supplied but the haplotype table is not. It also makes the allowed range of D transparent: none of the four reconstructed frequencies may be negative.

For instance, take p_A = 0.7, p_B = 0.4 and D = 0.08. The independent products for AB, Ab, aB and ab are 0.28, 0.42, 0.12 and 0.18. Applying the signs gives observed frequencies of 0.36, 0.34, 0.04 and 0.26. The A marginal is still 0.70, the B marginal is still 0.40, and r² = 0.0064/[0.7(0.3)0.4(0.6)] ≈ 0.127. If D were instead proposed as 0.15, the aB frequency would be 0.12 − 0.15 = −0.03, making that proposed set mathematically impossible. A negative reconstructed frequency is not a biological observation; it is evidence that the numerical inputs or assumptions are inconsistent.

It is possible for two marker pairs to have the same raw D yet different r². If both marginal frequencies are 0.5, D = 0.05 produces r² = 0.04. If both are 0.1, the same D gives r² ≈ 0.309 because the product of marginal variances is 0.0081. The absolute excess of the AB haplotype is 0.05 in both cases, but the association accounts for a larger proportion of standardized variation in the second case. This distinction is especially important in mapping, where the predictive correlation of markers can matter more than an unstandardized difference in frequency.

A related point is that r² can be less than one even when a particular haplotype is absent. Let p_A = 0.2 and p_B = 0.5, and suppose every A-bearing chromosome also carries B. Then x_AB = 0.2, x_Ab = 0, x_aB = 0.3 and x_ab = 0.5. D = 0.1, the largest positive value compatible with these marginals. Yet r² = 0.01/[0.2(0.8)0.5(0.5)] = 0.25. Knowing that a chromosome carries A determines that it also carries B, but knowing that it carries B does not determine whether it carries A. Perfect correlation requires a stronger relationship between allele indicators. This is why an LD coefficient near its allowable bound need not imply perfect predictive correlation.

Statistical uncertainty must also be distinguished from the calculated association. Suppose only ten haplotypes have been observed and one class is absent. This absence might occur by chance even if the corresponding population frequency is positive. The sample D may not be exactly zero even if the true population is in linkage equilibrium. A much larger independent sample can reduce this uncertainty, but correlated individuals, imperfect phasing and population mixture can make a naive interpretation unreliable. Introductory LD questions usually provide true or illustrative haplotype frequencies rather than uncertain estimates; real data require explicit attention to the distinction.

The LD measures discussed here are symmetric in the two loci. They do not specify which allele is the cause of a phenotype, which marker is biologically upstream, or whether the association is due to an interaction between the gene products. A genetic interaction such as epistasis is a statement about the effect of genotypes on a phenotype or fitness; LD is a statement about their joint population distribution. Epistatic selection can influence associations, but one concept cannot substitute for the other. Recognizing this distinction prepares students to interpret the statistical relationships that arise in polygenic and marker-mapping studies without confusing association with molecular mechanism.

Recombination and the decay of allele association

Recombination changes the combination of alleles carried on chromosomes during meiosis. In a random-mating diploid population, an AB chromosome can pair with an ab chromosome in a double heterozygote. Recombination between the two loci can generate Ab and aB gametes. A repulsion-phase individual can produce the opposite recombinant combinations. The net effect depends on the relative frequencies of the two double-heterozygote phases. With excess coupling haplotypes, recombination reduces that excess in expectation; with excess repulsion haplotypes, it moves the signed disequilibrium toward zero from the other direction.

Denote the recombination fraction by c, which ranges from zero to one-half under the standard two-locus genetic model. In a closed, randomly mating population with no selection, mutation, migration or drift, the haplotype recursion for AB is x'_AB = x_AB − cD. Because recombination changes combinations rather than marginal allele counts, p_A and p_B remain unchanged. Subtract their product from the recursion to get D' = (1 − c)D. Iterating yields D_t = D_0(1 − c)^t. The haplotypes Ab and aB each gain cD when D is positive, and AB and ab each lose cD. The net change in each allele frequency is therefore zero.

Suppose D_0 = 0.15 and c = 0.10. After one generation D = 0.135, and after three generations it is 0.15 × 0.9³ = 0.10935. Since the marginal allele frequencies are held constant in this model, r² declines by a factor of (1 − c)² each generation. For the previous example with initial r² = 0.36, after three generations the value is 0.36 × 0.9⁶ ≈ 0.1913. The decline of r² is faster than the proportional decline of D because the association is squared.

Even with independent assortment, c = 0.5, an initially nonzero D is halved in one generation rather than necessarily eliminated. Independence of transmission at meiosis does not imply that an entire population's historical associations vanish instantly. By contrast, if c = 0 and no other process intervenes, there is no recombination-driven change in D. These extreme cases are valuable checks on the meaning of the equation.

The decay model is not a rule that real populations must obey without exception. A pulse of immigration may introduce a new set of haplotypes. Selection may favor one haplotype relative to another, and finite populations experience random shifts in haplotype counts. Observed LD can therefore persist or even increase in a recombining population. Comparing the association of multiple marker pairs at different distances can reveal patterns consistent with recombination, but the inference requires attention to demography and allele frequencies. The mathematical expectation under the simplified model is a benchmark against which deviations can be interpreted.

Connecting meiotic transmission to the population recurrence

The recurrence for linkage disequilibrium can be derived directly from the frequencies of double heterozygotes. This is useful because the expression D' = (1 − c)D otherwise risks becoming a formula learned without understanding its biological origin. Assume a very large diploid population with random union of gametes. Let x₁, x₂, x₃ and x₄ represent AB, Ab, aB and ab respectively. A diploid carrying AB and ab can arise through either parental order, so its expected frequency is 2x₁x₄. Similarly, the expected frequency of Ab/aB double heterozygotes is 2x₂x₃. These are the two genotypic arrangements in which recombination between the markers changes the haplotype classes. Crossing over in an individual that is homozygous at either locus cannot create a distinguishable new combination at the two markers.

In a coupling double heterozygote, without recombination, one-half of the gametes would carry AB and one-half would carry ab. With a recombinant fraction c, the expected fractions of AB and ab become (1 − c)/2 each, and those of Ab and aB become c/2 each. The change in the contribution to AB from this genotype is therefore −c/2 per gamete multiplied by its genotype frequency 2x₁x₄. This gives −cx₁x₄. In a repulsion double heterozygote, AB is one of the recombinant classes, so the change from this genotype is +c/2 multiplied by 2x₂x₃, giving +cx₂x₃. The sum of these changes is −c(x₁x₄ − x₂x₃) = −cD. Thus the algebra reflects the competition between two meiotic routes that either break up or create AB haplotypes.

Notice that recombination does not always decrease the frequency of a given haplotype. If D is negative, x₂x₃ exceeds x₁x₄, and the net production of AB through recombination exceeds its loss. The frequency of AB then increases toward its independent expectation. The effect is to reduce the magnitude of disequilibrium, rather than universally to reduce AB or to increase a particular allele. The sign of the change depends on the initial population association and the labels used for the alleles.

The derivation assumes that genotypes form by random pairing of the gametes and that the two phase arrangements have equivalent reproductive contributions aside from recombination. If mating is nonrandom, the observed frequencies of double heterozygotes need not equal 2x₁x₄ and 2x₂x₃. If selection favors a particular genotype, its contribution to the gametic pool also changes. In either case the simple recurrence no longer follows directly from the same assumptions. The model is strongest when it identifies the biological process being varied and clarifies what has been held constant.

It is also important to distinguish the recombination fraction from the physical distance between loci. The fraction measures the proportion of gametes recombinant at the markers in the specified meiosis. Multiple crossover events can restore parental marker combinations, so the fraction does not increase without limit as physical separation grows. This is the basis of the one-half ceiling in the conventional linkage model. By contrast, D and r² are measured across the sampled population's haplotypes and are affected by its history. Two marker pairs with the same recombination fraction may display quite different LD because of differences in initial associations, allele frequencies, selection or migration. Knowledge of the meiotic map is useful but does not uniquely predict the present population haplotype distribution.

A brief historical perspective shows why the distinction emerged. The development of one-locus equilibrium models made it possible to ask how inherited states were distributed among individuals under ideal random mating. Early multilocus extensions then addressed a different problem: the rate at which nonrandom combinations of alleles are broken down by recombination. The historical review One Hundred Years of Linkage Disequilibrium (2018) traces these developments from early twentieth-century formulations to their later application in genetic mapping and demographic inference. The enduring value of these models is not an assumption that all genes assort independently in every population, but a mathematical baseline for recognizing what nonrandom associations require explanation.

To work through a recurrence, start from a valid haplotype distribution, compute the marginal frequencies and D, and update each haplotype with the appropriate sign. Suppose the initial frequencies are AB = 0.30, Ab = 0.10, aB = 0.20 and ab = 0.40. Here p_A = 0.40, p_B = 0.50 and D = 0.10. At c = 0.20, the correction cD = 0.02 gives next frequencies of 0.28, 0.12, 0.22 and 0.38. The marginals remain 0.40 and 0.50, and the new D is 0.08. Repeating the process gives D = 0.064 in the second generation. If a calculation instead predicts an increase in either marginal frequency under recombination alone, the result fails an immediate conservation check. These checks are more useful than memorizing every possible recursion separately.

Population mixture, admixture and multilocus associations

A mixture of records from separate populations is not the same event as biological admixture. In the first case, a researcher combines observations from groups without implying that their members have produced offspring together. In the second, individuals or gametes from previously differentiated populations contribute to a common breeding population. Both situations can create genetic associations, but they have different time courses. A Wahlund deficit in pooled parental samples may be removed after random fertilization among gametes of the pooled gene pool. The association between alleles at distinct loci need not disappear as rapidly, because recombination rather than merely random fertilization breaks associations carried along the same chromosome.

To see how mixture creates LD even when each source is in linkage equilibrium, consider two source populations of equal weight. In the first, p_A = p_B = 0.8. In the second, p_A = p_B = 0.2. Within each source, AB has frequency equal to the product of its marginal frequencies, giving 0.64 and 0.04 respectively. The pooled AB frequency is 0.34, while both pooled marginal frequencies are 0.5. Thus D in the mixture is 0.34 − 0.25 = 0.09. The association has arisen because an A-bearing chromosome is more likely to come from the source where B is common. No interaction between the functions of A and B is required.

More generally, if w_i are the mixing weights, D_i are the within-source signed LD coefficients, and p_Ai and p_Bi are the marginal frequencies, then D_pool = Σw_i D_i + Cov_w(p_Ai,p_Bi). To derive this result, replace the AB frequency in each source with D_i + p_Ai p_Bi, average across sources, and subtract the product of the pooled marginal frequencies. The second term measures whether A and B tend to be common in the same source populations. If the frequency contrasts have opposite signs, the covariance term is negative. If one locus has identical frequencies in all sources, the covariance term is zero, although the within-source LD component may still be nonzero.

Suppose there is one pulse of admixture and the resulting gene pool is closed and randomly mating thereafter. Unlinked markers have c = 0.5, so the admixture-induced D decays relatively quickly under the deterministic model. Closely linked markers retain the signal for longer. Continuous gene flow from the source populations changes this expectation because new associations are introduced every generation. It is therefore incorrect to infer that high LD proves very tight physical linkage without considering demographic history. A population with a recent mixture of genetically distinct sources may show associations across relatively distant regions or different chromosomes.

An empirical comparison might use markers known to occur in different regions of the genome and analyze associations both within source groups and after pooling. A widespread association pattern that weakens when the analysis accounts for source identity is consistent with population structure. However, because source classification is usually imperfect and individuals may have mixed ancestry, an arbitrary division of samples can leave residual structure. The appropriate resolution depends on the research question. The basic conceptual exercise is to identify the third variable—shared population history—that may explain correlation between two loci without a direct functional connection.

The same principle applies to phenotype association. Imagine that a marker allele is common in population A and rare in population B, and that an environmental condition experienced mainly by population A increases average plant height. A pooled analysis may find that the marker predicts height even if it has no biological effect on growth. Within each population, the relationship may be absent. The association is confounded by population membership. A genetic mapping population derived from controlled crosses can reduce some of this confounding, and comparisons within groups or statistical models accounting for ancestry can also be informative. These approaches have assumptions and do not automatically establish causality; their detailed use belongs in the later molecular-mapping chapter.

There is also a distinction between restricted gene flow and complete absence of gene flow. If two demes have different allele frequencies and each receives a fraction m of its breeding gene pool from the other, the next frequencies are p'_1 = (1 − m)p_1 + mp_2 and p'_2 = (1 − m)p_2 + mp_1 in a symmetrical two-deme model with equal-sized groups. Subtracting gives p'_1 − p'_2 = (1 − 2m)(p_1 − p_2). For m = 0.1, the difference falls to 80% of its previous magnitude after one generation. This is a mechanism by which exchange can reduce differentiation. But selection favoring different alleles in different habitats and finite-population drift can counteract homogenization, so measured differentiation reflects the combined history of several forces.

A high value of F_ST is not a direct measure of geographical distance or an unambiguous count of migrants. Two distant groups might exchange gametes frequently through dispersal, while adjacent groups might reproduce at different times and exchange few genes. The relationship between migration and differentiation depends on population size, history, marker behavior and model assumptions. Rather than interpreting a single statistic as a complete reconstruction of history, use it to formulate competing hypotheses. For an introductory examination, the most defensible conclusion is often that allele-frequency variation among defined populations increases the difference between H_T and H_S.

Processes that change allele frequencies

Natural selection changes allele frequencies when heritable differences cause unequal contributions to subsequent generations. To calculate a simple viability-selection model, let the starting zygote frequencies be p², 2pq and q² and assign relative survival values w_AA, w_Aa and w_aa. The mean relative fitness is w̄ = p²w_AA + 2pqw_Aa + q²w_aa. Multiplying each initial frequency by its survival value and dividing by w̄ gives the genotype proportions among survivors. The normalization is essential: without it the surviving proportions sum to the mean fitness rather than one.

For p = 0.6 and q = 0.4, suppose the relative viability of AA and Aa is one and that of aa is 0.5. The starting frequencies are 0.36, 0.48 and 0.16. The unnormalized survivor weights are 0.36, 0.48 and 0.08, so the mean fitness is 0.92. The survivor A frequency is (0.36 + 0.24)/0.92 ≈ 0.6522. If these survivors produce gametes in equal proportions according to Mendelian segregation and mate randomly, the next zygote generation has Hardy–Weinberg proportions at the new allele frequency. This demonstrates that random mating and evolutionary change can coexist.

Mutation changes allelic state. If A mutates to a with probability u per transmission and a mutates to A with probability v, the next A frequency in a mutation-only model is p' = p(1 − u) + qv. Thus Δp = qv − pu. At a deterministic mutation-only equilibrium with u + v positive, the two flows balance, giving p* = v/(u + v). For very small mutation probabilities, the frequency change in one generation may be small, but mutation creates allelic variation upon which selection and drift can act. The equilibrium expression cannot be used unchanged if fitness differences or immigration are present.

Migration changes a local allele pool when the migrants that contribute to reproduction differ genetically from the recipient population. If m is the fraction of the new reproductive gene pool contributed by immigrants with A frequency p_m, then p' = (1 − m)p + mp_m. For p = 0.2, p_m = 0.8 and m = 0.1, p' = 0.26. The direction depends on the frequency contrast, not on whether A is dominant. Immigration that does not contribute to reproduction need not have this effect. The model describes gene flow, rather than simply physical movement.

Genetic drift is the random change in allele frequencies caused by finite sampling of gene copies across generations. In an ideal diploid Wright–Fisher population of N reproducing individuals, the count of A copies in the next generation is binomial with 2N trials and probability p. The mean next frequency is p and the variance is pq/(2N). A population with p = 0.5 and N = 50 has a one-generation standard deviation of 0.05; with N = 5000 it is 0.005. Drift is random in direction but stronger, on average, in smaller populations. The mean across independent replicate populations may remain unchanged while individual populations move toward fixation or loss.

Effective population size N_e is the size of an idealized population that would show the same rate of a specified genetic process, such as drift or inbreeding, as the real population. It is not automatically the census number. Unequal reproductive success, variation in population size over time and skewed reproductive sex ratios can reduce effective size relative to the census. Under an idealized separate-sex model with N_m breeding males and N_f breeding females, the sex-ratio approximation is N_e = 4N_mN_f/(N_m + N_f). For ten breeding males and ninety breeding females, the result is 36 rather than 100. This expression assumes the appropriate idealized reproductive conditions; it is not a general estimator for all populations.

A bottleneck can intensify genetic drift by reducing the number of individuals contributing to later generations. A founder event occurs when a new population begins from a subset of a source population. In either case, the surviving or founding individuals may not represent the original allele frequencies, and the genetic consequences can persist after census size recovers. These processes are not necessarily adaptive. Replicate populations under the same environment can change differently through drift, whereas consistent directional changes associated with experimentally measured fitness support selection as an explanation. Neither pattern alone rules out the other process.

Life-cycle selection, mutation and stochastic transmission

In a selection calculation, fitness may mean viability to a specified stage, reproductive output, or the combined contribution to the next generation. Using adult genotype frequencies with a fitness value describing only embryonic survival risks counting the same selective episode twice. To avoid that error, draw the life cycle and label the stage at which each frequency is measured. Gamete formation, fertilization, development, adult reproduction and dispersal are biologically distinct events, even though a simplified model may combine several of them into one mathematical step.

Relative fitness is sufficient for calculating genotype frequencies after proportional selection. Suppose an illustrative study observes survival probabilities of 0.8, 0.8 and 0.4 for AA, Aa and aa. Dividing all three values by 0.8 gives relative viabilities 1, 1 and 0.5. The two sets produce the same normalized survivor genotype proportions. They do not produce the same absolute survivor counts if interpreted as absolute probabilities. In a population of 1000 zygotes, the actual survival values can predict expected surviving numbers, whereas the relative values describe their composition. In exam problems it is important to identify which type of fitness has been supplied.

The effect of dominance on selection is best understood by asking which genotypes expose an allele to a selective disadvantage. If a is a deleterious recessive and q is very small, most a copies occur in heterozygotes. The number of a copies in Aa is proportional to 2pq, whereas the number in aa is proportional to 2q². The fraction in aa is q, because the total number of a copies per individual in the population is 2q. At q = 0.01, only about one percent of a copies occur in homozygotes at random-mating proportions. Selection that affects only aa individuals therefore removes relatively few copies when the allele is rare. Increased homozygosity can change the number of exposed copies even without an immediate change in q.

This reasoning does not mean that dominance itself changes allele frequencies. Dominance is a description of the relationship between genotype and phenotype. Its evolutionary consequences depend on whether the phenotype influences reproductive contribution under the relevant conditions. A recessive phenotype can be advantageous or disadvantageous; a dominant allele can be neutral. The words dominant and common refer to different properties and should never be treated as synonyms.

Heterozygote advantage provides an example of selection that can maintain variation. If w_AA and w_aa are both less than w_Aa, the relative contribution of each allele depends on how frequently it is found in the high-fitness heterozygous state. When an allele is rare, a larger fraction of its copies are found in heterozygotes; under suitable conditions this can favor its increase. When common, more of its copies occur in the lower-fitness homozygote. Thus selection need not lead monotonically to the fixation of one allele. The important result here is conceptual: a departure from neutrality can preserve allele diversity rather than only remove it. The formal dynamics and wider evolutionary examples are developed in Unit 9.

Mutation can continually replenish a deleterious allele removed by selection. A simple illustration uses rare recessive alleles introduced at rate u from the common allele, with a selection coefficient s against aa homozygotes, no reverse mutation, random mating and a large deterministic population. When q is small, mutation adds approximately u to the frequency of a per generation, while selection removes approximately sq². Equating these approximate changes gives q* ≈ √(u/s). With u = 10⁻⁵ and s = 0.1, q* is approximately 0.01. The equilibrium is not the statement that an individual is immune to mutation or selection; it is a balance of expected frequency changes at the population level. The approximation is inappropriate when q is not small or when heterozygotes have appreciable selective disadvantages.

In an ideal diploid population under drift alone, the probability that a neutral allele will ultimately fix is its initial frequency. A rare neutral allele is therefore much more likely to be lost than to reach fixation, even though the expected change in allele frequency over a single generation is zero. There is no contradiction. Replicate populations in which the allele is lost are balanced in the ensemble average by the smaller number in which its frequency rises substantially. An expectation across replicates is not a prediction of a guaranteed trajectory within any one replicate.

The effect on heterozygosity can be derived by sampling two gene copies in the offspring generation of an ideal population. They have probability 1/(2N) of tracing to the same parental copy, in which case they cannot differ in state if mutation is absent. With the remaining probability they trace to different copies, which can differ according to the previous generation's expected diversity. In the standard model the expected heterozygosity therefore declines as E[H_t] = H_0(1 − 1/(2N))^t. For N = 20, the factor is 0.975 per generation, giving an expected fraction of approximately 0.776 of the original heterozygosity after ten generations. A larger population retains more diversity over the same number of generations, all else being equal.

A real population that has undergone a bottleneck may have recovered its number of individuals before it recovers its genetic variation. Population growth increases the number of descendants, but those descendants inherit the surviving alleles. Without mutation or immigration, a lost allele does not reappear merely because the population grows. This is why present census size and historical effective size are different concepts. Conversely, an immigrant carrying a previously absent allele may restore a component of genetic variation only if that allele enters the reproductive gene pool and persists.

Different evolutionary forces can act simultaneously, and their signatures can overlap. An isolated population might be founded by a few individuals, then experience selection in a new habitat, occasional migration and a shift toward self-fertilization. A heterozygote deficit, a change in allele frequency and high LD could all occur together. The correct reasoning does not force every observation into one category. Instead, separate the predictions that follow from each process, examine which variables are observed and identify additional measurements that would discriminate between candidate explanations.

Testing predictions using replicated populations

Population-genetic models are valuable partly because their predictions can be tested in experiments where reproductive history is observed rather than reconstructed from a single sample. A classical example is Peter Buri's 1956 study of eye-colour variants in laboratory populations of Drosophila melanogaster. The experiment tracked two allelic states at the brown locus in small replicate populations started at equal allele frequencies. One series comprised 107 populations, each maintained with 16 breeding flies, and allele frequencies were followed for up to 19 generations. The work is especially instructive because identical initial frequencies did not lead to identical outcomes among replicate populations. The published study and later analyses provide evidence for the importance of stochastic transmission in finite populations.

The fundamental experimental comparison is among replicate histories. If the initial allele frequency is one-half, a deterministic neutral model with an infinite gene pool predicts no change. A finite sampling model predicts a distribution of possible changes. Some populations move above one-half, some below, and eventually some reach boundaries at which one allele has disappeared. To examine that prediction, a researcher can plot a histogram of allele counts for all replicate populations at each generation. At the start all observations occupy the same category. As generations pass the distribution spreads. This broadening is different from a uniform directional shift and helps distinguish stochastic divergence from a common deterministic trend.

The interpretation is not that all deviations in a laboratory experiment must be exclusively due to random genetic drift. Variants may differ in fitness under particular culture conditions, and the actual number of parents contributing offspring may depart from an ideal sampling process. Buri's study considered selective differences as well as drift; subsequent treatments have used the observations to illustrate why effective population size can differ from the number of breeders. A comparison of observed and ideal distributions is more informative than an assumption that the ideal parameters must exactly match the experiment. Replication, known starting frequencies and carefully documented population sizes are the central strengths of this design.

A simple numerical example can be analyzed without claiming that its values are Buri's results. Consider a diploid population with four reproducing individuals and an allele frequency p = 0.5. There are eight gene copies in the next ideal generation. The probability that exactly four of them carry A is C(8,4)(0.5)^8 = 70/256 ≈ 0.273. The probabilities that the next count is zero or eight are each 1/256. The remaining probability is distributed among intermediate counts. Even when the current allele frequency is exactly one-half, the next generation has only about a 27% chance of matching that frequency exactly. A population with a very large number of gene copies still exhibits sampling variation, but the relative magnitude is smaller.

The probability distribution also illustrates the difference between sampling with replacement in the ideal model and a literal census of all alleles. In the idealized Wright–Fisher process, each offspring gene copy is modeled as an independent draw from the preceding gene pool, so some parental copies may be represented repeatedly while others have no descendants. The count of A among the new copies is not constrained to equal the previous count. A physical sampling procedure that takes each parental gene copy exactly once without replacement would eliminate this mechanism of drift, making it a poor analogy for unequal reproductive transmission. The replacement assumption is a mathematical description of random reproductive sampling, not a claim that chromosomes are physically drawn from a container.

For a second hypothetical comparison, consider replicate populations of 25 and 400 breeding diploids, both starting at p = 0.4. The one-generation variances are 0.24/50 = 0.0048 and 0.24/800 = 0.0003, respectively. The variance is sixteen times larger in the small populations; the standard deviation is four times larger, approximately 0.0693 versus 0.0173. This distinction between the ratios of variances and standard deviations is a common source of examination errors. A plot of frequency change across replicate populations should be more spread out for the smaller groups, but it need not show a consistent preference for increasing or decreasing A.

To distinguish drift from selection in a new experiment, the starting allele frequencies can be matched across replicate groups, and environmental conditions and breeding schedules can be controlled. A comparison of different breeding sizes tests the predicted size dependence of stochastic variation. Independent fitness measurements assess whether genotypes have different survival or reproductive contributions. If most replicate groups shift in the same direction and that shift corresponds to a measured fitness difference, the evidence for selection is stronger. If trajectories diverge in both directions and their spread follows population size, the evidence for drift is stronger. If both patterns occur, a combined model may be needed.

An experiment in which a single small population loses allele A does not establish that A is harmful. A neutral allele can disappear entirely by chance, and a favorable allele can also be lost, particularly when rare. A controlled comparison with multiple replicate populations provides a basis for estimating how often such outcomes occur. Conversely, an allele that rises in one replicate cannot be described as advantageous merely because it increased. Fitness is a property of reproductive contribution under specified conditions, while an observed change is a realized historical outcome. The distinction between cause and realization is one of the most important concepts in finite-population genetics.

A practical application of the same reasoning occurs when maintaining a collection of genetic lines or a conservation breeding population. If a small number of individuals supply most of the offspring, alleles present in other individuals may be lost despite a large visible collection. The relevant problem is the representation of lineages in the next generation. Managing genetic diversity therefore requires information about breeding contributions and relatedness, not just the total number of living individuals. These examples illustrate the value of the population-genetic model without implying that a single simple formula is sufficient for real conservation decisions.

Reasoning from genetic observations

The central task of population-genetic analysis is to connect observations with predictions that can distinguish mechanisms. A single snapshot of genotype frequencies gives a description of current structure but limited information about its history. To test a mating-system explanation, collect information about breeding partners or offspring from defined crosses. To test subdivision, retain group identity and compare within-group results with pooled data. To test viability selection, compare genotype frequencies before and after a defined developmental interval, controlling for movement and assay error. To investigate drift, compare temporal variation and independent replicate populations while accounting for the number of reproducing individuals.

An illustrative experiment begins with several populations of a short-generation organism at the same allele frequency. Some replicate populations are maintained at a small breeding size and others at a large breeding size, with environmental conditions held as similar as possible. If the small replicates display a wider distribution of frequency changes, including changes in opposite directions, the result supports the predicted size dependence of drift. One replicate alone could change strongly because of an unrecognized selective difference; replication provides information about the distribution of outcomes rather than merely its average.

In a different design, organisms are genotyped at the zygote stage and again after a developmental transition. Suppose the initial genotypes are near the expected Hardy–Weinberg proportions but one homozygote becomes rarer among survivors. This pattern is consistent with genotype-dependent viability. A reasonable alternative explanation is that the genotyping procedure behaves differently on samples from the two stages. Independent genotyping controls and documented survival of known genotypes strengthen the inference. The experiment should distinguish changes in relative genotype frequencies from absolute numbers of survivors.

In multilocus studies, compare both single-locus genotype proportions and phased haplotype frequencies. A population may be close to Hardy–Weinberg proportions at each locus and nevertheless have substantial D. Conversely, a single-locus heterozygote deficit does not directly quantify the correlation between loci. The equations used for these two problems require different data. A student who first identifies the observational unit—individual, gene copy, haplotype or deme—can avoid many common errors before beginning any arithmetic.

The next chapter considers traits affected by several genes and environmental variables. Population structure matters for such traits because the frequencies and correlations of contributing alleles vary among groups. However, differences between group phenotype averages do not prove a genetic cause; environmental variation and differences in sampling can produce similar patterns. The tools developed here are a foundation for examining those alternatives.

Integrated population data: competing explanations

The following illustrative investigation combines the concepts introduced so far. Consider a diploid insect species sampled in three sites. The goal is to decide whether a deficit of heterozygotes at a marker reflects inbreeding within sites or differences in allele frequencies among sites. Assume initially that the marker has two reliably detectable alleles, that the samples contain unrelated adults of comparable developmental stage, and that the individuals are independent observations within each site. These assumptions are hypotheses to be checked, not findings established by the numbers. Site X contains 100 individuals with 64 AA, 32 Aa and 4 aa. Site Y contains 100 individuals with 25 AA, 50 Aa and 25 aa. Site Z contains 100 individuals with 4 AA, 32 Aa and 64 aa.

Calculate the frequencies separately before pooling. The A frequencies are 0.80, 0.50 and 0.20. In each site, the observed genotype counts exactly match the expected counts given its own frequency. The mean within-site heterozygosity is (0.32 + 0.50 + 0.32)/3 = 0.38. Across sites there are 93 AA, 114 Aa and 93 aa, so the pooled A count is 300 of 600 allele copies. The pooled p is 0.50, and the expected genotype counts under a single randomly mating population are 75, 150 and 75. The pooled Pearson statistic is 324/75 + 1296/150 + 324/75 = 17.28. A naive pooled analysis would reject its model strongly, even though none of the individual site tables departs from its local expectation.

The evidence favors subdivision as an explanation for the pooled discrepancy because the difference is exactly accounted for by site-specific allele frequencies in this constructed example. H_T is 0.50 and H_S is 0.38, giving F_ST = 0.24. Because H_I also equals 0.38, F_IS is zero. This does not prove that every organism is unrelated or that absolutely no selection occurs. It establishes that no extra within-site heterozygote deficit is needed to explain the particular table. A claim of population subdivision remains dependent on whether sites are meaningful reproductive units; in real data it is possible that sites represent transient mixtures rather than breeding groups.

Now suppose a second marker has an allele B with frequencies 0.70, 0.50 and 0.30 in X, Y and Z, and that within each site A and B are in linkage equilibrium. The pooled AB frequency is the mean of 0.8 × 0.7, 0.5 × 0.5 and 0.2 × 0.3, which is (0.56 + 0.25 + 0.06)/3 = 0.29. Both pooled allele frequencies are 0.5. Thus D = 0.04 and r² = 0.0016/0.0625 = 0.0256. The same sampling structure that produced a single-locus heterozygote deficit also produced a multilocus association, but the two statistics are not interchangeable. The single-locus deficit depended on the variance of the A frequency; the mixture-induced LD depended on the covariance of the frequencies at A and B across sites.

Suppose a researcher then establishes a single breeding population from the three sources in equal proportions. For the ideal exercise assume random union of gametes in the new population and no other frequency-changing forces. At the first locus, the expected genotype proportions in the next zygotes are 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25. The pooled parental table no longer describes the zygote table because mating is now between the pooled sources. At the two-marker level, however, historical haplotype association is not erased merely by combining gametes. If the markers have c = 0.10, the gametic LD in the subsequent generation is predicted to fall from 0.04 to 0.036 under the recombination-only model. Thus single-locus proportions and multilocus associations respond on different time scales.

Consider an alternative dataset in which the local allele frequencies remain exactly the same but each site has a heterozygote deficit of 20% relative to its local expected value. The new H_I is 0.8 × 0.38 = 0.304. H_S and H_T are unchanged. Now F_IS = 0.20, F_ST = 0.24 and F_IT = 1 − 0.304/0.50 = 0.392. Check the relationship: (1 − 0.20)(1 − 0.24) = 0.8 × 0.76 = 0.608 = 1 − 0.392. The overall deficit cannot be described as exclusively subdivision because a within-site component remains. Nor should the two component coefficients simply be added: 0.20 + 0.24 = 0.44, which does not equal the hierarchical coefficient. Their complements multiply.

The next scientific question is what explains the local deficit. A mating-system hypothesis predicts changes in how partners are related or matched. A viability hypothesis predicts differences between early and later stages. An assay hypothesis predicts inconsistent calls when the same DNA is genotyped using independent methods. A finer population-structure hypothesis predicts that subdivision persists inside the currently defined sites. The best investigation is designed around these distinct predictions. The numerical F_IS estimate summarizes a pattern requiring explanation; it does not choose among hypotheses without additional evidence.

Imagine that in a follow-up study researchers observe random-mating proportions in embryos but the aa genotype is selectively depleted in adults. This gives evidence for a stage-dependent process rather than a simple mating-system explanation. However, if the embryo and adult samples come from different seasons or geographic locations, the comparison may confound survival with population composition. Matching the cohorts and sampling sites is therefore essential. Marking known individuals or observing well-defined family cohorts can help distinguish actual survival differences from changes in the composition of independent samples. The methodological principle is to align the measurements with the causal model.

Finally, consider a different follow-up in which allele frequencies shift in several small isolated populations, with changes in both directions among replicates and greater variability than in larger populations. This is the prediction of a finite-sampling model. It does not imply that the original subdivision was itself caused only by drift; selection and migration may have contributed to the starting differences. A sound conclusion states the specific process supported by the new data and the assumptions under which that support is valid. The strongest examination responses identify what additional result would weaken their chosen interpretation as well as what would support it.

Genetic structure in breeding, conservation and quantitative-trait studies

Genetic structure is not merely a statistical complication. It changes the kinds of biological questions that can be answered from a sample. In plant breeding, a self-fertilizing line may become genetically uniform at many loci through repeated selfing. Such uniformity can be useful when comparing line performance under controlled conditions because genetic segregation within a highly homozygous line is reduced. However, uniformity within a line is not equivalent to uniformity across all lines. Different lines may fix different alleles, and a collection of lines can retain considerable between-line diversity even if heterozygosity within each is low. This is another context in which within-group and pooled measures answer different questions.

A breeder crossing two homozygous lines may obtain heterozygous offspring at loci where the lines differ. For example, a cross between AA BB and aa bb produces Aa Bb offspring in coupling phase. Each offspring carries one chromosome of each parental type at the two markers. At the marker level, the initial cross is not a random-mating sample from a population with the pooled allele frequencies; it has been designed to produce heterozygotes. A subsequent segregating generation can contain new recombinant combinations. The same principles of Mendelian segregation and recombination underlie the construction of mapping populations discussed in 8.C.04 and later marker-trait analysis in 8.E.05.

In a genetic resource collection, the objective may be to preserve multiple alleles rather than maximize heterozygosity within every individual. A rare allele confined to a particular line can be important even if that line is homozygous. If the collection is managed by combining all seed from many lines without recording their reproductive output, prolific lines may contribute disproportionately and rare lineages may disappear. A strategy for preserving representation must distinguish the number of individuals sampled, the distribution of reproductive contributions and the diversity of allelic states. The relevant measures are complementary rather than interchangeable.

For conservation biology, habitat fragmentation can restrict reproduction between groups and increase drift in local populations. A decrease in genetic exchange may permit allele frequencies to diverge, while small effective population size can increase the probability of losing alleles. The resulting genetic structure does not necessarily indicate a single process: physical barriers, mating behavior, historical separation and selection in distinct environments can all contribute. In a field study it is useful to compare multiple genetic markers, information about dispersal and the distribution of habitat patches. Genetic data provide evidence about realized transmission over generations, while direct observations of movement reveal opportunities for gene exchange. An animal can move between sites without successfully reproducing there, so the two sources of evidence need not agree exactly.

The conservation implication of a small effective population is a risk of stochastic loss of diversity, not a guarantee that every allele will be lost or every individual will be less fit. Some populations naturally exhibit selfing, strong subdivision or low heterozygosity, and the effects depend on their histories and life cycles. A single statistic should not be used as an automatic management decision. In an examination, identify what process the statistic measures, explain its biological consequence under specified assumptions, and avoid conclusions that require information not provided.

Population structure is especially important when a quantitative trait is measured across several groups. If different groups have different allele frequencies, a pooled genetic variance may include differences between group means as well as genetic differences among individuals within each group. Environmental conditions may differ simultaneously. For example, plants from two geographic regions may differ in average leaf size because of growing conditions, genetic differences or both. A marker allele associated with region could appear associated with leaf size in the pooled records even if the marker has no functional relationship to leaf development. An experiment growing genotypes from both regions under common conditions can help investigate environmental versus inherited influences, although maternal effects and other inherited differences may still require controls.

This connection explains why genetic structure precedes the study of quantitative traits in the curriculum. Quantitative genetics requires a clearly defined population, its allele frequencies, the distribution of genotypes and the environment in which phenotypes are measured. A population-level estimate of genetic contribution to trait variation does not automatically apply to another population with different frequencies and environmental conditions. The details of variance partition and heritability belong in 8.E.04; the essential prerequisite here is an understanding of how the structure of the sampled population shapes the observed distribution of genotypes and alleles.

For the same reason, molecular mapping uses associations as evidence requiring interpretation. A marker may correlate with a causal locus because their alleles occur together on chromosomes. If recombination breaks the association in a different population, the same marker may become a weaker predictor. If a marker is correlated with population membership rather than a causal variant, an apparent association with phenotype may disappear after accounting for the structure. A controlled genetic cross, replication in an independent sample and appropriate comparisons can help separate these possibilities. The core principle is that marker effects, correlations and causal mechanisms are distinct, even though they can be investigated together.

The progression from this chapter to polygenic inheritance therefore rests on a precise understanding of variation. We have considered how the same allele frequencies can be distributed among genotypes in different ways, how different subpopulations can produce misleading patterns when combined, and how alleles at separate loci can be correlated even without a direct functional interaction. These distinctions prepare the student to reason about the cumulative effects of multiple loci without assuming that all genetic contributions are statistically independent. The following node develops the connection between discrete Mendelian states and continuous phenotype distributions.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Hardy–Weinberg departure proves allele-frequency change
Genotype reshuffling may preserve allele counts. Recalculate p from the genotype data and seek temporal information. Students often treat the two independent Hardy–Weinberg predictions as a single claim. A problem may give the same p in two samples with different genotype counts.
Random mating guarantees no evolution
Random union can restore zygote proportions even when selection or migration has changed the allele pool. Separate genotype and allele equilibrium. Random pairing is easy to confuse with all the equilibrium assumptions combined. A problem may compare pre-selection adults with newly formed zygotes.
A heterozygote deficit proves mating among relatives
Population pooling, allele dropout and selection may yield the same pattern. Inspect within-group data and controls. The inbreeding model predicts a deficit, tempting students to reverse the inference. A question can present locally equilibrium demes whose pooled data violate equilibrium.
Identity by state equals identity by descent
Matching allelic states need not trace to a common ancestral copy in the reference genealogy. The notation AA hides the histories of the two copies. A pedigree question may deliberately provide a common allelic state without a shared ancestor in the stated reference.
F is the observed homozygote fraction
F describes excess homozygosity relative to a baseline or a pedigree probability, not total homozygote frequency. F and homozygosity both increase in the simple model but have different baselines. An exam may give a high homozygote count at a very rare allele.
Negative F is a negative ancestry probability
A negative fitted deficit index indicates excess heterozygosity; a pedigree probability cannot be negative. Both ancestry probabilities and deficit estimates are often called inbreeding coefficients. An exam may show H_O greater than H_E and ask whether the negative estimate is possible.
Dominant alleles are necessarily more common
Dominance describes phenotype expression, not population frequency or relative fitness. Textbook crosses often use uppercase symbols for dominant alleles, which does not encode prevalence. A question may specify a dominant allele at frequency 0.01.
Selfing always changes p immediately
Under pure selfing and equal transmission, heterozygosity declines while expected allele frequencies remain stable. The disappearance of heterozygotes can be mistaken for disappearance of an allele. Calculate allele counts across selfed progeny rather than counting only heterozygotes.
Equal-weight averaging is always correct
Weights must correspond to the actual sampling frame or target population. Equal sample sizes are common in teaching examples and can conceal the weighting issue. A problem may provide one much larger deme.
High F_ST means that many individuals are inbred
F_ST compares expected heterozygosity across levels of subdivision; F_IS addresses within-group deficits. The shared letter F encourages the mistaken interpretation of all F-statistics as individual relatedness. An exam may show F_IS = 0 but F_ST above zero.
Physical linkage and LD are interchangeable
Physical linkage describes transmission; LD describes a nonrandom statistical association in a specified population. Both ideas concern alleles at multiple loci, making their terminology similar. An exam may present D above zero at markers on different chromosomes.
r² can be negative
It is a squared correlation. D can be negative and indicates phase-specific excess under chosen labels. The notation D and r² may be interchanged carelessly. An exam can reverse the allele labels and ask which measure changes.
r² is zero when one allele is fixed
The variance in the denominator is zero; the statistic is undefined. Absence of variation may sound like absence of association, but a correlation needs nonzero variance. Check the denominator before attempting division.
Unlinked loci lose all LD in one generation
With c = 0.5, D halves per generation in the recombination-only model. Independent assortment is often equated with immediate population-level independence. An exam may start with admixture LD at unlinked markers.
Migration always raises genetic diversity
The outcome depends on allele frequencies, reproductive contribution and the diversity measure. Immigration is often taught as a source of variation but its effect depends on what migrants carry. A problem may introduce common resident alleles only.
Drift favors beneficial alleles
Drift is stochastic sampling. Selection is the systematic component related to relative reproductive contribution. An allele's increase can tempt an adaptive explanation even when no fitness difference was measured. Compare replicate directions and measured fitness.
Census size and effective size are identical
Effective size matches a specified process in an ideal population; reproductive skew and history can alter it. Census counts are easier to observe than reproductive contribution. A question may specify a highly skewed breeding sex ratio.
A marker associated with a trait must cause it
Population structure or LD with another variant may explain the association; validation is required. Marker association and gene function are both used in mapping discussions. A question can introduce a confounding environmental difference between sampled groups.

Original examination practice

1. Part B

What does pedigree-defined F measure?

  1. Probability that the two copies are identical by descent relative to a reference population
  2. Frequency of the common allele
  3. Total observed homozygote fraction
  4. Proportion of alleles undergoing mutation
Answer and explanation

A. Probability of identity by descent at the locus relative to the specified base population. Identical allelic states are not sufficient to establish identity by descent. Total homozygosity includes copies that match in state without shared descent; allele and mutation frequencies describe different quantities.

2. Part B

Does pure inbreeding necessarily change allele frequencies?

  1. Yes, it always increases the common allele
  2. No, in the idealized equal-contribution model it changes genotype proportions but preserves allele frequencies
  3. Yes, it always eliminates recessive alleles
  4. Only if p = q
Answer and explanation

B. No. In the idealized model p² + Fpq + pq(1 − F) = p. Selection acting subsequently can change frequencies. The algebra holds for any valid p and q. Inbreeding can expose recessive alleles to subsequent selection, but that is a separate process.

3. Part C

Two equally sampled demes have p = 0.9 and 0.1, each in equilibrium. Calculate H_S, H_T and F_ST.

Answer and explanation

H_S = 0.18, H_T = 0.50, F_ST = 0.64. The deficit reflects subdivision despite local random mating.

4. Part C

AB = 0.30, Ab = 0.20, aB = 0.10 and ab = 0.40. Find D and r².

Answer and explanation

p_A = 0.50, p_B = 0.40, D = 0.10, r² = 0.01/(0.25 × 0.24) = 1/6.

5. Part C

If D = −0.12 and c = 0.25, predict D after two generations under recombination alone.

Answer and explanation

D₂ = −0.12 × 0.75² = −0.0675. Its magnitude falls; its sign is unchanged.

6. Part C

A population is in equilibrium at the zygote stage, but aa adults are rarer than predicted. What comparison tests viability selection?

Answer and explanation

Measure survival of known genotypes from the same cohort under common conditions, accounting for migration and genotyping errors. Adult frequencies alone do not establish selection.

7. Part B

Which observation defines a heterozygote deficit?

  1. Observed AA exceeds p² alone
  2. A frequency is less than 0.5
  3. Observed heterozygosity is less than the expectation 2pq
  4. The locus is physically linked to another locus
Answer and explanation

C. H_O is lower than 2pq for the specified reference population; the direction of the deficit is not a diagnosis of its cause. A heterozygote deficit is defined against the reference expectation, not a threshold on p or the physical map.

8. Part B

Which mating preference describes positive assortment?

  1. All partners are genealogically related
  2. Individuals preferentially mate with dissimilar phenotypes
  3. Partners are chosen independently
  4. Similar individuals pair more often than random pairing predicts
Answer and explanation

D. Similar individuals pair more often than random pairing predicts. Shared ancestry is not required. Assortment is about similarity of chosen partners, while genealogical inbreeding is about shared ancestry.

9. Part B

What does negative assortment tend to do at loci determining the preference?

  1. It can produce a heterozygote excess at relevant loci
  2. It fixes every allele in one generation
  3. It must reduce heterozygosity at all loci
  4. It has the same definition as drift
Answer and explanation

A. It can increase heterozygosity; the effect depends on the genotype–phenotype mapping and mating system. The outcome depends on how the mating phenotype relates to genotype; no universal fixation follows.

10. Part B

What is the genotype frequency of Aa under the inbreeding model?

  1. 2pq + Fpq
  2. 2pq(1 − F)
  3. p² + Fpq
  4. q² + Fpq
Answer and explanation

B. 2pq(1 − F); the IBD component contributes only homozygotes. The two other genotype formulas describe the homozygotes. An IBD pair cannot contribute a heterozygote in the model.

11. Part B

What does the Wahlund effect compare?

  1. Physical and genetic distances
  2. Observed and expected mutation rates
  3. Pooled-frequency heterozygosity and within-group heterozygosity
  4. Census size and body size
Answer and explanation

C. Observed or averaged within-group heterozygosity with the random-mating expectation from the pooled allele frequency. The deficit arises when differing allele-frequency groups are pooled; it is not a recombination or mutation measure.

12. Part B

Which term measures the sign of haplotype association?

  1. N_e
  2. F_ST
  3. r²
  4. D
Answer and explanation

D. D; r² loses direction by squaring the covariance. Only D among these measures retains a signed association between the specified pair of alleles; r² is squared.

13. Part B

When is r² undefined?

  1. When either locus is monomorphic
  2. Whenever D is zero
  3. Whenever the markers are unlinked
  4. When both allele frequencies are 0.5
Answer and explanation

A. When either locus is monomorphic, since its indicator variance is zero. When both loci are polymorphic, D = 0 gives r² = 0. Monomorphism instead makes its variance denominator zero.

14. Part B

What effect does recombination alone have on marginal allele frequencies?

  1. It always increases p_A
  2. It conserves marginal frequencies in the stated model
  3. It halves both allele frequencies
  4. It changes the identity of alleles by mutation
Answer and explanation

B. It rearranges haplotypes but does not change allele counts in the deterministic model. Recombination rearranges combinations of existing alleles, not their marginal counts.

15. Part B

Which force introduces new allelic states?

  1. Migration into an already polymorphic population
  2. Nonrandom pairing
  3. Mutation
  4. Recombination alone
Answer and explanation

C. Mutation. Migration can introduce an existing allele into a particular local population. Migration moves existing alleles between populations; recombination rearranges alleles at different loci.

16. Part B

What distinguishes immigration from gene flow?

  1. The two terms are identical
  2. Only migration can be observed
  3. Gene flow requires physical movement but not reproduction
  4. Gene flow requires contribution to the reproductive gene pool
Answer and explanation

D. Gene flow requires genetic contribution to the recipient reproductive pool; mere movement is insufficient. An organism can move and fail to reproduce; its arrival alone need not change inherited allele frequencies.

17. Part B

What is effective population size?

  1. Size of an ideal population with the same rate of the specified genetic process
  2. Always the number of living organisms
  3. Always the number of heterozygotes
  4. Number of distinct allelic states
Answer and explanation

A. The size of an ideal population matching a specified rate of a genetic process in the observed population. The relevant ideal size depends on the process being matched, such as drift or inbreeding.

18. Part B

Which process has a zero expected directional allele-frequency change under neutrality?

  1. Selection against aa
  2. Neutral genetic drift
  3. Mutation with only A-to-a transitions
  4. Migration from a source with lower p
Answer and explanation

B. Drift alone. Individual trajectories can still change in either direction. The alternatives can have directional expectations. Neutral drift has zero expected change but individual trajectories vary.

19. Part B

Does selection necessarily reduce variation?

  1. Yes, selection always eliminates all variants
  2. Yes, except for genes on sex chromosomes
  3. No, heterozygote advantage can maintain variation
  4. No, because fitness is unrelated to reproduction
Answer and explanation

C. No. Heterozygote advantage or other balancing processes can maintain allelic variation. Selection concerns relative reproductive contribution, and under some models different alleles are maintained.

20. Part B

How many degrees of freedom are usually used for a large-sample HWE test for two autosomal alleles when p is estimated?

  1. Four
  2. Three
  3. Two
  4. One
Answer and explanation

D. One: three categories minus one total constraint minus one estimated parameter. There are three genotype categories; fixing the total and estimating one allele-frequency parameter removes two degrees of freedom.

21. Part B

What is meant by haplotype phase?

  1. Which alleles at two or more loci are carried on the same chromosome
  2. The developmental age of a sampled individual
  3. The observed homozygote proportion
  4. The direction of DNA transcription
Answer and explanation

A. Which alleles at separate loci occur together on the same chromosome. The same unphased genotype can correspond to coupling or repulsion arrangements.

22. Part B

What is the maximum recombination fraction in the usual two-locus model?

  1. 1.0
  2. 0.5
  3. 0.25
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

B. One-half; independent assortment yields a recombinant fraction of one-half. Under the conventional two-locus model, independent assortment gives a recombinant fraction of one-half.

23. Part B

What changes when allele labels at one locus are swapped?

  1. D and r² both reverse sign
  2. Neither changes
  3. D changes sign, r² is unchanged
  4. r² becomes undefined for every marker pair
Answer and explanation

C. D changes sign; r² remains the same. Swapping one allele label reverses the signed indicator covariance, but the squared correlation is invariant.

24. Part B

Does an allele necessarily reappear when census size recovers after a bottleneck?

  1. Yes, always
  2. Yes, if the lost allele was dominant
  3. Yes, if the new population is large enough
  4. No, growth alone cannot regenerate lost alleles
Answer and explanation

D. No. Growth alone cannot regenerate a lost allelic state. Descendants inherit the surviving copies; reintroduction requires a process such as mutation or reproductive migration.

25. Part C

With p = 0.4 and F = 0.25, calculate AA, Aa and aa.

Answer and explanation

pq = 0.24 and Fpq = 0.06. The frequencies are 0.22, 0.36 and 0.42; p = 0.22 + 0.18 = 0.40.

26. Part C

A population has H_O = 0.28 and H_E = 0.40. Calculate the deficit index.

Answer and explanation

1 − 0.28/0.40 = 0.30. It is not by itself proof of a pedigree inbreeding probability.

27. Part C

Starting with H = 0.64, predict heterozygosity after four generations of complete selfing.

Answer and explanation

0.64 × (1/2)^4 = 0.04 under equal transmission and no other forces.

28. Part C

An ideal population has a selfing rate of 0.6. Find equilibrium F.

Answer and explanation

0.6/(2 − 0.6) = 3/7 ≈ 0.429; outcrosses must satisfy the independent reference assumption.

29. Part C

Two populations with p = 0.1 and p = 0.9 contribute 25% and 75%. Compute pooled p and the Wahlund deficit.

Answer and explanation

p = 0.70; H_T = 0.42; H_S = 0.18; deficit = 0.24.

30. Part C

H_I = 0.24, H_S = 0.30 and H_T = 0.50. Find F_IS, F_ST and F_IT.

Answer and explanation

F_IS = 0.2, F_ST = 0.4, F_IT = 0.52; 0.8 × 0.6 = 0.48 = 1 − F_IT.

31. Part C

AB = 0.1, Ab = 0.4, aB = 0.4 and ab = 0.1. Find D and r².

Answer and explanation

p_A = p_B = 0.5; D = −0.15; r² = 0.0225/0.0625 = 0.36. Repulsion combinations are overrepresented.

32. Part C

If p_A = 0.9 and p_B = 0.1, what is maximum positive D?

Answer and explanation

min(0.9 × 0.9, 0.1 × 0.1) = 0.01; the rarer compatible haplotype limits the association.

33. Part C

Initial r² = 0.64 and c = 0.5. Predict r² after two generations of recombination alone.

Answer and explanation

0.64 × 0.5⁴ = 0.04 when marginal frequencies are constant.

34. Part C

Two equally weighted sources have p_A = 0.9, p_B = 0.2 and p_A = 0.1, p_B = 0.8. Both have D = 0. Find pooled D.

Answer and explanation

AB = (0.18 + 0.08)/2 = 0.13; pooled product = 0.25; D = −0.12.

35. Part C

The resident A frequency is 0.8, migrant A frequency 0.2 and m = 0.25. Find p′.

Answer and explanation

0.75 × 0.8 + 0.25 × 0.2 = 0.65.

36. Part C

Two equal demes have p = 0.7 and 0.3, with reciprocal migration m = 0.2. Find the frequency difference after one generation.

Answer and explanation

(1 − 2m)(0.7 − 0.3) = 0.24. The new frequencies are 0.62 and 0.38.

37. Part C

p = 0.6, w_AA = 1, w_Aa = 0.5, w_aa = 1. Find the post-selection A frequency.

Answer and explanation

Mean fitness = 0.36 + 0.24 + 0.16 = 0.76. Numerator = 0.36 + 0.12 = 0.48. p′ ≈ 0.632.

38. Part C

At p = 0.25 and N = 50, find the variance of next-generation frequency under ideal drift.

Answer and explanation

pq/(2N) = 0.25 × 0.75/100 = 0.001875.

39. Part C

A population with 5 breeding males and 45 breeding females satisfies ideal sex-ratio assumptions. Compute N_e.

Answer and explanation

4 × 5 × 45/50 = 18; the census of breeders is 50.

40. Part C

For mutation A→a at u = 0.02 and a→A at v = 0.01, find mutation-only equilibrium p.

Answer and explanation

v/(u + v) = 1/3.

41. Part C

At p = 0.7, u = 0.02 and v = 0.01, calculate next p under mutation alone.

Answer and explanation

0.7 × 0.98 + 0.3 × 0.01 = 0.689.

42. Part C

A sampled genotype table has 36 AA, 48 Aa and 16 aa. Does it exactly match expected HWE counts?

Answer and explanation

Yes, p = 0.60 and n = 100, so 36, 48 and 16 are exactly expected; this does not demonstrate absence of every evolutionary force.

43. Part C

Genotype counts are 25 AA, 50 Aa and 25 aa but D between two markers is nonzero. Are the findings contradictory?

Answer and explanation

No. Single-locus genotype proportions and gametic multilocus associations are different properties.

44. Part C

A marker predicts a phenotype only when samples from two regions are pooled. Suggest a discriminating comparison.

Answer and explanation

Analyze within each region and compare environmental conditions; a pooled association can reflect structure rather than causality.

45. SET

Why does random mating after one episode of viability selection not necessarily restore the old allele frequency?

Answer and explanation

It restores zygote genotype proportions calculated at the new gametic p; it cannot recreate alleles eliminated by selection.

46. SET

Why might a large population exhibit a low effective size?

Answer and explanation

Only a subset may contribute disproportionately to reproduction, or past bottlenecks may dominate the process being measured.

47. SET

Why is a sample of close relatives unsuitable for assuming independent observations?

Answer and explanation

They share ancestry and may represent only a small number of parental gene copies, undermining the independent-sampling model.

48. SET

State a control for a suspected allele-dropout artifact.

Answer and explanation

Repeat genotyping with an independent assay and known heterozygous controls; compare call rates across sample groups.

49. SET

Why must expected rare-genotype counts be inspected before a chi-square test?

Answer and explanation

Small expected counts undermine the asymptotic approximation; an exact test may be more appropriate.

50. Part C

In a two-allele autosomal locus, p_m = 0.7 and p_f = 0.3 in independently sampled gamete pools. Which statement is correct?

A. The zygote heterozygote frequency is 0.42. B. It is 0.58, which exceeds the pooled p = 0.5 prediction. C. It is 0.5 exactly. D. Positive inbreeding is necessary.

Answer and explanation

B. Aa = 0.7 × 0.7 + 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.58. The gametes pair independently but do not come from identical allele distributions. A applies the wrong product, C assumes equal frequencies, and D confuses heterozygote excess with inbreeding.

51. Part C

Suppose p_A = 0.7, p_B = 0.4 and D = 0.15. Which conclusion follows?

A. The haplotypes are feasible. B. The aB frequency is −0.03, so the stated combination is impossible. C. r² must be 1. D. Recombination fraction must be 0.15.

Answer and explanation

B. q_Ap_B − D = 0.3 × 0.4 − 0.15 = −0.03. All haplotype frequencies must be nonnegative. Neither r² nor c is determined by D alone.

52. Part C

Two replicate experiments have ideal breeding sizes 25 and 400 and equal initial p. How do the one-generation standard deviations of allele-frequency change compare?

A. They are equal. B. The smaller group's standard deviation is 16 times greater. C. It is 4 times greater. D. It is one-quarter as large.

Answer and explanation

C. Variance is inversely proportional to N; the ratio is 400/25 = 16. The ratio of standard deviations is √16 = 4.

53. Part C

A marker has three alleles with frequencies 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2. If F = 0.1 in the ideal inbreeding model, what is total heterozygosity?

A. 0.62. B. 0.558. C. 0.38. D. 0.442.

Answer and explanation

B. H_E = 1 − (0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04) = 0.62. Multiplying by 0.9 gives 0.558; D is total homozygosity, A ignores F, and C is the equilibrium homozygosity.

54. SET

What information distinguishes biological admixture from a pooled collection of separately breeding individuals?

A. Identical census sizes. B. Evidence of reproductive contribution from the source populations to a common offspring gene pool. C. A positive D alone. D. A significant HWE test alone.

Answer and explanation

B. Admixture involves shared reproduction. Both nonzero D and equilibrium departures can occur in a collection that has never formed a common breeding population.
