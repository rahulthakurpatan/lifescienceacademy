From individual phenotypes to population variation

A breeder measures the grain mass of plants belonging to the same species and notices that the heaviest individuals are not always the parents of the heaviest offspring. A genetic explanation is plausible, because the plants carry different combinations of alleles. Yet a plant growing in a fertile patch can outperform a genetically superior plant growing in poor soil. The problem is not simply to decide whether genes or soil matter. Both matter. The problem is to estimate how much of the observed variation between individuals is associated with inherited differences, and which part of that inherited variation can predict the mean of a new generation.

Quantitative genetics joins Mendelian transmission with population statistics. The preceding study of polygenic inheritance explains how many segregating loci generate numerous possible genetic values. Environmental differences blur the phenotypic classes and often produce an approximately continuous distribution. In this chapter we treat such a distribution as a measurable outcome. Its position describes the average phenotype, while its spread describes differences among individuals. Neither feature alone identifies the genetic mechanism. A population of genetically identical plants can have a considerable spread of heights if water availability varies, whereas a population with segregating genes can appear very uniform under some measurement conditions.

The distinction was historically important because the resemblance of parents and offspring for continuous characters was sometimes interpreted as evidence that inheritance blended irreversibly. Mendel's particulate model appeared, at first, to describe a different kind of character. R. A. Fisher's 1918 analysis showed that numerous segregating Mendelian factors could account for continuous variability and correlations among relatives. This connection made it possible to discuss the inheritance of measured differences without treating a measured characteristic as a single indivisible hereditary unit. Later plant and animal breeding studies developed practical ways to estimate the variance that predicts response to selection.

Define the experimental unit before measuring any trait. If grain mass is the trait, a kernel is not necessarily an independent genetic sample from every other kernel on the same plant. Kernels from one maternal plant share developmental conditions and related genotypes. A study of plant-level yield may need to measure total mass per plant, then compare plants rather than counting all kernels as independent replicates. Similarly, adult body mass at a specified age and juvenile body mass are not interchangeable measurements. The definition of the trait, population, age and environment determines the interpretation of every later variance estimate.

The population mean is the expected value of the phenotype in the chosen population. A plant measuring 106 cm in a population with mean height 100 cm has a phenotypic deviation of +6 cm. Its deviation is not automatically a breeding value. Some of the excess could be the effect of unusually favorable soil, a non-additive genetic combination or ordinary measurement error. We must separate the directly observed measurement from the statistical expectations inferred from an experimental design.

A useful distinction is between predicting one offspring and predicting the average of many offspring. Mendelian segregation and recombination create differences even among offspring of the same parents. Quantitative-genetic predictions commonly concern expected averages. Individual outcomes need not equal those averages. A population-level prediction can therefore be useful even when the phenotype of a particular offspring remains uncertain.

Mean, variance and the phenotype model

Variance measures the average squared departure of observations from their mean. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling and gives larger deviations greater weight. For a population with N measured members, the variance of its complete measured distribution is the sum of squared deviations divided by N. For an independent random sample used to estimate the variance of an underlying population, the conventional unbiased sample variance uses n−1 in the denominator because the mean is estimated from that sample. The distinction is important when an examination specifies the complete population rather than a sample.

Consider the illustrative population of five plants with heights 96, 98, 100, 102 and 104 cm. Their mean is 100 cm. The five deviations are −4, −2, 0, 2 and 4 cm; the squared deviations sum to 40 cm². Treating these as the entire population gives a variance of 8 cm². Treating them as a random sample gives 10 cm². The standard deviation of the complete population is the square root of 8, about 2.83 cm. Variance has squared units, but standard deviation has the units of the original phenotype.

An elementary statistical model writes the measured phenotype P as μ + G + E, where μ is the population mean, G is the genotypic deviation, and E is the residual environmental deviation. These deviations are defined relative to the chosen reference distribution. A particular allele does not carry a fixed population mean inside it. Its statistical contribution is evaluated relative to the other alleles and environmental conditions represented in the population.

Suppose the genetic value of a plant is +4 cm relative to the mean and its environmental deviation is −3 cm. The observed deviation is +1 cm. This is a valid representation of the model, but a single measurement does not supply enough information to recover the two unknown deviations. Many different genetic and environmental combinations can yield exactly the same phenotype. Replication, relationships among individuals or controlled experimental comparisons are therefore needed to estimate components of variation.

The variance of a sum is obtained by expanding the square of the sum of centered variables. If P−μ = G + E, then V_P = V_G + V_E + 2 Cov(G,E). The covariance is the mean product of their centered deviations. When genotype and environment are assigned independently and the residual is defined to be uncorrelated with genotype, the covariance is zero in the model and the familiar V_P = V_G + V_E follows. The shortened expression is a conditional relationship, not a universal physical law.

Positive covariance can arise when genetically high-performing individuals systematically receive better conditions. Negative covariance can arise when poor-performing genotypes receive compensatory treatment. Suppose V_G = 16 cm², V_E = 9 cm² and Cov(G,E) = 3 cm². The total is 31 cm², not 25 cm². Covariance has the same units as the variance terms, and the sign matters. A randomized experiment aims to reduce systematic association between genotype and environmental placement, although residual associations can still arise through survival or incomplete sampling.

If genotype-specific responses to environments are of interest, a factorial model includes an interaction term, conventionally written G×E. In a balanced design with orthogonal effects, the corresponding variance components can be combined as V_P = V_G + V_E + V_G×E plus residual variance if the residual has not already been included in V_E. Notational consistency matters: one must not add the same environmental residual twice. A model with correlated terms requires the appropriate covariance contributions as well.

Phenotypic variance is descriptive; genetic and environmental components are inferred under a model. A variance estimate is not a microscopic count of genes, nor a direct chemical measurement of the expression of DNA. To evaluate an estimate, ask what changed among the individuals, what was held constant, how replicates were defined and which relationships the model used.

Sampling distributions and meaningful phenotype measurements

A sample is useful only when it represents the population about which the investigator wants to reason. Suppose a breeder measures the tallest ten plants in a field and calculates their variance. That variance describes the selected tail rather than the original population. Heritability calculated using a restricted range of phenotypes may not describe the unselected population, particularly if selection has changed relationships among breeding values and environmental deviations. Define the reference population before choosing the sample, and distinguish a selection experiment from a survey of variation.

The same issue arises with survivor bias. If a drought kills all plants with low growth rates before measurement, the surviving plants constitute a selected subset. Their measured variation does not necessarily represent the seedlings originally assigned to treatment. In a comparison of genetic variance across wet and dry conditions, survival may alter the genotype frequencies represented by the two samples. The drought treatment affects the measured phenotype and the composition of the sample. Recording mortality separately helps identify which conclusion is justified.

Mean and variance answer independent questions. Consider populations with complete phenotype sets of 9, 10 and 11 g versus 19, 20 and 21 g. The means differ by 10 g, but both distributions have variance 2/3 g². A constant environmental treatment that adds 10 g to every individual would produce exactly this change. Conversely, the sets 9, 10 and 11 g and 8, 10 and 12 g have equal means but the latter has four times the variance. These small examples make clear why one cannot infer the cause of differences in averages from variation alone.

Choice of units does not affect a ratio when numerator and denominator transform together. Converting a length from centimeters to millimeters multiplies every deviation by ten and every variance by one hundred. If V_A is 4 cm² and V_P is 10 cm², the ratio remains 0.4 after conversion to 400 and 1000 mm². A nonlinear transformation, such as a logarithm applied to positive body masses, can alter variance proportions because it changes differences between high-valued and low-valued individuals unequally. A heritability estimate must identify the scale on which the trait was analyzed.

Measurement error is a component of observed variability whenever it changes the readings independently of the underlying phenotype. Imagine repeated weighing of the same dry seed on an unstable balance. Differences between readings do not reflect genetic variation in seed mass. Calibration and repeat measurement can reduce this contribution. In contrast, a balance that consistently reads 1 g too high changes the mean but does not itself increase variance. The distinction between random and systematic error is important in genetic experiments. Systematic error may create spurious group differences if different instruments are used for different genotypes.

If residual environmental variation increases while additive variance stays constant, the correlation between the phenotype and the breeding value decreases. This creates a practical selection problem: individuals with high observed values are more likely to include individuals favored by temporary conditions. An investigator cannot fix that problem by merely sampling more individuals once each if the biological purpose is to estimate the breeding value of a particular individual. Replicated genotype measurements, family averages or appropriately designed progeny tests address a different source of uncertainty.

Quantitative-genetic data are frequently summarized as population parameters, but sample estimates remain uncertain. A regression coefficient estimated from four families may fit the points closely by chance and have a wide confidence interval. A variance component estimated from five lines is also unstable because the sampled genotypes constitute a small fraction of possible genotypes. Increasing independent genetic replicates improves the representation of genetic variation; increasing environmental replicates within each genotype improves the precision with which its mean is measured. These two improvements are not interchangeable.

An examination may present a table with several standard deviations and ask for the fraction of phenotypic variation explained genetically. Before subtracting or dividing, square the standard deviations if the model is defined on variances. For example, when total phenotypic standard deviation is 6 and environmental standard deviation is 4, the relevant variances are 36 and 16. Under independent components, V_G=20 and H²=20/36, about 0.556. Subtracting standard deviations would produce 2, a quantity that is not the genetic standard deviation under this model.

Additive, dominance and epistatic components

At one diploid locus, the phenotype associated with two copies of one allele can differ from that associated with two copies of the alternative allele. Let the genotypic values AA, Aa and aa be +a, d and −a relative to the midpoint of the homozygous values. The parameter a is half the difference between homozygotes. The parameter d measures the departure of the heterozygote from their midpoint. When d = 0, the three genotypic values lie on a straight line as allele dosage changes. When d = a, the heterozygote has the same genotypic value as AA, representing complete dominance of A on this measurement scale.

The genotypic value is not generally the breeding value. In a random-mating population, a parent's alleles are sampled into gametes and united with alleles from its mate. The statistical average effect of substituting an allele therefore depends on the alleles likely to be encountered in mating partners. This dependence is especially clear when the heterozygote does not lie at the midpoint. The average effect of substituting A for a is α = a + d(q−p), where p is the frequency of A and q = 1−p is the frequency of a under the conventional one-locus Hardy–Weinberg model.

To derive this expression, assign a linear predictor of genotypic value to the number X of A alleles carried by a diploid individual. Under Hardy–Weinberg frequencies, X has mean 2p and variance 2pq. The average effect α is the regression slope of genotypic value on X. The covariance calculated using the three genotypes is 2pq[a+d(q−p)], and division by 2pq gives the stated slope whenever both alleles segregate. The predicted deviation α(X−2p) is the additive genetic value for this locus. Because its mean is zero, its variance is α² times the variance of X. Thus V_A = 2pqα².

The residual genotypic deviation after the linear prediction is the dominance deviation at this locus. The Hardy–Weinberg model gives V_D = (2pqd)². This expression follows by subtracting the additive predictor and population mean from each genotype value and summing the squared residuals with their genotype frequencies. It is not simply d², because the prevalence of each genotype determines how much the departures contribute to population variation. In this model additive and dominance deviations are uncorrelated by construction, permitting the genetic variance to be split into these two terms.

For an illustrative locus with p=q=0.5, a=4 cm and d=2 cm, α=4 cm, V_A=8 cm², and V_D=1 cm². Total genetic variance from this locus is 9 cm² under the stated assumptions. The population mean relative to the homozygous midpoint is 1 cm, since the heterozygote occurs half the time and has a genotypic value of 2 cm. The variance calculation uses deviations around that mean, not around the homozygous midpoint.

A change in frequency alone can alter the additive variance. Keeping a=4 and d=2 but letting p=0.8 gives q=0.2 and α=4+2(−0.6)=2.8 cm. Additive variance becomes 0.32×7.84=2.5088 cm² and dominance variance is (0.64)²=0.4096 cm². The genotype values have not changed, yet the statistical effects and variance have. At fixation, p or q is zero; no variation at the locus remains within that population even if the gene is indispensable for development. The average-effect regression is not identifiable from within-population variation at fixation.

The multilocus extension recognizes epistasis: the effect of alleles at one locus depends on the genotype at another locus. Two enzymes in a biosynthetic pathway may both be required for production of a pigment. A functional allele at the first locus may have little effect when the second step is blocked. This biological interaction can generate statistical additive effects as well as residual interactions when evaluated in a population. One should not conclude that a biochemically interacting gene cannot contribute to additive variance.

Under a suitably orthogonal partition, V_G = V_A + V_D + V_I, where V_I represents epistatic variance. More detailed models distinguish additive-by-additive and other kinds of interaction. Linkage disequilibrium, inbreeding and differences in reference populations complicate these decompositions. For standard exam calculations the question often states the components explicitly and assumes they sum independently. In interpreting an experiment, the assumptions must be made explicit.

The additive component is particularly useful because a parent transmits alleles, not its complete diploid genotype intact through ordinary sexual reproduction. Dominance relationships are reassembled when offspring inherit an allele from each parent, while multilocus combinations can be broken by segregation and recombination. Non-additive effects are not biologically unimportant; they are simply not transmitted as a stable whole in the same way that average allele effects predict offspring means under the elementary random-mating model. Clonal propagation, in contrast, can preserve the genotype and many of its non-additive advantages.

A complete one-locus variance derivation

The single-locus model is worth developing in full because it shows exactly how familiar Mendelian genotypes become variance components. Write the frequency of A as p, the frequency of a as q, and assume random union of gametes so that the genotype frequencies are p², 2pq and q² for AA, Aa and aa. Using the genotypic values +a, d and −a, the population mean M is p²a+2pqd−q²a. Because p+q=1, p²−q²=p−q. Thus M=a(p−q)+2pqd. This is the mean relative to the midpoint of the homozygotes; it need not coincide with that midpoint.

For every genotype, subtract M to obtain a centered genotypic value. Let X be the number of A alleles, so X=2,1,0 for AA,Aa,aa. The expectation E(X) is 2p²+2pq=2p. The second moment E(X²) is 4p²+2pq. Subtracting the square of E(X) gives Var(X)=2pq. These identities require random union of alleles at the locus; an inbred population can have a different genotype distribution even if the allele frequencies remain unchanged.

The regression approach seeks the line that minimizes the frequency-weighted squared difference between observed genotypic values and predictions. The slope is Cov(X,G)/Var(X). Compute E(XG)=2p²a+2pqd. Subtract E(X)E(G)=2p[a(p−q)+2pqd]. Expanding and simplifying gives 2pqa+2pqd(q−p). Division by 2pq gives α=a+d(q−p). Thus the regression slope is not chosen arbitrarily; it is the coefficient that provides the best linear prediction of the three genotype values in the specified population.

The additive deviations, relative to the population mean, are 2qα for AA, (q−p)α for Aa and −2pα for aa. Their weighted average is zero. The additive variance can be evaluated directly by multiplying the squared deviations by genotype frequencies and summing. The resulting expression is 2pqα². It is equivalent to computing Var[α(X−2p)] and using the previously derived dosage variance.

The dominance deviations are the differences left after subtracting both M and the additive deviation. For AA the residual is −2q²d; for Aa it is 2pqd; for aa it is −2p²d. Notice that both homozygotes may have nonzero dominance deviations in this statistical decomposition. In ordinary descriptive genetics, dominance refers to the heterozygote's relationship to homozygotes. The quantitative-genetic term dominance deviation is different: it is the residual from a particular population regression. Confusing the two is a common source of exam errors.

To calculate dominance variance, weight the squares of these three residuals by p², 2pq and q². The result is 4p²q²d²(q²+2pq+p²). Because the bracket equals one, V_D=(2pqd)². The covariance between the additive and dominance deviations is zero because the residuals from a least-squares regression are orthogonal to the predictor. This property provides a statistical reason for adding the variance components rather than an assumption that biological mechanisms never interact.

Work through a case in which complete dominance is present. Let a=d=5, p=0.2 and q=0.8. The genotype values are +5, +5 and −5, and their frequencies are 0.04, 0.32 and 0.64. The mean is 0.36×5−0.64×5=−1.4. The average effect is α=5+5(0.6)=8. The additive variance is 2(0.2)(0.8)(64)=20.48, and dominance variance is [2(0.2)(0.8)(5)]²=2.56. Their sum is 23.04.

Verify the result independently using the distribution of genotypic values. A value of +5 occurs at frequency 0.36 and deviates by +6.4 from the mean. A value of −5 occurs at frequency 0.64 and deviates by −3.6. The weighted squared deviation is 0.36×40.96+0.64×12.96=14.7456+8.2944=23.04. This confirms that complete dominance does not mean that all genetic variance is dominance variance. In this population most of the genotypic variance is represented by average allele effects.

Now change p to 0.8 without changing the genotype values. The average effect becomes 5+5(−0.6)=2, additive variance becomes 1.28 and dominance variance remains 2.56. Genetic variance has fallen to 3.84. Most individuals now express the dominant phenotype because the dominant allele is common. The effect of an additional A allele on the average of its descendants is smaller when most mates already carry A. The population frequency change explains the change in statistical components.

An overdominant locus may exhibit an especially instructive limit. If the two homozygotes have identical values and the heterozygote has a value greater by d, then a=0. With equal allele frequencies, α=0 and V_A=0, but V_D=d²/4. At that frequency the genotype classes differ substantially, yet the linear average effect of allele substitution is zero. Selecting the heterozygotes does not make all offspring heterozygous under ordinary mating. The example is an idealized single-locus illustration, not a statement that overdominance always produces zero additive variance; unequal frequencies make α nonzero.

For multiple loci under linkage equilibrium, additive breeding values can be summed and their variances summed when the additive deviations are independent or uncorrelated. In the presence of linkage disequilibrium, additive contributions across loci can have covariance, so summing separate single-locus variances may be incorrect. The previous node's population-genetic treatment of linkage disequilibrium supplies the conceptual foundation. Here the relevant outcome is the variance of the combined breeding value, not a count of the loci.

Environment, reaction norms and covariance

The same genotype can produce different phenotypes across environmental conditions. A reaction norm describes the expected phenotype of a genotype as a function of environment. For two genotypes grown at two temperatures, plotting their mean growth rates against temperature provides a visual test of whether their responses are alike. If both genotypes improve by the same amount, the reaction norms are parallel on the chosen scale; the treatment has an environmental main effect, but this simple comparison does not demonstrate an interaction.

If one genotype grows much faster in warmth while the other changes little, the difference between their means depends on temperature. This is genotype-by-environment interaction even if the ranking does not change. If the lines cross, their ranking reverses. Crossing interaction is especially relevant to breeding because a genotype selected in one environment may be inferior in another. A change in rank is sufficient but not necessary evidence of interaction.

Consider two genotypes in two environments with illustrative mean heights of 20 and 30 cm for genotype A and 26 and 24 cm for genotype B. At the first site B is 6 cm taller, whereas at the second A is 6 cm taller. The overall mean for each genotype is 25 cm and the overall mean for each site is 23 and 27 cm. An analysis using only genotype marginal means might conclude there is no genetic difference, missing the strong conditional difference. Subtracting the grand mean and main effects yields residual interaction deviations of −3, +3, +3 and −3 cm in the four cells.

Interaction is not identical to genotype–environment covariance. Interaction describes differences in response; covariance describes nonrandom association of genetic and environmental deviations across observations. Randomizing cloned genotypes to soil treatments can remove systematic placement while revealing differences in their responses. Conversely, assigning large plants preferentially to better plots can generate association even if their reaction norms are parallel. The two mechanisms may occur together, but the remedies and interpretations differ.

Replication across sites, blocks and years allows the investigator to distinguish broad patterns from random measurement errors. The same genotype can be represented by cloned plants or sufficiently uniform inbred lines. Randomization reduces allocation bias, blocking accounts for local gradients, and measuring each genotype in every environmental treatment helps estimate the interaction. Without replication within genotype–environment cells, experimental noise and the interaction cannot be reliably separated.

The environment can also influence variation without changing genotype rankings. A controlled greenhouse might make water and temperature nearly uniform, decreasing environmental variance. A heterogeneous field might increase the spread of phenotypes within each genotype. Genetic variance may also change because expression of genetic differences depends on the environment. Consequently, a heritability estimate obtained from one condition cannot be transferred automatically to another.

Phenotypic plasticity refers to the capacity of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. It does not mean a trait lacks inherited variation. The slopes of reaction norms themselves can vary genetically, and the existence of plasticity is compatible with high heritability of a trait measured within a particular environment. An experiment that compares plasticity should measure each genotype under more than one condition rather than infer a reaction norm from unrelated individuals in different places.

Testing conditional genetic effects with factorial data

A factorial experiment measures all combinations of chosen factors. For a genotype-by-environment investigation, each genotype must be represented in each environment of interest, ideally with independent replication within the combinations. This design differs fundamentally from a survey in which the same genotype is found only at one geographical location. In the survey, the distribution of genotypes may be a result of historical adaptation or nonrandom movement, but the observed pattern alone does not identify how each genotype would perform in the other location.

Construct a hypothetical two-factor experiment involving four varieties of a crop and two irrigation regimes. All varieties are planted in both regimes and plants are randomly allocated to several independent plots within each treatment. Record plant survival and yield at a common developmental stage. It is useful to measure survival separately, because mean yield per surviving plant and mean yield per original planted plot are different outcomes. A drought-sensitive genotype may give high yield among its few survivors while giving poor total production. Trait definition changes the biological question as well as the interpretation of the variance.

The first comparison is between treatment means across genotypes. If drought reduces yield averaged over all varieties, there is an environmental main effect. The second comparison is among genotype means across treatments. A variety that produces more yield on average may have a genetic main effect. The third comparison asks whether the effect of genotype depends on the treatment. It is not answered by comparing the two sets of marginal means alone. Calculate differences within treatments, then compare those differences across treatments. This is the basic logic of an interaction contrast.

For example, suppose A produces 50 and 30 g per plant in well-watered and dry conditions, while B produces 42 and 34 g. All values are illustrative means. The effect of drought on A is −20 g and on B is −8 g. Their difference in treatment responses is −12 g, so the interaction contrast is nonzero. The genotype ranking reverses: A exceeds B by 8 g under adequate water, but B exceeds A by 4 g in dry conditions. An overall comparison with equal weight for both conditions gives means of 40 and 38 g and conceals the severity of A's drought response.

If the target farming environment is dry in three-quarters of seasons, an average giving equal weight to both conditions does not describe the expected performance in that target environment. With weights 0.25 and 0.75, the expected yields would be 35 g for A and 36 g for B, under the assumption that these two treatments adequately represent the relevant conditions. Genotype performance can thus depend on both the reaction norm and the distribution of environments. Genetic variance and heritability estimates derived from one sampling scheme may not describe another.

A second hypothetical example distinguishes interaction from an alteration of scale. In environment 1 the two genotype means are 10 and 20; in environment 2 they are 20 and 40. The absolute genotype difference increases from 10 to 20, giving nonparallel reaction norms on the raw scale. On a logarithmic scale, both environmental effects are the logarithm of two, and both genotype differences are also the logarithm of two. The interaction is absent from these two means on that transformed scale. This does not prove that the biological mechanisms have become independent. It shows that a statistical interaction depends on the measurement scale and the model of effects.

Biological interpretations should be tied to the measured responses. A drought experiment might lead a researcher to hypothesize that roots of one variety extract more water, or that its stomata regulate loss more effectively. The observed interaction establishes only that yields respond differently. To distinguish candidate mechanisms, additional physiological evidence is required. Measurement of root distribution, leaf water status or stomatal behavior could inform the explanation, but none of these mechanisms can be identified from two yield means alone. Quantitative genetics identifies a pattern requiring mechanistic investigation; it does not replace that investigation.

An important distinction is between environmental variance within a treatment and variation between treatment means. If every plant in a controlled chamber experiences the same temperature, the chamber temperature itself does not contribute to within-chamber environmental variance, although it may change the mean and genetic effects. If the target population contains individuals growing at several temperatures, the distribution of temperatures contributes to the overall phenotypic variance. The variance ratio depends on whether the scientific question conditions on a particular chamber or averages over a population of chambers.

Similarly, genotype-by-environment variance may be small when genotypes are tested over a narrow range of conditions and larger when tests include extremes. This does not make one estimate false. The two designs represent different environments. The practical implication is to report where, when and under which treatment conditions the phenotype was measured. A breeder concerned with performance under a future climate needs evidence from relevant conditions rather than a large heritability estimate obtained in an unrelated stable greenhouse.

In interpreting a factorial table, examine cell replication and uncertainty as well as mean differences. If the difference of differences is small relative to replicate variation, the means alone provide weak evidence of a reproducible interaction. An experiment with precise measurements and repeated genotype representation can support a stronger inference. A statistical interaction test compares the variability attributable to the interaction with the residual variability expected under the null model. Statistical significance and practical importance remain different: a small interaction may be detectable with many replicates but unimportant for selection decisions.

Broad-sense and narrow-sense heritability

Under the standard variance-component model, broad-sense heritability H² = V_G/V_P measures the fraction of phenotypic variance represented by total genetic variance. Narrow-sense heritability h² = V_A/V_P measures the fraction represented by additive genetic variance. Both are dimensionless population parameters. The superscript 2 belongs to conventional notation for a ratio of variances; an estimate reported as h² = 0.36 is already the heritability and must not be squared again.

Assume a trait has V_A = 18, V_D = 6, V_I = 3 and V_E = 13, all in the same squared units, with no covariance or unmodelled interaction. The total is 40 and V_G is 27. H² = 27/40 = 0.675; h² = 18/40 = 0.45. The difference 0.225 represents the combined non-additive variance divided by the total under the assumptions. It is not environmental variance. The environmental fraction is 13/40 = 0.325.

For a sexually reproducing population with random mating and the usual short-term assumptions, narrow-sense heritability helps predict the response when selecting parents by their measured phenotype. Broad-sense heritability may be especially informative when evaluating repeatable differences among clonally propagated genotypes in a particular environment. It is not automatically the appropriate coefficient for predicting gain through sexual reproduction. A clonally maintained cultivar can preserve a favorable heterozygous combination that would segregate in its seed progeny.

With nonnegative orthogonal variance components and positive V_P, the two ratios are between zero and one and h² cannot exceed H². A statistical estimate outside these bounds may occur from sampling error, an inappropriate estimator or violations of assumptions. It should be diagnosed rather than treated as proof that more than all of the population variation is genetic. If V_P is zero, neither ratio is defined; there is no observed variation to partition.

High heritability does not imply environmental insensitivity. Imagine a genetically diverse population of plants grown in uniform, nutrient-rich soil. Genetic variance might account for most of the remaining differences in height. Moving all plants to nutrient-poor soil may markedly reduce their average height even if genetic differences still account for most of the within-site variance. Heritability concerns differences among individuals in a defined population, not the size of an environmental treatment effect on the mean.

Low heritability likewise does not imply that genes are unnecessary for the phenotype. A population genetically uniform for essential developmental genes can show no genetic variance in a trait that absolutely depends on those genes. The genes are necessary for producing the phenotype, but allelic differences at those genes cannot explain between-individual variability when no allelic differences are present.

The proportion of variation explained within one population cannot by itself explain the cause of a difference between population means. Two populations may both show high within-population heritability for height yet differ in average height primarily because of nutrition. To investigate the cause of the difference, the populations must be compared under appropriate conditions. Without that evidence, an inference from a within-group variance ratio to a between-group causal explanation is invalid.

Choosing an estimator for the biological question

A heritability estimate is meaningful only when the numerator matches the biological question. Consider a plant nursery that maintains cultivars by cuttings. The genetic unit transmitted into new cuttings is a largely preserved genotype, so non-additive combinations can be relevant to selection among those cultivars. Compare this with a breeder producing seeds by crossing two unrelated individuals. Here the parental diploid combinations are reorganized by meiosis and fertilization; average allele effects predict the expected performance of the resulting offspring more directly. The two breeding systems can therefore use different quantities even when the underlying trait is the same.

For clonal selection, a replicated genotype trial allows comparison of total genetic differences among genotypes, provided stable non-genetic effects are controlled. An individual clone's phenotype is not perfectly repeatable because environment still varies. Replicating a genotype in multiple plots improves estimation of its mean genetic performance. If genotypes respond differently across plots or seasons, the meaning of that mean depends on the target set of environments. This is why the ratio for a genotype mean can be much larger than the ratio for one plant and why neither ratio should be described simply as the universal heritability of the species.

For a sexual breeding program, estimating additive effects requires information about transmission or an explicitly justified genetic model. A paternal half-sib experiment, an appropriate parent–offspring regression or a realized response estimate may supply such information. These approaches do not necessarily give identical numerical results. Half-sib covariance uses expected genetic relationships, regression measures resemblance across generations, and a selection experiment measures the outcome of a particular selective regime. Differences among results invite analysis of the assumptions, environmental conditions and uncertainty rather than automatic averaging.

An illustrative comparison emphasizes this point. Suppose a segregating crop population has a total variance of 50 squared units, of which 30 are attributed to genetic differences among replicated genotypes. An independently designed half-sib study estimates additive variance of 15 squared units in the same reference population. The broad-sense estimate is 0.60 and the narrow-sense estimate 0.30. If the breeder selects individuals with an observed differential of 10 units and uses them as randomly paired parents, the elementary prediction is a response of 3 units. Using the broad-sense ratio would predict 6 units and would assume that all the observed genetic superiority is transmitted in the same way as additive effects.

Now suppose the same breeder measures the first generation and finds that the selected line has improved by 4 units relative to the appropriate control. Realised heritability is 0.40. It is not necessary to conclude that one study must be fraudulent or that the underlying model is useless. The 0.30 estimate has sampling error; the 0.40 estimate is also a sample estimate. Selection can alter the genetic composition and family representation, and the control may imperfectly reflect environmental conditions. A repeated selection experiment and confidence intervals for the component estimates would help determine whether the discrepancy is meaningful.

A particularly useful exam strategy is to distinguish observational association from experimental control. A family covariance may reveal inherited resemblance, but shared environmental influences may contribute to it. A randomized genotype trial helps isolate genetic differences among the tested genotypes, but it may not establish the additive fraction. A selection trial can estimate response to the applied selection, but drift and environmental changes can influence the difference between generations. Each design solves a particular inference problem and leaves others unresolved.

The concept of residual variation also requires care. The environmental component in a simple model is not a complete catalogue of environmental causes. It often includes unmeasured microenvironment, developmental stochasticity and measurement error. If a known factor such as block or age is added explicitly to the model, the residual may shrink without any change in the molecular genetics of the trait. Heritability conditional on measured covariates and heritability calculated from unadjusted observations can consequently differ. The appropriate estimate depends on which variation the investigator wants to explain or predict.

Suppose animals from two age groups are weighed together. Age accounts for a large fraction of observed mass variation, and siblings are more likely to belong to the same age group. A raw family analysis may attribute age-related resemblance to family effects. Comparing age-adjusted weights changes the estimand to individual differences after accounting for average age effects. If age and genetic effects interact, a single adjustment may not be sufficient; estimating effects within age classes or explicitly modeling the interaction may be more informative.

One should avoid interpreting heritability as a ranking of biological importance among traits. A high ratio for a minor morphological trait does not make that trait more essential than a physiological trait with low heritability. Essential physiological pathways can be highly conserved, leaving little genetic variation. Conversely, a large genetic variance in coloration need not imply a large fitness effect. The ratio measures variability under specified conditions, not necessity, value, adaptiveness or the number of genes involved.

The same principle limits comparisons of traits with different units. Two traits may have identical heritabilities but very different additive variances and selection differentials. To compare predicted gain, calculate response in meaningful units or use a justified standardization. Even standardized response must be interpreted according to the breeding objective. A trait with high heritability is not automatically the best trait to select if its improvement has little benefit or causes adverse changes in other traits.

Experimental separation of genetic and environmental variance

One route to estimating broad-sense heritability is to replicate genotypes. If several genetically distinct inbred lines are cultivated in the same randomized experiment, individual measurements within a line primarily reflect environmental and measurement variation, while systematic differences among line means contain genetic effects. The inference is stronger when seed age, planting density and measurement procedures are standardized and when maternal carryover effects are minimized.

In an illustrative balanced experiment, let there be several lines, each represented by r = 4 plants. Suppose an analysis of variance gives a between-line mean square of 44 cm² and a within-line mean square of 12 cm². Under a random-line model with independent residuals and a common residual variance, the expected within-line mean square is V_E, while the expected between-line mean square is V_E + rV_G. Subtracting the two yields rV_G. Therefore the estimates are V_E=12 and V_G=(44−12)/4=8 cm². The variance for a single plant is estimated as 20 cm² and the broad-sense ratio is 8/20=0.40.

If selection is instead based on a line mean of four independent plants, its residual variance is 12/4=3 cm². The ratio for the line mean is 8/(8+3)=0.727. The two results do not conflict; they concern different observational units. Averaging independent environmental deviations improves the precision of a line mean. When plants share a plot or environmental exposure, their residuals may be correlated, so dividing by the number of plants can underestimate uncertainty.

Classical crosses can also help estimate environmental variance. Cross two homozygous lines to produce a genetically uniform F1 generation at the loci where the parents were fixed for alternate alleles. If the parental and F1 populations are genetically uniform within groups, their within-group phenotypic variation may provide a reference for the environmental contribution. The segregating F2 generation contains additional genetic variation. Subtracting a suitable estimate of environmental variance from F2 phenotypic variance gives an estimate of total genetic variance in that F2, provided the environmental variance is comparable and the generations are measured in similar conditions.

Suppose V_F2 is 36 g² and the environmental variance inferred from genetically uniform controls is 12 g². The estimated V_G is 24 g² and broad-sense heritability for the F2 is 24/36, about 0.667. These data alone cannot uniquely split the 24 into additive, dominance and epistatic terms. Additional crosses, related-family designs or assumptions are needed. This is an identifiability problem: numerous sets of genetic components are consistent with the same total.

Genetically uniform does not mean physiologically identical. Even identical genotypes differ because of microenvironmental variation, developmental stochasticity, organ position and measurement error. Inbred lines may differ in sensitivity to environmental variation. If the F2 has a different residual variance from the parental lines, the subtraction method becomes biased. Negative estimated genetic variance from a small or poorly controlled experiment is a warning to inspect sampling variability and the appropriateness of the model.

An experimental design must therefore match its intended quantity. Replicated clonal or inbred lines support estimation of genetic differences among those lines. Half-sib families can provide information about additive genetic variance because they share one parent. Full-sib families share more genetic material and may share dominance combinations, maternal influences and common rearing conditions. The larger resemblance of full siblings cannot automatically be assigned to additive inheritance.

What replication and analysis of variance can establish

An experiment with replicated genotypes produces observations organized into groups. The crucial statistical idea is that variation among group means contains both genuine differences among the groups and random errors in their estimated means. Replication reduces the second contribution. An analysis of variance uses the within-group variability to estimate that random contribution and subtract it from the variability among groups.

Consider an illustrative experiment with three clonal plant lines, each with three measured heights in centimeters. Line A has values 18, 20 and 22; B has 24, 26 and 28; C has 30, 32 and 34. The line means are 20, 26 and 32, and the grand mean is 26. Each line has the same within-line deviations −2, 0 and +2, so its squared residual sum is 8. Across three lines the within-group sum of squares is 24, with 3(3−1)=6 degrees of freedom. The within-line mean square is therefore 4 cm².

The between-line sum of squares is the number of replicates times the sum of squared line-mean deviations from the grand mean. It equals 3(36+0+36)=216. With three lines there are two between-line degrees of freedom, and the between-line mean square is 108 cm². If these lines are treated as a random sample of the target genotypes and the simple independent residual model applies, V_E is estimated as 4 and V_G as (108−4)/3=104/3, approximately 34.67 cm². Single-plant H² is approximately 34.67/(34.67+4)=0.897.

The ratio for line means is higher: divide the independent environmental component by three, giving H²_mean ≈34.67/(34.67+1.33)=0.963. The numerical difference demonstrates the benefit of replication when selecting lines, but it does not establish that the population parameter is known precisely. Only three lines were observed, and the between-line mean square has two degrees of freedom. The illustrative estimate is sensitive to which genotypes were sampled.

The assumption that lines are sampled randomly also matters. If the investigator deliberately chooses one exceptionally short, one intermediate and one exceptionally tall line, the among-line variance describes those chosen lines, not necessarily a representative population. A fixed-effect analysis can legitimately compare the three lines without pretending they are a random sample. Estimation of a population variance component is a different objective and requires an appropriate sampling frame.

Spatial block effects may reduce residual variability if measured plots within a block share conditions. Consider a field with a moisture gradient. If every plant of line A is planted near the water source and every plant of line B is planted farther away, genotype and moisture cannot be separated from these data. A randomized complete block design places each line once in each block. Comparing lines within blocks removes much of the spatial gradient from the residual comparison, while independent blocks provide replication. If one block lacks a line, the design is unbalanced and the elementary balanced formula may not apply directly.

A genotype tested in several locations requires a different distinction. A suitable balanced random-effects model may contain genotype variance, genotype-by-location variance and residual variance, in addition to location effects. When comparing genotype means across e independent environments with r independent replicates per environment, the error associated with an estimated overall genotype mean may include V_GE/e and V_error/(er). The resulting broad-sense ratio is V_G/[V_G+V_GE/e+V_error/(er)] when those components are orthogonal and the target is the mean over sampled environments. This is not the same as single-plot heritability.

Suppose V_G=20, V_GE=12 and V_error=24, with three environments and two replicates per environment. The interaction contribution to the genotype mean is 12/3=4 and the independent residual contribution is 24/6=4. Entry-mean heritability is therefore 20/(20+4+4)=0.714. The ratio for a single plot under the corresponding model is 20/(20+12+24)=0.357, excluding any common location main effect from the denominator as appropriate for the stated comparison. More environments improve estimates of general performance because they average over genotype-specific departures as well as local errors.

A common trap is to treat observations from repeated subunits of one plot as independent environments. Measuring ten leaves on the same plant may improve estimation of that plant's mean leaf size, but it does not supply ten genetically independent plants. Measuring multiple plants in one plot improves plot measurement without replicating the plot-level soil environment. A student should identify the levels of replication in the experimental design before deciding which denominator is appropriate.

These comparisons do not require advanced statistical software to understand. The expected mean squares and division by replication illustrate the central inference. More complex designs may need mixed models, but their purpose is the same: represent known relationships and separate components supported by the data. If a question omits information about randomization, genotype representation or independence, the numerical result should be stated conditionally rather than presented as an unconditional biological fact.

Covariance of relatives and parent–offspring regression

Related individuals are informative because their alleles are not sampled independently from the population. Under random mating, an offspring receives one of its two alleles at an autosomal locus from each parent. For additive genetic values, the expected offspring breeding value given the two parental breeding values is their average. Mendelian sampling creates deviations around this average; across many offspring, those deviations have mean zero under the model.

Let B be the breeding value of a parent and P its centered phenotype. Under an orthogonal additive model, P=B+N, where N contains the remaining deviations and Cov(B,N)=0. Hence Cov(B,P)=V_A. If one parent is known and the other is randomly sampled from the population, the covariance of offspring phenotype with the known parent's phenotype is one-half V_A, assuming independent residual environments and no relevant non-additive covariance. Dividing by parental phenotypic variance gives a single-parent regression slope of h²/2.

The regression on midparent phenotype is different. The midparent is the mean of the two parental measurements. If both parents have the same phenotypic variance and mate randomly, its variance is V_P/2. The covariance of offspring phenotype with that average is V_A/2, because each parent supplies the corresponding half-additive covariance. The regression slope is consequently (V_A/2)/(V_P/2)=h². The same numerator and smaller denominator account for the difference between the two regression designs.

For illustrative families with midparent phenotypes 8, 10, 12 and 14 and offspring family means 9, 10, 11 and 12, the midparent mean is 11 and the offspring mean is 10.5. The centered x values are −3, −1, 1 and 3; centered y values are −1.5, −0.5, 0.5 and 1.5. Their products sum to 10 and the squared x deviations sum to 20. The least-squares slope is 10/20 = 0.5. Under the standard assumptions this estimates h² as 0.5. If the predictor instead represented a single randomly mating parent, the corresponding estimate would be twice the slope. The regression coefficient has trait units divided by trait units; correlation is a different quantity normalized by both standard deviations.

The relationship can be confounded by shared family environment. Parents with favorable resources may provide offspring with similar conditions, generating resemblance that is not caused solely by genetic transmission. Assortative mating changes the distribution of parental pairs and the variance of their mean. Age differences and sex-specific scales may complicate comparisons between parents and offspring. Measurement error in the predictor can attenuate a regression slope. Parent–offspring regression is a powerful model-based approach, not a direct measurement of the molecular contribution of every gene.

For non-inbred diploid relatives in an elementary additive-dominance model without shared environmental effects or relevant epistatic covariance, paternal half-sibs have covariance V_A/4 and full siblings have covariance V_A/2+V_D/4. These expressions use probabilities of sharing alleles identical by descent, rather than merely sharing alleles identical in state. Full siblings sometimes inherit both homologous alleles from the same ancestral copies and therefore can share dominance deviations. Half-sibs share only one parent, allowing an elementary additive estimate from the between-family covariance.

Suppose the paternal half-sib covariance is 3 and the full-sib covariance is 8, in identical squared trait units. V_A=4×3=12. The additive portion of full-sib covariance is 6, leaving 2 to be attributed to one-quarter of dominance variance under the assumptions; V_D=8. If maternal and shared environmental effects contribute to the full-sib covariance, the calculation overattributes that extra resemblance to dominance. A cross-fostering design can help distinguish genetic family from rearing family, though prenatal and maternal carryover influences need separate attention.

Family resemblance, segregation and sources of bias

Consider a set of paternal half-sib families in a plant or animal breeding population. Each sire contributes offspring to several unrelated dams. Under random mating and regular Mendelian inheritance, two offspring of the same sire share an expected quarter of their additive alleles identical by descent. The covariance of their additive genetic values is one-quarter of V_A. This relationship is the basis of the familiar fourfold conversion from a paternal half-sib covariance to additive variance.

The expectation is an average over many segregating loci and meioses. Two paternal half-siblings do not each share exactly one quarter of every chromosome. They may inherit different homologous segments from the sire, and the fraction actually shared can differ across the genome. Quantitative-genetic covariance formulas describe expected resemblance averaged over the relevant distribution. They are not deterministic predictions of the exact phenotype of each pair.

Full siblings have an expected additive relationship of one-half. In addition, at an autosomal locus they have probability one-quarter of inheriting both ancestral allele copies from the same parental homologs, under standard outbred assumptions. This allows covariance of dominance deviations. The simple expression Cov_FS = 0.5 V_A + 0.25 V_D ignores epistatic covariance and shared environmental effects. A dataset providing only full-sib covariance does not allow V_A and V_D to be estimated separately unless further information or assumptions are provided.

The parental sex used for half-sib families can be important. Offspring sharing a mother may also share prenatal conditions, egg provisioning or maternal care. Their resemblance can therefore exceed the prediction based solely on additive genetic transmission. Offspring sharing a father but different randomly assigned mothers may have fewer such shared maternal influences in some species. Paternal designs are not automatically immune to environmental bias: common housing, semen batch effects, parental age and nonrandom choice of mates can still alter estimates.

For illustration, suppose a half-sib analysis yields an among-sire variance estimate of 2.5 g², and total individual phenotypic variance is 25 g². If the standard paternal half-sib assumptions hold, additive variance is 10 g² and h²=0.4. If all offspring of each sire were reared in a distinct pen whose effect contributes 1 g² to among-family variance, the assumed genetic covariance would be inflated. Subtracting that known environmental contribution gives a genetic half-sib covariance of 1.5, corresponding to V_A=6 and h²=0.24 if total phenotypic variance remains 25. This illustrates the magnitude and direction of a particular confounder.

Cross-fostering provides one conceptual route to separate common postnatal environments from family genetic effects. If offspring from each biological family are distributed among several foster families, resemblance according to biological family and foster family can be compared. The design is especially helpful when assignment is random and all families are represented across multiple environments. Prenatal or egg-mediated effects remain associated with biological parentage and require additional evidence to distinguish from inherited genetic differences.

Parent–offspring regression can also be examined through residuals. Suppose families with large midparent measurements consistently produce lower-than-predicted offspring in a low-resource year. The regression may still be positive, but the intercept shifts. If all offspring are measured under that same condition, a shared mean shift may change the intercept without changing slope. If parents with high phenotypes are disproportionately associated with that year, both slope and intercept can be distorted. Plotting residuals against year, site and family size helps identify whether assumptions are plausible.

Family means should be handled thoughtfully when the number of offspring per family varies. Averaging ten offspring generally estimates a family expectation more precisely than averaging one, but treating every offspring as an independent parental predictor repeats the same parental measurement and may overrepresent prolific families. A simple exam example may assign equal family weights; an actual study should specify whether the target is the mean across families or across offspring, and use a method appropriate to the unequal precision and relatedness.

An apparent difference between two heritability estimates can also reflect estimation uncertainty rather than a real difference in parameters. An estimate of 0.40 from one experiment and 0.48 from another does not establish environmental dependence unless the designs and uncertainty are evaluated. A confidence interval represents the precision of an estimation procedure; it does not make the point estimate a universal truth. The reasoning lesson is to connect statistical evidence to the scope of the claim, not to memorize a numerical cutoff for genetic importance.

Twin and family studies

Human family studies use naturally occurring variation in relationships rather than experimentally arranging mating. Monozygotic twins arise from the same zygote and generally share nearly all their inherited nuclear genetic variation. Dizygotic twins originate from separate fertilizations and, like ordinary full siblings, share on average half their additive segregating genetic effects under the classical model. They share an age and often a household, which makes comparison useful, but genetic and environmental resemblance still require careful interpretation.

For a standardized trait, an elementary ACE model assigns proportions A, C and E to additive genetic, shared environmental and individual-specific residual variation. Assuming additive genetic effects, equal relevant shared-environment covariance for both twin types, random mating and no important interaction or correlation terms, the expected correlations are r_MZ=A+C and r_DZ=0.5A+C. Subtracting the latter from the former gives 0.5A. Thus A=2(r_MZ−r_DZ), C=2r_DZ−r_MZ and E=1−r_MZ.

If r_MZ=0.70 and r_DZ=0.45, then A=2(0.25)=0.50, C=0.90−0.70=0.20 and E=0.30. These are illustrative estimates, not universal properties of a human characteristic. E includes measurement error as well as nonshared environmental influences under this model. Estimates depend on the sampled population, measurement conditions and the assumptions. The model does not provide a percentage decomposition of any individual person's phenotype.

A difference between monozygotic and dizygotic twin correlations may also involve non-additive genetic effects, assortative mating or systematic environmental differences. For instance, if genetically identical twins receive more similar treatment relevant to the trait than dizygotic twins, the equal-environments assumption may be violated. Identical genetic origin also does not imply perfectly identical developmental histories. The elementary three-component model has limited power to separate alternative mechanisms from only two correlations.

An unusually low dizygotic correlation relative to the monozygotic correlation can motivate consideration of dominance, but does not uniquely establish dominance. A simple ADE model uses additive, dominance and unique environmental components and expects dizygotic genetic correlations of one-half for additive effects and one-quarter for dominance effects. Two observed twin correlations cannot simultaneously identify arbitrary amounts of additive, dominance and shared environmental variation without more information. Extended pedigrees and additional designs are needed to test the alternatives.

Adoption and twins reared apart can provide complementary comparisons, but they do not remove every confounder. Prenatal environment, selective placement and continuing contact can influence resemblance. Ethical and practical constraints also shape the available data. The correct exam response identifies the estimand and the assumptions rather than interpreting a correlation as causal proof.

Selection differential, response and the breeder’s equation

Selection begins when some individuals contribute more offspring than others because of a measured character or a correlated attribute. In artificial selection the breeder chooses potential parents; in natural selection differential reproductive success occurs through ecological and physiological processes. This chapter concentrates on the short-term quantitative prediction for selection on a measured phenotype.

The selection differential S is the mean phenotype of the selected parents minus the mean phenotype of the original candidate population. It is expressed in the original trait units and can be positive, zero or negative. If individuals differ in reproductive contribution, the appropriate selected mean is weighted by their contributions. Choosing an individual as a parent does not guarantee equal contribution to the next generation.

Response R is the change in mean phenotype between generations that is attributable to selection when compared under suitable environmental conditions. If the base population has a mean seed mass of 20 g and selected parents average 26 g, S is +6 g. If narrow-sense heritability is 0.4, the elementary breeder's equation predicts R=h²S=2.4 g. The expected offspring mean under comparable conditions is 22.4 g. The selected parents' mean is not the expected offspring mean because their superiority includes residual influences that are not transmitted as additive genetic effects.

The equation follows from the regression of offspring phenotype on midparent phenotype. If the selected midparent deviation has mean S relative to the candidate population, and the offspring regression slope is h², averaging the predicted offspring deviations gives h²S. Equivalently, linear regression of breeding value on phenotype has slope Cov(B,P)/V_P=V_A/V_P; averaging over selected parents gives a predicted change in mean breeding value. This derivation requires an appropriate reference population and assumes that the regression remains useful over the selected range.

When selection occurs in only one sex, the other sex still supplies half the additive genetic contribution. If the selected males exceed the male candidate mean by 10 units and females are randomly chosen, the average parental differential is 5 units under equal contributions and comparable scales. At h²=0.4, R is predicted to be 2 units rather than 4. If both sexes have different selection differentials, the simple model uses their average. More complex designs may require sex-specific variances and genetic relationships.

Selection intensity i standardizes S by the phenotypic standard deviation σ_P, so S=iσ_P. The expression R=i h²σ_P makes it possible to compare selection strategies on a common scale. With σ_P=5 g, i=1.2 and h²=0.30, the predicted differential is 6 g and response is 1.8 g. Under a normal-distribution truncation model, smaller selected proportions tend to correspond to higher positive selection intensity when selecting the upper tail. Without a normal model or an observed differential, a percentile alone does not uniquely specify S.

The correlation between phenotype and breeding value is h, the square root of h², in the orthogonal model. Indeed Cov(B,P)/(σ_Bσ_P)=V_A/(√V_A√V_P)=√(V_A/V_P). Selection accuracy describes how well a selection criterion predicts the breeding value. It differs from the regression slope of breeding value on phenotype. These quantities coincide numerically only at limiting values such as zero and one.

Selection on the phenotype may have little response when additive variance is low even though total genetic variance is high. Conversely, a trait with moderate heritability can respond appreciably if the selected differential is large. A heritability estimate without the scale and variance of the phenotype cannot determine the absolute response. Breeding objectives may also involve multiple traits and trade-offs, so improvement in one measured character does not necessarily improve overall fitness or agricultural performance.

Prediction, regression toward the mean and selection accuracy

When individuals are selected for a high observed phenotype, part of the selected superiority may be environmental. Even when the expected environmental deviation in the original population is zero, conditioning on unusually high phenotypes tends to select positive residual deviations as well as positive breeding values. The selected parents are therefore not a random sample of residual environments. Their offspring do not automatically inherit these favorable residuals.

A simple model gives a clear prediction. Suppose V_A=9 and V_P=25, so h²=0.36 and h=0.6. A candidate has a phenotype 10 units above the population mean. The linear prediction of its breeding value is 0.36×10=3.6 units. This is not a statement that the candidate physically contains 3.6 units of genetic material. It is a prediction of the additive deviation under the statistical model. If it mates with a random individual whose expected breeding value is zero, its expected offspring breeding deviation is half of 3.6, or 1.8 units.

If two selected parents have phenotypic deviations of +10 and +6, their midparent deviation is +8. The expected offspring deviation is 0.36×8=2.88. The prediction is compatible with some offspring exceeding both parents and some falling below the population mean, because Mendelian sampling and environmental residuals still generate variation around the expectation. Regression toward the population mean is an expectation about selected families, not an assertion that every offspring is necessarily intermediate.

If both the selected parents and their offspring experience a superior environment, resemblance can be high even if the genetic response is modest. For example, selected parents raised in a fertilized plot may be used to establish offspring in another fertilized plot, while the base population was raised in an unfertilized plot. Comparing these populations combines environment and selection. A common comparison environment is needed to separate the genetic change from the treatment effect.

Selection based on an alternative criterion introduces the idea of accuracy. If the criterion is correlated with the breeding value, selecting high values of the criterion is expected to change the mean breeding value. The direct phenotype is only one possible criterion; a replicated line mean or a progeny mean can be more informative. If a standardized criterion has correlation r with breeding value and its selected differential is i standard deviations, the linear predictor gives expected gain i r σ_A. For selection on an individual phenotype, r=h and σ_A=hσ_P, recovering R=i h²σ_P. This is an algebraic connection, not an invitation to apply the equation without specifying the selection criterion.

An illustrative comparison helps show why accuracy matters. Consider a trait with additive variance 16 and phenotypic variance 100. Then h²=0.16, h=0.4 and σ_A=4. For i=1.5, individual phenotypic selection gives predicted R=1.5×0.4×4=2.4 units. If an independently justified family criterion has accuracy 0.7 at the same intensity and with the same additive variation, the predicted gain is 1.5×0.7×4=4.2 units. The second prediction does not require changing the trait's single-record heritability; it changes the quality of the information used for selection.

Accuracy is not the only breeding consideration. Progeny evaluation may require waiting for offspring to mature, increasing generation interval. Selection strategies should compare gain per unit time when generation lengths differ. A method producing four units of gain in four years may be less productive per year than one producing three units in two years, assuming comparable objectives and stable conditions. The simple ratio illustrates why response per generation and response per year should not be conflated.

High selection intensity may also reduce the number of parents contributing to the next generation. If only a few individuals reproduce, effective population size may decline and random genetic drift and inbreeding can become important. Short-term gain and preservation of future variation are distinct objectives. The quantitative-genetic approach predicts the first using current variance and selection intensity, while population-genetic reasoning helps evaluate the second. More detailed evolutionary mechanisms belong to the subsequent unit; for this chapter the essential point is that changing the breeding population changes the conditions of the prediction.

Realised heritability, selection experiments and limits

A selection experiment can estimate realised heritability by comparing the observed response with the imposed differential. If the selected parents differ from the base mean by S=8 units and their offspring exhibit an adjusted mean response of R=3 units, realised h²=3/8=0.375. The ratio is undefined when S=0. An observed mean change should not automatically be equated with genetic response, because the generations may differ in environmental conditions.

A valuable experimental design maintains an unselected control line alongside the selected line. Suppose the selected line mean rises from 50 to 56 units while an unselected control rises from 50 to 52 under similar conditions. An adjusted response of 4 units is more informative than the raw 6-unit increase, if the control captures the shared environmental shift. For a differential of 10 units, the realised estimate is 0.4 rather than 0.6. Control lines can themselves drift genetically; independent replicate lines improve confidence that the contrast represents selection rather than random sampling.

Across several generations the cumulative response can be plotted against the cumulative selection differential. In a simple constant-heritability model, the slope approximates realised heritability. Suppose cumulative differentials are 0, 5, 10 and 15 units with cumulative responses 0, 2, 4 and 6. These illustrative data fit a slope of 0.4 exactly. Departures from a straight line can signal estimation noise, changes in genetic variance, environmental changes or failure of a linear response model.

Selection changes allele frequencies and can change associations among loci. This changes the distribution of breeding values. As favorable alleles approach fixation, their contributions to segregating variance may decline. Strong selection can create linkage disequilibrium that changes additive variance even before fixation. Small breeding populations also experience drift and inbreeding. As a result, a one-generation estimate must not be extrapolated indefinitely by multiplying the same response by the number of future generations.

A plateau does not necessarily mean the phenotype has reached an absolute biological maximum. The selected population may have exhausted much of its accessible additive variation, or a correlated fitness cost may oppose further change. A different genetic background, different conditions or new segregating variation could alter the response. An examination asking why a line stops responding should distinguish depletion of selectable variation from insufficient selection intensity, environmental confounding and a true physiological constraint.

Classical long-term selection experiments provide evidence that quantitative variation can sustain repeated changes in population means. The Illinois long-term maize selection experiment began in 1896 and selected for high and low oil and protein concentrations. Its value for this topic is the contrast between lines subjected to divergent selection and the persistence of response over many generations. The historical finding is not evidence that the original heritability remained constant throughout; changes in allele frequencies and selection environment must be considered. Numerical outcomes are not needed to establish the conceptual point here.

Direct selection response can also be affected by genetic correlations with other traits. A selected plant with high seed number may have reduced individual seed mass if the underlying allocation traits are negatively genetically associated. Phenotypic correlation alone does not establish genetic correlation, since common environmental effects can induce association. The univariate equation is useful within its domain but does not model every consequence of selection on an integrated organism.

Interpreting a multigeneration selection experiment

A selection experiment is stronger when it distinguishes the applied selective pressure, the observed phenotypic change and the inferred genetic response. Begin by choosing a well-defined base population and trait. Record the candidate phenotypes before selection, identify which individuals contribute offspring, and estimate their mean contribution using appropriate reproductive weights. Maintain a control line drawn from the same base population without deliberate selection on the trait. Rear selected and control lines under comparable conditions at each measurement. Replicate lines where feasible to distinguish the effects of selection from stochastic population changes.

Consider a hypothetical experiment in which a breeder selects for increased seed mass over three generations. At generation zero the selected and control populations both have mean 30 g. Selected parents have mean 35 g, so the first differential is 5 g. In generation one the selected population has mean 33 g and the control mean has risen to 31 g. The adjusted response is 2 g. The first realised estimate is 2/5=0.40. The raw selected-line change of 3 g would instead give 0.60 and would overlook the control's environmental change.

In generation one the candidate mean in the selected population is 33 g and selected parents average 39 g. The new differential is 6 g. Generation two has selected mean 37 g and control mean 32.5 g. The selected-line change since generation one is 4 g, and the control-line change is 1.5 g. Adjusted response for that generation is 2.5 g and the realised estimate is 2.5/6≈0.417. Cumulative differential is 11 g and cumulative adjusted response is 4.5 g. These cumulative quantities must be formed by adding generation-specific differentials and adjusted responses, not by subtracting every selected parent mean from the original base mean.

In generation two the candidate mean is 37 g and selected parents average 43 g. The differential is again 6 g. Generation three has selected mean 39 g and control mean 33 g. Selected-line change is 2 g and control-line change is 0.5 g, giving adjusted response 1.5 g. The generation-specific estimate is 0.25. Across all three generations the cumulative differential is 17 g and the cumulative adjusted response is 6 g. The simple cumulative ratio is about 0.353. A fitted regression using multiple cumulative points can give a different estimate depending on how the intercept and weighting are handled. Always specify which estimate is being calculated.

What might account for the declining response in the last generation? First, allele frequencies may have shifted, reducing additive variance. Second, the strength of linkage disequilibrium generated by previous selection may have changed the combined breeding-value variance. Third, an environmental condition may affect the selected and control lines differently, so subtraction of their mean shifts may not fully remove environmental effects. Fourth, random sampling and finite numbers of parents introduce noise. The three-generation dataset cannot establish which mechanism caused the decline.

A useful additional measurement would estimate additive variance among families at each generation using comparable designs. If additive variance declined while environmental variance remained similar, a reduction in h² would be plausible. However, the variation could recover if mating or recombination alters allele associations, so a single decline does not establish exhaustion of all genetic variation. Monitoring genetic diversity and parent representation can also help distinguish drift and inbreeding from changes expected under deliberate selection.

The Illinois maize experiment provides a historical contrast to the illustrative calculations. The original question concerned whether repeated selection could change kernel composition over many generations. Starting in 1896, separate lines were selected in opposing directions for oil and protein. Their long-term divergence demonstrated that quantitative characters can respond repeatedly to artificial selection. Later investigators used descendants and crosses derived from these lines to examine the genetic architecture underlying selection response. The experiment is a particularly effective conceptual bridge from variance-based prediction to mapping: divergence provides evidence of inherited changes, whereas molecular analyses address the genomic regions associated with those changes.

It is important not to invent a particular response curve or exact numerical change when discussing the historical experiment without examining the relevant published data. The hypothetical table above is deliberately separate from the historical study. In an exam, published data may be presented as a graph of concentration versus generation. Identify the lines, the direction of selection and the trait units before interpreting their trajectories. An upward trend supports response to upward selection, but the slope against generation number is not itself realised heritability unless the selection differentials are known.

The shape of a long-term response curve may reveal that response changes over time. A steep initial slope and later flattening are consistent with several processes, including loss of additive variation or increasing constraints. Continued response despite many generations of selection is consistent with a large reservoir of variation or other sources that sustain selectable differences. Neither pattern identifies the exact number of contributing loci on its own. A response curve is evidence about population change, not a direct census of molecular variants.

Directional selection is not the only possible design. A breeder can select for decreased trait values; in that case S and the expected R are both negative when h² is positive. If S=−5 and h²=0.4, the predicted R is −2, not +2. Divergent selection applies upward and downward selection in separate lines. Comparing the divergence can help detect inherited differences in response, particularly when both lines experience similar external changes. But asymmetrical response can arise if the two directions encounter different genetic architectures, physiological bounds or correlated fitness effects.

A negative realised ratio can arise when an observed response has the opposite sign from the imposed differential. Such an estimate should trigger investigation of control conditions, sampling noise, opposing fitness effects and trait definition. It does not mean that the true variance ratio in a nonnegative orthogonal model is negative. Similarly, estimates above one may indicate sampling variation, environmental confounding or an inappropriate linear approximation. Distinguish a population parameter from an unconstrained empirical estimator before declaring a biological impossibility.

An important limit of the breeder's equation is that the selective differential must refer to the phenotype or appropriately specified criterion used in the derivation. In nature, individuals with a particular measured phenotype may reproduce more because that trait is correlated with another trait that directly affects fitness. The observed differential is evidence of phenotypic selection, but causal interpretation requires consideration of correlated characteristics. The simple univariate equation is most defensible when the relevant regression is stable and the selection is appropriately described by the measured variable. A full multivariate analysis is beyond the needs of this chapter, but the conceptual warning is important for interpreting ecological questions.

Repeatability and persistent individual differences

Some traits can be measured repeatedly on the same individual: milk production across lactations, leaf expansion in successive intervals or a repeated behavioral assay. A high value on one occasion may predict a high value on another because of genotype, stable individual environment or both. Repeatability quantifies this persistence under a specified repeated-measures model.

Write the deviation on occasion j for individual i as G_i + PE_i + TE_ij, where G is a genetic deviation, PE is a persistent environmental deviation and TE is a temporary residual. Assuming these components are uncorrelated, the variance of a single record is V_G+V_PE+V_TE. The covariance between two records on the same individual is V_G+V_PE if temporary residuals are independent between occasions. Repeatability t is this covariance divided by the variance of a record, yielding t=(V_G+V_PE)/V_P.

For an illustrative trait with V_G=15, V_PE=5 and V_TE=20, total single-record variance is 40 and repeatability is 20/40=0.50. Broad-sense heritability for the single record is 15/40=0.375, and narrow-sense heritability is at most this value under the simple partition. The persistent environmental contribution raises repeatability without raising inherited variation. A consistently better-fed animal can repeatedly outperform others even if its inherited genetic value is average.

Under these conditions repeatability can provide an upper bound for single-record heritability, because the repeatable component includes genetic variance and stable non-genetic deviations. The inequality is conditional: different trait definitions, age-dependent genetic effects, heterogeneous variance or correlated temporary residuals require a different model. It is not valid to compare an arbitrary repeatability measured for one population with heritability from another.

Averaging k independent temporary records reduces their variance to V_TE/k, but does not remove the persistent environment. With four records in the example the variance of the individual average is 15+5+20/4=25. The broad-sense ratio for the average is 15/25=0.60. Additional records improve precision but cannot distinguish persistent genetic and environmental differences without a design that supplies genetic relationships or replication of genotypes.

Repeated measures are not independent individuals. Treating four measurements of one animal as four independent animals inflates sample size and understates uncertainty. For a plant measured at different ages, growth changes the mean systematically, and records may be temporally correlated. Age or occasion effects need appropriate treatment before applying a simple repeatability equation. Biological stability, statistical precision and genetic transmissibility are related but separate questions.

Putting the estimators together: an examination case study

A research question may supply several datasets that refer to the same trait but different designs. The student must identify which quantity each dataset estimates before combining any information. Consider a hypothetical diploid, sexually reproducing plant population. A trial with genetically uniform controls estimates a residual environmental variance of 20 g². A segregating F2 population grown alongside them has phenotypic variance 50 g². Under the assumption that the residual variance is comparable, estimated total genetic variance is 30 g², and broad-sense heritability is 0.60.

The breeder subsequently obtains paternal half-sib families from that same reference population, with an estimated half-sib covariance of 4 g². Under the elementary model, V_A=16 g² and h²=16/50=0.32. The difference between the total genetic and additive components is 14 g². The F2 and half-sib data together do not by themselves distinguish how much of this difference is dominance variance and how much is epistatic variance. To do so, further designs or assumptions are necessary.

Suppose the breeder also measures full-sib covariance of 11 g². If epistatic covariance and shared environment are negligible, the additive contribution to full-sib covariance is 0.5×16=8 g². The remaining 3 g² is assigned to one-quarter of dominance variance, giving V_D=12 g². With V_G=30 and V_A=16, this leaves 2 g² for an additional component in the orthogonal model. Since the full-sib equation omitted epistatic covariance, the calculation is approximate if the residual genetic component truly represents epistasis. This internal consistency check is more informative than mechanically treating every estimate as exact.

If full-sib families were reared together, a portion of their covariance could instead reflect common plot effects. Suppose independent evidence establishes that 2 g² of full-sib covariance is due to common environment. Subtracting this contribution yields adjusted covariance of 9 g². Under the same additive-dominance approximation, V_D=4×(9−8)=4 g² rather than 12. The remaining genetic variance cannot simply be declared known epistatic variance, since other assumptions may also be violated. The example demonstrates how strongly a component estimate can depend on experimental separation of shared environment.

The breeder then selects parents with mean seed mass 10 g above the candidate mean, and both sexes contribute equally to the next generation. Using the half-sib estimate, expected response is 0.32×10=3.2 g. This prediction concerns the mean of offspring evaluated in comparable conditions. It does not predict the largest seed, the proportion of offspring exceeding the parental mean or the distribution of individual offspring without additional information about residual and segregation variance.

A subsequent trial finds that the selected line exceeds its previous mean by 5 g while a control line increases by 1 g. The adjusted response is 4 g and the realised estimate is 0.40. The difference between 0.32 and 0.40 should be interpreted in the context of the uncertainty of both estimates and the possibility of changing population composition. This result does not by itself establish a violation of Mendelian inheritance. It illustrates how empirical estimates can differ while retaining a common theoretical framework.

Finally, the breeder grows the new generation in a second environment and observes a reduction in absolute yield for all genotypes. The heritability estimate for the first environment does not specify the amount of this decline. It may help predict differences among individuals within that first environment, but the second environment may have a different additive variance, residual variance and ranking of genetic values. To predict selection response in the second environment, obtain relevant variance estimates or evidence about genetic relationships across conditions. The change of environment is a change in the prediction problem.

A student confronting a complex multiple-statement question should proceed in a consistent order. Identify the reference population and trait unit. Determine whether supplied quantities are variances, standard deviations, correlations, slopes or means. Write down the assumptions associated with the equation before substitution. Calculate the appropriate numerator and denominator separately. Check whether the answer is dimensionless or in trait units. Evaluate whether the numerical result is within the expected range of the idealized model and, if not, distinguish model incompatibility from biological interpretation.

These steps also help in graphical questions. In a reaction-norm graph, inspect axes and compare genotype-specific slopes rather than only the vertical ranking at one site. In a parent–offspring plot, distinguish a regression slope from a correlation coefficient and determine whether the predictor is a single parent or a midparent. In a selection experiment, identify whether the horizontal axis is generation number, cumulative differential or another quantity; only the correctly defined differential axis supports the simple realised slope interpretation. In a repeated-record graph, persistent resemblance does not isolate additive variation without genetic information.

The next conceptual question is why two populations with similar heritabilities can have entirely different genetic architectures. One may contain many alleles of small effect, while another may contain a small number of segregating alleles with relatively large effects. Both can produce the same additive variance because variance depends jointly on effects and frequencies. Environmental variation can further change their ratios. Heritability therefore provides a summary of variation and predictive resemblance, not a map of the chromosomes. The next node introduces marker-based evidence and quantitative trait loci to address the location of genomic contributors. This progression separates the population-statistical question from the genomic-location question, even though both are parts of quantitative genetics.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Heritability is the fraction of an individual caused by genes.

It is a ratio of variances for a defined population and environment. A measurement of one individual cannot be partitioned by substituting the population ratio.

Students confuse a population statistic with the phenotype of an organism. In a statement-based question, reject any wording that partitions a single person's or plant's measurement into percentages.

High heritability excludes a strong environmental effect.

A shared treatment can change the mean while leaving most within-environment variance genetic. Separate mean changes from variance partition.

The ratio describes within-population differences, not the magnitude of a shared treatment. In exam graphs, compare within-group spread separately from group means.

Low heritability means no genes are involved.

Essential genes may be fixed. Only segregating differences contribute to genetic variance.

A necessary gene can be invariant among individuals. A question about a fixed essential allele tests the distinction between genetic causation and segregating variance.

H² and h² are interchangeable.

Total genetic and additive variance differ. Sexual selection prediction uses the latter in the elementary model.

The two symbols look similar, but the numerators answer different questions. For a breeder’s equation problem, identify V_A rather than selecting the larger ratio.

A value given as h² should be squared again.

The square is part of the conventional symbol; 0.4 is inserted directly into R=h²S.

The notation resembles an instruction to calculate a square. In a numerical question, use the reported h² as the ratio; only take a square root when the model asks for h.

Dominance variance equals the heterozygote deviation squared.

Genotype frequencies weight the deviation. Under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions V_D=(2pqd)².

Students often substitute d directly for a variance component. In a one-locus question, inspect frequencies and square the weighted expression, not just d.

Biochemical epistasis implies zero additive variance.

Population regression can capture average allele effects even when proteins interact.

Molecular interaction and statistical partition are easily conflated. A question can contain an interacting pathway and substantial additive variation without contradiction.

G×E requires crossing reaction norms.

Nonparallel norms indicate interaction even without rank reversal.

Crossing lines are visually striking, so students overlook unequal slopes that do not cross. Compare changes across the same environmental intervals.

G×E and genetic–environmental covariance are the same.

One describes differential response; the other describes systematic association between deviations.

Both concepts mention genes and environments. In an allocation experiment ask whether genotype placement is associated with environment, and separately whether responses differ.

Single-parent and midparent slopes both equal h².

Under standard assumptions the single-parent slope is h²/2 and the midparent slope is h².

The factor of two is easily missed when the predictor is one parent rather than the average of both. Check the regression predictor before substituting a slope.

Twin correlations prove causality without assumptions.

Equal relevant environments and the specified genetic model are required.

Genetic similarity can be mistaken for experimental control. Look for equal relevant environments, mating assumptions, model limitations and measurement error.

Selected parent mean equals predicted offspring mean.

Selection differential includes nonadditive and environmental variation; only the predicted additive response shifts the mean.

Selection is often imagined as copying the selected phenotype. Subtract the base mean to obtain S and use h²S for the expected next-generation response.

Response equals the raw change between any two generations.

Environmental shifts and drift can mimic response; controls and replication improve inference.

Comparing generations without a contemporaneous control can hide a shared seasonal effect. Check whether the question requests raw phenotypic change or adjusted response.

Repeatability is necessarily genetic.

Stable environmental effects contribute to repeated-record resemblance.

Repeated performance seems like evidence of stable genes. Identify permanent environmental variation before assigning all covariance to genetic effects.

A fixed heritability predicts indefinite gain.

Selection alters allele frequencies and genetic variance; prediction is conditional and usually short-term.

A straight line fitted to the initial generations encourages extrapolation. Ask whether additive variance, environmental conditions and the selective differential remain comparable.

  3. 0.3 and 0.2
  4. 0.4 and approximately .267

Answer: D. Unadjusted V_A=4×3=12, h²=.4. Adjusted covariance=2, so V_A=8 and h²=8/30≈.267. The common environment had inflated the additive estimate.

SET and integrated practice

M1. Assertion: A highly heritable trait can be strongly altered by an environmental intervention. Reason: heritability concerns variation in a reference population, whereas a treatment may change its mean.
  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true

Answer: A. An environmental intervention can shift a mean even if most existing within-population variation was genetic. The reason directly explains the assertion.

M2. Assertion: Under the elementary model h² cannot exceed H². Reason: V_G includes V_A and nonnegative dominance and interaction components, with the same denominator.
  1. Both true but unrelated
  2. Both true and reason explains assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true

Answer: B. For nonnegative orthogonal components, V_G≥V_A. Division by positive V_P preserves this inequality. Estimates may violate the ideal bound because of sampling or misspecification.

M3. An experiment reports V_G=20, V_GE=12 and V_error=24. Three environments and two replicates per environment are independent. What is entry-mean broad-sense heritability under the stated model?
  1. 0.357
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.714
  4. 0.833

Answer: C. The denominator is 20+12/3+24/(3×2)=28. The ratio is 20/28≈0.714; 0.357 is the single-record ratio.

M4. The base mean is 50, selected parent mean 45, and h²=0.6. Predict the offspring mean under comparable conditions.
  1. 53
  2. 45
  3. 42
  4. 47

Answer: D. S=−5; R=−3, giving 47. Downward selection does not change the sign of heritability.

M5. Which statements are correct? I. A constant shift changes variance. II. A nonlinear transformation may change heritability. III. Genetic variance depends on allele frequency. IV. Heritability is a fraction of an individual's phenotype.
  1. II and III
  2. I and II
  3. I and IV
  4. III and IV

Answer: A. II and III are correct. A constant does not change deviations around the mean. Heritability is a population variance ratio, not an individual decomposition.

M6. Four families have midparent values 8,10,12,14 and offspring means 9,10,11,12. Estimate the regression slope.
  1. 0.25
  2. 0.5
  3. 1
  4. 2

Answer: B. The mean-centered cross-product sum is 10 and the x-squared sum is 20; b=0.5. The ideal midparent design estimates h² directly.

M7. In a twin study r_MZ=.80 and r_DZ=.30. Under the elementary ACE formula, which is the best interpretation?
  1. A=.50, C=.20, E=.30
  2. All three components are valid nonnegative values
  3. A=1, C=−.20, E=.20; assumptions or sampling require scrutiny
  4. Heritability is necessarily negative

Answer: C. A=2(.8−.3)=1, C=.6−.8=−.2, E=.2. A negative C is incompatible with a nonnegative ACE partition; alternative models and uncertainty should be examined.

M8. A repeated trait has V_G=15, V_PE=5 and V_TE=20. With four independent temporary records, what is the broad-sense ratio for their average?
  1. 0.375
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.80
  4. 0.60

Answer: D. Averaged temporary variance=20/4=5. Total variance of the average is 15+5+5=25. H²=15/25=.6; persistent environmental variance does not average away.

M9. A selected line improves from 30 to 33 while its control improves from 30 to 31. The selection differential is 5. Which realised estimate uses the control?
  1. 0.4
  2. 0.2
  3. 0.6
  4. 0.8

Answer: A. The adjusted response is (33−30)−(31−30)=2. Dividing by 5 gives .4. A raw selected-line calculation would give .6.

M10. Two environments give genotype A yields 50 and 30 and genotype B yields 42 and 34. Which conclusion is most defensible?
  1. There is no environmental effect
  2. The difference of differences is −12 for dry-minus-wet responses A minus B, demonstrating rank reversal in the cell means
  3. A is superior in every environment
  4. The means identify the causal drought-resistance gene

Answer: B. A changes by −20 and B by −8, giving −12. B is higher in the dry condition, and A is higher in wet conditions. The cell means do not establish molecular mechanism or statistical significance without replication.

Revision and mastery

Distinguish observed variance, total genetic variance and additive variance. State the model before partitioning variance. Use broad-sense heritability for total genetic variation and narrow-sense heritability for standard sexual selection predictions. Interpret regression, twin and repeated-record estimates within their assumptions. The next chapter uses molecular markers to ask which genomic regions contribute to quantitative variation; a heritability estimate alone does not identify those regions.

  • Can you derive V_P = V_G + V_E + 2 Cov(G,E)?
  • Can you calculate H² and h² with nonnegative orthogonal components?
  • Can you distinguish a reaction-norm interaction from environmental covariance?
  • Can you calculate and critically interpret a response to selection?

Essential terminology and formula recall

Phenotypic variance
V_P, squared-unit dispersion of measurements around the reference mean.
Breeding value
Predicted additive genetic deviation under the specified mating population.
Average substitution effect
α=a+d(q−p) for the elementary biallelic Hardy–Weinberg model.
Reaction norm
Expected phenotypic response of a genotype across environments.
Selection differential
S, difference between the selected-parent and candidate means, weighted appropriately for reproductive contribution.
Realised heritability
R/S, an empirical ratio conditional on a nonzero differential and credible adjustment for environmental change.
Repeatability
Expected correlation between repeated records under the stated model, including persistent genetic and non-genetic variation.
