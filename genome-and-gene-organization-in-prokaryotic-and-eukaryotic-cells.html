1. The problem of storing a living cell’s instructions

A bacterium may be only a few micrometres long, yet it must contain the instructions for building thousands of different proteins and RNAs. A human cell faces an even greater physical problem: the total length of its nuclear DNA, if extended into ordinary double helices, is roughly two metres in a typical diploid nucleus before DNA replication. Neither cell can solve the problem by randomly compressing DNA into a solid ball. A cell has to read selected instructions, copy its hereditary material and distribute complete sets of information to its descendants. The stored material must therefore be compact, protected, accessible and organized at the same time.

In the preceding subunit, we learned how cellular compartments and molecular motors organize activities in space. We now examine a different sort of organization: the arrangement of the information from which many of those cellular components arise. The nucleus described in 2.B.01 is the main location of hereditary information in most eukaryotic cells, whereas bacterial cells have no conventional membrane-enclosed nucleus. This difference affects where DNA is stored and how it is used, but both kinds of cells depend on the same basic chemical principle: information resides in the sequence of nucleotides.

There are two independent meanings of organization. The first is informational: which sequences occur, in what order, on which DNA molecule, and with which neighbors? The second is physical: how do those long molecules occupy three-dimensional space? A gene may have exactly the same base sequence before and after a chromosome changes shape. Thus compaction does not imply a change in genetic information. Conversely, replacing a single nucleotide changes information even when the chromosome remains visually identical. Throughout this chapter, keep these two questions separate and then ask how they influence each other.

The necessary chemical foundation is small. DNA is normally a double-stranded polymer; complementary bases pair across the strands, which run in opposite directions. The order of bases along either strand can be represented by letters. A base pair (bp) is one paired position, not two sequential positions. A 1,000 bp DNA duplex therefore contains approximately 2,000 nucleotides in its two strands. Kilobase pairs (kb) and megabase pairs (Mb) represent 10³ and 10⁶ base pairs, respectively. These are decimal units, not computer-storage units. When a genome is described as 4 Mb, the statement describes the number of paired positions, rather than the physical size of a cell or the number of genes.

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to move from a gene map to a chromosome map, identify bacterial and eukaryotic genomic compartments, interpret exceptions to textbook patterns, compute DNA lengths and gene densities, and distinguish genomic evidence from conclusions that require other experiments. These skills establish the foundation for understanding operons, interrupted genes, repeated sequences and chromatin in the following chapters.

2. From hereditary factors to genes, loci and gene products

Before molecular biology, a hereditary factor could be recognized by the way a characteristic was transmitted from one generation to the next. Such inheritance patterns reveal discrete units of heredity but do not by themselves identify the material carrying those units. Once chromosomes were connected with inheritance and DNA was established as the principal hereditary material of cellular organisms, it became possible to locate hereditary information along a molecular sequence rather than treating a gene only as an abstract factor. We can now ask which particular sequence produces a functional molecule and how its activity contributes to the cell.

In its useful molecular sense, a gene is a DNA region whose expression gives rise to a functional product, either an RNA or a polypeptide. A protein-coding gene produces RNA that can be translated into a chain of amino acids; an RNA gene produces an RNA whose function does not require translation. Ribosomal RNA provides the structural and catalytic core of ribosomes, and transfer RNA connects codons with amino acids. Neither is a protein-coding product. Consequently, counting only potential protein-coding sequences underestimates the number of functional genes.

A gene is not identical to its coding sequence (CDS). In a protein-coding gene the CDS specifies the polypeptide, including the termination signal according to the convention used to annotate it. Other transcribed sequences may remain untranslated in the mature RNA, and introns may interrupt the stretches that are retained in the mature transcript. A promoter is a DNA region that helps specify where and in which direction transcription begins; a regulatory sequence may influence when or how frequently a gene is expressed. Some regulatory elements lie outside the transcribed gene interval or act at a distance. Different annotation conventions may include different regulatory boundaries, so a numerical problem must define which interval is being measured.

A locus is a position or interval on a chromosome or other genetic molecule. The word is about location, not the chemical identity of a product. A locus can contain a protein-coding gene, a functional RNA gene, a marker sequence or some other feature. Alternative versions of a sequence at the same locus are alleles. For example, homologous chromosomes in a diploid individual may have a normal and an altered version of the same gene at corresponding positions. This is not necessarily two different genes. In contrast, two duplicated related genes at different loci can be separate members of a gene family. We will study the distinction more deeply in 2.C.05.

A chromosome is a physically organized hereditary DNA unit associated with proteins. A single unreplicated typical chromosome contains one continuous double-stranded DNA molecule. After DNA duplication, a typical eukaryotic chromosome consists of two sister chromatids, each containing its own DNA duplex. The chromosome is not a single enormous gene: many gene intervals and non-gene sequences lie along it. A map converts the continuous DNA sequence into positions at which features can be recognized.

Notice the hierarchy in the illustration: a chromosome contains loci, and the DNA sequence at a locus can be described as a particular allele. Changing an allele need not move the locus. Moving a DNA segment need not change the sequence within the gene. A gene map and a sequence comparison therefore answer different questions. This distinction is especially important when interpreting experimental observations that report the presence of a marker without measuring its expression.

3. What belongs to a genome?

The genome is the total hereditary sequence complement under a specified definition. For an organism with one chromosome per genome, the chromosome may account for most of the DNA. However, many genomes are divided among several separate molecules, and cells can contain additional genetic elements. A chromosome is consequently a component of a genome, not a synonym for the genome. The term nuclear genome refers specifically to DNA organized in nuclear chromosomes; mitochondrial genome refers to mitochondrial DNA; plastid genome refers to plastid DNA. If a study reports the whole DNA complement of a cell, it may include all of these. Always check the stated denominator when a question asks for genome size.

Genome size is often given for one complete set of chromosomes rather than the total DNA present in one cell at one instant. For a diploid species, two homologous sets are present in a typical somatic nucleus. When a human reference assembly contains roughly 3.1–3.2 billion base pairs, it represents approximately one nuclear genome equivalent rather than all DNA molecules in a diploid cell. Individual genomes differ in sequence and length, and different reference assemblies or conventions need not have the exact same totals. This is why estimates are better treated as rounded examples unless the problem names a specific reference.

The word gene count usually means the number of distinct annotated gene loci in a reference genome, not the number of physical copies in a diploid cell, and not the number of protein molecules made by the cell. A duplicated gene at a new genomic location can add a new locus; doubling all chromosome sets increases the physical copies without immediately adding new gene types. Similarly, an actively expressed gene may give rise to many RNA molecules without increasing DNA copy number. Gene count, transcript abundance and DNA content are three separate measurements.

A complete chromosome complement is a set of physical molecules, while the genome sequence is a representation of hereditary information. A chromosome map has coordinates assigned along each molecule. The coordinates are convenient human conventions, and the position labeled 1 in a circular chromosome sequence does not indicate a natural physical end. On a linear chromosome, coordinates have ends, but which direction is displayed is also a convention. A gene can be oriented in either direction relative to the chosen reference sequence. A map arrow shows a direction of transcription, not the direction in which a chromosome moves or the direction of cell division.

Consider a bacterium that possesses two different chromosomes and a plasmid. A complete chromosomal genome set includes both chromosomes. Ten plasmid molecules may be present in the same cell, yet these ten copies do not automatically imply ten different plasmid sequences. If there is one plasmid type, its sequence is counted once when describing distinct genomic content but ten times when determining the mass of that cell's DNA. Scientists make this distinction because the physical amount of DNA can change as a cell grows, while the characteristic genomic sequence complement may remain the same.

4. A bacterial chromosome occupies a nucleoid, not a nucleus

In the familiar model of Escherichia coli, the principal chromosome is a circular double-stranded DNA molecule occupying an irregular region called the nucleoid. A nucleoid has no enclosing lipid bilayer separating the genome from the cytoplasm. It is nevertheless a recognizable region enriched in DNA and its associated proteins. The nucleoid may change position, shape and degree of compaction as the cell grows. A DNA-rich region that does not possess a nuclear envelope should never be described as a miniature membrane-bound nucleus.

Why does a large chromosome form a localized region at all? The long polymer can interact with itself and with many DNA-binding proteins. The DNA backbone carries negative charge, which favors repulsion between nearby segments. Cellular ions and DNA-binding proteins can reduce electrostatic repulsion, bend the polymer and allow interactions between different segments. Further physical constraints arise from the twisting of DNA, contacts with other macromolecules and confinement by the boundaries of the cell. Together these effects produce a compact but dynamic DNA–protein assembly.

The nucleoid is not a homogeneous solid and its boundary is not a membrane. Its organization leaves many sites accessible to RNA polymerases and other DNA-interacting proteins. Ribosomes tend to be enriched outside dense DNA regions, although the distributions overlap and transcription and translation can occur in close association in bacteria. Absence of a conventional nuclear envelope makes this coupling possible, rather than proving every newly made bacterial RNA is translated immediately. Functional noncoding RNAs, for example, are not translated at all.

One consequence of this arrangement is that changes in chromosome topology or protein binding can alter access to genes without changing the DNA sequence itself. Another is that packaging must be continually reorganized when the chromosome is copied and when the cell divides. The biochemical machinery of those processes belongs to Unit 3 and the dynamics of microbial growth to 2.E; the essential point here is that a nucleoid is a functional organization of chromosomal DNA rather than a permanently fixed anatomical object.

DNA-specific staining or fluorescence can reveal the location of a nucleoid, but a bright region alone cannot reveal how many continuous DNA molecules are present or whether they are circular. Stain intensity may depend on DNA content, dye binding and imaging conditions. These limitations become important when experimental evidence is used to distinguish a chromosome from a plasmid or one chromosome from two.

5. How long is chromosomal DNA, and why must it be folded?

We can estimate the physical size of the packaging problem from the structure of B-form DNA. The rise per base pair along the helical axis is approximately 0.34 nanometres under ordinary B-DNA conditions. If a double helix has N base pairs and the rise is a, the contour length L is the sum of N small axial advances. Ignoring end effects, L = Na. Contour length is the length along the path of the helix if straightened without stretching its covalent backbone; it is not the distance between two ends of a folded molecule. For a circular molecule, the contour length is the circumference measured along the DNA path, even though the molecule has no free ends.

For a rounded 4.6 Mb bacterial chromosome, N = 4.6 × 10⁶ bp and a = 0.34 nm/bp. Multiplication gives 1.564 × 10⁶ nm. Since 1 μm is 1,000 nm, the same length is 1,564 μm, or 1.564 mm. A bacterium approximately 2 μm long contains a chromosome whose contour length is 782 times the long dimension of the whole cell. The ratio gives the magnitude of folding required; it is not a precise volume compaction ratio, because it compares two lengths and does not account for DNA thickness, shape, or nucleoid dimensions.

Now take a nuclear haploid genome of 3.2 × 10⁹ bp. Its B-DNA contour length is 1.088 × 10⁹ nm, equivalent to 1.088 m. A diploid G₁ nucleus with two such sets contains approximately 2.176 m of DNA, ignoring small differences between homologs and sex chromosomes. The DNA is distributed among multiple chromosomes, so this does not imply one uninterrupted two-metre chromosome. If the nuclear diameter is 10 μm, the ratio of total contour length to diameter is 217,600. This is only a comparison of scale; the nuclear DNA is not packed as a single straight rod passing through the nucleus.

One more useful quantitative relation links molecular length to mass. For double-stranded DNA, an average molar mass of 660 g per mole of base pairs is often used in elementary calculations. A genome of N bp has approximate molar mass 660N g/mol of genomes. The mass per individual genome is obtained by dividing by Avogadro's constant, approximately 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. For 4.6 Mb this gives (4.6 × 10⁶ × 660)/(6.022 × 10²³) = 5.04 × 10⁻¹⁵ g, or 5.04 femtograms. This is an average for one copy of the DNA sequence and excludes associated proteins. Base composition, chemical form and experimental calibration affect the exact mass.

For a haploid 3.2 Gb genome, the same formula yields about 3.51 × 10⁻¹² g (3.51 pg), and for two genome equivalents about 7.01 pg. If two cells from the same species contain different measured DNA amounts, that need not mean their gene repertoires differ. The difference may result from DNA replication, ploidy or organelle copy number. DNA mass is therefore informative only when the biological state and the measurement are specified.

6. How the bacterial chromosome folds while remaining accessible

To understand chromosome compaction, first imagine a flexible circular duplex with no protein attached. If its strands are intact and cannot rotate freely past one another, changing the number of helical turns imposes a constraint. The DNA can respond by changing local twist or by coiling its axis through space. This secondary coiling is called supercoiling. A negatively supercoiled DNA molecule has a linking number below that of its relaxed reference state. The tendency of the duplex to unwind slightly can assist local strand separation for transcription, although excessive or inappropriate supercoiling can also interfere with DNA functions.

The topological relationship for a closed duplex is Lk = Tw + Wr. The linking number Lk describes the total number of times the two closed strands are linked; twist Tw measures their winding around the local helical axis; writhe Wr describes the coiling of that axis in space. Lk is an integer for closed strands and cannot change continuously without strand breakage and passage or rotation. Twist and writhe can trade off while the linking number stays constant. This is why bending or coiling a closed DNA circle into a compact shape is not equivalent to breaking its backbone.

For relaxed B-DNA, an approximate helical repeat is 10.5 bp per turn. A closed circle of 2,100 bp has relaxed linking number Lk₀ ≈ 200. If its measured Lk is 188, ΔLk = 188 − 200 = −12 and the superhelical density σ = ΔLk/Lk₀ = −0.06. The negative sign means that its linking number is deficient relative to the relaxed reference. It does not mean the DNA has lost twelve base pairs or that precisely twelve superhelical crossings must appear in a micrograph, because the deficit is distributed between twist and writhe. In a linear DNA molecule with freely rotating ends, torsional strain can relax; in a constrained chromosomal loop, it can be retained even when the entire chromosome is linear.

Topoisomerases regulate this constraint by temporarily breaking and rejoining DNA. Bacterial DNA gyrase uses ATP to introduce negative supercoils; bacterial topoisomerase I can relax excessive negative supercoiling. These actions are balanced by transcription, replication and other processes that introduce torsional strain. The detailed strand-passage reaction and enzyme mechanisms belong to Unit 3. Here the structural implication is that chromosome folding and access to the sequence are actively maintained and cannot be explained solely by electrostatic condensation.

Supercoiling is only part of nucleoid organization. Nucleoid-associated proteins, or NAPs, bind bacterial DNA and alter its shape and interactions. For instance, HU can bend or wrap DNA, IHF bends selected sites strongly, Fis can bend DNA and participate in chromosome organization, and H-NS can bridge separated DNA segments or form extended nucleoprotein structures. Dps is important in protection of DNA in conditions such as starvation. The precise roles and abundance of these proteins depend on species and growth conditions. No single protein is a universal bacterial equivalent of the eukaryotic histone octamer.

A useful mental model combines local bending, supercoiled loops and bridging. If two segments are held together, the loop between them can behave approximately as a topological domain: twisting one constrained region need not instantly propagate throughout the chromosome. Proteins that connect or organize loops therefore help generate distinct local neighborhoods. Structural maintenance of chromosomes (SMC) complexes and related systems also contribute to large-scale chromosome organization in many bacteria. Their presence and specific mechanisms vary, so a generic model should not imply every species uses exactly the same set of architectural proteins.

Changes in packaging have functional consequences. If a DNA-bridging protein is removed, separated loci may interact less frequently and some local DNA regions may become more accessible. However, a larger fluorescent nucleoid could also arise from altered transcription, replication, cell size or ionic conditions. A good experimental test compares mutant and control cells at the same growth stage, measures DNA content independently and checks restoration of the phenotype when the affected protein is supplied again. The structural role is supported more strongly by these complementary observations than by a single image.

7. Circular chromosomes, linear exceptions and multipartite genomes

Most introductory diagrams use a bacterium with one circular chromosome because this arrangement is common and illustrates the absence of free ends clearly. It is not a definition of a prokaryote. A circular DNA chromosome has no terminal ends in its normal intact state; its sequence can still be written as a linear list by selecting an arbitrary starting coordinate. A linear chromosome has two physical ends, which must be maintained by mechanisms appropriate to that organism. The existence of linear bacterial chromosomes demonstrates that a nucleus is not required to maintain linear DNA.

For example, species of Streptomyces and Borrelia can possess linear chromosomes, while Vibrio cholerae carries two distinct circular chromosomes. Agrobacterium tumefaciens is a useful example of a bacterium with both circular and linear chromosomal DNA. These examples break different parts of the simplified model: chromosome shape, chromosome number, or coexistence of different shapes. The examples do not mean that circular bacterial chromosomes are rare or that every species of a genus has identical genome architecture.

Heidelberg and colleagues reported in 2000 that a sequenced strain of V. cholerae possessed two circular chromosomes of 2,961,146 bp and 1,072,314 bp. These two molecules contain complementary parts of the organism's chromosomal gene repertoire. They are not two homologous copies of a single chromosome: their sequences, sizes and gene content differ. A bacterium can thus possess a multipartite genome and still have one complete chromosome set per genome equivalent. If the cell holds several copies of that set during growth, the physical copy number is a different issue.

The data for the two Vibrio chromosomes provide a simple quantitative example. Their combined length is 4,033,460 bp, approximately 4.033 Mb. The smaller chromosome contributes 1,072,314/4,033,460 × 100 ≈ 26.6% of the combined chromosomal sequence. This calculation says nothing directly about the fraction of genes on it, because the genes may be distributed at unequal densities. Nor does it establish that 26.6% of cellular DNA is always on the smaller molecule: that depends on the relative copy numbers at the time of measurement.

Prokaryotes include both Bacteria and Archaea. Many archaeal genomes are circular and relatively gene-dense, and their DNA is likewise not enclosed in a conventional eukaryotic nucleus. However, many archaeal species possess histones related to eukaryotic histones; other species use different architectural proteins. Several archaea also maintain multiple genome copies. Therefore, it is misleading to equate prokaryotic organization with one specific species of bacterium or to say that histones are universally absent from prokaryotes.

The familiar statement that bacteria are haploid is best understood as a statement about a common genomic organization, in which one complete complement contains one copy of most loci. Rapid growth can cause partially overlapping rounds of chromosome duplication, yielding more than one copy of certain regions before division. Some bacteria are genuinely polyploid, maintaining multiple complete sets under ordinary growth conditions. A count of DNA foci or chromosomes alone cannot distinguish stable polyploidy from incomplete chromosome replication; the cell's growth state and the identity of the DNA molecules matter.

8. Extrachromosomal DNA and the logic of plasmid organization

A bacterial cell may contain DNA molecules in addition to its chromosome or chromosomes. A plasmid is a genetic element that is maintained as a distinct replicating DNA molecule, commonly but not always a small circular duplex. The distinction matters because plasmid-borne information can be gained or lost independently of much of the main chromosomal complement. A plasmid does not have to be physically enclosed in its own organelle; it coexists in the same cytoplasm as chromosomal DNA.

The principal chromosome normally carries a large share of the genes for basic cellular functions, such as RNA synthesis, translation and metabolism. Plasmids often encode traits whose usefulness depends on particular conditions, such as alternative metabolic capabilities or resistance to environmental stresses. This is a useful generalization, not an absolute rule. Some large plasmid-like replicons carry genes needed for essential functions in particular organisms, and the border between a megaplasmid and a secondary chromosome can require evidence about both gene content and maintenance. The size of the molecule is insufficient to determine its identity.

A plasmid must be copied and retained through successive generations if it is to remain in a lineage. A plasmid typically possesses a region permitting initiation of replication in a suitable host, but it depends on cellular machinery and may encode some of its own maintenance factors. High-copy and low-copy plasmids differ in the average number of physical molecules present. We do not need to study replication initiation or partitioning proteins here; it is sufficient to recognize that an independently maintained molecule can vary in abundance separately from the main chromosome.

Imagine two genetically similar bacteria that share a 4 Mb chromosome. One contains an additional plasmid with a gene enabling metabolism of an unusual substrate. In medium without that substrate, the plasmid may provide no advantage, and a cell lacking the molecule may still grow if the plasmid has no essential function under those conditions. In medium where the substrate is the only usable resource and the plasmid supplies the missing capability, plasmid-bearing cells may have an advantage. The function depends on the gene and its expression, not simply on the fact that DNA is circular.

Suppose a bacterium has one 4.0 Mb chromosome and six copies of a 10 kb plasmid. Its total DNA is 4,000 kb + 6 × 10 kb = 4,060 kb. The plasmid fraction of physical DNA is 60/4,060 × 100 = 1.48%. A nonredundant list of the distinct sequences instead contains 4,000 + 10 = 4,010 kb, assuming the plasmid has no shared sequences with the chromosome and the six copies are identical. In an experiment comparing DNA amounts, the first number is relevant; in a simple inventory of unique molecules, the second is relevant.

To test whether a phenotype is associated with a plasmid, one might compare strains with and without the element, check the presence of a marker sequence and examine whether the phenotype is restored by reintroducing the plasmid. Merely observing that a cell possesses an extra DNA band is insufficient to show that the band causes the phenotype: it could be correlated with another genetic difference. A controlled loss-and-restoration comparison links the DNA element to a functional consequence. Even then it does not by itself identify the specific gene responsible.

9. The organization of eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes

Unlike the bacterial nucleoid, the eukaryotic nucleus surrounds most genomic DNA with a double membrane. The nuclear pores provide selective communication with the cytoplasm. This physical separation allows RNA transcription and many RNA-processing steps to take place inside the nucleus, whereas most translation takes place in the cytoplasm. Nuclear DNA is distributed across distinct, usually linear chromosomes, each comprising DNA associated with numerous proteins. The DNA–protein complex is called chromatin.

Chromatin is not something that appears only when a cell divides. It is the normal state of nuclear chromosome material in both interphase and dividing cells. During interphase, different chromosomes generally occupy preferred spatial regions rather than remaining uniformly mixed as completely free molecules. Parts of the same chromosome may be brought close by folding, even though they lie far apart in base-pair coordinates. Many sequences remain accessible for transcription and other activities. In mitosis, most chromosomes become highly condensed, making their individual shapes easier to recognize using microscopy. The transition changes physical arrangement rather than creating chromosomes from previously unorganized DNA.

The core structural principle of chromatin is association with histones, proteins that permit DNA to be wrapped and folded at multiple scales. At the elementary level a nucleosome contains DNA wrapped around a core of histones; the DNA between neighboring cores is called linker DNA. The association reduces the spatial extent of a long DNA molecule and creates a repeatable packaging unit. However, compaction is not simply increased to the maximum possible level. Histone association, local folding and other protein interactions create regions with different levels of accessibility. Detailed histone composition, protection experiments and higher-order packing are reserved for 2.C.06 and 2.C.07.

Linear chromosomes also require special structural landmarks. Telomeres protect chromosome ends from being treated as ordinary broken DNA ends; centromeres help specify the region where chromosome-segregation machinery connects during cell division. A chromosome arm is the region extending from a centromere toward an end. These features permit the cell to maintain individual DNA molecules and distribute them reliably. Their detailed morphology and chromosome types will be developed in 2.C.08; here they explain why the linear organization requires more than just a long chain of genes.

When an unreplicated chromosome is duplicated, it consists of two sister chromatids. Each sister contains one DNA duplex representing a copied chromosome sequence. During the stage when the sisters are joined, the pair is ordinarily counted as one replicated chromosome. This distinction prevents a frequent examination error: doubling DNA amount during S phase does not immediately double the chromosome number. Likewise, a homologous chromosome pair is not equivalent to a pair of sister chromatids. Homologs are corresponding chromosomes in separate sets, typically carrying the same kinds of loci but possibly different alleles; sisters are products of chromosome duplication.

Chromosome number is not a measure of the total amount of DNA or biological complexity. Splitting a long DNA molecule into two maintained chromosomes could increase chromosome number without greatly increasing the number of genes. Fusion could decrease the number without eliminating most of the sequence. Species differ in their characteristic chromosome complements, and changes in chromosome number may arise by several mechanisms. The purpose here is not to study chromosome evolution in detail, but to avoid treating chromosome number, genome size and gene number as interchangeable quantities.

10. Genomes outside the eukaryotic nucleus

When eukaryotes are described as possessing linear chromosomes, the statement usually refers to nuclear DNA. Mitochondria contain their own genetic material in many eukaryotes, and plastids contain DNA in photosynthetic eukaryotes and many other plastid-containing organisms. These organelles retain parts of the genomic legacy of their bacterial ancestors. The overwhelming majority of proteins required for their functions, however, are encoded in the nucleus in familiar plant and animal cells. The organelle therefore has a genome without being genetically independent of the cell.

In mammals the mitochondrial genome is a small circular DNA molecule containing genes for some respiratory-chain components and mitochondrial RNAs. Numerous copies can occur in one cell. Their physical arrangement in mitochondrial nucleoids involves DNA-binding proteins, unlike the histone-based packaging characteristic of nuclear chromosomes. Plastid genomes often have a circular reference map, but their physical DNA molecules can occur in several forms. Across diverse eukaryotes, organelle genomes may be circular, linear or multipartite. The word organelle does not determine DNA topology.

The compartment in which a protein functions does not establish where its gene lies. A mitochondrial enzyme may be encoded by a nuclear chromosome, synthesized in the cytoplasm and imported into mitochondria. Conversely, some mitochondrial gene products are synthesized inside the organelle. To locate a gene, we need genomic evidence, not just a protein-localization image. This principle links chromosome organization with the organelle targeting material studied in 2.B.07 and 2.B.08.

If a cell has a 3,000 Mb haploid nuclear genome, is diploid and unreplicated, and contains 500 mitochondrial DNA molecules of 16 kb each, the nuclear DNA amounts to 6,000 Mb while mitochondrial DNA contributes 8,000 kb = 8 Mb. The mitochondrial fraction of the total DNA, in this hypothetical cell, is 8/(6,000 + 8) × 100 ≈ 0.133%. Cells with the same nuclear genotype may have different mitochondrial copy numbers and therefore different total DNA content. Mitochondrial abundance cannot be inferred solely from ploidy.

Some specialized cells lose their nucleus during differentiation; mature mammalian erythrocytes are a familiar example and also lose mitochondria. This is an exception at the level of individual cell type, not evidence that mammals lack nuclear genomes. Likewise, some organisms or lineages have highly reduced organelles and may lose organelle DNA. Exam questions must specify the kind of cell being compared rather than assuming that every mature cell contains every genomic compartment typical of its species.

11. Reading a gene map: intervals, strands and transcription units

Having located the genome in the cell, we can now examine how genetic information is arranged within one DNA molecule. A gene map usually represents one reference strand as a horizontal coordinate axis. A right-pointing arrow denotes a gene whose transcription runs toward increasing coordinates; a left-pointing arrow denotes the opposite direction. Both genes are present in the same double-stranded molecule. RNA polymerase uses one of the two strands as template for each transcription unit, and the strand chosen for one gene need not be the template for its neighbor.

Consider a hypothetical interval in which gene A occupies positions 101 through 400 and gene B occupies positions 501 through 800. Gene A is drawn pointing right, gene B pointing left. Both intervals have 300 bp when coordinates are inclusive: the number of integers from a to b is b − a + 1. The positions 401 through 500 constitute a 100 bp intergenic interval. Whether the genes share a regulatory feature cannot be inferred simply from their proximity; orientation and transcript evidence are needed.

If arrows point toward each other, they are convergent; if arrows point away from a shared region, they are divergent; if both point in the same direction, they are co-oriented. These terms describe geometry. A co-oriented arrangement may be compatible with a single transcription unit, but there could instead be independent promoters and transcripts. Divergent genes may have nearby regulatory regions, yet this does not prove that the same protein regulates both. Gene maps identify possible arrangements; transcription measurements are needed to decide which arrangement is actually used.

A transcription unit is the DNA interval that is transcribed into an RNA from a particular initiation site to its endpoint. In many bacteria, a single RNA can contain coding information for multiple polypeptides. Such an arrangement can form part of an operon, in which linked genes are transcribed together under shared control. A transcript containing coding information for several polypeptides is called polycistronic. Many bacterial genes, however, are independently transcribed. Many eukaryotic nuclear protein-coding genes are predominantly transcribed as individual units, but there are exceptions including operon-like arrangements in some organisms and organelles. Thus gene count and transcription-unit count need not be equal in either group.

The next chapter will examine the promoter, operator and structural genes of classic operons. Here we only establish the logic required for that discussion: gene orientation, physical adjacency and the number of RNA products are distinct observations. If a gene diagram shows two separate arrows and an RNA assay shows a long RNA spanning both, the combination supports joint transcription under the tested conditions. If shorter RNAs also occur, they might represent independent transcription starts, RNA processing or degradation; the map alone does not decide between those explanations.

12. Coding and noncoding regions: the genome is not a collection of only protein instructions

When reading a genome map, a beginner may expect every base to belong to a protein-coding sequence. In reality, the genome includes different kinds of information as well as sequences whose function is unknown or not established. Protein-coding segments provide triplets that specify amino acids or termination. Functional RNA genes produce RNAs that are not translated. Regulatory DNA can alter the expression of nearby or distant genes. Other sequence elements contribute to chromosome structure, such as regions important for chromosome ends or segregation. Repeated sequences may have structural, regulatory, evolutionary or no known function depending on the particular sequence.

Noncoding DNA means DNA not translated into a polypeptide. It does not mean DNA that is never transcribed, and it does not mean DNA that is useless. An rRNA gene is non-protein-coding but transcribed into an essential RNA. An intron is transcribed into a primary RNA in a typical interrupted gene but is removed from the mature RNA. A promoter need not be part of the mature RNA to influence expression. At the same time, it would be equally mistaken to claim that every noncoding base must have a selected biological function. Detectable transcription or protein binding is evidence of activity, but does not on its own establish that a particular sequence is required for fitness or an organismal phenotype.

Noncoding and intergenic are also different terms. Intergenic DNA lies between annotated gene intervals, whereas intragenic noncoding DNA lies inside a gene interval, as in an untranslated region or an intron. A single interval may be classified differently depending on the transcript or overlapping genes under discussion. Therefore a careful genome analysis distinguishes the total lengths of gene spans, their protein-coding parts and the union of intervals that overlap. It does not subtract the same overlapping bases twice.

Many bacterial genomes are compact in the informational sense: a large fraction of their sequence is occupied by genes, and intergenic intervals are frequently short. This does not imply that a bacterial genome lacks regulatory DNA, RNA genes or repeats. Many eukaryotic nuclear genomes possess longer introns and intergenic regions and more extensive repetitive DNA, but compact eukaryotic genomes also exist. Yeasts are a useful reminder that some eukaryotes have relatively dense genes. Some bacteria with reduced genomes have a high proportion of nonfunctional remnants or unusual gene arrangements. Averages are useful for comparison, not definitions.

One simple example clarifies how these fractions differ. Suppose a gene spans 1,000 bp from its transcription start to end. Of this, 300 bp are intronic and 118 bp of the retained exons are untranslated. The exonic length is 1,000 − 300 = 700 bp; the coding length, under the stated convention, is 700 − 118 = 582 bp. If the coding interval includes a single terminal stop codon and contains no internal stop, 582/3 = 194 codons specify 193 amino acids and one stop signal. The numerical purpose is to show that genomic span, retained exon length, coding length and polypeptide length are distinct quantities.

Genome comparisons sometimes report the fraction of bases lying within annotated genes instead of the fraction within coding sequences. Suppose a 20 Mb genome has 8 Mb of gene spans with no overlap, of which 3 Mb are CDS and 5 Mb are introns and untranslated or RNA-gene sequence. Forty percent of the genome lies within genes, but only fifteen percent is protein-coding. The remaining twelve Mb is intergenic under that annotation. These three fractions answer distinct questions and must sum correctly: 3 + 5 + 12 = 20 Mb. If a second annotation extends gene boundaries, the estimated gene-span fraction may increase without any mutation in the DNA. A well-posed comparison specifies annotation conventions and denominator before assigning biological meaning.

Ultimately, a genome's informational organization is described at several levels. Some sequences occur once, others many times; some genes are related by duplication; some mobile sequences can occupy different locations. These categories can overlap. A repeated segment can itself be functional, and a gene family need not consist of identical copies. The next four chapters will distinguish those categories systematically so that repetition, duplication and interruption are not confused.

13. Genome size, gene density and coding fraction

To compare genomes quantitatively, we first need to choose a common unit. Let G denote the number of annotated genes in a haploid or otherwise defined reference genome, and S its size in megabase pairs. Gene density D is G/S, expressed as genes per Mb. The reciprocal, S/G, is the mean amount of genome sequence per annotated gene; if S is in Mb, multiplying by 1,000 gives kb per gene. This is an average including coding and noncoding regions. It is not mean gene length, because many bases occur outside annotated gene intervals.

Coding fraction asks a different question. Let C be the number of distinct genomic base pairs covered by protein-coding intervals and N be the number of genomic base pairs in the specified genome. The coding fraction is f = C/N and the percentage is 100f. We use the union of coding positions so that overlapping intervals are counted once. When a problem provides nonoverlapping coding lengths, their sum equals C. If it provides the mean protein-coding length only for protein-coding genes, multiply that mean by the number of such genes, not by the number of all RNA and protein-coding genes combined.

Consider two illustrative genomes, P and Q. Genome P is 4 Mb and contains 3,600 genes, of which 3,400 encode proteins with an average coding length of 900 bp. Assuming nonoverlapping coding regions, gene density is 3,600/4 = 900 genes/Mb; mean genome sequence per gene is 4,000/3,600 = 1.11 kb. Its total protein-coding length is 3,400 × 900 = 3,060,000 bp, corresponding to 76.5% of its genome. Gene density describes the number of annotations; coding fraction describes their occupancy by coding bases.

Genome Q is 120 Mb and contains 18,000 genes, of which 16,000 are protein-coding with a mean coding length of 1,500 bp. Its gene density is 18,000/120 = 150 genes/Mb and mean sequence per gene is 120,000/18,000 ≈ 6.67 kb. The total coding length is 16,000 × 1,500 = 24 Mb, giving a coding fraction of 20%. Q has five times as many genes as P, yet its gene density is only one-sixth as high. Larger genome size need not imply a larger fraction of protein-coding sequence.

Genome R illustrates why density cannot be derived directly from coding fraction. Q and R can have the same genome size and coding fraction but different gene counts if the mean coding interval differs. With 8,000 protein-coding genes occupying 24 Mb, for example, R would have an average coding length of 3,000 bp under the nonoverlap assumption. Identical coding fractions do not establish identical gene numbers, expression programs or biological capabilities.

In real data, human nuclear DNA has roughly three billion base pairs and on the order of twenty thousand protein-coding genes; only a small percentage directly encodes polypeptides. An approximate count of 20,000 protein-coding genes divided by 3,200 Mb gives about 6.25 protein-coding genes per Mb. That is not the density of all gene types, because it excludes RNA genes. Many bacterial genomes contain thousands of genes within only a few megabases. The comparison reveals a large difference in organization, but the exact values depend on species, reference assembly and annotation conventions.

14. The C-value problem and the difference between genome size and complexity

Before large genome sequences were available, scientists could estimate nuclear DNA amounts by quantitative staining or other biochemical measurements. They found that the amount of DNA in a complete unreplicated haploid chromosome complement, called the C-value, varies enormously among eukaryotes. In some comparisons, organisms with apparently simpler body organization possess considerably more nuclear DNA than organisms with more elaborate organs or cell types. This observation contradicted the intuitive expectation that total DNA amount directly measures the number of functional genes or biological complexity.

The original C-value paradox becomes easier to understand when we remember the different types of DNA. A genome can expand through the accumulation of repeated elements, duplication of large intervals or whole chromosome sets, and changes in the lengths of introns and intergenic regions. Not every additional base encodes a new protein. Similarly, a small genome can support many functions through the coordinated expression and interaction of its products. The amount of DNA and the diversity of cellular activities therefore cannot be converted into one another by a fixed formula.

Many genome-size differences are associated with changes in repetitive DNA, and gene duplication and polyploidy are important in some lineages. Yet no one mechanism explains all differences among eukaryotes. Genome-size variation remains a biological question involving both sequence gain and sequence loss. Describing noncoding DNA as the complete explanation for every case would be too strong, just as it would be wrong to infer that large genomes are inefficient simply because they have a low coding percentage.

The C-value is not the same thing as DNA content per cell. In a conventional diploid cell cycle, the unreplicated somatic nucleus contains two haploid genome equivalents (2C). After the nuclear DNA has been copied it contains 4C while ploidy remains diploid. In a typical haploid gamete the nuclear DNA content is 1C. This convention does not count mitochondrial DNA as part of the nuclear C-value. Polyploid organisms require additional care because the monoploid basic chromosome set and the haploid gametic chromosome complement can differ. Unless a question defines an unusual convention, treat C as the DNA amount in the specified unreplicated haploid complement.

For example, two diploid species have unreplicated somatic DNA contents of 4 pg and 20 pg. Their C-values are approximately 2 pg and 10 pg respectively. The second has five times the nuclear DNA per haploid complement, but this does not establish five times as many genes or five times as many chromosomes. To test whether repeated DNA accounts for much of the difference, one would need sequence-composition evidence or other experiments that distinguish repeated and unique sequences. This is the intellectual link to 2.C.04.

15. How scientists distinguish chromosome structure from appearance

Three questions need different types of evidence: where is DNA located, how many distinct molecules form the genome, and what are their sequence arrangements? Microscopy with DNA-specific staining can address the first. It reveals the position of a nucleoid or the distribution of DNA in a nucleus, but close molecules may not be resolved individually. Molecular maps and DNA separation techniques address the second; a sequence assembly and annotation address the third. Strong evidence usually combines information from more than one level.

John Cairns investigated bacterial DNA using autoradiography in the early 1960s. Cells incorporated radioactive thymidine into newly synthesized DNA. After careful release of the DNA, radioactive emissions produced a detectable pattern in photographic material. The resulting images supplied direct physical evidence consistent with circular DNA in E. coli, including structures interpreted as replication intermediates. The importance of the work was that the bacterial chromosome could be studied as a long physical molecule instead of being inferred only from inherited traits. The images did not imply that DNA from every bacterial species must form a circle.

Autoradiography has important interpretive limitations. An extracted DNA molecule can break or relax during handling; overlapping DNA paths in two dimensions can resemble crossings. A fragment that appears linear may be part of a previously circular molecule. The quality of the preparation and agreement between multiple molecules matter. A sensible comparison includes preparations with expected DNA structures and conditions that minimize fragmentation. The full replication mechanism supported by the classic observations will be developed in Unit 3; here the focus is the physical arrangement of the molecule.

A complementary approach uses DNA fragments produced by cutting molecules at known sequence sites. For an ideal circular DNA molecule with exactly one restriction site, complete cleavage at that site produces one linear fragment of the same total length. For an ideal linear molecule with exactly one internal site, cleavage gives two fragments whose lengths sum to the original length. The distinction follows directly from the geometry: cutting one point on a ring opens it without creating two independent pieces, whereas cutting an internal point on a line separates the path into two. This provides a useful reasoning model for experimental questions.

Suppose an isolated 12 kb molecule has one restriction site. After complete digestion a single 12 kb linear fragment is observed and the total DNA mass is recovered. The result supports a circular molecule under those assumptions. For a linear molecule cut 4 kb from one end, two fragments of 4 and 8 kb are expected. However, the number of gel bands need not equal the number of fragments: equal-sized fragments can migrate together, and small fragments may be undetected. Partial digestion and DNA damage may create misleading patterns. An appropriate interpretation includes a size marker, undigested sample, a digestion control, and, when necessary, additional independent cleavage patterns.

Physical mapping and separation of very large DNA molecules also helped establish multipartite bacterial genomes. Ordinary separation methods are poorly suited to intact megabase DNA because very large molecules may migrate similarly or remain trapped. Pulsed-field electrophoresis changes the direction of the electric field periodically so that large linear DNA fragments reorient at different rates. It permits informative size separation in the appropriate preparation. In V. cholerae, physical mapping evidence for two unique chromosomes was later reinforced by the chromosome sequences reported in 2000. Neither two bands nor two DNA-rich foci alone would have established two complete independent chromosomes.

Sequencing and annotation now permit direct calculation of base counts, gene positions, orientation and sequence composition. Nevertheless, an incomplete assembly may have missing or collapsed repeated regions, and an annotation can misidentify an open reading frame as a gene or miss an RNA gene. An open reading frame is a stretch of potentially translatable sequence in a particular reading frame; evidence is needed to decide whether it produces a functional product. A reference assembly is not a perfect representation of every individual cell. These qualifications are sufficient for interpreting basic genome data without moving into advanced genome sequencing methods.

16. Integrating physical and informational organization

We can now compare the typical bacterial and eukaryotic arrangements without treating either as universal. In bacteria, genetic information is often concentrated in a compact chromosomal genome, generally held within a nucleoid. Its DNA is associated with architectural proteins, regulated supercoiling and other organization systems. Genes may be close together and transcribed in linked groups. Independent plasmids sometimes supply additional functions. These are related features, but none follows automatically from any single one. A circular chromosome does not prove that it contains operons, and a plasmid can possess either a simple or a more complex gene arrangement.

In eukaryotes, nuclear DNA is usually partitioned among linear chromosomes, packed into chromatin and physically separated from most translation. Many protein-coding genes contain introns, and considerable DNA may occur between genes or in repeated sequences. Nuclear chromosomes have specialized ends and segregation landmarks, while mitochondria and plastids may retain separate genomes. This organization permits access and regulation at multiple structural scales. However, gene density varies widely between eukaryotes, and highly compact genomes are possible.

To reason from an unfamiliar example, begin with the observation instead of the name of the organism. If two DNA molecules have different essential gene sets, they could be distinct chromosomes belonging to one complete genome. If two DNA molecules have the same loci in corresponding order but different alleles, they may represent homologous copies. If a small DNA molecule is maintained independently and carries an accessory trait, it is consistent with a plasmid, but size alone is not decisive. If a region is not translated, ask whether it is an RNA gene, a regulatory element, an intron, structural DNA or a sequence without known function before assigning its significance.

For another comparison, imagine that a cell with a known circular chromosome is treated so that a DNA-binding protein no longer bridges distant segments. The DNA sequence and number of loci remain the same, but the probability of contact between particular regions can fall. A transcription assay might then reveal changes at only some loci. That result is consistent with structural effects on local access rather than wholesale loss of hereditary material. To separate direct effects from indirect stress responses, compare the same growth state and test whether restoring the protein restores both organization and expression. The same logic applies to a eukaryotic chromatin alteration: a change in DNA accessibility is not a demonstration that a DNA sequence was deleted. This distinction between structure and sequence is the conceptual foundation of later chromatin chapters.

Finally, keep the physical scales separate. A gene sequence is measured in base pairs; genome size is the sum of specified sequence lengths; DNA content includes copies; gene density has genes per unit length; compaction describes spatial organization. A good answer identifies which quantity changes and which does not. A change in DNA folding can alter access without altering base-pair count. A change in plasmid copy number can alter DNA content without changing the distinct repertoire of plasmid genes. Doubling nuclear DNA before cell division changes C-content without immediately changing ploidy. These distinctions make later chromosome and gene-expression questions far easier to interpret.

The subsequent chapter, Operons and the Organization of Prokaryotic Genes, follows naturally from the gene-map problem. We have already learned how to recognize co-oriented genes and why physical adjacency does not prove shared transcription. We can now ask how neighboring genes can be organized into a single functional transcriptional unit and which DNA regions define its control.

17. Original Part B practice

These original examination-style questions use synthetic data unless a real example is explicitly named. Select the strongest answer under the stated assumptions, then examine the reasoning.

B1. Which definition best describes a molecular gene?

  1. Only a sequence translated into protein
  2. A region expressed to yield a functional RNA or polypeptide
  3. Any DNA molecule in a cell
  4. A chromosome position irrespective of sequence
Answer and explanation

B. A region expressed to yield a functional RNA or polypeptide — A gene can encode a functional RNA or a polypeptide. A excludes RNA genes; C confuses genes with whole molecules; D defines a locus.

B2. Which statement about a nucleoid is correct?

  1. It is enclosed by a double nuclear membrane
  2. It contains only naked DNA
  3. It is a DNA-rich organized region without a conventional nuclear envelope
  4. It is a bacterial ribosome
Answer and explanation

C. It is a DNA-rich organized region without a conventional nuclear envelope — The nucleoid comprises DNA and associated components without a nuclear envelope. A confuses nucleus and nucleoid; B ignores proteins; D confuses information storage and translation.

B3. Which statement is supported by the V. cholerae example?

  1. All bacteria have two circular chromosomes
  2. Two distinct chromosomes may constitute a single complete genome set
  3. Two circular chromosomes necessarily mean diploidy
  4. All plasmids are chromosomes
Answer and explanation

B. Two distinct chromosomes may constitute a single complete genome set — Two different molecules form the genome complement. A universalizes one species, C confuses different chromosomes with homologous sets, and D ignores replicon identity.

B4. A functional tRNA gene is best classified as:

  1. Protein coding
  2. Non-protein-coding and transcribed
  3. Intergenic by definition
  4. An untranslated DNA molecule with no function
Answer and explanation

B. Non-protein-coding and transcribed — Its product is a functional RNA. A confuses expression with translation; C is false because the locus is a gene; D denies an established function.

B5. Which property establishes a locus?

  1. Its physical position
  2. Its protein abundance
  3. The size of the entire genome
  4. Whether it is transcribed at this moment
Answer and explanation

A. Its physical position — A locus is a position or interval. B and D describe expression, and C describes a much larger quantity.

B6. Which bacterial protein is associated with introducing negative DNA supercoils?

  1. DNA gyrase
  2. Histone H1
  3. RNA ribosomal subunit
  4. Tubulin
Answer and explanation

A. DNA gyrase — Gyrase is an ATP-dependent topoisomerase. Histone H1 is linker associated in eukaryotic chromatin; ribosomal RNA and tubulin are not DNA gyrase.

B7. Which comparison is usually valid?

  1. Nucleoid equals nucleus
  2. Eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes are generally linear
  3. All organelle DNA is linear
  4. Archaea never contain histones
Answer and explanation

B. Eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes are generally linear — Most eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes are linear. A ignores compartment boundaries, C ignores diversity of organelle genomes, D ignores archaeal histones.

B8. Assertion: In a typical replicated eukaryotic chromosome, the amount of DNA per chromosome is twice that before replication. Reason: Replication produces two joined sister chromatids, each containing one DNA duplex.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true and reason explains assertion — Replication creates two sister DNA duplexes from one, doubling DNA amount per replicated chromosome while the sisters remain joined. B fails the explanatory link; C and D falsely reject one of the true statements.

B9. Which is NOT equivalent to protein-coding fraction?

  1. Unique coding positions divided by genome size
  2. Percentage of DNA that codes for polypeptides
  3. Number of genes per Mb
  4. Coding bp divided by total bp
Answer and explanation

C. Number of genes per Mb — Genes per Mb is density, not occupancy. A, B and D all describe coding fraction under the specified counting convention.

B10. Which statement about plasmids is most defensible?

  1. They must always be circular
  2. Their genes are always dispensable
  3. They can occur in varying copy numbers independently of the chromosome
  4. They cannot encode RNAs
Answer and explanation

C. They can occur in varying copy numbers independently of the chromosome — Plasmid abundance can differ. A ignores linear plasmids; B is too absolute; D wrongly rules out RNA genes.

18. Original Part C practice

These original examination-style questions use synthetic data unless a real example is explicitly named. Select the strongest answer under the stated assumptions, then examine the reasoning.

C1. A 3.9 Mb double-stranded DNA molecule is in B form. Its contour length is closest to:

  1. 1.326 μm
  2. 1.326 mm
  3. 13.26 mm
  4. 2.652 mm
Answer and explanation

B. 1.326 mm — 3.9 × 10⁶ × 0.34 nm = 1.326 × 10⁶ nm = 1.326 mm. A misses a factor of 1,000; C adds a factor of 10; D double-counts strands.

C2. A closed 4,200 bp DNA circle has relaxed helical repeat 10.5 bp/turn and Lk = 380. What is its superhelical density?

  1. −0.05
  2. +0.05
  3. −20
  4. +20
Answer and explanation

A. −0.05 — Lk₀ = 4,200/10.5 = 400; ΔLk = −20; σ = −20/400 = −0.05. B reverses the sign, C and D report linking-number differences rather than normalized density.

C3. A bacterium has 5 Mb of chromosomal DNA and eight copies of a 20 kb plasmid. What fraction of cellular DNA is plasmid DNA?

  1. 0.40%
  2. 3.10%
  3. 16%
  4. 3.20%
Answer and explanation

B. 3.10% — Plasmid DNA = 160 kb; total = 5,160 kb; 160/5,160 × 100 ≈ 3.10%. A ignores copy number; C confuses copies with percentage; D divides by chromosome alone.

C4. Genome X has 5,000 genes in 10 Mb; genome Y has 10,000 genes in 100 Mb. Which statement is correct?

  1. Y has double the density of X
  2. X has five times the density of Y
  3. Both have identical density
  4. Y has necessarily more coding DNA as a fraction
Answer and explanation

B. X has five times the density of Y — X has 500 genes/Mb, Y has 100 genes/Mb. A and C contradict these calculations; D cannot be inferred without coding lengths.

C5. A 6 Mb genome has 5,000 protein-coding genes averaging 960 bp of nonoverlapping coding sequence. What is the coding fraction?

  1. 80%
  2. 83.3%
  3. 48%
  4. 8%
Answer and explanation

A. 80% — 5,000 × 960 = 4.8 Mb; 4.8/6 × 100 = 80%. B uses an inappropriate rounded length, C treats Mb as percent, D introduces a tenfold error.

C6. Gene A occupies inclusive coordinates 100–499 and B occupies 550–949. What are their lengths and the gap?

  1. 399 bp each; 51 bp
  2. 400 bp each; 50 bp
  3. 400 bp each; 51 bp
  4. 399 bp each; 50 bp
Answer and explanation

B. 400 bp each; 50 bp — Each length = end − start + 1 = 400; gap positions 500–549 total 50. The other choices omit an endpoint or count a gene boundary as intergenic.

C7. After cutting a purified DNA molecule at its one unique site, two fragments of 3 kb and 7 kb appear, all DNA recovered. Which model fits most simply?

  1. One intact 10 kb circular molecule cut once
  2. One 10 kb linear molecule cut internally
  3. A 3 kb circular molecule only
  4. A 7 kb plasmid necessarily
Answer and explanation

B. One 10 kb linear molecule cut internally — A single internal cut in a linear molecule yields two fragments. A would yield one 10 kb fragment; C and D omit observed DNA, and size alone does not prove plasmid identity.

C8. A mutant has a visibly larger nucleoid than wild type. The same total DNA mass and cell size are measured. Complementing the missing DNA-binding protein restores compaction. What is most supported?

  1. The mutant has necessarily doubled its genome
  2. The protein contributes to nucleoid organization
  3. The protein must be a histone octamer
  4. The mutant must lack transcription
Answer and explanation

B. The protein contributes to nucleoid organization — Matched DNA content and restoration support an organizational role. A contradicts measured mass; C and D exceed the evidence.

C9. Two co-oriented genes lie 40 bp apart. What further finding most directly supports joint transcription?

  1. Both are present in the genome
  2. One continuous RNA spans both coding intervals under the tested condition
  3. The organism lacks a nucleus
  4. Both genes have the same length
Answer and explanation

B. One continuous RNA spans both coding intervals under the tested condition — A spanning transcript gives evidence that their sequences occur in one RNA. A, C and D are compatible with separate transcripts and do not establish an operon.

C10. A diploid G₁ nucleus has 12 pg of nuclear DNA. Its G₂ nucleus has 24 pg. Which explanation is most consistent?

  1. The number of chromosome sets necessarily doubled
  2. Chromosome duplication raised DNA content while ploidy remained unchanged
  3. The nuclear genome has permanently gained 12 pg of novel sequence
  4. Mitochondrial DNA was necessarily lost
Answer and explanation

B. Chromosome duplication raised DNA content while ploidy remained unchanged — G₁ is 2C and G₂ 4C in the conventional diploid cycle. A confuses ploidy and replication; C confuses copy number with new information; D is unrelated.

C11. A genome has two circles with disjoint essential gene sets. A second strain has two copies of the first circle but none of the second. Which is correct?

  1. The strains have the same complete gene complement
  2. The second strain lacks the essential loci unique to the missing circle
  3. The first strain is necessarily diploid
  4. The second strain must have more distinct genes
Answer and explanation

B. The second strain lacks the essential loci unique to the missing circle — Copying one chromosome cannot replace genes confined to another. A and D confuse copies and repertoire; C confuses distinct chromosomes with complete sets.

C12. Two species have identical gene counts but C-values of 1 pg and 8 pg. What is the strongest conclusion?

  1. The second has eight times the gene density
  2. Their coding fractions must match
  3. The second has eight times the DNA per unreplicated haploid complement
  4. The first lacks introns
Answer and explanation

C. The second has eight times the DNA per unreplicated haploid complement — C-value measures nuclear DNA amount per haploid complement. A reverses the density relationship; B requires coding lengths; D is not established.

C13. A 100 kb region has two coding intervals of 30 kb each, overlapping by 10 kb. What fraction is covered by these coding intervals?

  1. 60%
  2. 50%
  3. 40%
  4. 70%
Answer and explanation

B. 50% — The union is 30 + 30 − 10 = 50 kb, so 50%. A counts overlap twice, C subtracts it twice, D adds the overlap.

C14. A previously uncharacterized protein is detected exclusively in mitochondria. What does this establish about the genomic location of its encoding gene?

  1. It must be encoded by mtDNA
  2. Its DNA must be a plasmid
  3. Protein localization alone does not identify the encoding DNA; a nuclear gene can supply imported protein
  4. The nuclear genome cannot encode organelle proteins
Answer and explanation

C. Protein localization alone does not identify the encoding DNA; a nuclear gene can supply imported protein — Protein destination is distinct from gene location. A, B and D confuse cellular localization and genomic origin.
