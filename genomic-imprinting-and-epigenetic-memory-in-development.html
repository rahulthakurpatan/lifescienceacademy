1. The difference between possessing a gene and using it

An animal starts development as a cell containing genetic information from two parents. When the fertilized egg begins to divide, each daughter cell ordinarily inherits a complete set of chromosomes. As the embryo grows, cells in different locations adopt different roles. A cell in the future nervous system may produce a protein that is absent from a cell in the future skeleton. The difference does not generally arise because the skeletal cell has thrown away the gene for that protein. Instead, it arises because the two cells have different regulatory states. A regulatory state describes which genes can be used, which signals a cell can interpret, and which cellular programs are currently operating. Many aspects of developmental biology concern the origin, stability and eventual alteration of these states.

Genomic imprinting presents an additional distinction. Rather than comparing two different cells, compare two copies of one gene within a single cell. In a diploid mammalian cell, an autosomal locus normally has two homologous copies, one inherited through the egg and one through the sperm. Each copy is called an allele, although the two alleles can have identical base sequences. The transcriptional machinery does not always treat the two copies equivalently. For a relatively small subset of genes, expression depends on whether the copy entered the individual through a female or a male gamete. This parent-of-origin dependence is the defining feature of genomic imprinting. At a particular imprinted gene, one allele may make nearly all the RNA while the other contributes little or none. At other loci, parental origin produces a quantitative bias rather than absolute monoallelic expression.

It is useful to begin with an ordinary, nonimprinted autosomal gene. Imagine a gene whose protein is required in a particular tissue. A cell inherits a copy from each parent. If both alleles contain intact promoters and coding regions, the tissue-specific regulatory machinery can activate both. A sequence mutation may prevent one allele from producing a functional protein, but the mutation has its effect regardless of which parent transmitted that copy, provided there are no other parent-dependent factors. This is the familiar genetic situation in which one asks whether a variant is dominant, recessive or dosage sensitive. Imprinting changes the expression assumptions before one even considers the functional effect of a mutation. A perfectly functional allele may be silent in the tissue being studied because its parental origin places it in a particular regulatory state.

The terms maternal allele and paternal allele refer to immediate transmission. If a man inherited a chromosome from his mother and later transmits that chromosome to his child, the transmitted copy is paternal in that child. If a woman inherited a chromosome from her father and transmits it through an egg, the copy is maternal in her child. This distinction is the key to interpreting changes in expression across generations. It is not sufficient to label a chromosome permanently according to the grandparent from whom it came. Development must include a mechanism that can remove an earlier parental identity and establish the appropriate identity for the next generation of gametes.

The chromosome carrying an imprint is not a special kind of chromosome. Most autosomal genes are expressed without the strong parent-dependent restriction characteristic of imprinted loci. Furthermore, both maternal and paternal copies may be present in the DNA even when only one is expressed. A DNA-based test therefore answers a different question from an RNA-based test. The former asks which sequences are physically present; the latter asks which sequences are contributing transcripts at the time of sampling. A protein measurement asks yet another question because the amount of protein depends on translation, protein stability and sometimes secretion from another tissue. Keeping these levels separate prevents mistaken conclusions about which allele is missing, silenced or functionally defective.

The term epigenetic describes mechanisms that influence the use of genetic information without requiring an alteration in the base sequence. In developmental settings, the term often emphasizes a regulatory state that persists after the original signal is no longer present, including propagation through cell division. Imprinting is a particularly clear case because it carries information about the origin of an allele between gametes and offspring. Not every epigenetic change is an imprint. For example, a progenitor that establishes a stable muscle-specific transcription program has acquired a cellular identity, but the program need not distinguish the two parental copies of its muscle genes. Nor must every regulatory difference be epigenetic: a newly introduced mutation in an enhancer may change transcription simply by changing which protein can bind the DNA.

A central developmental consequence of imprinting is that the functional dosage of a gene can differ from its physical copy number. Two copies of an imprinted gene can be present while only one contributes significant expression. In a growth-sensitive tissue, losing the functioning copy may not be compensated by the other copy. Conversely, inappropriate activation of a normally repressed allele can raise the dose beyond its usual range. These are not necessarily defects of cell fate specification itself. They can alter proliferation, resource use, timing, or communication between tissues, all of which influence whether an embryo develops normally.

2. The experiments that established parental genome nonequivalence

In the twentieth century, developmental biologists investigated how much of an egg's ability to develop comes from its cytoplasm and how much comes from the nuclear genomes. A useful observation was that activating an unfertilized mammalian egg could initiate cell divisions. Such activation did not reliably produce a normally developing mammal. This result alone could not establish a specific requirement for paternal gene expression. An activated egg may differ from a fertilized egg in multiple ways. It can have an abnormal chromosome complement, a different activation history, or a diploid genome with an unusual degree of homozygosity. Any of these factors might impair development independently of the parental origin of its chromosomes.

The early mammalian zygote offers a powerful experimental opportunity because the egg-derived and sperm-derived genomes initially occupy separate pronuclei. Before the genomes participate in the first embryonic mitosis, it is possible in experimental models to distinguish the two nuclear contributions. Investigators used this separation to examine developmental outcomes when the normal combination of maternal and paternal nuclear material was changed. The important conceptual advance was not merely manipulation of an egg. It was the comparison of embryos that possessed diploid nuclear complements but differed in parental origin. Holding chromosome-set number constant helps distinguish genomic origin from simple haploidy or polyploidy.

In 1984, McGrath and Solter reported mouse pronuclear replacement experiments in which embryos with two pronuclei of maternal origin or two of paternal origin were compared with embryos receiving replacements of the same parental type as the pronucleus removed. The latter comparison was a manipulation control: it helped establish that the transfer procedure itself was compatible with development when the usual parental combination was preserved. If the two parental genomes had been entirely interchangeable, replacing a maternal pronucleus with a second paternal one, or the reverse, should not have produced the observed systematic failure of complete development merely because of origin. The uniparental nuclear combinations did not support normal development to term under those experimental conditions. The study established a fundamental asymmetry between the functional contributions of the two nuclear genomes [1].

Independent work by Surani, Barton and Norris used reconstituted mouse eggs to address related hypotheses. Their findings helped distinguish the competence of the egg cytoplasm from the suitability of the nuclear complement. Restoring a suitable combination of nuclear genomes could permit development in a cytoplasm that might otherwise have been blamed for a failed parthenogenetic outcome. The researchers proposed that different regulatory instructions were acquired during the formation of eggs and sperm. Further work connected the paternal genome particularly strongly to the development of extraembryonic tissues in these mouse experiments [2,3].

The term extraembryonic refers to tissues associated with supporting development rather than making up the body of the future animal. Trophoblast-derived structures contribute to the fetal part of the placenta, through which nutrient and gas exchange with the maternal environment becomes possible. Embryonic tissue growth and extraembryonic function are coupled: a fetus cannot continue growing normally if its support tissues are severely abnormal. In the classical mouse experiments, embryos with two maternal nuclear contributions tended to show relatively better development of embryonic structures than of extraembryonic structures, whereas embryos with two paternal nuclear contributions showed disproportionate extraembryonic development and poor embryonic development. These are relative tendencies in a particular experimental setting, not statements that one parent provides genes exclusively for the fetus and the other exclusively for the placenta.

A correct interpretation also requires attention to what the experiment did not show. The pronuclear manipulations did not identify every gene responsible for the abnormal phenotype. They could not by themselves establish DNA methylation as the molecular carrier of the difference. Nor did they show that every gene inherited from a given parent is more or less active. They identified an origin-dependent developmental requirement at the whole-genome level. Molecular experiments were needed to narrow the effect to particular loci, identify regulatory regions and explain the mechanism by which those regions distinguish parental copies.

The distinction between observation and explanation is especially important in examination questions. Suppose an embryo containing two maternal chromosome sets reaches a cleavage stage but fails later. The early divisions show that some cellular programs can operate without a normal paternal complement. They do not show that development to term is possible. Suppose a control embryo subjected to nuclear replacement survives. Its survival helps rule out the manipulation as the sole explanation for the experimental phenotype. It does not prove that every experimentally reconstructed embryo will survive. Biological procedures can reduce survival in all groups; the inference depends on the appropriate comparison between the groups, the developmental stages examined and the reproducibility of the difference.

Modern experimental work has also shown that particular changes to imprinting control regions can partly overcome the usual developmental barriers imposed by parental genome origin in mice. This is important conceptually because it supports a regulatory rather than an immutable chemical distinction between male-derived and female-derived DNA. It does not imply that a normal diploid mammalian egg can routinely develop into a healthy adult without the usual parental genomic contributions. Such findings concern modified experimental conditions, and the relevant imprinting requirements are species and locus dependent [4].

3. How a parental identity can be attached to a chromosome

An epigenetic imprint must meet a difficult biological requirement. It must be different on the two parental copies of a locus, remain interpretable when these copies occupy the same nucleus, and survive enough cell divisions to influence development. At the same time, it cannot be permanently assigned to that DNA molecule according to its ancient ancestry. The parental identity must be changed during germline development. This combination of selective stability and scheduled reversibility makes imprinting a useful model for studying the relationship between heredity and developmental gene regulation.

A major molecular carrier of mammalian imprints is DNA methylation. The relevant modification is usually the addition of a methyl group to carbon 5 of a cytosine in a CpG dinucleotide. CpG means that a cytosine and a guanine occur consecutively on one DNA strand, with a phosphate-containing linkage between them. Because the complementary sequence also contains a CpG in the opposite orientation, the two cytosines can both be methylated. A methylated cytosine still pairs with guanine. It therefore retains its basic role in transmitting nucleotide sequence information. The methyl group, however, projects into the major groove of the DNA helix and can influence the binding of regulatory proteins. Certain proteins recognize methylated DNA; others bind their target less efficiently when particular cytosines are methylated. Consequently, two chemically different versions of the same DNA sequence can recruit different regulatory complexes.

Methylation is often associated with transcriptional repression when present at particular promoter regions. That association is not a universal rule for all methylated DNA. A methylated region can prevent binding of a repressor or insulator, indirectly permitting transcription of a gene elsewhere. The developmental effect depends on the function of the modified region, not simply on the number of methyl groups. For this reason, a question asking whether methylation activates or represses a gene cannot always be answered without knowing the identity and location of the methylated regulatory element. The Igf2/H19 example later in the chapter illustrates why this distinction is indispensable.

A differentially methylated region, abbreviated DMR, is a DNA region that exhibits different levels or patterns of methylation between compared samples or alleles. At an imprinted locus, the relevant difference commonly occurs between the maternal and paternal homologs. A germline DMR is established in a parental gamete and maintained at least to some degree in subsequent development. Secondary DMRs may acquire an allele-specific pattern after fertilization, depending on upstream regulatory events. Thus, finding a DMR in fetal tissue does not establish that its methylation difference was directly deposited in a sperm or oocyte. A developmental time series is needed to distinguish the earliest inherited mark from later consequences of that mark.

An imprinting control region, or ICR, is a regulatory DNA region with a functional role in coordinating parental-origin expression. Many well characterized ICRs are germline DMRs. The terms DMR and ICR are nevertheless not identical. DMR describes an observed methylation difference; ICR describes a causal regulatory function. If an investigator detects differential methylation at a region near an imprinted gene, that observation makes the region a candidate regulator. It does not prove that deleting the region or changing its methylation state would change the gene's expression. Functional evidence is necessary to identify a control region. A single ICR can govern several linked genes, which explains why changes to a noncoding sequence can affect multiple gene products and generate a substantial developmental phenotype.

Consider an imprinted cluster in which an ICR regulates a long noncoding RNA. The RNA need not encode a protein to affect nearby genes. Its transcription, interaction with chromatin or recruitment of repressive activities can influence expression in the surrounding chromosome domain. In some mammalian imprinting clusters, a noncoding transcript is expressed only from one parental chromosome and participates in silencing nearby genes on that same chromosome. This is called a cis effect. A cis regulator acts predominantly on the physically linked DNA molecule. A trans-acting protein, in contrast, can move within the nucleus and interact with appropriate targets on either homolog. A cis-acting mechanism is especially useful for maintaining different expression states of two homologs that otherwise share many of the same diffusible regulators.

DNA methylation is not the only form of inherited regulatory information. Histones are proteins around which nuclear DNA is wrapped, and modifications of histone tails can affect chromatin organization and recruitment of other regulatory proteins. In some mouse loci, parent-dependent expression during early development can arise from maternal histone modifications rather than a canonical germline methylated ICR. Some of these differences are temporary or are converted into a different regulatory form in particular extraembryonic cell lineages. These are examples of noncanonical imprinting [5]. They are important exceptions to the statement that all imprints begin as CpG methylation. For standard examinations, DNA methylation is the major mechanism to understand, but the broader principle is inherited parent-specific regulatory information.

4. The imprint life cycle: establishment, maintenance and reset

Follow one autosomal locus through two generations. A newly formed embryo receives one chromosome copy from an egg and one from sperm. Assume that this locus has a germline DMR that is normally methylated on the paternal copy and unmethylated on the maternal copy. At fertilization the two copies enter a shared cytoplasm and subsequently a shared nuclear environment. Unless the methylation difference is preserved, both copies may eventually become similar in their regulatory behavior. The early embryo therefore needs ways to protect the inherited difference, and growing tissues need ways to recreate it after DNA replication.

This is not a trivial task because the genome of an early mammalian embryo undergoes extensive epigenetic reprogramming. The transition from a highly specialized gamete to a developing embryo requires substantial changes in gene regulation. Broad remodeling of DNA methylation accompanies this transition. Different parental genomes may undergo demethylation with different dynamics, and the details vary by species and developmental stage. Many canonical imprinted germline DMRs are relatively protected during this early wave of reprogramming. The general decrease in genomic methylation must not be interpreted as complete removal of every methyl group at every locus. Protection of appropriate imprints allows the embryo to reorganize its developmental program without indiscriminately eliminating the regulatory distinctions needed for normal parental dosage.

Maintenance methylation solves a second problem. DNA replication produces daughter duplexes containing one old strand and one newly synthesized strand. If a CpG was methylated on both strands before replication, each daughter duplex initially contains a methylated old strand and an unmethylated new strand. Such a duplex is called hemimethylated. DNMT1 is a major maintenance DNA methyltransferase in mammals. Together with factors such as UHRF1, it helps identify and restore methylation at appropriate sites on newly synthesized DNA. If this copying activity does not occur, replication can progressively dilute a preexisting methylation pattern. A DNA polymerase can copy the sequence accurately while an epigenetic mark is lost. Genetic fidelity and epigenetic fidelity therefore require overlapping but distinct mechanisms.

At many canonical imprinted regions, additional factors contribute to selective protection and maintenance. For example, in the mouse, the protein ZFP57 can recognize particular methylated sequence motifs at imprinted regions and recruit a regulatory complex involving TRIM28. Such complexes help maintain a chromatin environment compatible with preservation of parental methylation differences. Other factors, including ZNF445, have relevant roles that vary between mammals and loci [5,6]. These names illustrate how a mark can be selectively read and protected; a student need not memorize every protein interaction to understand the developmental logic. A mark must be established, recognized, and copied or otherwise preserved. Failure at any one of those stages can change the expression of its target genes.

The inherited parental distinction is not supposed to persist unaltered through the germline of the offspring. Primordial germ cells are the embryonic precursors of future gametes. As these cells develop, they undergo extensive epigenetic remodeling, including erasure of many existing imprint marks. At the stage when the old imprints are removed, the two homologs can no longer be distinguished by their previous maternal or paternal mark at the relevant DMR. This erasure allows each chromosome to receive the appropriate imprint for the sex of the germline in which it is developing. It is a programmed reset, not a failure of epigenetic maintenance in a somatic tissue.

New sex-specific marks are established as germ cells mature. In the mouse, acquisition of paternal DNA methylation imprints occurs during male germline development beginning in fetal prospermatogonia for many such regions, whereas maternal methylation imprints are established largely during oocyte growth. The exact timing is locus dependent. DNMT3A, assisted by DNMT3L in important mammalian germline contexts, participates in the deposition of new methylation patterns. This activity is called de novo methylation because it can create methylation at sites that were not previously methylated. Unlike maintenance methylation, it does not depend simply on copying a methyl group already present on the complementary strand. The contrast between these activities is important: establishment makes the correct new pattern; maintenance preserves that pattern during subsequent divisions.

Imagine a female embryo that received a paternal methylated copy of a paternally marked DMR from her father. In her somatic cells this copy retains the paternal state at the locus. In her developing germline, the old imprint is erased. When she produces eggs, both homologs acquire the appropriate maternal state before either can be transmitted. Thus, if she transmits the chromosome previously inherited from her father, it enters the next embryo with a maternal imprint. In a male carrying the same chromosome sequence, the germline instead establishes the paternal state. The chromosome's genetic variants can remain the same across these events, but the regulatory history used by the offspring changes.

This life cycle resolves a common apparent paradox. An imprint can be inherited across fertilization without being transmitted indefinitely in exactly the same form. Stability during a particular developmental interval and reversibility at a later stage are compatible. Failure to distinguish the somatic and germline lineages leads to the incorrect claim that a maternally imprinted copy can never become paternally imprinted. The correct answer requires asking which individual, cell lineage, locus and developmental stage are being discussed.

A further nuance concerns what is meant by a maternal imprint. Some authors use the phrase to describe a mark placed during oogenesis, whereas students sometimes interpret it to mean that the maternal allele is the expressed copy. These meanings are not equivalent. A methylated maternal DMR could silence a maternal promoter, activate a linked gene indirectly, or influence a chromatin domain. It is more precise to state that the maternal DMR is methylated or that the maternal allele of a named gene is expressed. This explicit terminology avoids a great deal of confusion when comparing loci with opposite expression patterns.

5. The mouse Igf2/H19 locus: a regulatory mechanism that explains opposite expression

A good textbook example should connect a developmental phenotype with a molecular mechanism and allow the student to make predictions. The mouse Igf2/H19 region provides all three. Igf2 encodes insulin-like growth factor 2, a protein that participates in regulation of growth during fetal development. H19 produces a long noncoding RNA. The two genes lie close to one another on the same chromosome, and their regulation involves shared enhancer sequences. In many relevant mouse fetal tissues, the paternal Igf2 allele and the maternal H19 allele are expressed preferentially. The opposite parental copies are relatively silent. This pattern is a tissue-qualified description of the canonical model; it should not be generalized to every promoter, developmental stage or species without evidence.

An enhancer is a DNA regulatory element that can stimulate transcription from a promoter, often over a considerable genomic distance. Enhancers are recognized by proteins that help assemble or stabilize a transcriptionally active configuration. An enhancer need not lie immediately beside the gene whose transcription it influences. Chromatin folds in three-dimensional space, allowing DNA segments separated along a chromosome to approach one another. It is consequently possible for a regulatory element to influence one gene strongly while another nearby gene remains poorly expressed. The pattern depends on promoter compatibility, chromatin organization and intervening regulatory elements.

At the canonical mouse locus, an imprinting control region lies upstream of H19, between Igf2 and H19 in the relevant linear organization. On the maternally inherited chromosome, this ICR is usually unmethylated. The protein CTCF, which binds specific DNA sequences, can occupy sites within this unmethylated region. In this context CTCF contributes to an insulator or enhancer-blocking function. Its presence limits productive communication between the shared downstream enhancers and the maternal Igf2 promoter. The same enhancer system can support maternal H19 expression. The maternal chromosome thus contains an intact Igf2 coding sequence but restricts its use by changing its regulatory access. There is no need to invoke deletion of the maternal gene or a special maternal version of the IGF2 protein.

On the paternal chromosome, the imprinting control region is methylated. Methylation prevents efficient CTCF binding at the relevant sites. The enhancer-blocking function associated with maternal CTCF occupancy is therefore absent or substantially reduced. The downstream enhancers can participate in activation of paternal Igf2 in appropriate tissues. Paternal H19 is silenced in the canonical configuration, with additional methylation and chromatin changes associated with repression at its promoter. This is a striking example in which methylation of an ICR indirectly supports expression of a protein-coding gene. It does so by interfering with a regulatory protein that would otherwise restrict enhancer access. Methylation is not acting as a universal on switch for Igf2, nor as a universal off switch for every linked gene.

A linear drawing of the locus should show the direction of the regulatory relationship without misleading the student into treating DNA as a rigid straight line. An insulator is not a physical wall in the nucleus. It contributes to regulatory architecture and selective interactions between chromosomal elements. Likewise, saying that an enhancer is shared does not require every cell to distribute its activity between two promoters in the same way. Tissue-specific transcription factors and promoter-specific regulatory programs still matter. The simple CTCF model describes an important mechanism within a larger developmental regulatory system.

The molecular evidence for this explanation came from studies that tested the relationship between methylation, CTCF binding and enhancer blocking. Bell and Felsenfeld and, independently, Hark and colleagues reported in 2000 that CTCF-dependent boundary function at this region was sensitive to methylation [7,8]. Binding studies helped connect the methylation state of relevant sites to protein recognition. Functional studies examined the consequences of disrupting the boundary or its recognition sites. The combined evidence supported the model that changing an epigenetic mark could change enhancer access without changing the sequence of the protein-coding gene. Later studies of CTCF binding sites and chromatin organization further clarified the relationships between maternal ICR occupancy, differential methylation and long-range regulation [9].

Consider how to test the model experimentally. A comparison between methylated and unmethylated copies of a DNA binding sequence can ask whether CTCF binds differently when the sequence itself is the same. An appropriately designed control should include a DNA element lacking the specific recognition site or a validated reference binding condition. Such a test demonstrates biochemical sensitivity but does not by itself establish the developmental function of the site in an embryo. A functional allele-specific perturbation can ask whether loss of the boundary changes expression of the nearby genes. If maternal boundary disruption allows maternal Igf2 transcription, that result supports a role for the boundary in silencing maternal Igf2. If the same perturbation also affects H19 transcription, that is consistent with coordinated regulation of the domain rather than independent control of each coding sequence.

A mutation that disrupts the CTCF binding sequence should be distinguished from a pure change in methylation. The first is genetic: the DNA sequence has changed. It can alter an epigenetic regulatory mechanism, but it is still inherited as a sequence variant. The second can be an epigenetic alteration even if the underlying sequence remains intact. Both perturbations may produce similar expression consequences, such as inappropriate activation of maternal Igf2. Therefore a phenotype or expression measurement alone may not tell which kind of alteration occurred. Direct assessment of the control sequence and its methylation state would help discriminate them. This relationship is a valuable general lesson in developmental genetics because genetic and epigenetic mechanisms are not mutually exclusive explanations.

The growth role of Igf2 was established through genetic evidence. DeChiara, Robertson and Efstratiadis examined the consequences of disrupting the mouse Igf2 gene in relation to the parent that transmitted the disruption [10]. In relevant imprinted tissues, inheritance of a disrupted active paternal allele has a greater effect on the available IGF2 product than inheritance of a disrupted normally silent maternal allele. The developmental growth phenotype therefore depended on parental origin. The genetic variant was not a different chemical mutation when it came from a father rather than a mother. Its phenotypic effect changed because the expression system used the two copies unequally. The study also emphasized tissue-dependent imprinting, making it inappropriate to treat this particular parental expression rule as a statement about every cell in the mouse.

An examination might present a mother carrying an Igf2 loss-of-function allele and a father carrying the same allele in an otherwise comparable model. The first step is to identify the active allele in the specified fetal tissue. The second step is to identify the origin of the disrupted copy in the offspring, not in its parent. The third step is to ask whether the remaining expressed copy can provide the normal amount of functional product. A disrupted maternal copy can remain largely inconsequential in the canonical imprinted tissue if the paternal copy is functional. A disrupted paternal copy can cause a substantial deficiency even when a normal maternal sequence is present. These predictions require the stated assumptions of strong paternal expression and no effective compensation; tissue-specific relaxation of imprinting or other regulatory changes may modify the outcome.

6. Reciprocal crosses reveal parental expression rather than sequence preference

Reciprocal crossing is one of the clearest ways to investigate imprinting because it changes parental origin while keeping the offspring's allele combination comparable. Consider two inbred mouse strains, A and B, that carry different harmless nucleotide variants within a gene. These variants can be used as markers to distinguish transcripts originating from the two homologs. A female of strain A crossed with a male of strain B produces an offspring whose relevant genotype is A/B, with A maternal and B paternal. Reversing the cross produces offspring with the same two sequence variants, but B is maternal and A is paternal. A difference between these two crosses can therefore be informative about the role of parental origin.

There are several possible expression patterns. In a nonimprinted gene with no strain-dependent transcriptional difference, each allele may contribute roughly half the RNA. In a gene with a cis sequence difference that makes allele A more active than allele B, A may predominate in both reciprocal crosses. The direction of expression follows the identity of the DNA variant rather than the sex of the parent transmitting it. In a maternally expressed imprinted gene, A predominates in the first cross and B in the reverse cross. In a paternally expressed gene, B predominates in the first cross and A in the reverse cross. These comparisons distinguish a marker allele from the regulatory state imposed by its origin.

An allele-specific expression assay uses a sequence difference to assign RNA molecules to parental copies. For example, the two strains may have a single-nucleotide polymorphism in a transcribed region of the gene. If genomic DNA from an A/B individual contains both markers, but RNA obtained from a particular tissue overwhelmingly carries the paternal marker, the result is consistent with paternal expression. A robust conclusion requires more than a single observation. Both reciprocal crosses should be examined, and the mapping or detection method should be checked for preferential recognition of one sequence. Genomic DNA from the heterozygote can be a useful control for allele-detection bias and copy number. The biological tissue and developmental stage must also be appropriate for the hypothesized imprinting pattern.

A simple hypothetical dataset makes the reasoning quantitative. In the cross A female by B male, suppose 18 RNA reads carry marker A and 182 carry marker B. In the reciprocal cross, suppose 176 carry marker A and 24 carry marker B. The paternal fractions are 182/200, or 91 percent, and 176/200, or 88 percent, respectively. Although the preferred nucleotide marker switches from B to A, the preferred parental origin remains paternal. This is strong evidence for parent-dependent expression in the sampled tissue, assuming accurate assignment, equivalent developmental stages and no other parental effects that explain the observations. The small maternal contribution should not automatically be described as complete escape from imprinting. It might arise from partial expression, a mixture of different cell types, technical noise or contamination. Additional evidence is needed to distinguish these explanations.

Now consider another hypothetical dataset. Allele A contributes 90 percent of reads in both cross directions. The preferred parental origin switches when the cross is reversed, but the preferred DNA marker remains A. This pattern supports an allele-sequence-dependent regulatory difference rather than a simple maternal or paternal imprint. It could also reflect a technical detection bias favoring A. Comparison with genomic DNA and use of a reference-aware analysis help evaluate that possibility. The reciprocal-cross design does not by itself eliminate every confounder, but it provides the central comparison required to tell these two hypotheses apart.

The idealized conclusion becomes more complicated when biological parents differ in traits other than the marker sequence. The mother's genotype can affect the uterine environment or components deposited into the egg. A paternal genotype can influence sperm properties. In plants, maternal seed coat contamination can contribute maternal transcripts to samples of developing endosperm. Such effects can produce differences between reciprocal crosses without demonstrating that the two alleles within the offspring's cells have acquired different parent-specific expression states. An investigator should therefore combine reciprocal-cross expression data with careful tissue sampling, allele-specific methylation or chromatin evidence where relevant, and genetic or epigenetic perturbation when possible. An examination asking for the strongest conclusion may reward this distinction between suggestive evidence and a demonstrated causal mechanism.

One practical distinction is between a parent-dependent organismal phenotype and genomic imprinting of a particular gene. A cross may show different body weights when one strain is the mother rather than the father. That difference is a clue, not proof of allele-specific expression. To establish imprinting at a candidate gene, the investigator must show that the allele inherited from a particular parent is used differently from its homolog in the relevant cells. To establish that imprinting causes the phenotype, further evidence should connect a perturbation of the regulatory state or active allele to a change in developmental outcome. Each step answers a different question: association with parental origin, identification of an imprinted locus, and demonstration of developmental function.

7. From allele-specific expression to developmental phenotypes: dosage and timing

The presence of a molecular asymmetry is not sufficient to explain an abnormal embryo. We must connect the asymmetry to the behavior of cells, tissues and the whole organism. A gene product can influence development by regulating cell division, survival, migration, differentiation or communication between neighboring tissues. An imprinted gene may encode a secreted protein that influences the growth of nearby cells, a receptor that changes how a cell responds to extracellular factors, or an RNA that modifies transcription across a chromosomal domain. The physiological effect of expression from one allele rather than two depends on the role of the gene product and how its abundance is controlled.

A simple dosage model helps establish the logic before considering real examples. Assume that a normal active allele produces one unit of functional product and that two active alleles would each produce one unit independently. A nonimprinted gene with two functioning copies then produces two units. If one copy is inactivated, the predicted output becomes one unit. A completely paternally expressed imprinted gene normally produces one unit because its maternal copy is silent. A loss-of-function mutation in its paternal allele reduces its functional output to zero under these assumptions. A mutation of the silent maternal copy has little direct effect. If the normally silent maternal allele becomes active while both coding sequences remain functional, the predicted output becomes two units, twice the ordinary imprinted dosage.

This model does not claim that RNA expression or protein abundance is always exactly proportional to active copy number. Feedback regulation can change transcription from the remaining allele, and tissues may differ in the extent of allele-specific expression. A secreted protein can move from its site of production, and receptor binding can saturate at a concentration below twice the normal level. Therefore doubling the number of active alleles does not guarantee that an embryo will grow twice as large. The model separates the first molecular prediction from the subsequent physiological prediction. The first concerns the source and quantity of a gene product; the second concerns the response of a developmental system to that quantity.

For a growth-promoting signal, increased availability can favor proliferation in responsive cells, but the response may depend on receptor abundance, nutrient supply and developmental stage. A growth-inhibiting product can have the opposite effect. Imprinting of a growth regulator may influence the balance between the fetal demand for resources and the capacity of supporting tissues to deliver them. This is particularly important in mammals because fetal growth occurs in connection with a maternal organism and a placenta. Changes in trophoblast behavior can influence the size and efficiency of placental exchange surfaces. Changes in fetal signaling can influence the use of resources after they arrive. The same imprinted locus can thus have consequences at more than one organizational level.

A useful way to examine this relationship is to compare an imprinted growth regulator with a classic morphogen. A morphogen supplies information about position and can generate different cell identities depending on concentration, duration and competence. A dosage-sensitive imprinted growth regulator need not specify positional identity in this way. Its quantity may instead alter the rate of proliferation or the amount of tissue growth while the main spatial pattern remains recognizable. Both kinds of regulation can be developmentally important, but they answer different questions. An embryo can contain all the expected tissue identities and still have serious growth defects because the relative proportions or functional maturation of those tissues are disturbed.

Timing matters because the requirement for a gene product can change across embryonic development. If a product is needed mainly during formation of the placenta, loss of its expression after that developmental window may produce a different phenotype from loss beginning immediately after fertilization. Likewise, an allele-specific pattern detected in an adult tissue cannot be assumed to represent the pattern in early embryonic cells. Tissue-specific transcriptional programs may use different promoters or different regulatory elements at different stages. The complete developmental explanation therefore requires a time axis as well as an allele axis. A result such as normal expression in the adult liver does not establish that expression was normal in the fetal placenta.

A second quantitative example considers tissue-specific relaxation of imprinting. Suppose an allele normally described as paternal contributes 80 units of RNA in a tissue and the maternal allele contributes 20 units. The total is 100 units, and the paternal fraction is 0.80. In another tissue, each allele contributes 50 units. The total remains 100 units even though the parental distribution is completely different. A total RNA measurement alone cannot distinguish the two tissues. If a paternal mutation abolishes functional output without altering transcript abundance, both tissues may still contain similar total RNA, but the quantity of functional protein available from the normal maternal allele differs. Under a simplifying proportionality assumption, it is 20 units in the first tissue and 50 in the second. This illustrates why a molecular dosage analysis should consider allele-specific function as well as total expression.

Conversely, a tissue may retain the same parental expression ratio while its total expression changes. For example, a paternal fraction of 0.9 can occur when the total transcript output is 100 units or when it is 20 units. The relative contribution of the alleles is identical, but the absolute paternal output differs fivefold. An expression graph showing only percentages can therefore hide a major difference in developmental dosage. A well-designed analysis often benefits from both allele fractions and normalized total abundance. The fraction describes which allele is being used; the abundance describes how much product is available, subject to the limitations of measuring RNA instead of protein.

Developmental phenotypes can also reveal a relationship between different imprinted genes in the same domain. If a single ICR controls two genes with opposite effects on tissue growth, a regulatory change may simultaneously increase the product of one gene and decrease the product of the other. The net phenotype cannot necessarily be predicted by treating the two changes as independent. It depends on whether the products act in the same pathway, whether the effects occur in the same tissue and whether one perturbation changes the responsiveness to the other. A rescue experiment targeting only one gene can help establish how much of the domain-level phenotype is attributable to that gene. Partial rescue may indicate that several linked products contribute.

8. What happens when imprints are erased or maintained at the wrong time?

The imprint life cycle makes it possible to classify developmental errors according to the stage at which regulation fails. The distinction matters because several failures can produce superficially similar observations in the offspring. One possibility is that the appropriate mark was never established in the germline. Another is that an established mark was lost after fertilization. A third is that a mark was not properly erased in the primordial germ cells of the previous generation. A fourth is that the mark is present but a protein required to interpret it no longer functions. In each case, the expression of a linked gene may be abnormal, but the underlying causal event is different.

If establishment fails in a gamete, the embryo begins development without the normal origin-specific distinction at the affected locus. For a paternally methylated ICR, loss of methylation during male germline development means that the sperm-derived copy enters the embryo in an inappropriate state. Both homologs might initially resemble the usual unmethylated maternal configuration at that region. The expression consequence depends on the function of the ICR. At the canonical Igf2/H19 locus, loss of paternal ICR methylation can permit CTCF occupancy and contribute to reduced paternal Igf2 expression. This prediction is specific to the regulatory configuration of that locus; it is not a general rule for every paternally methylated region. Additional events at linked promoters can modify the eventual phenotype.

A maintenance error after fertilization begins with a correctly established difference. The embryo receives the appropriate parental marks, but later divisions fail to preserve them in some or all cells. The outcome can be mosaic. Here the word mosaic refers to the presence of cell populations with differing regulatory states within an individual, even if their underlying DNA sequence is identical. If the first failure occurs in a cell that contributes mainly to an extraembryonic lineage, the resulting epigenetic abnormality may be concentrated in a supportive tissue. If it occurs earlier in a precursor contributing to many tissues, the distribution can be broader. The timing of the failure therefore influences the anatomical distribution of its effects.

A hypothetical methylation time series illustrates how these alternatives could be distinguished. Suppose a paternal ICR is normally close to fully methylated in sperm, and the same allele remains strongly methylated in a blastocyst. In a mutant animal, sperm shows the expected methylation pattern but blastocyst samples show much lower paternal methylation. This observation is consistent with failed maintenance or abnormal demethylation after fertilization. It does not primarily support an establishment failure in sperm. In a different mutant, the sperm already lacks methylation at that ICR. This result shifts the focus toward the germline establishment or preservation of the mark before fertilization. The same low methylation in fetal tissue could arise in both mutants; the earlier measurements discriminate the histories.

Failure of germline erasure is a different problem. Imagine a male whose germ cells retain a maternally inherited state at a locus that normally acquires a paternal mark. If the retained maternal state prevents correct paternal establishment, sperm carrying that homolog may transmit an inappropriate epigenetic state. Not every erased mark must disappear at precisely the same time, and not every residual methyl group necessarily causes a transmission defect. The relevant question is whether residual information interferes with establishment of the state required by the developing gamete. A germline reset defect can potentially affect multiple loci, making the resulting phenotype more complicated than mutation of a single ICR.

A reader or interpreter defect can leave an epigenetic mark apparently intact. For instance, a methylation-sensitive binding protein could be absent from the cell or unable to bind its recognition sequence. If the regulatory effect of a parental mark depends on that protein, the gene expression pattern may change without a detectable loss of methylation at the time measured. This is analogous to a normal extracellular signal that cannot produce its effect because the responding cell lacks a receptor. The signal is present, but its interpretation is impaired. In both cases, measuring the signal alone does not establish that the regulatory system is functioning.

It is essential not to treat all epigenetic alterations as independent of the genome. A mutation in a DNA methyltransferase, an imprint-protection protein, or a local DNA binding site is a genetic change that can produce an epigenetic phenotype. The mutation can disrupt establishment, preservation or interpretation of parent-specific marks. Conversely, a mark may become abnormal in the absence of an identified DNA sequence mutation. In that situation the term epimutation may be used for an abnormal regulatory state. The distinction is useful, but diagnosis of its origin requires direct evidence. A normal sequence in one coding region does not exclude a mutation in a distant regulatory element or in a gene encoding a trans-acting regulator.

Nuclear reprogramming offers another developmental context in which the balance between erasure and maintenance is important. A differentiated somatic nucleus generally contains many marks associated with its tissue identity as well as parent-of-origin information at imprinted loci. During somatic cell nuclear transfer, an enucleated oocyte must reorganize the donated nucleus so that it can participate in embryonic development. Reprogramming must overcome inappropriate somatic regulatory states while allowing a suitable configuration at genes required for development. Abnormal imprint regulation is one of several possible contributors to poor developmental outcomes in cloned mammals. The occurrence of such defects does not imply that the donated nucleus lost the corresponding genes. It illustrates the distinction between possession of genomic information and correct developmental use of it.

The successful reprogramming of a cell should not be equated with indiscriminate elimination of all marks. A developmental transition often requires selective remodeling. Some regulatory states must disappear because they belong to a previous cellular identity; others must be preserved or reconstructed because they are needed for the new state. In the germline, parental imprints must be reset according to the future gamete. In the early embryo, canonical parental marks frequently need protection while broad reprogramming proceeds. In a differentiating somatic cell, many newly established regulatory states must become stable enough to support the cell's function. These contrasting objectives explain why different developmental lineages use related molecular machinery in different ways.

9. Epigenetic memory, determination and developmental plasticity

When a cell becomes committed to a developmental fate, its regulatory behavior often becomes more resistant to changes in its surroundings. For instance, a cell that has entered a muscle differentiation pathway may maintain expression of muscle-associated genes even after the original inductive signal is removed. This persistence is a form of developmental memory. It allows a transient event in the embryo, such as exposure to a signal at a particular position, to influence descendants long after the cells have moved away from their initial environment. Without mechanisms of memory, each cell might need continuous exposure to all of the signals that first established its identity.

There are several ways to maintain such a state. A transcription factor can activate its own gene directly or indirectly through a regulatory network. As long as enough factor remains after cell division, the network can renew its activity in the descendants. Inhibitory interactions between two alternative fate programs can also help stabilize a choice. Once one program becomes active, it can repress the alternative, making spontaneous switching less likely. Chromatin-based mechanisms can support these regulatory networks by maintaining accessibility at genes required for one fate and limiting accessibility at genes associated with other programs. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive, and a stable cell identity can arise from the combined operation of several mechanisms.

One simple example is a gene that encodes a transcription factor X. Imagine that an inductive signal stimulates X expression, and X subsequently activates a regulatory element in its own gene. The initial signal may be brief, but X can remain abundant because its product supports continued production. When a cell divides, the protein and RNA are partitioned between descendants. If the amount in each descendant remains sufficient to restart the feedback loop, the active state can continue. If the amount becomes too low or a strong inhibitory signal acts, the loop may collapse. This is a conceptual example of state-dependent memory without any requirement for differential expression of maternal and paternal alleles. It illustrates why the phenomenon of developmental memory is broader than imprinting.

Chromatin provides another route to persistence. Nucleosomes and associated factors can influence whether transcriptional regulators gain access to DNA. During replication, the organization of chromatin must be rebuilt on daughter DNA molecules. Some existing histones can be redistributed, and regulatory complexes can help restore characteristic marks and accessibility. The restored state need not be an exact physical copy of every molecular feature of the previous cell. What matters functionally is whether the regulatory program is reproduced sufficiently to maintain gene expression and cell behavior. A stable differentiated state can therefore be more robust than any individual molecular mark. Loss of one mark may have little consequence if redundant feedback maintains the overall network.

Imprinting differs from this usual form of cellular determination in the information that it preserves. A canonical parental imprint was established in a gametic context, before the tissue-specific fate of the offspring's cell was selected. It tells the regulatory machinery something about the origin of a particular chromosome copy. A somatic fate memory commonly arises within the individual in response to developmental signals and tells a cell something about its own regulatory history. The two systems can interact: a tissue-specific fate program may determine whether an imprinted gene is transcriptionally active at all, while the imprint determines which parental copy can contribute when the gene is activated. For example, both homologs could be silent in an unrelated cell type even though the parental ICR asymmetry is maintained there. The existence of an imprint does not force transcription in every tissue.

This interaction illustrates the difference between competence and expression. An imprinted promoter may be competent to respond on the paternal chromosome but still fail to produce RNA if the transcription factors required in a particular tissue are absent. A maternal copy may have a complete coding sequence but lack the appropriate enhancer access in that same tissue. Expression consequently depends on both the shared cellular environment and the allele-specific regulatory environment. An examination question may describe the presence of a methylation difference without any transcription in a tissue. That is not necessarily contradictory. The mark can be maintained in cells where the affected gene is normally inactive and become functionally apparent only in a context where the gene would otherwise be expressed.

Determination is an experimentally defined aspect of fate commitment. A determined cell tends to follow its established fate even when transferred to an environment that would normally promote a different fate. The test is based on developmental behavior, not simply on detecting methylation at a marker gene. An epigenetic state may help account for determination, but the presence of a particular mark is not itself proof of determination. The experiment needs to assess the fate of the cell or its descendants under a changed context. Likewise, observing the same parental imprint in different cells does not prove that they have the same fate. A muscle cell and a neuron can share an imprint pattern at a locus while maintaining very different tissue-specific regulatory programs.

Developmental plasticity refers to the ability of cells or tissues to alter their state under appropriate conditions. Plasticity is not the absence of memory. It is the capacity to change a state that may have been quite stable under ordinary conditions. Reprogramming to induced pluripotency, for example, can alter extensive aspects of a differentiated cellular program. However, the acquisition of pluripotent characteristics does not guarantee that every imprinted locus has retained or acquired its appropriate methylation pattern. An induced pluripotent cell may express expected markers and still have epigenetic abnormalities that affect its developmental potential. This is one reason why marker expression, potency, regulatory fidelity and functional developmental assays should not be treated as interchangeable measures of quality.

A further distinction concerns lineage. If a cell inherits a state from its somatic precursor, the persistence of that state across mitosis is an example of cellular inheritance. If a gamete transmits a parental imprint to the offspring, the regulatory information has crossed a generational boundary. The latter still does not necessarily establish unrestricted transgenerational inheritance through many generations because germline resetting can erase the earlier state. In mammals, a claim that an environmentally induced somatic mark persists through descendants across multiple generations requires evidence beyond the observation that canonical imprints are inherited at fertilization. Imprinting is a specific, regulated exception to broad reprogramming, not proof that any acquired mark can pass indefinitely through the germline.

10. Distinguishing imprinting from other parent-associated or monoallelic phenomena

Several biological mechanisms can produce an observation that initially looks like imprinting. The first is the maternal effect. During oogenesis, an egg receives proteins, RNAs and organelles that are synthesized or organized under the control of the mother's genes. These components can influence early development before the embryonic genome has assumed its later regulatory role. If a mother's genotype determines whether an essential maternal product is present in the egg, an offspring may show a phenotype determined primarily by her genotype rather than its own genotype at the relevant locus. This is a maternal genetic effect, and it need not involve different transcriptional states of the offspring's maternal and paternal alleles.

Consider a hypothetical gene D whose protein is required during early cleavage. Assume that a mother with two nonfunctional D alleles deposits no functional protein in her eggs. A father carrying a normal D allele can transmit it to the embryo, but the embryo may not express enough of the gene before the early developmental requirement arises. The resulting phenotype depends on a missing maternal product. A different situation occurs at an imprinted gene G that is actively transcribed from the paternal allele during fetal development. Here the parent dependence arises from which inherited chromosome copy is used at the time of gene expression. Both situations can produce reciprocal-cross differences, but their molecular explanations and expected time courses differ.

Localized cytoplasmic determinants are related to maternal provisioning but introduce an additional spatial component. An oocyte may contain maternal RNA or protein concentrated near one region. During cleavage, different daughter cells can receive different quantities of the localized material. This difference can initiate distinct gene-expression programs in the daughters even when their nuclear genomes contain the same alleles. Imprinting, by contrast, distinguishes two homologs that can coexist in the same cell. The localization of a cytoplasmic determinant and the methylation state of an ICR may both be established before fertilization, but the location, target and mode of inheritance of the information are different. A dye-tracing experiment following an inherited cytoplasmic component does not directly reveal parental allele-specific transcription at a nuclear locus.

Mitochondrial inheritance provides another important contrast. In many animals, most offspring mitochondrial DNA is transmitted through the egg. A mitochondrial genetic variant can therefore show a strongly maternal inheritance pattern. The underlying explanation concerns the transmission of an organellar genome, not necessarily repression of a paternal copy of an autosomal nuclear gene. In a canonical imprinted autosomal locus, both parental copies are ordinarily present in nuclear DNA, but their expression differs. In a typical maternally inherited mitochondrial genome, the paternal mitochondrial contribution is absent or greatly reduced. Detecting a maternal phenotype alone cannot distinguish these cases; the location of the gene and evidence about the physical inheritance of its copies are crucial.

Random monoallelic expression can also create cells that predominantly use one of two homologous copies. If different cells independently select one allele without a systematic preference for maternal or paternal origin, the resulting tissue may contain cells expressing either copy. A bulk sample can even appear approximately biallelic because the two cell populations contribute opposite alleles. Genomic imprinting has a different organizing principle: the choice or bias is correlated with parental origin. A single cell with only one expressed allele is therefore insufficient to establish imprinting. An investigator needs to know the parental origins of the alleles and whether the bias follows that origin across appropriate biological comparisons.

X chromosome inactivation is another example of stable chromosomal regulation, and it illustrates why the distinction between random choice and imprinting must be qualified by developmental context. In many female mammalian somatic lineages, X inactivation involves a choice of one X chromosome that is then maintained through divisions. In the mouse, an imprinted pattern of paternal X inactivation occurs in particular early and extraembryonic contexts, whereas random X inactivation is characteristic of many embryonic somatic lineages after the relevant resetting events. Thus, it is incorrect to claim either that all X inactivation is genomic imprinting or that X inactivation can never be parent-of-origin dependent. The appropriate answer depends on the species, developmental lineage and stage. Detailed dosage compensation mechanisms belong to the earlier molecular biology curriculum; the developmental distinction is the focus here.

Sex linkage is a different inheritance principle. An allele on an X chromosome can show different transmission patterns in males and females because of the distribution of sex chromosomes during reproduction. For example, a father ordinarily transmits his X chromosome to daughters rather than sons in an XY system. This fact by itself does not demonstrate epigenetic imprinting. A parent-specific difference in phenotype may arise from the sex chromosome composition of the offspring, not from differential expression of two homologous alleles present in the same cell. A properly controlled reciprocal-cross analysis therefore focuses on the relevant autosomal locus or explicitly accounts for sex chromosome inheritance where necessary.

Finally, genetic dominance should not be confused with the identity of the active imprinted copy. A dominant allele can influence a phenotype in a heterozygote because of the biochemical or regulatory properties of its product. In an imprinted system the ability of an allele to produce that product can depend on parental origin. A mutation that would be harmful when expressed may have little effect when inherited in a silenced state. This does not mean that the allele's sequence has become recessive in the ordinary molecular sense. The relationship between genotype and phenotype has acquired an additional regulatory variable. To solve a problem, first identify the transmitted sequence, then the parent-specific expression state, and only then the functional effect of the product.

11. Investigating imprinting: what each experiment can and cannot establish

The identification of an imprinted gene requires the combination of different types of evidence. A useful starting point is to define the biological sample accurately. A developing mammalian placenta contains fetal-derived cell populations and may be associated with maternal tissues. A seed contains maternal tissue, embryo and endosperm. If the samples are not separated appropriately, RNA from maternal cells may be mistaken for preferential maternal expression within the offspring's cells. This problem is not restricted to genomics. Many developmental experiments depend on knowing the tissue of origin of the material that is analyzed. An apparently precise molecular measurement is only as informative as the biological identity of its sample.

An appropriate first genetic measurement establishes that both parental markers are present in the offspring's DNA. This comparison checks the assumption of a biparental locus and helps identify unusual copy-number patterns. If only the maternal marker is present in the DNA of a sampled diploid cell population, maternal RNA expression cannot be interpreted as ordinary repression of a paternal homolog without further investigation. The paternal copy might be physically absent, or two chromosome copies might derive from one parent. If both markers occur in genomic DNA, the next question is whether their RNA contributions differ. A stable expression bias favoring the same parental origin across reciprocal crosses is a more direct indication of imprinting than a total abundance difference alone.

Bisulfite-based DNA methylation analysis provides one way to investigate whether a candidate control region carries an allele-specific methylation pattern. In conventional bisulfite analysis, unmethylated cytosines are converted in a manner that allows them to be distinguished from methylated cytosines by sequencing, although standard bisulfite analysis does not distinguish 5-methylcytosine from some other protected modified bases such as 5-hydroxymethylcytosine. The method is informative when measurements can be assigned to maternal and paternal chromosomes through linked sequence variants or another valid phasing approach. Without allele assignment, a methylation percentage can be misleading. An average methylation value of 50 percent might indicate that every paternal copy is fully methylated and every maternal copy unmethylated. It could also indicate that both homologs are partially methylated, or that different cells carry different patterns. These situations have different regulatory implications.

Suppose a hypothetical ICR contains ten CpG sites. In one embryo, sequencing reads assigned to the paternal allele show methylation at nine or ten sites per molecule, whereas reads assigned to the maternal allele show methylation at zero or one. A second embryo gives the same overall methylation percentage, but each parental allele contains molecules with mixed methylated and unmethylated CpGs. The total average could be close to one-half in both cases. The first pattern is consistent with strong parental differentiation, while the second suggests loss of that distinction or another form of heterogeneity. Examination questions sometimes present an average value and ask whether imprinting has been preserved. Unless parental assignment or equivalent information is available, the correct response is that the average is insufficient.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and related occupancy measurements can address whether a specific protein or histone modification is enriched near an ICR. Conceptually, these experiments associate DNA fragments with a particular chromatin component and then identify the genomic regions represented among those fragments. If sequence markers can distinguish parental alleles, the method can test whether CTCF is enriched on the maternal Igf2/H19 ICR rather than the paternal ICR in an appropriate cell population. The interpretation requires controls for nonspecific recovery, input DNA representation and the specificity of the detected factor. Enrichment supports a physical association but does not establish that the association is necessary for the observed expression state. That requires a perturbation.

Chromosome conformation methods can provide evidence about the relative proximity of DNA regions. In the context of the Igf2/H19 model, such measurements can help determine whether shared enhancers associate preferentially with different parts of the domain on the two parental chromosomes. These observations should be interpreted as evidence about chromatin organization in the sampled population, not as a photograph of a fixed loop present identically in every cell. Differences in measured interaction frequency can be consistent with regulatory access, but they do not automatically establish that a particular contact causes transcription. A convincing mechanistic account is strengthened when altered interaction patterns are accompanied by changes in factor occupancy, allele-specific transcription and the expected response to targeted perturbation.

Genetic perturbation can test necessity. If a candidate regulatory region is deleted from the maternal homolog and a normally silent maternal allele becomes active, the region was necessary for the normal repression in that context. If paternal deletion produces a different outcome, that difference provides information about the two regulatory states. The design should distinguish the parental origin of the altered chromosome, confirm its sequence and compare littermates or equivalent controls. It is also important to examine the expression of several linked genes when perturbing a control region because the region may coordinate an entire domain. A phenotype attributed to one gene may actually arise from combined changes in several products.

A complementary experiment tests the role of a trans-acting factor, such as a methylation maintenance protein. Because the factor may act at many loci, its loss can cause wide developmental disturbances. Such a result establishes that the factor has an important function but may not identify which imprinted gene is responsible for a specific phenotype. A local ICR perturbation can be more informative about the direct role of a particular domain, while a trans-acting perturbation can reveal how multiple parental marks are maintained. The two approaches complement each other. A student analyzing experimental results should ask whether the perturbation is local or global, whether it affects the establishment or the reading of marks, and whether the resulting expression change is a direct consequence or a later response to abnormal development.

Rescue experiments strengthen causal inference. Imagine a maternal ICR alteration that increases the expression of a growth-promoting gene and reduces that of a linked noncoding RNA. If a second perturbation restores the growth-promoting gene to its ordinary dosage while leaving the RNA abnormal, the developmental result can help distinguish the contributions of the two changes. Complete rescue of a specified growth phenotype suggests that altered growth-gene dosage was sufficient to account for that outcome under the tested conditions. Partial rescue suggests additional contributors or incomplete restoration of the relevant expression pattern. Lack of rescue could result from an incorrect causal hypothesis, but could also reflect restoration at the wrong developmental time, tissue or dosage. A negative rescue is informative only when the restoration itself has been verified.

12. Uniparental disomy and domain-level defects: developmental implications without clinical diagnosis

A normal diploid chromosome count does not guarantee that each chromosome pair contains one maternal and one paternal copy. Uniparental disomy describes a situation in which both copies of a chromosome or chromosomal region are inherited from the same parent. Depending on the mechanism, the copies may be different homologs from that parent or identical copies of a single homolog. If an imprinted domain is included, the two copies may possess the same parental regulatory identity even though the amount of DNA is normal. This provides a direct illustration of why a developmental system can depend on the distribution of parental origins rather than total chromosome number alone.

Consider an idealized chromosome region containing two independent imprinted genes. Gene P is expressed exclusively from its paternal copy, and gene M is expressed exclusively from its maternal copy. In an individual with ordinary biparental inheritance, each gene has one active copy. In a cell with maternal uniparental disomy of the region, both copies of P carry the normally silent maternal state, while both copies of M carry the normally active maternal state. Under an independent equal-output model, P has no active copy and M has two. The reverse holds for paternal uniparental disomy. A deletion of the maternal region, however, removes one physical copy rather than replacing it with another paternal copy. Under the same assumptions it removes expression of M, but does not double P dosage. Thus, a deletion and paternal uniparental disomy can share a deficiency while differing in their overall expression consequences.

The distinction between isodisomy and heterodisomy can matter because recessive genetic variants may also contribute to the phenotype. Isodisomy can make an individual homozygous for a variant carried on a single parental homolog. Heterodisomy can retain different parental homologs but still produce the same parental-origin imbalance at imprinted loci. Therefore, an abnormal phenotype associated with uniparental inheritance can arise through imprinting, exposure of a recessive mutation, or a combination of these mechanisms. Demonstrating that a chromosome pair is derived from one parent identifies an important genomic circumstance; it does not by itself prove the cause of every developmental feature.

Human imprinting disorders illustrate the developmental importance of such dosage effects without requiring a clinical diagnostic discussion. Different parent-dependent disruptions in chromosome 15q11-q13 are associated with Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes, involving distinct expression requirements in the region. The familiar example of UBE3A is tissue dependent, with preferential maternal expression in many neurons rather than universal maternal-only expression throughout the organism. At the chromosome 11p15 imprinted domains, disturbances in regulation can be associated with abnormal growth, as illustrated by Beckwith-Wiedemann and Silver-Russell syndromes. These examples should be learned as evidence that parental origin can affect developmental regulation and growth, not as a shortcut for diagnosing an individual or as a claim that each condition has only one molecular cause. [15–17]

The concept of loss of imprinting refers to disruption of the ordinary parent-specific expression pattern. In common usage, a normally silent allele may become expressed, causing biallelic expression or reduced parental bias. The expression term should be distinguished from loss of methylation, which describes the modification state of DNA. One can observe a methylation change without the anticipated transcriptional change if the tissue lacks relevant transcriptional machinery. One can also observe an altered allelic expression pattern through a regulatory perturbation that does not remove the measured methylation mark. A full causal account therefore connects the modification, the regulatory consequence and the developmental outcome rather than treating them as synonyms.

13. Comparative development: imprinting in flowering plants and evolutionary explanations

Genomic imprinting is not confined to mammals. Flowering plants provide another important context, especially in the endosperm. The comparison is useful because it shows that parent-dependent gene regulation can evolve in very different developmental systems. During the usual double fertilization of many angiosperms, one sperm contributes a haploid genome to the egg to form a diploid embryo, and another sperm fuses with the central cell to initiate endosperm development. The central cell commonly contains two maternal haploid genomic contributions, so the resulting endosperm is often triploid, with two maternal genome copies and one paternal copy. This tissue supports embryonic development and may remain as a nutrient-rich component of the mature seed or be consumed during development.

This chromosome ratio introduces an important quantitative difference from the diploid mammalian situation. For a nonimprinted endosperm gene whose copies each contribute equally to transcription, maternal transcripts are expected to represent two-thirds of the total and paternal transcripts one-third. A maternal fraction of approximately 0.67 is therefore not by itself evidence of maternal imprinting. A claim of biased parental expression should be evaluated relative to the expected dosage and the actual copy number in the sampled tissue. Strong maternal-specific expression would approach a maternal fraction of one, whereas strong paternal-specific expression would approach zero. Intermediate values can reflect partial parental biases, unequal transcription per copy, mixed cell types or technical effects. An examination question that silently assumes a 1:1 baseline for normal triploid endosperm is testing a biologically incorrect null model.

Mechanisms of plant imprinting share some broad principles with mammalian imprinting but differ in important molecular details. In Arabidopsis, the DNA glycosylase DEMETER participates in removal of methylated cytosines in the female central cell. As a result, some loci have different methylation states in the maternally and paternally inherited copies within endosperm. MET1 contributes to maintenance of methylation in plants, while Polycomb-group-mediated chromatin regulation is important at particular imprinted loci. A maternally expressed gene may be activated because methylation is removed from its maternal regulatory region; in other cases, maternal chromatin repression can support preferential paternal expression. The particular outcome depends on the locus. It would be incorrect to transfer the mammalian CTCF-dependent Igf2/H19 mechanism to all plant loci merely because the organisms share the broad phenomenon of imprinting [11,12].

Plant endosperm also offers a clear example of the problem of maternal tissue contamination. The seed coat develops from maternal tissues and has the maternal genotype. If RNA from seed coat cells is included in an endosperm sample, it can increase the apparent maternal fraction even for a gene that is not imprinted in endosperm. The relevant prediction depends on how strongly the gene is expressed in the contaminating tissue, not merely on the amount of contaminating material. A small number of cells expressing a very abundant transcript can substantially alter a bulk RNA measurement. Careful tissue dissection or methods that assign transcripts to the relevant nuclei strengthen the conclusion that the parental bias is intrinsic to the developing endosperm.

The functional importance of imprinting in both mammalian placental development and plant endosperm development has inspired evolutionary explanations. One influential hypothesis is the parental conflict or kinship theory. In simplified form, the maternal and paternal alleles of an offspring can differ in their average relatedness to other offspring competing for resources, especially where a mother produces offspring with different fathers. Under such conditions, an allele inherited through the father may benefit from increased resource acquisition by the individual offspring even if this imposes a cost on maternal relatives, whereas an allele inherited through the mother may have a different balance of relatedness to those relatives. Natural selection can therefore favor different expression strategies for some loci depending on parental origin. [13,14]

This reasoning provides a possible explanation for the association of certain imprinted genes with fetal or seed growth. It should not be treated as a rule that all paternally expressed genes promote growth and all maternally expressed genes restrict it. The predictions depend on assumptions about mating patterns, relatedness, resource allocation, the particular function of a gene and how expression affects fitness. Other explanations of imprinting involve coadaptation between maternal and offspring traits, dosage regulation or historical relationships with mechanisms that control repetitive DNA. Different mechanisms and selective pressures may contribute at different loci. The presence of parental-origin expression demonstrates a regulatory pattern, not the evolutionary reason why that pattern arose. Evidence for an evolutionary hypothesis requires comparative and functional information beyond the demonstration of imprinting itself. [13,14]

The comparison also highlights the need to separate tissue function from organismal analogy. Endosperm and placenta both participate in supplying resources to a developing offspring, but they have different embryological origins, organization and chromosome compositions. Imprinting in mammalian somatic and placental tissues and imprinting in plant endosperm are not identical developmental programs. The most useful generalization is that parent-dependent regulation can affect dosage in resource-supporting tissues and other developmental contexts. The molecular details must be learned from the appropriate organism, and quantitative assumptions must match its tissue genetics.

14. Integrating methylation dynamics with quantitative developmental reasoning

A useful quantitative model of imprint maintenance begins with a fully methylated CpG duplex. Consider a population of cells that divides synchronously. Assume that every CpG is copied accurately at the DNA sequence level, that no new methyl groups are added after replication, and that existing methyl groups are not actively removed. Initially each duplex has two methylated cytosines at the site. After one round of replication, each daughter duplex has one methylated cytosine on its inherited strand and one unmethylated cytosine on its newly synthesized strand. The original methylated cytosines have not disappeared, but they are distributed across twice as many strands. The fraction of methylated cytosines is therefore half of its initial value. After a second division, the fraction is one quarter of the original value. In the idealized model, the fraction after n divisions is the starting fraction multiplied by two raised to the power negative n.

This dilution model helps distinguish passive and active loss. Passive loss follows from replication when restoration of methylation is incomplete. It can occur without enzymatic removal of the original modified cytosine. Active demethylation involves biochemical processes that alter or replace modified cytosines independently of simple dilution. In real embryos both kinds of process may contribute to observed methylation dynamics, and maintenance is not necessarily completely absent or completely effective. The simple formula is not a universal description of embryonic methylation; it makes a clear prediction under stated assumptions. If a site loses almost all detectable methylation in a nondividing cell, passive dilution through DNA replication alone cannot explain the observation. If a proliferating cell population loses methylation in a pattern consistent with the number of divisions, passive dilution is a plausible mechanism, but other explanations still need consideration.

We can extend the model to a simplified partial maintenance situation. Assume that the probability of methylation being added to a newly synthesized cytosine opposite an old methylated cytosine is q. Assume further that existing methyl groups are not removed and that no methylation is added opposite an unmethylated old cytosine. For each old methylated cytosine, the next replication cycle retains one existing methyl group and adds an average of q new methyl groups. The total number of cytosines doubles during replication. Consequently, the expected fraction of methylated cytosines after one round is the initial fraction multiplied by (1 + q)/2. With constant q and the other assumptions unchanged, repeated divisions multiply this factor repeatedly. If q equals one, the fraction is maintained; if q equals zero, the passive dilution model is recovered. With q equal to 0.8, the retention factor is 0.9 per division. After five divisions, an initially fully methylated population would have an expected methylated fraction of about 0.5905 in this model. This is an illustrative calculation, not a measured maintenance efficiency for any particular embryonic tissue.

The calculation also helps explain why an apparently modest imperfection can become substantial over many divisions. Development is not a single cellular transition; it involves branching lineages that proliferate at different rates. If one lineage divides rapidly and another divides slowly, an identical defect of maintenance machinery may produce different degrees of methylation dilution by the same chronological time. Conversely, a rapid apparent decline in a sample may reflect expansion of a low-methylation population rather than continued loss within each individual cell. A developmental experiment must therefore distinguish changes occurring within cells from changes in the composition of the sample. A time series with cell lineage information can be more informative than a bulk measurement at two ages.

A related model asks how an allele-specific expression ratio changes when two cell populations are mixed. Suppose population A has ten cells, each producing ten units of RNA from the paternal allele and none from the maternal allele. Population B has ten cells, each producing one unit from each allele. The sample contains 110 paternal units and 10 maternal units, so the paternal fraction is 110 divided by 120, approximately 0.917. The sample appears strongly paternally biased even though half its cells are biallelic. If population B instead produces twenty units from each allele per cell, its contribution dominates the sample: paternal units total 300 and maternal units total 200, giving a paternal fraction of 0.6. The proportion of cells with monoallelic expression has not changed. The relative output of the populations has changed. This is why a bulk allele fraction is a transcript-weighted average, not a direct measurement of the percentage of cells expressing a particular parental copy.

The distinction between fraction and absolute abundance has a broader developmental significance. Cells at different stages may increase their total RNA output, change their size, or begin producing a transcript that was previously rare. A tissue can therefore display an apparent change in imprinting strength even if its component cell populations each retain their own expression rules. Conversely, stable bulk ratios may hide changes in particular lineages. A cell-specific analysis, together with an assessment of lineage markers and expression abundance, can help distinguish these interpretations. In examination problems, the safest approach is to state what the measured quantity actually represents before converting it into a claim about developmental regulation.

15. An integrated case: reconstructing the cause of a parent-dependent growth defect

Imagine a hypothetical mouse study centered on a growth-promoting gene called G and a nearby noncoding RNA called R. Previous work indicates that G is normally expressed mainly from the paternal chromosome in fetal tissue, while R is expressed mainly from the maternal chromosome. A researcher observes that offspring carrying a variant in a candidate control region show increased growth when the variant is inherited from the mother, but not when it is inherited from the father. The first temptation is to identify the variant as an imprinting mutation. However, the observation by itself is a parent-dependent phenotype, not yet proof of the molecular mechanism. The variant could influence an imprinted domain, the mother's physiology, or another process associated with the particular cross.

The next step is to establish the relevant DNA composition and expression. Suppose the experimental offspring carry one maternal and one paternal copy of G and R in both cross directions. There is no detectable coding mutation in either gene. RNA measurements from carefully matched fetal tissues show that maternal transmission of the control-region variant leads to expression of G from both homologs and a reduction in R expression. Paternal transmission does not produce that pattern. These observations narrow the plausible explanation toward a local parent-dependent regulatory defect. Nevertheless, it remains important to compare total G abundance as well as its allele ratio. If the maternal copy becomes active while paternal transcription decreases by a matching amount, biallelic expression might not increase total G dosage. If both alleles contribute substantially without compensation, the dosage increases and offers a possible explanation for the growth phenotype.

Suppose an occupancy measurement shows that a regulatory protein normally binds the maternal control region but binds poorly when the variant is present. Methylation measurements show that the maternal copy remains largely unmethylated in early development. The binding defect could then arise directly from the altered DNA recognition sequence rather than from an initial failure to remove methylation. This hypothesis can be examined by comparing binding to normal and variant DNA under otherwise equivalent conditions. If the variant reduces binding even when both DNA samples are unmethylated, the sequence change itself can account for the biochemical defect. In this example, the inherited alteration is genetic, but its effect is on an allele-specific epigenetic regulatory system.

The developmental role of excess G expression can be examined by restoring G dosage in the affected offspring while leaving the control-region variant and its effect on R unchanged. If normal growth is restored, the result strengthens the causal link between excess G and the measured growth phenotype. It does not prove that reduced R is biologically irrelevant in every tissue or at every stage. Other traits may remain abnormal. If growth is only partly restored, it is necessary to check whether G dosage was corrected in the relevant tissue and at the appropriate time before concluding that R is responsible for the residual phenotype. A rescue result is most informative when the molecular correction and the developmental outcome are measured separately.

This case connects the major ideas of the chapter without assuming that all real imprinted genes behave like G and R. It begins with a reciprocal inheritance pattern, examines the physical DNA copies, tests parent-specific RNA production, explores the role of a local regulatory element, distinguishes sequence-dependent binding from methylation-dependent binding, and asks how expression produces a developmental consequence. Each stage removes a different alternative explanation. The final claim is more precise than the starting observation: a parent-dependent regulatory perturbation changes the functional dosage of a growth-related gene in a defined tissue, and correction of that dosage can test whether it accounts for a particular developmental phenotype. This style of reasoning is central to the next node, which considers mutants and transgenic models as experimental tools for distinguishing correlation from developmental causation.

Misconceptions and exam traps

  1. An imprinted allele has been deleted. The sequence usually remains present; expression is regulated.
  2. All maternal alleles are silent. Expression direction varies with gene and tissue.
  3. All genes are imprinted. Only a limited subset shows parent-dependent regulation.
  4. Methylation always represses every nearby gene. At Igf2/H19 it prevents an enhancer-blocking protein from binding.
  5. Imprinting means the same as maternal determinants. Cytoplasmic determinants act through partitioned products; imprints distinguish chromosome copies.
  6. An imprint stays grandparental forever. Germline erasure and sex-specific establishment reset origin.
  7. DNMT1 establishes all germline marks. DNMT1 mainly supports maintenance; DNMT3A contributes to de novo establishment.
  8. Reprogramming erases every mark at fertilization. Many canonical germline imprints are selectively protected.
  9. A DMR automatically proves an ICR. Functional perturbation is needed to establish regulation.
  10. Monoallelic expression always proves imprinting. Random or sequence-biased expression may also be monoallelic.
  11. A 50% bulk methylation value proves normal imprinting. Allele-resolved measurements are necessary.
  12. A 2:1 maternal RNA ratio in triploid endosperm proves maternal imprinting. Equal per-copy expression predicts that baseline.
  13. Maternal UPD equals maternal deletion. UPD retains two same-origin copies; deletion reduces copy number.
  14. Fate determination always requires parental imprinting. Somatic feedback and chromatin states can sustain fate independently.
  15. Growth doubles when RNA doubles. Dose-response, developmental timing and compensation matter.
  16. All imprinting begins with methylation. Noncanonical histone-based imprinting exists in defined contexts.
  17. A reciprocal phenotype proves the candidate locus is imprinted. Maternal physiology, provisioning and other effects must be excluded.
  18. All paternal imprinted genes promote growth. Kinship theory is a conditional evolutionary hypothesis, not a universal rule.
  19. A control-region DNA variant cannot change epigenetic regulation. Sequence changes can disrupt establishment or reading of a mark.

High-yield mechanism recap

Parental origin determines regulatory state at selected loci. Establishment in gametes, protection in the early embryo, maintenance during somatic divisions and germline erasure form a developmental cycle. At canonical mouse Igf2/H19, an unmethylated maternal ICR binds CTCF, restricting maternal Igf2 expression; the paternal methylated ICR lacks that boundary configuration, enabling paternal Igf2 while paternal H19 is repressed. Distinguish allele expression from copy number and from tissue-specific developmental memory.
