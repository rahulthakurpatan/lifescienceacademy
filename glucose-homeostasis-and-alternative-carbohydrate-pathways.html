1. The glucose problem: why breakdown is only half the story

A meal supplies carbohydrate over a limited period, but cells need energy continuously. Between meals the intestine may deliver little glucose while the brain, red blood cells and other tissues continue consuming it. During muscular work, glucose demand rises within seconds. Conversely, after a meal the body must accommodate a temporary surplus without allowing extracellular glucose to rise indefinitely. These conflicting demands explain why glycolysis alone is insufficient. A living organism needs a system that can store carbohydrate, release it, manufacture glucose from other molecules and divert part of the carbohydrate supply into useful products that are not ATP.

Glucose homeostasis means regulation of glucose availability despite changing input and output. It does not mean every cell maintains an identical glucose concentration or that glucose metabolism stops at a set point. It is a dynamic balance of fluxes. A blood glucose pool can remain nearly constant while molecules enter and leave rapidly. To reason correctly, distinguish a concentration (amount per volume) from a flux (amount per time). In a simple well-mixed pool of fixed volume, the rate of change of glucose amount equals intestinal delivery plus endogenous production minus tissue uptake. If all three rates change but input and output remain equal, the pool does not change. This mass balance is the basis of fed and fasting reasoning.

Inside a cell, glucose is usually phosphorylated at carbon 6 by hexokinase or glucokinase. Glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) carries negative charge at physiological pH and does not leave through an ordinary glucose transporter. That chemical change creates a crossroads: G6P can enter glycolysis for ATP and carbon skeletons, become glucose-1-phosphate for glycogen, enter the pentose phosphate pathway to supply reducing power and five-carbon sugars, or be dephosphorylated to free glucose in cells equipped to export it. Thus the same intermediate can have quite different fates depending on enzyme expression, energy demand and hormonal conditions.

The previous chapters establish glycolysis, the TCA cycle and mitochondrial energy conversion. Here those principles are used to explain glucose economy. Keep three separate ledgers while studying each route: carbon atoms, energy (ATP or equivalent phosphate bonds), and reducing equivalents (NADH or NADPH). A route may conserve carbon but consume ATP, or sacrifice carbon as carbon dioxide while producing reducing power. The correct interpretation follows from these balances rather than from a memorised pathway name.

2. Glycogen architecture: a chemical solution to storage and rapid release

Free glucose is a useful transport fuel but an inefficient bulk intracellular reserve. Each dissolved molecule contributes to osmotic pressure. Packaging thousands of glucosyl residues into a hydrated glycogen particle therefore reduces the number of independently dissolved solute particles relative to the same number of free glucose molecules. Glycogen is not completely osmotically inert, but polymerisation makes large carbohydrate reserves much more compatible with cellular water balance.

The polymer consists mainly of α-D-glucosyl residues linked by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds, with α(1→6) linkages at branches. The carbon numbered 1 in one residue is linked to carbon 4 of the next along a chain; at a branch the linkage is to carbon 6. These bonds explain both the chemistry of synthesis and the specificity of degradation enzymes. A typical animal glycogen molecule has frequent branches, often described as one branch per roughly 8–12 residues on average, although actual chain lengths vary. Each added branch creates one additional nonreducing end. Most synthetic and degradative enzymes work at these exposed ends, so a branched particle can be processed at many sites at once.

The reducing end is the end whose anomeric carbon would be free in an unmodified chain. In ordinary protein-primed glycogen, the core reducing end is attached to a tyrosine of glycogenin. A tree-like particle has one root but many growing tips. Do not confuse a branch point with a nonreducing end; a branch point is an internal residue carrying an extra linkage. A simple connectivity exercise makes the advantage clear: a connected unbranched chain has one nonreducing tip, and each new binary branch adds one more tip. If a particle has 24 such branch points, its idealised tree has 25 nonreducing ends. Its actual rate of degradation will also depend on accessibility and enzyme availability.

Glycogen is abundant in liver and skeletal muscle, but the two reserves serve different purposes. Liver glycogen can support blood glucose because hepatocytes possess the glucose-6-phosphatase system. Skeletal muscle glycogen is largely a local fuel; muscle cells have no significant capacity for that final glucose-export reaction. Muscle may contain more glycogen in total because muscle mass is large, even if its concentration is lower than in liver. The distinction between total amount and tissue concentration is essential for interpreting exam statements.

Glycogen particles are associated with metabolic enzymes and regulatory proteins. They are not naked static deposits. A reserve must be built and dismantled by selective enzymes, and a branch architecture is useful only if those enzymes can handle both kinds of linkage. This leads directly to glycogenesis.

3. Glycogenesis: activating glucose and extending a branched polymer

The first step in building glycogen is to direct phosphorylated glucose toward the position required for glycosyl transfer. Phosphoglucomutase interconverts G6P and glucose-1-phosphate (G1P). It acts through a phosphorylated enzyme and a transient glucose-1,6-bisphosphate intermediate: a phosphate is first placed at the other position and then one phosphate is returned to the enzyme. The net result is a relocation of phosphate without direct ATP hydrolysis. This reversibility is useful because the same enzyme serves glycogen breakdown later.

G1P is not itself the immediate donor for glycogen synthase. UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase reacts G1P with UTP to form UDP-glucose and pyrophosphate (PPi). Hydrolysis of PPi into two inorganic phosphates helps pull the activation reaction forward. UDP-glucose is a nucleotide sugar: the UDP group provides a leaving group and makes the anomeric carbon suitable for enzyme-catalysed transfer. The apparent expense is a solution to a thermodynamic problem, because a controlled bond-forming reaction must have a favourable overall driving force. The UDP released during transfer can be converted back to UTP by nucleoside diphosphate kinase using ATP.

G1P + UTP → UDP-glucose + PPi; PPi + H₂O → 2 Pi

Glycogen synthase attaches the glucosyl group from UDP-glucose to the hydroxyl at carbon 4 of a terminal glucose residue, extending an α(1→4) chain. Unlike many polymerases, the usual mammalian enzyme cannot efficiently begin a new chain from individual glucose molecules. Glycogenin provides the classical initiation mechanism by transferring glucose to its own tyrosine residue and adding a short chain of further residues. Glycogen synthase then elongates this primer. Glycogenin-independent glycogen formation has been observed in some contexts, so primer dependence describes the canonical pathway rather than an absolute rule for every cell.

An elongating chain still lacks the branching needed for rapid access. The branching enzyme, a 4:6 transferase, breaks an α(1→4) bond and transfers a terminal segment of a sufficiently long chain to the C6 hydroxyl of another residue on the same or a neighbouring chain. This creates an α(1→6) linkage and an extra nonreducing end. In standard textbook examples, a segment of around six or seven residues may be moved, with minimum lengths and spacing constraints; precise chain distributions vary. Branch formation rearranges existing bonds, rather than adding a new glucose residue or consuming a separate ATP for every branch. The balance between extension and branching establishes particle architecture.

For energetic accounting, one glucose arriving as free glucose costs one ATP to become G6P, then one UTP to become UDP-glucose. If UTP is regenerated with ATP, this is two ATP equivalents per residue added to a pre-existing glycogen molecule. Starting from G6P instead costs one ATP equivalent. Avoid counting hydrolysis of PPi as another ATP molecule in this ledger: it supplies driving force but is a product of the same activation step. The exact bookkeeping depends on what starting substrates the question specifies. If 40 residues are added from free glucose and all nucleotide triphosphates are regenerated, the cost is 40 ATP for phosphorylation and 40 ATP equivalents for UTP regeneration, or 80 in total.

The glucose stored in glycogen is not trapped forever. It must be released quickly under different conditions, without paying the full energetic price of hydrolysing every bond and phosphorylating each glucose again. The chemistry of glycogen breakdown solves this problem.

4. Glycogenolysis: phosphorolysis, branch removal and tissue-specific products

Glycogen phosphorylase catalyses the reaction of an α(1→4) bond with inorganic phosphate rather than water. The bond breaks, and the glucosyl residue leaves as glucose-1-phosphate. This is phosphorolysis. The reaction preserves some chemical energy of the glycosidic linkage in a sugar phosphate and avoids the ATP requirement that would otherwise follow the release of free glucose. The enzyme uses pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), whose phosphate group participates in acid–base catalysis. PLP here has a different role from its familiar role in amino acid transamination. Phosphorylase works progressively from nonreducing ends.

A glycogen particle cannot be completely dismantled by phosphorylase. Its active site cannot approach an α(1→6) branch closely, and it typically stops about four residues from the branch point, leaving a phosphorylase limit dextrin. A mammalian debranching enzyme provides two activities in the same polypeptide. Its transferase moves three outer residues from the short branch onto a neighbouring α(1→4) chain, exposing a single residue still attached by an α(1→6) bond. Its α(1→6)-glucosidase activity hydrolyses that remaining bond, yielding free glucose. Phosphorylase can resume attack on the newly accessible chain. Therefore most residues leave as G1P, while branch-point residues leave as free glucose. The fraction of free glucose depends on actual branch frequency, not on an invariant percentage.

Phosphoglucomutase converts G1P to G6P. In skeletal muscle G6P can enter glycolysis without the hexokinase investment. For conversion to two lactate molecules, the later pathway produces four ATP and consumes one ATP at phosphofructokinase-1; net three ATP per phosphorylated glucosyl residue, instead of two ATP from free glucose. The branch-derived free glucose requires phosphorylation and yields two ATP under the same conditions. For a simplified breakdown of 100 residues, 90 via phosphorolysis and 10 via hydrolysis, the muscle obtains 90 × 3 + 10 × 2 = 290 ATP if all are converted to lactate. This is a local gross pathway gain; it excludes the prior energy used to manufacture glycogen.

In liver, G6P can enter the endoplasmic reticulum through a transporter and be hydrolysed by the luminal glucose-6-phosphatase system. Free glucose can return to the cytosol and leave the cell through a facilitative glucose transporter. The liver thus sacrifices the sugar phosphate bond to meet the organism's glucose requirement. Kidney cortex and intestinal tissue also express relevant glucose-producing machinery under appropriate conditions. A question describing isolated skeletal muscle should not assume that muscle can use its glycogen to deliver large quantities of free glucose to blood.

A minor but important alternative degradation route occurs in lysosomes, where acid α-glucosidase hydrolyses glycogen. It is separate from the principal cytosolic phosphorolytic pathway; its deficiency produces lysosomal glycogen accumulation (Pompe disease). This distinction illustrates how the same substrate may require different enzymes in different compartments. Cytosolic phosphorylase cannot simply take over degradation of glycogen sequestered in a lysosome.

Synthesis and degradation are therefore chemically distinct pathways, not reversals of a single enzyme reaction. Their simultaneous full activation would consume nucleotide triphosphates with little useful net storage or release. The next step is to understand how the cell chooses which direction to favour.

5. Choosing storage or mobilisation: reciprocal glycogen regulation

A change in hormone concentration cannot itself break a glycosidic bond. It must change enzyme activity through an intracellular signalling network. In a fasting liver, glucagon activates its G protein-coupled receptor, raising cAMP through adenylyl cyclase. cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates phosphorylase kinase. The activated kinase phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase b to phosphorylase a, promoting glycogenolysis. PKA also phosphorylates glycogen synthase at regulatory sites and reduces its activity, discouraging glycogen synthesis. One signal therefore favours release and disfavors storage.

Adrenaline (epinephrine) mobilises glycogen in liver and skeletal muscle during acute demand. In skeletal muscle, contraction provides another signal: Ca²⁺ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum binds the calmodulin subunit of phosphorylase kinase, enabling glycogen mobilisation to respond to the same ion that initiates contraction. Rising AMP in active muscle can activate the b form of muscle phosphorylase allosterically, while ATP and G6P oppose that activation. Muscle does not normally respond to glucagon through a significant direct glycogenolytic signalling pathway. A high AMP signal in muscle represents local energy stress, not necessarily low blood glucose.

After a meal, insulin promotes glycogen synthesis by encouraging glucose use and dephosphorylation of regulatory proteins. In skeletal muscle it stimulates GLUT4 recruitment, increasing glucose entry. Insulin receptor signalling through Akt inhibits glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3); this reduces inhibitory phosphorylation of glycogen synthase. Protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) removes phosphates from phosphorylase kinase and phosphorylase, tending to shut down breakdown, and removes inhibitory phosphates from glycogen synthase, increasing synthesis. These actions cooperate rather than acting as independent on/off switches. G6P also activates glycogen synthase allosterically, allowing substrate availability to favour storage.

The liver has a specialised glucose-sensing relationship: glucose binding to liver phosphorylase a favours a less active conformation and promotes its dephosphorylation by PP1. This facilitates the transition from glycogen breakdown to synthesis after glucose becomes abundant. The muscle isozyme responds much more strongly to its own energy state. Thus phosphorylation does not have a universal effect on all proteins, and even forms of the same enzyme can behave differently. Phosphorylation favours activity of phosphorylase, while it generally inhibits glycogen synthase; these opposite effects are precisely what makes a coordinated switch possible.

Consider an illustrative assay where liver phosphorylase activity is 80 units before and 24 units after insulin, whereas synthase activity increases from 15 to 60 units. The phosphorylase activity falls by (80 − 24)/80 = 70%, and synthase activity rises fourfold. These changes support a switch toward storage, but activity measured in an extract is not itself a complete flux measurement. Intracellular substrate levels, transport and enzyme localisation also determine the actual rate. This distinction links the regulatory principles of 1.D with the carbon accounting of this chapter.

6. Gluconeogenesis: why the liver cannot simply reverse glycolysis

Glycogen is a finite reserve. Even before it is exhausted, the liver begins to form new glucose from noncarbohydrate precursors. This process is gluconeogenesis. It becomes increasingly important during fasting, sustained exercise and conditions where carbohydrate intake is low. It is not the same as glycogenolysis: breaking glycogen releases previously stored glucosyl residues, whereas gluconeogenesis assembles a six-carbon sugar from smaller molecules. The liver is a major site, and renal cortex contributes especially during prolonged fasting. Some intestinal gluconeogenesis can also occur. The pathway spans mitochondria, cytosol and endoplasmic reticulum.

The metabolic route is easiest to understand by looking back at glycolysis. Many glycolytic reactions have small actual free-energy changes in the cell and can change direction when reactant and product concentrations change. Three reactions, however, are strongly displaced from equilibrium under usual glycolytic conditions: glucose phosphorylation by hexokinase or glucokinase, fructose-6-phosphate phosphorylation by phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1), and conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate by pyruvate kinase. Simply reversing those reactions would be energetically unfavourable under physiological conditions. The cell uses alternative enzyme-catalysed bypasses instead.

A thermodynamic distinction matters: an enzyme never changes the equilibrium constant of a reaction; it changes the speed of approach to equilibrium. A bypass changes the overall chemistry, often by coupling to nucleotide hydrolysis or hydrolysing a phosphate ester rather than making ATP. This provides a favourable route in the reverse direction at the expense of energy. It also creates separate regulatory points so glucose consumption and glucose production need not run strongly at the same time.

The principal glucogenic precursors are lactate, pyruvate, glycerol, and carbon skeletons of glucogenic amino acids such as alanine. Lactate dehydrogenase oxidises lactate to pyruvate using NAD⁺, generating cytosolic NADH. Alanine aminotransferase transfers the amino group from alanine to α-ketoglutarate, forming pyruvate and glutamate; the carbon becomes available for glucose but the nitrogen must be handled separately. Glycerol enters at the triose-phosphate level, bypassing the pyruvate and PEP reactions. Glucogenic amino acids entering the TCA cycle may provide oxaloacetate or other intermediates that can be converted to it. Their net contribution depends on the pathway and anaplerotic balance.

Even-chain fatty acid acetyl-CoA is not a source of net glucose in mammals. The pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction cannot normally be reversed, and the TCA cycle regenerates its catalytic oxaloacetate rather than producing a net additional four-carbon acceptor from acetyl-CoA. Fatty acid oxidation nevertheless provides ATP, NADH and acetyl-CoA that support gluconeogenesis. Glycerol from triacylglycerol is glucogenic; propionyl-CoA from odd-chain fatty acids can enter metabolism as succinyl-CoA and provide glucogenic carbon. Organisms with the glyoxylate cycle can conserve acetyl-derived carbon in ways mammals cannot. Distinguish the source of energy from the source of glucose carbon.

7. The first bypass: pyruvate carboxylase, PEPCK and compartmental redox

The reverse of pyruvate kinase would require forming the high phosphoryl-transfer-potential compound phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) directly from pyruvate using ATP. Instead, gluconeogenesis proceeds through oxaloacetate. In the mitochondrial matrix, pyruvate carboxylase adds bicarbonate-derived carbon to the three-carbon pyruvate, producing four-carbon oxaloacetate. ATP activates bicarbonate and enzyme-bound biotin temporarily carries the activated carboxyl group. The enzyme transfers that carboxyl group to pyruvate. Biotin is a covalently attached swinging carrier, moving the intermediate between catalytic sites; this is a good example of vitamin-derived cofactors serving reaction chemistry rather than being consumed as fuels.

Pyruvate + HCO₃⁻ + ATP → oxaloacetate + ADP + Pi

Acetyl-CoA allosterically activates mammalian pyruvate carboxylase. The signal makes biological sense: when fatty acid oxidation is generating abundant acetyl-CoA, pyruvate can be spared from oxidative decarboxylation and used to generate oxaloacetate for glucose synthesis or replenish TCA intermediates. Acetyl-CoA is an activator and energy-related signal, not the carbon donor in the carboxylation reaction.

Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) subsequently decarboxylates oxaloacetate and transfers phosphate from GTP, forming PEP. Decarboxylation and phosphoryl transfer cooperate to produce an energetically valuable enol phosphate. The carbon introduced by pyruvate carboxylase is released again in the direct two-step route, so bicarbonate is not retained as an additional net carbon in glucose made this way. Each pyruvate costs one ATP and one GTP before reaching PEP. Per glucose, two pyruvates require two ATP and two GTP just for this bypass.

Oxaloacetate + GTP → PEP + GDP + CO₂

A membrane problem now appears: oxaloacetate does not freely cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. There are mitochondrial and cytosolic forms of PEPCK, and the route depends on tissue, substrate and redox requirements. With cytosolic PEPCK, mitochondrial oxaloacetate may be reduced to malate by mitochondrial malate dehydrogenase using NADH. Malate is transported outward and oxidised back to oxaloacetate by cytosolic malate dehydrogenase, producing NADH in the cytosol. Cytosolic PEPCK then generates PEP. This malate route transfers both carbon skeleton and reducing equivalents to the compartment where the reverse glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase reaction will need NADH.

If lactate is the precursor, its oxidation to pyruvate already produces the cytosolic NADH required later. A route that makes PEP in the mitochondrion and exports PEP can therefore be favourable in suitable tissues. Oxaloacetate can also participate in transamination and aspartate exchange. Do not memorise one universal transport route: the deciding constraints are enzyme localisation and the requirement for cytosolic reducing equivalents. Electron carriers are not simply a freely shared pool across mitochondrial membranes. This explains why the substrate in an exam question can alter the preferred route even when the same amount of glucose is formed.

Pyruvate carboxylase also replenishes TCA intermediates in non-gluconeogenic settings. The reaction's presence does not prove that all oxaloacetate is converted into glucose. PEPCK expression and the other bypass enzymes must be considered. This is an example of a shared enzyme serving different pathway fluxes according to cellular demand.

8. The remaining bypasses and the complete energy ledger

Once PEP is available, the reversible glycolytic steps run in the glucose-forming direction. Enolase converts PEP into 2-phosphoglycerate, phosphoglycerate mutase shifts phosphate to make 3-phosphoglycerate, and phosphoglycerate kinase consumes ATP to make 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase then consumes NADH and converts this intermediate to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP), releasing inorganic phosphate. The two three-carbon molecules must be brought together in different chemical forms: triose phosphate isomerase converts one GAP to dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP), and aldolase combines DHAP with GAP to produce fructose-1,6-bisphosphate.

The second bypass is fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBPase-1). Rather than synthesising ATP from fructose-1,6-bisphosphate and ADP, the enzyme hydrolyses the phosphate at carbon 1 to give fructose-6-phosphate and Pi. Phosphoglucose isomerase subsequently interconverts fructose-6-phosphate and G6P. The third bypass, glucose-6-phosphatase, hydrolyses G6P to glucose plus Pi within the endoplasmic reticulum lumen. The compartmentalised hydrolysis allows glucose-producing tissues to release glucose to the circulation. Its absence from ordinary muscle metabolism prevents significant systemic glucose output even if G6P is abundant.

Now derive the energy bill for one glucose from two pyruvates. Two pyruvate carboxylase reactions use two ATP. Two PEPCK reactions use two GTP. Running phosphoglycerate kinase backwards for two three-carbon intermediates uses two more ATP. Two NADH reduce the two molecules of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to GAP. The two phosphatases hydrolyse their substrates but do not recover nucleotide triphosphates. Overall, four ATP plus two GTP are consumed, along with two NADH. We refer to six high-energy phosphate equivalents, since GTP can be regenerated from GDP at the expense of ATP. NADH is accounted for separately as reducing power, not silently added as ATP. One commonly used biochemical net equation, with water and protons written in the usual convention, is:

2 pyruvate + 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH + 2 H⁺ + 6 H₂O → glucose + 4 ADP + 2 GDP + 6 Pi + 2 NAD⁺

The equation is a net representation; carbon dioxide added and removed in the two-step bypass cancels in the carbon ledger (bicarbonate/CO₂ proton bookkeeping depends on the acid–base convention). It shows why reversing the overall process is expensive. Glycolysis of free glucose to two pyruvates gives only two net ATP and two NADH. If both pathways were to run at equal flux, carbon and NADH would recycle but four high-energy phosphate equivalents would be dissipated per glucose. Some substrate cycling can aid regulation or heat production, but unrestricted simultaneous high flux would be wasteful.

Worked example: a liver preparation converts 0.75 mmol of pyruvate to glucose with no other glucogenic carbon source. Carbon conservation gives 0.375 mmol glucose. ATP consumption is 4 × 0.375 = 1.50 mmol; GTP consumption is 2 × 0.375 = 0.75 mmol; cytosolic NADH consumption at the reducing step is 0.75 mmol. The total is 2.25 mmol nucleotide triphosphate equivalents. If the substrate is lactate instead, two lactate oxidations supply the required NADH internally per glucose; the ATP/GTP requirement is still six equivalents. This distinction is useful in pathway balance questions.

Glycerol requires a shorter route. Glycerol kinase uses ATP to form glycerol-3-phosphate. Cytosolic glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase oxidises this to DHAP while reducing NAD⁺. Two DHAP molecules can yield one glucose by converting one to GAP and using aldolase, FBPase-1, phosphoglucose isomerase and glucose-6-phosphatase. Thus two glycerol molecules require two ATP for phosphorylation and generate two NADH in this simplified cytosolic route. The pathway enters above the ATP- and GTP-consuming pyruvate bypass and above the reverse GAP dehydrogenase reaction. This shows why the cost of glucose synthesis depends on its precursor, not just the final product.

9. Reciprocal regulation of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis

The most economical way to direct flux is to regulate the bypasses and their competing irreversible reactions in opposite directions. At the fructose phosphate junction, PFK-1 is stimulated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate (F2,6BP) and inhibited by abundant ATP and citrate in typical mammalian regulation. FBPase-1 is inhibited by AMP and F2,6BP. An increase in AMP therefore signals that energy is scarce and discourages ATP-consuming glucose synthesis while favouring glycolytic energy generation. In contrast, high citrate can signal abundant carbon and energy; it favours FBPase-1 activity and opposes PFK-1 in liver.

F2,6BP is especially instructive because it is a regulator, not the fructose-1,6-bisphosphate intermediate consumed by aldolase. The positions of the phosphate groups are different. F2,6BP is synthesised from fructose-6-phosphate by the PFK-2 activity and degraded by the FBPase-2 activity of a bifunctional enzyme. In the classical liver isoform, glucagon raises cAMP and activates PKA, which phosphorylates the bifunctional enzyme. This lowers PFK-2 activity and raises FBPase-2 activity. F2,6BP falls; the activation of PFK-1 is weakened, and inhibition of FBPase-1 is relieved. The net direction shifts toward glucose synthesis.

Insulin in the fed liver favours the dephosphorylated state of this regulatory enzyme, raising F2,6BP and stimulating glycolysis relative to gluconeogenesis. These are isoform-specific statements: phosphorylation of some cardiac PFK-2 isoforms increases kinase activity instead. Do not infer the direction from the word phosphorylation alone. The exam question must specify tissue or enzyme form if the distinction matters. A mutation that locks the liver enzyme in its kinase-active state during fasting would be predicted to sustain F2,6BP, favour PFK-1 and oppose FBPase-1, although whole-cell glucose output would also depend on other control points.

At the pyruvate junction, liver pyruvate kinase is inhibited by PKA-dependent phosphorylation during glucagon signalling, limiting conversion of newly made PEP back to pyruvate. Acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase; sufficient fatty acid oxidation provides ATP and reducing equivalents. Insulin and glucagon also change expression of metabolic genes over longer periods, including PEPCK and glucose-6-phosphatase. These transcriptional responses operate on a different timescale from allosteric regulation and reversible phosphorylation. In fasting, a coordinated response increases glucose output rather than merely activating one enzyme in isolation.

Redox conditions can defeat an otherwise favourable hormonal programme. Excess cytosolic NADH shifts lactate dehydrogenase toward lactate and can shift the malate dehydrogenase equilibrium toward malate. This reduces availability of pyruvate and oxaloacetate for glucose production from suitable precursors. Ethanol oxidation is a classical biochemical example of elevated NADH/NAD⁺ interfering with gluconeogenesis, especially when glycogen is low. The lesson is not that NADH is harmful: NADH is required at the reverse GAPDH step, but its compartmental concentration and the directions of coupled reactions matter.

A useful substrate-cycle calculation follows from the opposing reactions. At the F6P/F1,6BP junction, PFK-1 uses ATP, while FBPase-1 hydrolyses F1,6BP to F6P and Pi. If the forward flux is 12 μmol/min and reverse flux 9 μmol/min, net flux is 3 μmol/min toward glycolysis, while 9 μmol/min of ATP is dissipated by cycling at this junction. The measured forward PFK flux is 12 μmol/min, not 3. Reciprocal regulation helps limit that unnecessary expense while permitting sensitive control.

10. Cori and glucose–alanine cycles: cooperation between tissues

A contracting muscle does not always oxidise pyruvate completely in mitochondria. When glycolytic flux is high relative to mitochondrial oxidative capacity, pyruvate reduction to lactate regenerates NAD⁺ for glycolysis. Mature mammalian red blood cells, which lack mitochondria, continuously form lactate through glycolysis even when oxygen is available in their surroundings. Lactate can travel through blood to liver, where it is oxidised to pyruvate and used to synthesise glucose. The newly made glucose returns to the circulation. This inter-tissue carbon recycling is the Cori cycle, associated with the classical work of Carl and Gerty Cori on carbohydrate metabolism.

Lactate is not simply a dead-end waste molecule. Heart and oxidative skeletal muscle can oxidise it, and the liver or kidney can use it for gluconeogenesis. The direction depends on tissue energy status, concentration gradients and enzyme/redox conditions. The cycle is particularly useful as a model of separation between a tissue needing rapid ATP and a tissue able to invest energy in glucose regeneration. The blood provides transport, not a reaction vessel performing the enzymes.

Calculate the Cori cycle by following one glucose. Glycolysis and lactate formation in muscle produce two lactate and two net ATP; the NADH formed during glycolysis is consumed during lactate production. Liver oxidises two lactate to two pyruvate, recovering two cytosolic NADH, and spends four ATP and two GTP to regenerate one glucose. The organism gains two ATP in the peripheral tissue but spends six high-energy phosphate equivalents in the liver, so its net expense is four equivalents. This is not an energy-generating cycle for the whole body. Its value is that useful ATP is produced where it is immediately needed, while the energetic cost is transferred to a tissue that can obtain energy from fatty acid oxidation. If 10 mmol glucose completes this idealised cycle, the peripheral gain is 20 mmol ATP, hepatic expenditure 60 mmol equivalents, and net cost 40 mmol equivalents. Do not use three ATP here unless the question starts from a glycogen-derived phosphorylated residue and explicitly accounts for that different starting state.

Muscle also releases alanine. During amino acid metabolism, amino groups can be transferred to glutamate and then to pyruvate by alanine aminotransferase. The resulting alanine carries a three-carbon skeleton and an amino group in one molecule. In liver, the reverse transamination produces pyruvate and glutamate. Pyruvate contributes to gluconeogenesis; nitrogen from glutamate can be directed toward ammonia handling and urea formation. Glucose returns to muscle. This glucose–alanine cycle therefore has a dual role in carbon recycling and nitrogen transfer.

For two alanines converted to one glucose, the core gluconeogenic pathway still requires four ATP, two GTP and two NADH, but alanine transamination does not itself generate the cytosolic NADH needed by glucose synthesis. Additional redox transfer is required. Nitrogen disposal is an additional metabolic expense: synthesis of one urea uses three ATP molecules but four high-energy phosphate bonds, because one ATP is converted to AMP and PPi. If two alanine-derived nitrogen atoms ultimately enter one urea, the simple combined hepatic ledger is six equivalents for glucose synthesis plus four for urea synthesis, or ten equivalents, excluding other energetic effects of the surrounding network. In vivo, individual amino groups mix with larger nitrogen pools, so this is an idealised accounting assumption, not a fixed cost assigned to every transported alanine.

The difference between these two cycles is most readily remembered through conservation: lactate moves carbon and participates in redox balance, while alanine moves carbon and nitrogen. Both permit muscle and liver to cooperate; neither creates free energy or new carbon from nothing. These cycles also reveal why metabolic pathways should be drawn across tissue boundaries rather than treated as disconnected diagrams.

11. Pentose phosphate pathway: spending carbon to obtain reducing power

A cell needs energy, but energy alone cannot make fatty acids, repair oxidised proteins or supply the ribose of nucleotides. These tasks require a different currency: NADPH, the reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate. NADPH and NADH share nicotinamide redox chemistry, but their extra phosphate distinguishes enzyme binding. Many cells maintain NADPH in a relatively reduced state for reductive synthesis and antioxidant protection, while NAD⁺ is maintained relatively available for oxidative catabolism. These pools are regulated separately and are not interchangeable simply because both carry hydride equivalents.

The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), also called the hexose monophosphate shunt, occurs mainly in the cytosol in mammalian cells. It branches from G6P before the committed glycolytic phosphorylation step. The oxidative phase is effectively irreversible under normal cellular conditions and produces NADPH and a five-carbon sugar. The nonoxidative phase rearranges sugar phosphates reversibly, allowing the products to meet changing requirements for ribose or glycolytic intermediates. There is no direct ATP generation by the PPP itself; ATP may be generated later if its products enter glycolysis.

In the first oxidative reaction, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) oxidises the aldehyde-equivalent carbon at position 1 of G6P to a lactone, 6-phosphoglucono-δ-lactone, while transferring reducing equivalents to NADP⁺. This is a major regulatory step. The lactone is hydrolysed by 6-phosphogluconolactonase to 6-phosphogluconate, opening the ring and yielding a carboxyl group. Finally, 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase oxidises the substrate and catalyses oxidative decarboxylation to give ribulose-5-phosphate, CO₂ and a second NADPH. Carbon 1 of the original glucose phosphate is lost as CO₂; the other five carbons form the pentose skeleton. Note the distinction between ribulose-5-phosphate, a ketopentose, and ribose-5-phosphate, an aldopentose.

The net oxidative reaction is:

G6P + 2 NADP⁺ + H₂O → ribulose-5-P + CO₂ + 2 NADPH + 2 H⁺

Every passage of one G6P through this phase generates two NADPH and one CO₂. Starting with 3 mmol G6P and assuming complete single-pass oxidation, the output is 6 mmol NADPH, 3 mmol CO₂ and 3 mmol ribulose-5-P. Carbon conservation is 18 mmol carbon atoms in and 15 in pentose plus 3 in CO₂ out. Such a calculation does not predict the total NADPH possible if the pentose products are recycled into G6P; that requires the nonoxidative rearrangements.

G6PD responds to the availability of its oxidised electron acceptor NADP⁺ and is inhibited by its product NADPH. When NADPH is consumed, the NADP⁺/NADPH balance shifts toward the oxidised form, increasing the opportunity for G6PD flux. In lipogenic tissues, enzyme abundance can also increase with appropriate nutritional signalling. Thus the oxidative branch is coupled to the cell's demand for reducing power, not to ATP production in the same way as glycolysis. High NADH does not directly substitute for NADPH in the enzymes of this route.

12. Nonoxidative PPP: rearranging carbon without losing it

The pentose product of the oxidative branch can be used to make nucleotides, but the demand for NADPH and ribose is not always in the same ratio. A cell making fatty acids may require considerable NADPH but little new ribose, whereas a proliferating cell may need ribose faster than its need for NADPH. The nonoxidative reactions solve this problem by moving carbon fragments among sugar phosphates without further oxidation or decarboxylation.

Ribulose-5-phosphate is converted into ribose-5-phosphate by phosphopentose isomerase, or into xylulose-5-phosphate by phosphopentose epimerase. Isomerisation changes an aldose–ketose arrangement; epimerisation changes configuration at a single stereocentre (carbon 3 in the ribulose/xylulose pair). The resulting aldose and ketose provide the required substrates for transketolase and transaldolase.

Transketolase transfers a two-carbon unit from a ketose donor to an aldose acceptor. It requires thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) and a divalent cation such as Mg²⁺. TPP stabilises a carbanion-like intermediate, making cleavage and transfer of the two-carbon fragment possible. The first transketolase reaction transfers two carbons from xylulose-5-phosphate to ribose-5-phosphate, forming glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (three carbons) and sedoheptulose-7-phosphate (seven carbons). The carbon balance is 5 + 5 = 3 + 7.

Transaldolase transfers a three-carbon unit from sedoheptulose-7-phosphate to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. The products are fructose-6-phosphate and erythrose-4-phosphate (7 + 3 = 6 + 4). Mammalian transaldolase forms a Schiff base with an active-site lysine to stabilise the transferred fragment; it does not require TPP. The second transketolase reaction transfers two carbons from another xylulose-5-phosphate to erythrose-4-phosphate to produce fructose-6-phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (5 + 4 = 6 + 3). If one adds these three equations, sedoheptulose-7-phosphate, erythrose-4-phosphate and one GAP cancel as internal intermediates. The net reaction is:

2 xylulose-5-P + ribose-5-P ⇌ 2 fructose-6-P + glyceraldehyde-3-P

Both sides contain 15 carbons and three phosphate groups; no NADPH or CO₂ is produced in these rearrangements. Starting with three ribulose-5-P molecules, the isomerase and epimerase can supply one ribose and two xylulose molecules, so the net outcome is two six-carbon sugars and one three-carbon sugar. The common exam trap is to report two GAP products: GAP made in the first transfer is consumed in the transaldolase step. Only one GAP remains net. Another error is to assign three-carbon transfer to transketolase merely because one product happens to contain three carbons. Name the transferred fragment, not the size of the product.

These reactions are reversible under suitable intracellular conditions. If ribose is required more urgently than NADPH, glycolytic F6P and GAP can be rearranged in the reverse direction to generate pentoses without running the oxidative phase. If NADPH is required more urgently than ribose, the oxidative phase can continue and the pentose carbon can return to glycolysis or be recycled toward G6P. This flexibility is the essential biological purpose of the nonoxidative branch. It does not need to have its own irreversible regulatory step analogous to G6PD, because mass action and withdrawal of products help determine its direction.

13. Matching NADPH and ribose demand: pathway modes, evidence and applications

The first useful case is when a cell needs both NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate. Running the oxidative phase and isomerising ribulose-5-phosphate to ribose supplies two NADPH for each ribose. Ribose-5-phosphate can be activated to phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP), which will be studied in nucleotide metabolism. If a cell needs more ribose than NADPH, reverse nonoxidative reactions use F6P and GAP to make pentose phosphates. For example, two F6P and one GAP contain fifteen carbons and can supply three pentose phosphates; no NADPH needs to be made in that reaction set.

The second case is abundant NADPH demand with relatively little ribose demand. Six G6P molecules passing once through the oxidative branch yield six pentoses, six CO₂ and twelve NADPH. Twice the net nonoxidative reaction rearranges the six pentoses into four F6P and two GAP. Two GAP can be converted into one F6P through triose isomerisation, aldolase and FBPase-1, provided the necessary enzymes are available. The five F6P can be isomerised to five G6P. Thus six G6P entered but five were regenerated, leaving net consumption of one six-carbon G6P and production of six CO₂ and twelve NADPH. This is an ideal complete recycling calculation, not the universal output of a single pass or a claim that all tissues can recycle pentoses this way. ATP and Pi bookkeeping depends on the specified starting substrate and regeneration reactions; no ATP is directly produced in the oxidative reactions. If free glucose rather than G6P is the input, initial phosphorylation also consumes ATP.

In a third useful mode, NADPH and ATP are needed, while ribose demand is low. Pentose carbon becomes F6P and GAP, which enter glycolysis rather than being fully recycled. Glycolysis then provides ATP and pyruvate, whereas the oxidative PPP supplies NADPH. If an experimental question reports increased CO₂ from the oxidative branch, this does not automatically mean mitochondrial oxygen consumption has increased. PPP decarboxylation reduces NADP⁺ and does not directly consume molecular oxygen. The respiratory chain and oxidative branch have different immediate electron acceptors.

Classical carbon-label experiments help distinguish PPP from glycolysis. In a short experiment with otherwise identical conditions, cells are separately supplied glucose labelled at carbon 1 or carbon 6 and early labelled CO₂ release is measured. Oxidative PPP releases glucose carbon 1 directly as CO₂, while carbon 6 remains in the pentose during the first pass. Preferential early labelled CO₂ from carbon 1 supports oxidative PPP flux. In glycolysis, carbons 1 and 6 are mapped symmetrically into the methyl carbons of the two pyruvate molecules; their immediate removal by pyruvate dehydrogenase is not expected. During longer experiments, carbon rearrangement, recycling and mitochondrial oxidation complicate this simple inference. A larger early carbon-1 signal is evidence of differential carbon fate, not by itself an exact estimate of pathway flux without further assumptions.

Consider a deliberately simplified short incubation. Labelled carbon-1 glucose generates 18 units of labelled CO₂ and carbon-6 glucose generates 6 units under otherwise matched conditions. If downstream oxidation contributes equally to both labels and isotope recovery is comparable, the excess carbon-1 signal is 12 units. It supports a carbon-1-selective decarboxylation route, consistent with PPP. It does not establish that precisely two-thirds of all glucose is entering PPP; the total amount of glucose metabolised, recycling and label recovery were not supplied. This distinction between inference and unsupported numerical claims is typical of CSIR-NET Part C.

NADPH provides reducing equivalents to fatty acid synthesis and other reductive biosynthetic pathways. It is also essential for maintaining the reduced glutathione pool. Glutathione peroxidase reduces hydrogen peroxide using two molecules of reduced glutathione (GSH), forming oxidised glutathione (GSSG). Glutathione reductase uses NADPH to convert GSSG back to two GSH. Consequently a single NADPH can support removal of one peroxide molecule through this coupled system, provided substrates and enzymes are available. Mature red blood cells have no mitochondria and depend strongly on the PPP for NADPH. In G6PD deficiency, oxidative challenge can overwhelm this protection, oxidising haemoglobin and membrane components and damaging red cells. This mechanism demonstrates why glycolytic ATP alone cannot protect a cell against oxidant stress.

2 GSH + H₂O₂ → GSSG + 2 H₂O; GSSG + NADPH + H⁺ → 2 GSH + NADP⁺

14. Fructose metabolism: another hexose enters the network

Glucose is not the only dietary six-carbon sugar. Fructose is a ketohexose found free in food and as part of sucrose. Its carbon can enter the same central pathways as glucose, but its first phosphorylation and cleavage steps differ across tissues. In some extrahepatic tissues, hexokinase can phosphorylate fructose to fructose-6-phosphate. Glucose is generally a preferred substrate, so this route is limited in many physiological settings. Fructose-6-phosphate then joins the usual glycolytic pathway and remains subject to PFK-1 regulation.

In liver, and also in kidney and small intestine, a major route uses ketohexokinase (fructokinase) to phosphorylate fructose at carbon 1 using ATP. The product is fructose-1-phosphate, not fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. Aldolase B cleaves fructose-1-phosphate into DHAP and glyceraldehyde. DHAP already carries a phosphate and can be converted to GAP by triose phosphate isomerase; glyceraldehyde needs phosphorylation by triokinase using a second ATP, becoming GAP. The two triose phosphates thus enter central metabolism below PFK-1. They may continue to pyruvate, participate in gluconeogenesis or supply glycerol-3-phosphate and acetyl-CoA for lipid synthesis according to energy state.

The bypass of PFK-1 does not mean fructose metabolism is completely unregulated. Uptake, fructokinase capacity, triose metabolism, cellular ATP availability, subsequent glycolytic reactions and whole-body demand still matter. It means that the major feedback checkpoint at PFK-1 is not encountered by fructose carbon entering through hepatic fructose-1-phosphate. It also does not mean fructose necessarily produces more net glycolytic ATP: two ATP are spent on fructokinase and triokinase; two GAP molecules subsequently yield four ATP and two NADH. If both are converted to lactate, NADH is reoxidised, giving two net ATP per fructose in this simplified pathway. Exactly the same ATP count as free glucose to lactate can arise from a different arrangement of investment steps.

Two enzyme deficiencies illustrate the logic of pathway blockages. In essential fructosuria, ketohexokinase deficiency limits the first phosphorylation step. Some fructose remains unphosphorylated and can be excreted; the condition is often benign. In hereditary fructose intolerance, aldolase B deficiency blocks breakdown of fructose-1-phosphate after ATP has already been used to form it. The accumulating phosphorylated sugar traps phosphate, can lower ATP availability, and impairs hepatic glucose production from glycogen and gluconeogenic precursors. Hypoglycaemia after fructose exposure is therefore mechanistically different from the first-step block. These are biochemical examples, not clinical diagnostic instructions.

A simple paired enzyme experiment shows how one might distinguish them. In a fructose-incubated liver-cell model, sample A has little fructose-1-P and normal ATP, whereas sample B has high fructose-1-P and reduced ATP. If fructose transport is normal in both, an early phosphorylation defect is consistent with sample A, and an aldolase B defect is consistent with sample B. The product concentration alone does not establish a genotype; enzyme activity or genetic evidence is needed for definitive identification. This is a general principle of metabolic reasoning: a block after phosphorylation may accumulate a charged intermediate and alter the availability of energy cofactors.

15. Galactose metabolism: nucleotide sugars and the Leloir pathway

Galactose is an aldohexose that differs from glucose in the configuration of the hydroxyl group at carbon 4. Lactose, the major milk disaccharide, contains one glucose and one galactose residue. The spatial difference at carbon 4 is enough to prevent galactose from simply being treated as glucose by every glycolytic enzyme. A set of reactions called the Leloir pathway converts its carbon into a form accepted by glucose metabolism and simultaneously interconverts nucleotide sugars needed for biosynthesis.

Galactose mutarotase can facilitate interconversion of the α and β anomers, enabling the appropriate form to enter the reaction sequence. Galactokinase then consumes ATP to produce galactose-1-phosphate. This first phosphorylation, like glucose phosphorylation, traps the sugar in the cell. The problem is now how to change the configuration at carbon 4. Direct epimerisation of free galactose-1-phosphate is not the route used by the classical mammalian Leloir pathway. Galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (GALT) transfers a uridylyl group between sugar phosphates: galactose-1-phosphate reacts with UDP-glucose to yield UDP-galactose and glucose-1-phosphate. The reaction operates through a transient enzyme–UMP intermediate and is a familiar example of a ping-pong mechanism from enzyme kinetics.

UDP-galactose 4-epimerase (GALE) then reversibly converts UDP-galactose to UDP-glucose. Its tightly bound NAD⁺ temporarily accepts a hydride as carbon 4 is oxidised to a keto intermediate; hydride is returned to the opposite face to establish the alternate configuration. NAD⁺ is regenerated within the reaction cycle. There is no net production of freely released NADH in this epimerisation. UDP-glucose is therefore regenerated and can participate in further GALT reactions. The net chemistry of the classical pathway is one galactose plus ATP converted to glucose-1-phosphate plus ADP, ignoring proton conventions. Phosphoglucomutase subsequently supplies G6P.

The carbon bookkeeping has a subtle feature. In a single GALT turnover, the G1P released originally belonged to the UDP-glucose acceptor, while the incoming galactose carbon is now in UDP-galactose. Epimerisation regenerates UDP-glucose containing that carbon, and later turnovers can release it as G1P. At steady state the overall carbon flux converts galactose to G1P, but in an isotope pulse one should not assume instantaneous appearance of all labelled galactose carbon in released G1P after the first molecular turnover. The nucleotide-sugar pool is both a carrier and a biosynthetic substrate.

If one galactose enters glycolysis via the complete Leloir pathway, the galactokinase step uses one ATP and PFK-1 uses one ATP; lower glycolysis generates four ATP. The net yield to lactate is two ATP, ignoring any expansion or turnover of the UDP-sugar pool. Galactose-derived UDP-galactose also contributes directly to glycosylation of proteins and lipids and to lactose synthesis in mammary glands. Thus the pathway supplies building materials as well as energy.

Defects illustrate the importance of the individual reactions. GALT deficiency causes classical galactosaemia, with accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate and disturbed galactose metabolism. Galactose can also be reduced to galactitol by aldose reductase, using NADPH; galactitol accumulation in the lens contributes to cataract formation. Galactokinase deficiency can also cause galactitol accumulation without the same major G1P trapping pattern as GALT deficiency. GALE deficiencies vary in tissue distribution and severity. These distinctions concern the chemical sites of pathway blocks and the fate of accumulated substrates; a clinical presentation cannot identify the enzyme with certainty without appropriate tests. Galactose metabolism is another example of how a phosphorylated intermediate, a nucleotide carrier and an epimerisation reaction are linked by metabolic design.

16. Putting the pathways together: fed, fasting, exercise and tissue context

The biochemical network now has several ways to distribute glucose carbon. A fed hepatocyte receives glucose after digestion, and elevated insulin relative to glucagon favours glycogen formation and glycolysis. Glucose oxidation supplies ATP and acetyl-CoA; if energy and carbon are abundant, cytosolic acetyl-CoA can support fatty acid synthesis. The oxidative PPP supplies NADPH for such synthesis, and nucleotide-sugar intermediates supply glycoconjugates. Glycogen storage has a finite capacity, so additional carbohydrate does not simply continue accumulating indefinitely as glycogen.

During the early postabsorptive period, falling insulin and rising glucagon favour hepatic glycogenolysis, while gluconeogenesis contributes at the same time. The two processes overlap, rather than one starting only after the other ceases. As fasting continues and liver glycogen declines, gluconeogenesis accounts for a larger share of endogenous glucose production. Fatty acid oxidation increasingly supplies energy for the liver, while glycerol, lactate and amino acid carbon are available for glucose synthesis. Renal gluconeogenesis becomes more important during prolonged fasting. The brain can use ketone bodies increasingly during prolonged fasting, but it still needs some glucose; red blood cells remain dependent on glycolysis because they lack mitochondria.

Skeletal muscle is regulated by its workload and hormonal context. In fed resting muscle, insulin stimulates glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis. During active contraction, Ca²⁺ and AMP favour glycogen mobilisation and energy production, even without a direct glucagon signal. Muscle can release lactate and alanine, indirectly supporting hepatic glucose production. It cannot directly export much free glucose from its glycogen because it lacks the necessary significant G6Pase activity. The liver can export glucose even though it is itself consuming ATP from other fuels. These opposing local actions are coherent at the organism level.

Adipose tissue responds to insulin by increasing glucose uptake and supporting storage of triacylglycerol; glucose-derived DHAP can supply glycerol-3-phosphate. During fasting, mobilised triacylglycerol releases fatty acids and glycerol. Fatty acids support oxidation in several tissues; glycerol travels to glucose-producing organs. This makes a useful bridge to the next subunit: lipid oxidation provides the energy for glucose synthesis, and PPP-derived NADPH is one source of reducing power for lipid synthesis.

A mass-balance worked example distinguishes pathway fractions from absolute fluxes. Suppose hepatic glucose output in an illustrative preparation is 10 mmol/h, consisting of 6 mmol/h glycogenolysis and 4 mmol/h gluconeogenesis. Later output is 8 mmol/h, consisting of 2 and 6 mmol/h, respectively. Gluconeogenesis changes from 40% to 75% of output, but its absolute increase is only 2 mmol/h. Total output fell by 2 mmol/h. The percentage contribution of a pathway can rise even while total glucose output falls; it is the flux, not simply the fraction, that answers a question about the actual amount made. If peripheral consumption remains 8 mmol/h and there is no intestinal input, the pool is steady in the later state. It would increase at 2 mmol/h in the earlier state if all other assumptions held.

The exam-level goal is to predict a pattern rather than memorise fixed fasting hours or concentrations. Hormonal responses, exercise intensity, diet and prior glycogen stores vary; tissues are not identical. When a question provides specific conditions, identify the organ, state of energy and redox carriers, enzyme forms present, substrate availability, and net direction. These five pieces are sufficient for most classical pathway integration questions. Detailed endocrine feedback and whole-body physiological control are studied in Unit 7.

17. Pathway perturbations and scientific interpretation

A good way to test whether a pathway has been understood is to interrupt a reaction and predict which molecules accumulate and which products decrease. This requires knowledge of alternative routes; a simple upstream/downstream rule can fail when the substrate has another fate. Glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency, for instance, prevents efficient conversion of G6P to free glucose. Both hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis are affected at the shared terminal step, while G6P may instead be stored or diverted to glycolysis and the PPP. Accumulating glycogen and increased lactate can accompany the loss of glucose output. The classical glucose-6-phosphatase system defect is associated with von Gierke disease (glycogen storage disease type I); defects in different components of the system must not be conflated with a single gene.

Muscle phosphorylase deficiency (McArdle disease) prevents normal use of muscle glycogen, particularly when rapid ATP supply is needed. The muscle's ability to take up and use blood glucose is not abolished. A debranching enzyme deficiency (type III) leaves abnormal short outer chains or limit-dextrin-like glycogen because phosphorylase alone cannot process branch points. A branching enzyme deficiency (type IV) gives glycogen with relatively few branches and long chains, producing poorly soluble polyglucosan-like deposits. The structural defect can be predicted from the normal action of the missing enzyme. In contrast, lysosomal acid α-glucosidase deficiency causes glycogen accumulation in a different compartment. These examples are useful because they distinguish defects in mobilisation, polymer construction, debranching and export.

Experimental measurement can separate pathways that otherwise share intermediates. For example, two hepatic cell preparations are supplied lactate with ATP-producing substrates. Preparation A produces normal glucose but releases little glucose from glycogen. Preparation B produces little glucose from either substrate, although intracellular G6P rises. A defect in glycogen mobilisation is compatible with A; the shared terminal G6Pase reaction is compatible with B. This evidence narrows the possible step; it does not alone prove which protein is mutated. A transporter defect in the ER G6Pase system can affect the same functional endpoint.

Use four safeguards when interpreting metabolic data. First, enzyme activity is not identical to whole-pathway flux: a high measured catalytic capacity may be unused in the cell. Second, a large metabolite pool may indicate high input, low output, or both; concentration alone cannot specify direction. Third, cofactors are compartmentalised, so NADH or ATP produced in one compartment may not be immediately available in another. Fourth, distinguish the consequences of a direct biochemical block from later hormonal adaptation. A simplified exam experiment often holds other variables constant, whereas a whole organism may compensate through altered uptake, synthesis or substrate choice.

These safeguards also explain common misconceptions. The PPP is not another ATP-producing version of glycolysis; its oxidative products are NADPH and pentose, with glycolytic ATP possible only downstream. Glycogen phosphorylase does not hydrolyse every bond, and phosphorolysis is not the same as phosphorylation by an ATP-dependent kinase. Gluconeogenesis is not solely a late starvation pathway, and acetyl-CoA cannot provide net glucose carbon in normal mammalian metabolism even though fatty acid oxidation supports the process. Finally, fructose-2,6-bisphosphate is a regulator, not a replacement name for fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. A student who can explain the chemistry behind these distinctions has a stronger foundation than one who merely recognises the names.

18. Part B: conceptual and assertion–reason practice

These original questions test enzyme specificity, tissue differences, regulation and pathway chemistry. Select an answer before opening the explanation.

Question 1

Which immediate glucosyl donor is used by glycogen synthase?

A. Free glucose; B. Glucose-1-P; C. UDP-glucose; D. Glucose-6-P

Answer and explanation

C. UDP-glucose provides an activated glucosyl group and UDP as leaving group. A lacks activation; B is the precursor used to form UDP-glucose; D must first be rearranged.

Question 2

Which bond is hydrolysed by the glucosidase activity of mammalian glycogen debranching enzyme?

A. α(1→4); B. α(1→6); C. β(1→4); D. Peptide bond

Answer and explanation

B. The branch-point residue is attached through α(1→6). A is processed by phosphorylase or transferase in this system; C is characteristic of other polysaccharides; D is irrelevant to glycogen.

Question 3

Which statement explains why ordinary skeletal muscle cannot sustain blood glucose from glycogen directly?

A. It has no glycogen; B. It lacks significant glucose-6-phosphatase activity; C. It lacks phosphorylase; D. It lacks glycolysis

Answer and explanation

B. Muscle can produce G6P and use it locally, but cannot efficiently dephosphorylate it for export. A, C and D are all inconsistent with the major role of muscle glycogen.

Question 4

Which pair bypasses the pyruvate kinase step in gluconeogenesis?

A. Pyruvate dehydrogenase and citrate synthase; B. Pyruvate carboxylase and PEPCK; C. Enolase and aldolase; D. PFK-2 and FBPase-2

Answer and explanation

B. ATP-dependent carboxylation and GTP-dependent decarboxylation/phosphorylation produce PEP. A sends pyruvate into acetyl-CoA metabolism; C names reversible steps; D regulates F2,6BP.

Question 5

Which precursor supplies cytosolic NADH directly when converted to pyruvate?

A. Alanine; B. Lactate; C. Acetyl-CoA; D. Oxaloacetate

Answer and explanation

B. Lactate oxidation reduces NAD⁺. Alanine transamination is not a redox reaction; acetyl-CoA does not convert directly to pyruvate; oxaloacetate is a downstream four-carbon intermediate.

Question 6

In the classical hepatic bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2 isoform, glucagon causes which change?

A. Higher F2,6BP; B. Lower F2,6BP; C. Direct conversion of F2,6BP to glucose; D. Activation of muscle GLUT4

Answer and explanation

B. PKA phosphorylation favours FBPase-2 and lowers F2,6BP. A is the opposite; C ignores the specific hydrolysis to F6P; D is an insulin-associated response, not the hepatic switch.

Question 7

Which enzyme requires TPP in the nonoxidative PPP?

A. Transaldolase; B. Transketolase; C. G6PD; D. Ribose phosphate isomerase

Answer and explanation

B. TPP stabilises the two-carbon transfer intermediate. Transaldolase uses a Schiff base; G6PD uses NADP⁺; isomerase rearranges the pentose carbonyl.

Question 8

Which is the immediate five-carbon product of oxidative PPP?

A. Ribose-5-P; B. Ribulose-5-P; C. PRPP; D. Xylulose-5-P

Answer and explanation

B. 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase produces ribulose-5-P. A and D arise by isomerisation/epimerisation, and C is a downstream activated ribose compound.

Question 9

Assertion: Even-chain fatty acids cannot yield net glucose in mammals. Reason: Acetyl-CoA cannot be converted back to pyruvate by reversal of PDH, and TCA cycling does not yield net oxaloacetate from acetyl-CoA.

A. Both true, reason explains; B. Both true, no explanation; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Together the irreversible PDH step and lack of net oxaloacetate production explain the carbon restriction. This does not exclude glucose synthesis from glycerol or propionyl-CoA.

Question 10

Assertion: GALT deficiency and galactokinase deficiency are biochemically identical. Reason: Both may lead to increased galactitol.

A. Both true, reason explains; B. Both true, no explanation; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

D. Both can lead to galactitol production, but only the downstream GALT block characteristically traps galactose-1-P. Shared metabolite effects do not imply identical enzyme defects.

Question 11

Assertion: A defect in the hepatic glucose-6-phosphatase system can reduce glucose release from both glycogen and lactate. Reason: Both pathways converge on G6P, which must be hydrolysed to permit efficient hepatic release of free glucose.

A. Both true, reason explains; B. Both true, unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Both carbon routes reach G6P and share the terminal hydrolysis step. A defect there compromises free glucose release from either source even though the upstream reactions differ. The explanation directly accounts for the assertion.

19. Part C: quantitative and analytical practice

These original problems use idealised conditions and explicit assumptions. Write separate carbon, phosphate-energy and reducing-equivalent ledgers before checking the solutions.

Question 1

A muscle cell degrades 120 glycogen residues, 108 as G1P and 12 as glucose. All carbon becomes lactate. Calculate net ATP and compare with 120 free glucose molecules.

A. 240 and 240; B. 348 and 240; C. 360 and 240; D. 348 and 360

Answer and explanation

B. G1P enters after hexokinase and yields three ATP, free glucose two. 108 × 3 + 12 × 2 = 348; 120 × 2 = 240. C ignores branch hydrolysis; A ignores the saving; D reverses the comparison.

Question 2

A hepatocyte adds 75 glucosyl units to an existing glycogen particle from G6P. Assuming UTP regeneration through ATP, how many ATP equivalents are used? What if the starting material is free glucose?

A. 75, 150; B. 150, 225; C. 0, 75; D. 150, 150

Answer and explanation

A. Each activated nucleotide sugar costs one UTP equivalent. Starting at G6P removes the hexokinase investment; starting with free glucose adds 75 ATP. B double-counts PPi hydrolysis, C ignores UDP-glucose formation, D ignores starting substrate.

Question 3

A liver preparation uses 8 mmol pyruvate for gluconeogenesis. Find the maximum glucose and the ATP, GTP and NADH consumption in the core route.

A. 4 glucose, 16 ATP, 8 GTP, 8 NADH; B. 8 glucose, 16 ATP, 8 GTP, 8 NADH; C. 4 glucose, 8 ATP, 8 GTP, 0 NADH; D. 4 glucose, 24 ATP, 8 GTP, 8 NADH

Answer and explanation

A. Two pyruvates supply one glucose. Multiply 4 ATP, 2 GTP, 2 NADH per glucose by four. B violates carbon balance; C omits reverse PGK and reduction costs; D double-counts nucleotide energy.

Question 4

A tissue uses 3 mmol glucose in an ideal Cori cycle. What are the peripheral ATP gain, liver nucleotide-triphosphate expenditure and net whole-body expenditure?

A. 6, 18, 12 mmol; B. 9, 18, 9 mmol; C. 6, 6, 0 mmol; D. 18, 6, −12 mmol

Answer and explanation

A. Free glucose glycolysis to lactate gives two ATP each; resynthesis uses six equivalents. 3 × 2 = 6; 3 × 6 = 18; net cost 12. B assumes glycogen-derived G1P, C treats gluconeogenesis as a reversal, D swaps gains and costs.

Question 5

A cell receives 9 mmol G6P for one oxidative PPP pass. The resulting pentoses are then rearranged fully by nonoxidative PPP. What is the product balance?

A. 18 NADPH, 9 CO₂, 6 F6P, 3 GAP; B. 9 NADPH, 9 CO₂, 6 F6P, 3 GAP; C. 18 NADPH, 0 CO₂, 9 F6P; D. 18 NADPH, 9 CO₂, 6 F6P, 6 GAP

Answer and explanation

A. Each G6P produces 2 NADPH and 1 CO₂. Three groups of three pentoses give 6 F6P and 3 GAP. Carbon: 54 input = 9 + 36 + 9. D overcounts GAP, B misses one oxidative reduction, C ignores decarboxylation.

Question 6

In ideal complete oxidative PPP recycling, 2 mmol G6P is consumed net. How much NADPH and CO₂ are produced?

A. 4 and 2 mmol; B. 24 and 12 mmol; C. 12 and 24 mmol; D. 24 and 2 mmol

Answer and explanation

B. Six G6P passes regenerate five G6P while yielding 12 NADPH and 6 CO₂. Per net G6P, all six carbon atoms are released. Two net G6P give 24 NADPH and 12 CO₂. A describes a single pass; C swaps amounts; D violates carbon accounting.

Question 7

A fasting liver mutation maintains abnormally high F2,6BP despite high glucagon. Which paired change is most direct?

A. PFK-1 inhibited and FBPase-1 activated; B. PFK-1 activated and FBPase-1 inhibited; C. Both activated; D. Both inhibited

Answer and explanation

B. F2,6BP is an activator of PFK-1 and inhibitor of FBPase-1. A is normal fasting direction after F2,6BP falls. C and D disregard the reciprocal effects.

Question 8

At the fructose phosphate substrate cycle, forward flux is 21 μmol/min and reverse flux 13 μmol/min. Calculate net glycolytic flux and ATP expenditure specifically attributable to cycling.

A. 8 and 13 μmol/min; B. 34 and 21; C. 13 and 8; D. 8 and 21

Answer and explanation

A. Net flux is 21 − 13 = 8. Thirteen forward turnovers are cancelled by thirteen hydrolytic reverse turnovers, consuming thirteen ATP without net carbon flow. Total PFK-1 ATP use is 21, but only 13 is attributable to the cycle; D confuses these.

Question 9

An early tracer experiment gives 28 units of CO₂ from [1-labelled] glucose and 8 from [6-labelled] glucose. Assuming equal non-PPP contributions, what is justified?

A. Exactly 71.4% of glucose flux is PPP; B. The differential signal is 20 units, supporting carbon-1-selective decarboxylation; C. The PPP cannot be active; D. Both labels must be removed by glycolysis immediately

Answer and explanation

B. The matched difference is 28 − 8 = 20. Absolute glucose flux is not supplied, so A is unjustified. Glycolysis does not release CO₂ directly; C and D contradict the carbon mapping.

Question 10

A liver cell supplied fructose accumulates fructose-1-P and shows falling ATP. Which block best fits?

A. Ketohexokinase; B. Aldolase B; C. PFK-1; D. Galactokinase

Answer and explanation

B. ATP-dependent fructose phosphorylation persists but F1P cannot be efficiently cleaved. A would reduce F1P formation; C is bypassed by hepatic fructose entry; D belongs to galactose metabolism.

Question 11

A labelled galactose molecule enters the Leloir pathway in the presence of initially unlabelled UDP-glucose. What immediate GALT product contains the incoming labelled carbon?

A. UDP-galactose; B. Free glucose; C. G1P exclusively; D. UMP

Answer and explanation

A. The entering galactose-1-P receives UMP, while the pre-existing UDP-glucose sugar leaves as G1P. Later GALE and GALT turnovers can release labelled G1P. B and D are not the carbon-containing immediate product; C overlooks pool turnover.

Question 12

A liver model has normal formation of G6P from lactate and glycogen but releases little free glucose from either source. Which enzyme system should be investigated first?

A. PEPCK; B. Glycogen phosphorylase; C. Glucose-6-phosphatase system; D. Transketolase

Answer and explanation

C. Both routes reach G6P; the shared final hydrolysis/export system is implicated. A selectively affects gluconeogenesis; B selectively affects glycogen; D rearranges pentoses.

Question 13

In a glucose-producing organ, glycerol provides two molecules for each glucose. Assuming the cytosolic glycerol-3-P dehydrogenase reaction, what is the direct ATP cost and NADH balance per glucose?

A. 2 ATP consumed, 2 NADH formed; B. 6 ATP equivalents, 2 NADH consumed; C. 2 ATP formed, no NADH; D. No ATP, 2 NADH consumed

Answer and explanation

A. Each glycerol kinase costs ATP; each glycerol-3-P oxidation reduces NAD⁺. The trioses enter above reverse GAPDH and PGK, so B describes a pyruvate-based ledger; C and D reverse or omit reactions.

Question 14

During a simplified fast, hepatic glucose output falls from 12 to 9 mmol/h. Gluconeogenic contributions rise from 25% to ⅔. How do absolute gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis change?

A. 3→6 and 9→3; B. 3→9 and 9→0; C. 9→3 and 3→6; D. Both increase

Answer and explanation

A. Initial gluconeogenesis 12 × 0.25 = 3, remaining 9. Final gluconeogenesis 9 × ⅔ = 6, remaining 3. A higher fraction need not imply higher total output. B misuses the fraction; C swaps pathways; D contradicts the totals.

Question 15

A cell has an oxidative challenge consuming 7 mmol H₂O₂ through glutathione peroxidase. Assuming complete regeneration of GSH solely via glutathione reductase and one-pass PPP, what minimum G6P is needed to supply NADPH?

A. 3.5 mmol; B. 7 mmol; C. 14 mmol; D. 0.5 mmol

Answer and explanation

A. Each peroxide consumes two GSH, producing one GSSG; each GSSG regeneration uses one NADPH. Seven NADPH require 7/2 = 3.5 mmol G6P. B neglects the second NADPH per oxidative pass; C counts GSH instead of NADPH; D ignores the total peroxide.

Question 16

Two alanine carbon skeletons produce one glucose and their two nitrogen atoms ultimately form one urea. Under a simplified ledger, what is the combined hepatic high-energy phosphate expenditure, excluding other network costs?

A. 6 equivalents; B. 9 equivalents; C. 10 equivalents; D. 12 equivalents

Answer and explanation

C. Four ATP plus two GTP for glucose = six; urea synthesis uses three ATP molecules but four phosphate-bond equivalents because one ATP becomes AMP. Six plus four = ten. B counts molecules not bonds; A omits nitrogen disposal; D overcounts.

Question 17

In a debranching-deficient preparation, phosphorylase activity is normal on a linear glucan but glycogen breakdown stalls at branched residues. Which observation is expected?

A. Normal complete hydrolysis to free glucose; B. Accumulation of short outer chains near branches and less accessible α(1→4) substrate; C. Absence of all α(1→6) bonds; D. Increased production of UDP-glucose by phosphorylase

Answer and explanation

B. Phosphorylase stalls near branches, so residual limit-dextrin-like material remains. A ignores specificity; C suggests defective branch formation instead; D confuses glycogenolysis with synthesis.

20. Consolidation and the next chapter

The glucose network has three broad tasks: ensure that glucose is available where it is needed, conserve carbohydrate when it is abundant, and allocate some of its carbon to molecules needed for synthesis and protection. Glycogen structure enables simultaneous processing at many ends, UDP-glucose provides activated glucosyl units, and phosphorolysis conserves a sugar phosphate. Gluconeogenesis spends nucleotide triphosphates and reducing equivalents to bypass the irreversible steps of glycolysis. Cori and alanine cycling redistribute carbon and, in the second case, nitrogen between tissues. Oxidative PPP exchanges one carbon for two NADPH per pass; nonoxidative PPP rearranges carbon to match biosynthetic demand. Fructose and galactose enter central metabolism through distinct chemical intermediates and can reveal different consequences of enzyme deficiency.

To revise, draw G6P at the centre of a page. From it, trace the route to glycogen, the route to free glucose in liver, the route into glycolysis, and the oxidative pentose route. Then start with pyruvate, glycerol and lactate and draw the routes back toward G6P; annotate which reactions consume ATP, GTP or NADH. Add fructose and galactose at their actual entry points. Finally, mark the two opposing enzyme switches at glycogen synthesis/degradation and the F6P/F1,6BP junction. The result should be a connected pathway map, not a list of unrelated enzyme names.

This network explains why fat metabolism is the next subject. During fasting, oxidation of fatty acids powers gluconeogenesis without supplying net glucose from even-chain acetyl-CoA; during feeding, carbohydrate-derived acetyl-CoA and NADPH support fatty acid synthesis. The next chapter, 1.F.01 — Fatty Acid and Ketone Body Metabolism, develops these complementary processes and their energetic balance.
