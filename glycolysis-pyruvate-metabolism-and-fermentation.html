1. Why cells break glucose in stages

A muscle cell may suddenly need to contract, a yeast cell may grow in a sugar-rich liquid, and a red blood cell must maintain its membrane even though it has no mitochondria. These apparently different situations share one biochemical problem: a cell must obtain usable chemical energy without releasing the whole energy content of a nutrient as heat. Glucose contains six carbon atoms and many carbon–hydrogen bonds. Complete oxidation of glucose would release a large amount of free energy, but a cell cannot directly use a combustion reaction to drive a protein pump or assemble a macromolecule. It needs controlled chemical steps that capture some free energy as ATP and transfer electrons to suitable carriers.

Glycolysis, also called the Embden–Meyerhof–Parnas pathway, is the classical route by which a six-carbon glucose molecule becomes two three-carbon pyruvate molecules. It occurs in the cytosol of animal cells and in the corresponding soluble compartment of many other organisms. Neither molecular oxygen nor a mitochondrial membrane participates directly in its ten reactions. This is why glycolysis can operate in cells lacking mitochondria, but only if the oxidised electron acceptor NAD⁺ remains available. The pathway is not the same as complete cellular respiration. It is the first stage of glucose breakdown and leaves a considerable amount of chemical energy in pyruvate.

Three ideas from bioenergetics will guide the chapter. A kinase transfers a phosphoryl group; in this pathway some kinases consume ATP and others generate it. NAD⁺ accepts two electrons and one proton as a hydride to form NADH; it is an electron carrier rather than a phosphoryl donor. Substrate-level phosphorylation means ATP formation by direct transfer of a phosphoryl group from a metabolic intermediate to ADP, without a proton gradient. By tracking these three processes separately, we can reconstruct the energy balance rather than memorise it.

In the late nineteenth century, Eduard Buchner showed that a cell-free yeast extract could ferment sugar. This observation undermined the idea that an intact living cell was necessary for alcoholic fermentation. Subsequent biochemical studies separated individual reactions, identified intermediates and established the glycolytic sequence. The modern pathway is consequently an experimentally supported network of chemical transformations, not just a diagram of arrows. Its logic becomes clearer when we ask at each step which carbon atom, phosphoryl group or electron has changed its chemical environment. The history is useful because it explains why adding a missing soluble cofactor to an extract can restore metabolism even when no intact cell is present.

2. The investment and payoff strategy

A six-carbon sugar cannot immediately yield two identical oxidisable aldehyde molecules. Glucose first needs to be rearranged and given two phosphoryl groups so that cleavage creates two three-carbon phosphates. The first five reactions accomplish this preparation. Two ATP are invested per glucose; carbon atoms are not discarded. The products are initially different, but an isomerase makes them equivalent for further processing. From this point onward every reaction occurs twice for each glucose molecule passing completely through the classical pathway.

The final five reactions constitute the payoff stage. Each three-carbon molecule is oxidised once, allowing NAD⁺ reduction, and yields ATP twice through two chemically distinct phosphoryl donors. Thus four ATP are formed in the payoff phase, but two have already been consumed. A useful way to read a metabolic chart is to write its multiplicity beside each step: steps one to four occur once, step five converts the one DHAP produced by cleavage into GAP, and steps six to ten operate twice. Without this bookkeeping, an otherwise correct list of enzymes can produce a wrong net yield.

A phosphorylated intermediate is often more soluble and less freely permeable through the lipid bilayer than its unphosphorylated counterpart. Phosphorylation also changes how enzymes recognise the molecule and makes it possible to conserve energy in later intermediates. It does not automatically make every phosphorylated sugar a good ATP donor. The free energy of hydrolysis depends on the relative stability of the reactant and products. We will see that ordinary sugar phosphate esters have less group-transfer potential than the acyl phosphate of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and the enol phosphate of phosphoenolpyruvate.

3. Step 1 — retaining glucose in the cytosol

Glucose enters many mammalian cells through facilitated transporters, although the transport mechanism varies among tissues. Once inside, it is a substrate for hexokinase. The enzyme transfers the terminal phosphoryl group of MgATP to the hydroxyl group on carbon 6, producing glucose-6-phosphate (G6P) and ADP. Mg²⁺ associates with ATP and helps organise its negatively charged phosphate groups for transfer. The conformational closure of hexokinase after glucose binding brings the two reactants together and helps exclude water, which would otherwise compete as an acceptor of the phosphoryl group.

Glucose + ATP → G6P + ADP + H⁺ (using a common biochemical ionic convention).

The transfer is favourable under usual cellular conditions because ATP hydrolysis supplies the free energy needed to form the sugar phosphate. The reaction therefore functions as an effectively irreversible entry step in most physiological situations. Irreversible here means strongly biased in the forward direction at normal concentrations; it does not mean that a reverse chemical event is physically impossible. The cell uses a different enzyme, glucose-6-phosphatase in appropriate tissues, to release free glucose when required.

Hexokinase is not the first committed step of glycolysis, because G6P is an intersection. It can enter glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway or glycogen synthesis. Product inhibition of many mammalian hexokinase isoforms by G6P prevents unnecessary ATP consumption when downstream pathways are not using the sugar. In contrast, liver glucokinase (hexokinase IV) has lower glucose affinity than typical hexokinases, high capacity, and is not directly inhibited by G6P. It is suited to processing abundant glucose after feeding. Its kinetics are sigmoidal despite being monomeric, so a simple Michaelis constant is only an approximate description; S₀.₅ is often used. Glucokinase in pancreatic beta cells contributes to glucose sensing. In liver, glucokinase regulatory protein can sequester glucokinase in the nucleus; fructose-6-phosphate favours sequestration while fructose-1-phosphate favours release. This is a distinct regulatory mechanism from direct G6P product inhibition.

The first phosphate was purchased using ATP, not obtained free from inorganic phosphate. Keep this fact in mind when the pathway later incorporates inorganic phosphate without spending ATP.

4. Step 2 — why glucose must become fructose

Phosphoglucose isomerase converts G6P into fructose-6-phosphate (F6P). Both molecules have six carbon atoms and the same gross elemental composition; they differ in the position of the carbonyl group in their open-chain form. Glucose is an aldose with its carbonyl at carbon 1; fructose is a ketose with its carbonyl at carbon 2. The enzyme opens the sugar ring, facilitates proton transfers through an enediol or enediolate intermediate, and allows ring closure of the rearranged sugar. No ATP, NAD⁺ or carbon dioxide is consumed or released.

Why rearrange the carbonyl before splitting the molecule? Aldol cleavage of the phosphorylated ketose can occur between carbons 3 and 4, producing two three-carbon units. If the carbonyl were positioned differently, the chemistry of the preferred aldol reaction would not generate this convenient pair of products. The rearrangement therefore prepares the substrate for symmetrical carbon division, without wasting a second redox or energy carrier.

Phosphoglucose isomerase is readily reversible in cells. As the next enzyme consumes F6P, the ratio of F6P to G6P falls and the equilibrium is displaced toward the forward reaction. The enzyme accelerates the approach to equilibrium; it cannot change the equilibrium constant. This illustrates why a pathway can show sustained forward flux even when several individual reactions have small free-energy changes and are easily reversible. A later irreversible reaction can pull a sequence of near-equilibrium reactions forward.

5. Step 3 — committing carbohydrate to glycolysis

Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1) transfers a second phosphoryl group from MgATP to F6P, this time at carbon 1. The product, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (F1,6BP), bears phosphate groups at two separate carbon positions. The word bisphosphate distinguishes separate ester groups from a diphosphate in which the phosphate groups are linked to one another. F1,6BP is chemically prepared for cleavage, with a phosphoryl group destined for each three-carbon fragment.

F6P + ATP → F1,6BP + ADP + H⁺.

This reaction is strongly favourable in the usual forward direction and constitutes the first committed step of the classical glycolytic route from G6P. It is a major regulatory point because an unnecessary second ATP investment would waste resources if the cell already has abundant energy or cannot process downstream intermediates. PFK-1 responds to both substrates and cellular signals; ATP can be a substrate at its catalytic site and an inhibitor at a distinct regulatory site at sufficiently high concentration. This is not a contradiction. The sites serve different purposes, and occupancy of the regulatory site can favour a less active conformation with reduced apparent affinity for F6P.

AMP and, in many mammalian contexts, ADP relieve ATP inhibition, indicating that energy demand has increased. Citrate can strengthen inhibition, communicating that downstream carbon oxidation and biosynthetic supply are already sufficient. Low pH inhibits muscle PFK-1, helping restrain excessive glycolytic flux in acidifying conditions. Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate is a potent activator of mammalian PFK-1 and decreases the effectiveness of ATP inhibition. It is not the product of step three and is not an obligatory intermediate of the ten-step pathway. Its formation by PFK-2 and destruction by fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase provide a way for hormonal signals to adjust the sensitivity of glycolysis to available glucose.

Control is shared among enzymes, transport and demand; describing PFK-1 as a major control site does not imply that its catalytic rate alone always determines flux in every cell. For instance, severe NAD⁺ depletion can stop the payoff stage even when PFK-1 is active.

6. Steps 4 and 5 — splitting and matching the two trioses

Aldolase cleaves the bond between carbons 3 and 4 of F1,6BP. The products are dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GAP). Both carry three carbons and one phosphoryl group, but DHAP is a ketose phosphate whereas GAP contains an aldehyde at carbon 1. Only GAP is the direct substrate of the first payoff-stage enzyme. If half the carbon remained as DHAP, the pathway would recover only half of its potential energy.

Animal class I aldolases use an active-site lysine that forms a reversible Schiff base with the substrate carbonyl. This covalent intermediate acts as an electron sink and stabilises the carbanion-like intermediate during carbon–carbon bond cleavage. Class II aldolases in many microorganisms use a divalent metal to stabilise charge instead. Both catalytic strategies produce the same cleavage products; the mechanistic difference does not change carbon stoichiometry. Under standard biochemical conditions the aldolase reaction is energetically uphill, yet in cells product consumption and substrate concentrations allow the forward reaction. A positive standard free-energy change alone cannot predict the actual direction.

Triose phosphate isomerase (TPI) converts DHAP into GAP through acid–base catalysis involving an enediolate intermediate. The equilibrium of isolated TPI favours DHAP, but GAP is continually withdrawn by the next enzyme. Consequently DHAP is replenishing GAP during sustained glycolysis. The enzyme is highly efficient and suppresses unwanted side reactions by controlling the intermediate within its active site. DHAP can also supply glycerol-3-phosphate for lipid biosynthesis, a reminder that pathway intermediates have competing fates.

For each glucose there is one GAP directly released by aldolase and one GAP produced from DHAP. Carbon tracing is especially useful here. Glucose carbons 1 and 6 ultimately become the methyl carbons (C3) of the two pyruvates; carbons 2 and 5 become C2, the keto carbons; carbons 3 and 4 become the carboxyl carbons (C1). No carbon is removed in the classical glycolytic sequence. This mapping will later predict the carbon dioxide released by pyruvate dehydrogenase.

7. Step 6 — capturing oxidation energy without ATP

The payoff phase begins with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). An aldehyde can be oxidised to a carboxyl derivative, and this chemical transformation can release free energy. Simply hydrolysing all of that energy would not produce a usable phosphoryl donor. GAPDH instead couples oxidation of GAP to attachment of inorganic phosphate (Pi), yielding 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate (1,3-BPG) while reducing NAD⁺ to NADH.

GAP + Pi + NAD⁺ ⇌ 1,3-BPG + NADH + H⁺.

The active-site cysteine attacks the aldehyde carbonyl, forming a covalent thiohemiacetal. Transfer of a hydride to bound NAD⁺ oxidises the substrate portion into a thioester attached to the enzyme. The thioester retains much of the oxidation energy because hydrolysis would produce relatively stable products. Instead of water, inorganic phosphate attacks this acyl group, freeing the enzyme and forming an acyl phosphate at carbon 1 of 1,3-BPG. The phosphate already at carbon 3 remains. Thus one phosphate comes from the preparatory phase and another comes from free Pi. No ATP is consumed in step six.

The reaction connects carbon oxidation, electron transfer and energy conservation. If NAD⁺ is absent, the covalent intermediate cannot proceed through the oxidation step efficiently. If Pi is unavailable, product formation is constrained even if glucose and ATP are abundant. Inhibitors that modify the catalytic cysteine can arrest flux through GAPDH. The consequences are more extensive than losing NADH: the cell also loses access to both downstream ATP-forming steps. This makes GAPDH a particularly instructive example of how a single enzyme creates the energetic opportunity for later reactions.

The equation is reversible, and its actual direction depends on the ratio of NADH to NAD⁺ and the other substrates and products. A high cytosolic NADH/NAD⁺ ratio tends to oppose GAP oxidation. The reaction is a direct reason that sustained glycolysis requires a route for regenerating NAD⁺. Neither adding oxygen to a purified glycolytic enzyme mixture nor adding an unlimited supply of glucose solves the problem unless the system has a means of transferring electrons away from NADH.

8. Step 7 — the first repayment of ATP

Phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) transfers the phosphoryl group at carbon 1 of 1,3-BPG directly to ADP. The product is 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PG), in which carbon 1 is a carboxylate and the phosphate at carbon 3 remains. ATP is generated by substrate-level phosphorylation, not through ATP synthase. The acyl phosphate has sufficiently favourable hydrolysis free energy to drive the otherwise unfavourable phosphorylation of ADP.

1,3-BPG + ADP ⇌ 3-PG + ATP.

The approximate standard transformed free energies of hydrolysis for 1,3-BPG and ATP are −49 and −30.5 kJ mol⁻¹, respectively. To calculate the standard free energy of the transfer, combine acyl phosphate hydrolysis with the reverse of ATP hydrolysis: −49 + 30.5 = −18.5 kJ mol⁻¹. Values vary with the biochemical reference conditions. The favourable standard value does not make PGK universally irreversible; under cellular substrate and product concentrations the reaction can operate close to equilibrium and in the reverse direction during gluconeogenesis.

Each glucose generates two 1,3-BPG molecules, so PGK forms two ATP per glucose. The original investment is now repaid, leaving a running ATP balance of zero. The pathway still needs another ATP-forming reaction to achieve a net energy gain.

In red blood cells, a fraction of 1,3-BPG can be diverted by bisphosphoglycerate mutase to 2,3-BPG, which binds preferentially to deoxyhaemoglobin and lowers oxygen affinity. A phosphatase can then return the carbon skeleton to 3-PG, bypassing PGK. This shunt sacrifices one ATP opportunity per diverted triose but supports oxygen delivery. It illustrates a metabolic trade-off: maximal ATP yield is not always the only useful outcome. The detailed haemoglobin binding mechanism belongs to protein structure–function studies; here the crucial point is the energetic cost of bypassing a substrate-level phosphorylation.

9. Steps 8 and 9 — preparing a second energetic phosphate

Phosphoglycerate mutase relocates the phosphate of 3-PG from carbon 3 to carbon 2, forming 2-phosphoglycerate (2-PG). There is no net ATP consumption or production. In the common mammalian cofactor-dependent enzyme, a phosphorylated histidine donates a phosphate to 3-PG, transiently forming 2,3-BPG; the original phosphate is then transferred back to the enzyme. A small amount of 2,3-BPG can prime the enzyme. Other organisms possess cofactor-independent forms that carry out the same net rearrangement through a different catalytic mechanism. The phosphates need not literally be the same individual phosphate group during a catalytic turnover, even though the net molecular transformation is a relocation.

The purpose of relocation is to prepare the molecule for enolase. Enolase removes the elements of water from 2-PG, generating phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP). Its Mg²⁺-dependent catalytic site assists proton abstraction and stabilisation of negative charge during dehydration. The resulting molecule bears a phosphoryl group on an enol oxygen. Water is released, but there is no direct ATP production and no NADH formation in this step.

2-PG ⇌ PEP + H₂O.

Dehydration changes the group-transfer potential dramatically. After PEP donates its phosphate, the initially produced enol form of pyruvate rearranges to the much more stable keto form. This favourable tautomerisation lowers the free energy of the products and helps explain why PEP is such an effective phosphoryl donor. Do not describe the phosphate bond as containing energy that is released merely by breaking the bond: bond breaking requires energy, and the favourable overall reaction arises from the stability of the products relative to the reactants.

Fluoride can inhibit enolase in the presence of phosphate and magnesium, providing a classical biochemical way to interrupt glycolytic flux. A blocked enolase reaction prevents PEP formation and therefore removes the substrate of the last ATP-generating enzyme. An experiment that measures accumulation of 2-PG and a decline in PEP is more informative than one that reports ATP loss alone, since several different enzyme defects can lower ATP.

10. Step 10 — obtaining the net ATP profit

Pyruvate kinase transfers the phosphoryl group from PEP to ADP. This reaction requires suitable metal ions, commonly Mg²⁺ and K⁺ in mammalian enzymes. After phosphoryl transfer, the enol product rearranges to keto pyruvate. This product stabilisation makes the overall reaction strongly favourable in the glycolytic direction under normal cellular conditions.

PEP + ADP + H⁺ → pyruvate + ATP.

Approximate standard free energies of hydrolysis are −62 kJ mol⁻¹ for PEP and −30.5 kJ mol⁻¹ for ATP. The standard transfer energy is therefore about −31.5 kJ mol⁻¹. Two PEP molecules are produced per glucose and two ATP are formed in this final step. These are the net ATP gain once the investment and PGK repayment are accounted for. Pyruvate is not carbon dioxide; it still contains three carbons and can be oxidised further or used in other processes.

Many pyruvate kinase isoforms are activated by F1,6BP, the product of PFK-1. This is feed-forward activation rather than feedback inhibition. It allows the final reaction to respond to increased carbon entry into the committed pathway before a large backlog of intermediates develops. ATP and alanine inhibit important mammalian isoforms, signalling energy availability and availability of a pyruvate-derived amino acid. Liver L-type pyruvate kinase is inhibited by PKA-mediated phosphorylation during glucagon signalling; this helps spare PEP for glucose production in fasting. Not all tissue isoforms share this phosphorylation response.

The reverse conversion of pyruvate to PEP is not achieved in mammals simply by reversing pyruvate kinase. Gluconeogenesis bypasses the step through pyruvate carboxylase and PEP carboxykinase, requiring additional high-energy nucleotide expenditure. This illustrates how the existence of irreversible steps creates a need for separate regulation of opposite pathways. Detailed bypass stoichiometry belongs to the later glucose homeostasis chapter.

11. The complete reaction ledger and carbon balance

Writing all ten transformations in a ledger reveals a central pattern: no carbon is lost, but the free energy of oxidation is conserved in ATP and NADH. The only redox reaction in the classical ten-step pathway is GAPDH. Phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase are the two ATP-producing enzymes. Hexokinase and PFK-1 are the two ATP-consuming enzymes. The isomerases, aldolase, mutase and enolase reorganise the molecule without directly producing ATP.

For net stoichiometry, add the first five reactions to twice the last five. G6P, F6P, F1,6BP, DHAP, GAP, 1,3-BPG, 3-PG, 2-PG and PEP appear as both products and reactants and cancel. Four ADP are used in the payoff stage and two ADP are released in the investment stage; two remain as net reactants. Four ATP are made and two are used; two remain as net products. The phosphoryl groups incorporated from ATP in the preparation phase are recovered in the ledger along with the inorganic phosphate incorporated at GAPDH.

Glucose + 2 ADP + 2 Pi + 2 NAD⁺ → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH + 2 H⁺ + 2 H₂O.

This is a common biochemical representation; explicit proton coefficients depend on the chosen ionic species and proton-buffer convention. The carbon, ATP and redox balances do not depend on those notational differences. Six carbons enter and six leave in pyruvate. Each NAD⁺ accepts two electrons, so four electrons are transferred to the two NADH molecules. Each enolase event releases one water, producing two per glucose. Oxygen is absent from the net equation, and carbon dioxide is absent as well. If an examination problem includes oxygen uptake or carbon dioxide release, it is asking about a larger metabolic system rather than glycolysis alone.

12. Free energy, direction and metabolic flux

An enzyme is not an energy source. It lowers an activation barrier in both directions, while the free-energy difference between substrates and products determines the thermodynamic bias. At fixed temperature and biochemical reference pH, the actual transformed reaction free energy is approximated by ΔG = ΔG°′ + RT ln Q, where R is 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is the absolute temperature in kelvin and Q is the dimensionless reaction quotient formed from activities relative to standard states. Dilute-solution calculations often approximate activities with concentrations divided by 1 M. A negative ΔG favours forward transformation; zero denotes equilibrium.

For illustration, suppose a reversible isomerisation has ΔG°′ = +1.7 kJ mol⁻¹ and Q = 0.10 at 310 K. Then RT = 2.577 kJ mol⁻¹, ln(0.10) = −2.303, and ΔG = 1.7 − 5.93 = −4.23 kJ mol⁻¹. Although the standard reaction looks unfavourable, continuous removal of its product can make the actual forward reaction favourable. The same reasoning explains how consumption of GAP helps both aldolase and TPI maintain forward flux.

Under typical conditions hexokinase, PFK-1 and pyruvate kinase have substantial negative actual free-energy changes. The other seven reactions generally lie closer to equilibrium, though the exact values depend on cell type and metabolite concentrations. The sum of free-energy changes for each individual event, with stoichiometric multiplicities, gives the free-energy change for the pathway. Intermediates cancel because free energy is a state function. A pathway need not have every reaction strongly downhill; a chain of reactions can maintain favourable total conversion while several reversible transformations respond to local concentration ratios.

Do not equate free energy with rate. A strongly favourable reaction can be slow if enzyme activity is low, and a near-equilibrium reaction can support large flux if enzyme capacity is high. At steady state, pathway intermediates may remain at nearly constant concentrations while molecules continuously enter and leave. If a downstream block initially makes a metabolite accumulate, feedback and alternate pathways may eventually decrease upstream flux. A steady-state concentration is therefore not equivalent to the amount of material passing through a reaction per unit time. This distinction is essential for interpreting experiments involving inhibition and metabolic regulation.

13. Regulation from cellular demand to tissue-specific signals

A cell must coordinate ATP production with ATP consumption. If ATP consumption rises, ADP generally becomes more available for ATP-forming reactions. Adenylate kinase links adenine nucleotide pools through 2 ADP ⇌ ATP + AMP, so modest changes in ADP can be accompanied by comparatively large relative changes in AMP. AMP therefore provides a sensitive signal of increased energy demand. PFK-1 activation by AMP and relief of ATP inhibition can accelerate carbon entry into glycolysis. As energy availability recovers, the inhibitory signal becomes stronger. This is a negative feedback relationship that stabilises energy balance.

A second type of regulation coordinates glycolysis with other tissues. In the fed liver, insulin signalling promotes glucose utilisation, glucokinase expression and, for the liver form of the bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2 enzyme, a phosphorylation state favouring synthesis of fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. PFK-2 converts F6P and ATP into F2,6BP, whereas the phosphatase domain hydrolyses F2,6BP to F6P and Pi. The metabolite activates PFK-1 and inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, thereby favouring glycolysis over the opposing glucose-producing pathway.

During fasting, glucagon signals through cAMP and PKA in hepatocytes. Phosphorylation of the liver bifunctional enzyme decreases its PFK-2 activity and increases its FBPase-2 activity. F2,6BP declines, PFK-1 becomes less activated and gluconeogenic fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase is less inhibited. PKA also phosphorylates liver L-pyruvate kinase, reducing its activity. These changes avoid simultaneously driving opposing reactions and spending ATP unnecessarily. The hormonal response of the liver is not a universal rule for every tissue: cardiac PFK-2 isoforms, for example, can respond differently to phosphorylation. The muscle's immediate priorities during contraction differ from the liver's role in blood glucose supply.

Glucose supply also sets a boundary on possible flux. Insulin can increase glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue through GLUT4 recruitment; brain and red cells use different transporters. The amount of phosphorylated substrate reaching the committed step can therefore change independently of PFK-1 activity. Enzyme expression changes over longer times, while allosteric binding and phosphorylation often change activity more rapidly. Distinguishing these time scales allows a student to infer whether an immediate experimental response could plausibly result from new enzyme synthesis.

Consider an experiment comparing an ATP-rich resting muscle extract with a high-AMP extract at the same F6P concentration and sufficient catalytic ATP. Greater PFK-1 activity in the AMP-rich condition is consistent with relief of allosteric inhibition. If adding AMP fails to restore whole-pathway ATP generation, this does not necessarily disprove allostery: Pi, NAD⁺, ADP or another enzyme may be limiting. The system must be analysed as a series of coupled reactions.

14. Why oxygen availability changes pyruvate fate

The amount of NAD⁺ in a cell is finite. If glycolysis runs without a reaction that reoxidises NADH, the accessible NAD⁺ pool declines. Eventually GAPDH cannot accept electrons from GAP, so the downstream production of ATP slows or stops. This problem arises whether the cell has ten glucose molecules or ten thousand. Glycolysis therefore depends on electron disposal even though its own chemistry does not require oxygen.

In a respiring mammalian cell, cytosolic NADH transfers reducing equivalents to mitochondria through shuttle systems because the inner mitochondrial membrane does not freely permit NADH to cross. The respiratory chain ultimately transfers electrons to oxygen, and the resulting proton gradient supports oxidative phosphorylation. Pyruvate can enter the mitochondrial matrix and be oxidised to acetyl-CoA. The exact ATP equivalent of the two cytosolic NADH depends on the shuttle and the coupling efficiency; it should never be included in the direct substrate-level yield of glycolysis without stating the assumption. Detailed malate–aspartate and glycerol phosphate shuttle mechanisms belong to 1.E.04.

When respiratory NADH oxidation is insufficient for the glycolytic rate, a cytosolic reaction can use an organic acceptor to regenerate NAD⁺. This is fermentation in the usual biochemical sense. It does not require an external terminal electron acceptor or a respiratory electron-transport chain. Pyruvate or a derivative receives electrons originally removed from GAP. The regenerated NAD⁺ can enter another round of glycolysis. The fermentation reaction itself need not yield ATP; it enables continued ATP generation in the preceding reactions.

Oxygen presence does not prohibit fermentation. Mature mammalian red blood cells lack mitochondria and produce lactate even when carrying abundant oxygen. Rapidly proliferating cells and some microorganisms may also produce fermentation products in oxygenated conditions when carbon supply and glycolytic flux exceed respiratory capacity or when regulation favours that route. Conversely, certain anaerobic microorganisms use respiratory chains with terminal electron acceptors other than oxygen. Anaerobic respiration is therefore not synonymous with fermentation. Distinguish the terminal acceptor, the electron-transfer machinery and the ATP-generating mechanism rather than classifying metabolism by oxygen alone.

15. Lactate fermentation — closing the NAD cycle

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) catalyses the reversible reduction of the keto group of pyruvate to the secondary alcohol group of L-lactate in mammals. NADH donates a hydride to carbon 2, and a proton is incorporated in the reduction. The enzyme does not remove carbon dioxide or create a high-energy phosphate.

Pyruvate + NADH + H⁺ ⇌ L-lactate + NAD⁺.

Add this reaction twice to the glycolytic net equation. Both pyruvates cancel, both NADH cancel against NADH consumption, and the two NAD⁺ consumed by GAPDH are returned. The simplified combined equation is glucose + 2 ADP + 2 Pi → 2 lactate + 2 ATP + 2 H₂O under the same conventional proton accounting. The net yield is two ATP per glucose. NAD⁺ functions catalytically over multiple turns because its oxidised form is restored. No net NADH is exported from the combined pathway.

LDH can operate in either direction. A high lactate concentration and a suitable oxidised NAD pool can favour lactate oxidation to pyruvate; heart and other oxidative tissues can use circulating lactate as a carbon fuel. LDH isoenzymes differ in kinetic properties, but their reaction equilibrium is the same. The direction is set by the actual metabolite and redox ratios rather than the enzyme's tissue name alone. In an approximate near-equilibrium model at fixed pH, K′ = [lactate][NAD⁺]/([pyruvate][NADH]), so [lactate]/[pyruvate] = K′ × [NADH]/[NAD⁺]. A higher cytosolic NADH/NAD⁺ ratio tends to increase the lactate/pyruvate ratio, provided the assumptions are reasonable. This relation does not mean that blood lactate by itself measures cytosolic redox state exactly.

During intense contraction, glycolytic ATP production may temporarily outpace mitochondrial oxidation. Lactate production permits rapid NAD⁺ recycling. Lactate can leave the cell with a proton through monocarboxylate transporters and later be oxidised or used for hepatic glucose formation. Lactate itself is not simply a waste toxin, nor does the LDH reaction release a proton in the written direction; it consumes one. Acid–base changes during exercise involve ATP turnover, transport, buffering and multiple metabolic reactions. The common statement that lactate alone directly causes all muscle acidity or delayed soreness is misleading.

In homolactic fermentation by suitable bacteria, conversion of sugar to lactate also regenerates NAD⁺ and acidifies the environment; this has applications in food fermentation. Some microorganisms use heterolactic pathways with different products and yields, so the two-lactate stoichiometry is specific to classical glycolysis followed by homolactic conversion. The Cori cycle can transfer lactate carbon to the liver for glucose synthesis, but glucose regeneration has an energy cost; detailed whole-body accounting is reserved for 1.E.05.

16. Alcoholic fermentation — a two-reaction electron sink

Yeast and some microorganisms solve the NAD⁺ recycling problem by first changing pyruvate into a more suitable organic electron acceptor. Pyruvate decarboxylase removes the carboxyl carbon of pyruvate as carbon dioxide, forming acetaldehyde, a two-carbon aldehyde. The reaction requires thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) and Mg²⁺. TPP stabilises the carbon-centred intermediate produced during decarboxylation. The two electrons later used for reduction do not come from this decarboxylation step; NADH made during glycolysis supplies them.

Pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO₂.

Alcohol dehydrogenase then transfers a hydride from NADH to acetaldehyde, converting it into ethanol while regenerating NAD⁺. In common yeast alcohol dehydrogenase, zinc helps coordinate the reacting groups. The reduction is chemically analogous to reducing pyruvate to lactate but uses a different substrate and enzyme.

Acetaldehyde + NADH + H⁺ ⇌ ethanol + NAD⁺.

For each glucose, two pyruvates produce two acetaldehydes and two carbon dioxide molecules; reduction of both acetaldehydes consumes the two NADH produced by glycolysis. The combined classical reaction is glucose + 2 ADP + 2 Pi → 2 ethanol + 2 CO₂ + 2 ATP + 2 H₂O. Six glucose carbons are accounted for: four in ethanol and two in carbon dioxide. The ethanol and CO₂ are not produced by the ten glycolytic reactions themselves. The gas explains dough expansion during yeast-based bread making, while ethanol production is exploited in fermentation processes.

Yeast pyruvate decarboxylase is distinct from the E1 component of pyruvate dehydrogenase. Both use TPP and catalyse decarboxylation, but the free acetaldehyde product of the fermentation enzyme differs from the acetyl group transferred within the oxidative complex. In an enzyme extract, blocking alcohol dehydrogenase can initially allow acetaldehyde and CO₂ formation while NADH accumulates; prolonged glycolysis then declines as NAD⁺ becomes depleted. Blocking pyruvate decarboxylase instead prevents acetaldehyde formation. Observing both gas and redox behaviour helps distinguish the two blocks.

In many organisms oxygen suppresses fermentation by enabling more efficient respiratory ATP production, a phenomenon traditionally termed the Pasteur effect. Certain yeasts can still ferment at high glucose even in oxygenated conditions (the Crabtree effect). These examples demonstrate that substrate supply and regulation matter in addition to oxygen. They do not alter the stoichiometry of the two classical fermentation reactions.

17. Pyruvate as a junction between pathways

Pyruvate is a small α-keto acid with a carboxyl group at carbon 1, a keto group at carbon 2 and a methyl group at carbon 3. Its three-carbon structure makes it useful as both an oxidative fuel and a precursor of larger molecules. The fate chosen depends on cell type, oxygen-independent electron disposal, mitochondrial access, energy demand and the activities of competing enzymes. The glycolytic pathway does not itself commit pyruvate to one particular downstream route.

In many aerobic eukaryotic cells, pyruvate crosses the inner mitochondrial membrane through the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier and reaches the matrix. Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) converts it to acetyl-CoA. This reaction removes one carbon as CO₂ and oxidises the remaining two-carbon fragment, retaining part of its energy in NADH and an energy-rich thioester. Acetyl-CoA can subsequently enter the citric acid cycle or contribute carbon to biosynthesis. PDH is not a reaction of the citric acid cycle itself; it provides a major entry substrate.

Another matrix enzyme, pyruvate carboxylase, uses ATP, bicarbonate and covalently attached biotin to convert pyruvate into oxaloacetate. This adds a carbon rather than removing one. It replenishes oxaloacetate when intermediates are withdrawn for biosynthesis and supplies a gluconeogenic precursor in appropriate tissues. Acetyl-CoA activates mammalian pyruvate carboxylase, linking abundant acetyl groups to the need for oxaloacetate. Unlike PDH, this reaction does not produce NADH. The ATP cost matters when comparing pathways.

Alanine aminotransferase converts pyruvate and glutamate reversibly into alanine and α-ketoglutarate using pyridoxal phosphate. This is a transfer of an amino group, not a reduction by NADH. Alanine can transport carbon and amino nitrogen from muscle to liver. Conversion of pyruvate to alanine preserves all three carbons; conversion to acetyl-CoA loses carbon 1. The carbon skeleton of acetyl-CoA cannot provide a net yield of glucose in mammals via reversal of PDH or ordinary TCA cycling. Plants and some microorganisms have additional routes such as the glyoxylate cycle, discussed with TCA integration. Some anaerobic bacteria instead use pyruvate formate-lyase, yielding acetyl-CoA and formate rather than the NADH and CO₂ of PDH. These boundaries prevent applying one organism's textbook pathway to every form of life.

18. Pyruvate dehydrogenase — a multienzyme oxidative gateway

The conversion of pyruvate into acetyl-CoA is more demanding than simple decarboxylation. The residual fragment must be oxidised, attached to coenzyme A and the electrons must be removed. A single released acetaldehyde molecule would be reactive and could diffuse away. The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex solves these tasks by organising three catalytic components into a large assembly. In mammalian cells it resides in the mitochondrial matrix; related complexes in bacteria operate without mitochondria. The overall transformation is an oxidative decarboxylation.

Pyruvate + CoA-SH + NAD⁺ → acetyl-CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺.

The E1 component is pyruvate dehydrogenase (decarboxylating). Its bound TPP contains a thiazolium ring that stabilises the carbanion equivalent at its reactive carbon. The activated cofactor attacks the carbonyl of pyruvate, then loss of CO₂ leaves a hydroxyethyl-TPP intermediate. The cofactor stabilises the electron distribution that would otherwise make decarboxylation energetically difficult. TPP is regenerated later in the catalytic cycle; it is not consumed stoichiometrically.

The next event is reductive acetylation of a lipoyl group attached covalently to a lysine residue in a mobile domain of E2. Oxidised lipoamide contains a disulfide bond. The hydroxyethyl fragment on TPP is oxidised to an acetyl group as the lipoamide disulfide is reduced. An acetyl thioester is formed on one lipoyl sulfur and the other sulfur bears a thiol. TPP is restored. This transfer is often described as the second step of the overall cycle, occurring through cooperation of E1 and the E2-bound lipoyl substrate.

E2, dihydrolipoyl transacetylase, now transfers the acetyl group from the lipoyl thioester to the thiol group of free coenzyme A. Acetyl-CoA is released, while the lipoyl group remains reduced with two thiols. Its mobile arm physically carries bound reaction intermediates among the catalytic sites, increasing effective local availability and limiting release of reactive intermediates. This is substrate channeling; it is not a claim that the entire complex is sealed against solvent.

The lipoyl arm cannot repeat this process while its disulfide remains reduced. E3, dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase, reoxidises it through its redox-active disulfide and bound FAD. Electrons pass to FAD, generating a reduced flavin state, and then to NAD⁺, producing NADH. The lipoyl disulfide and FAD are regenerated. This is why FAD is a required cofactor but FADH₂ is not a net product of the complex. The net soluble reduced carrier is NADH. No ATP is directly formed in the PDH reaction.

The five organic cofactors form an ordered relay: TPP supports carbon cleavage, lipoamide accepts both the acetyl group and reducing equivalents, CoA receives the acetyl group, FAD mediates electron transfer, and NAD⁺ receives the final reducing equivalents. TPP is derived from thiamine (B1), CoA contains pantothenate (B5), FAD derives from riboflavin (B2) and NAD⁺ derives from niacin (B3); lipoic acid is a distinct cofactor and not one of these four vitamin-derived cofactors. Mg²⁺ assists TPP-dependent chemistry but is not included in the standard five-organic-cofactor list. TPP, FAD and the lipoyl group remain enzyme-associated during repeated turnover; CoA and NAD⁺ serve as diffusible cosubstrates.

One glucose produces two pyruvates, so complete passage through PDH gives two acetyl-CoA, two CO₂ and two additional NADH. Adding glycolysis and PDH gives two net ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation and four NADH in two compartments (two cytosolic, two mitochondrial in a mammalian cell). The remaining four carbon atoms reside in the two acetyl groups. Labeling glucose carbons 3 and 4 with carbon-13 therefore labels the two CO₂ molecules released at PDH; labels at glucose carbons 1 and 6 remain in the methyl positions of the acetyl groups. This carbon balance prepares us to follow acetyl carbon into the citric acid cycle.

19. Regulation of PDH and consequences of an enzyme defect

PDH is a major control point because oxidation of pyruvate commits its carbon to acetyl-CoA in a physiologically irreversible reaction in mammals. Once the carboxyl group has been lost as CO₂, the complex cannot simply restore pyruvate by reversing the sequence. The cell must decide how much pyruvate to oxidise, preserve for glucose production or divert to other purposes.

A high acetyl-CoA/CoA ratio indicates that acetyl groups are accumulating relative to free acceptor. A high NADH/NAD⁺ ratio indicates that reducing equivalents are not being removed rapidly enough. Both ratios favour inhibition of the complex directly and through regulation of PDH kinase. When oxidative metabolism is supplying sufficient ATP, PDH kinase (PDK) uses ATP to phosphorylate regulatory serine residues of the mammalian E1 α subunit. This decreases E1 activity, and therefore acetyl-CoA formation. Pyruvate and ADP can oppose PDK activity, depending on its isoform and conditions. This coordination means abundant substrate and energy demand can favour continued oxidation.

PDH phosphatase (PDP) removes the inhibitory phosphate from E1. Ca²⁺ activates important phosphatase isoforms in muscle, providing a connection between contraction and enhanced carbohydrate oxidation. Insulin promotes PDH activation in some tissues, such as adipose tissue, linking glucose availability to provision of acetyl groups for synthesis. PDK and PDP are regulatory enzymes distinct from E1, E2 and E3. Their tissue-specific responses must not be assumed identical across all organisms. The cofactor regeneration cycle is likewise distinct from the covalent phosphorylation cycle.

A deficiency of thiamine can impair TPP-dependent catalysis, affecting PDH and other TPP-dependent enzymes. An E1 defect lowers oxidative pyruvate entry even if glycolysis itself remains functional; under suitable conditions pyruvate can accumulate and be diverted to lactate or alanine. A defect in E3 can prevent regeneration of the oxidised lipoyl arm, even if the first turnover begins normally. An inhibitor of PDK can increase the dephosphorylated active fraction of a functioning mammalian complex but cannot restore an absent catalytic component. These distinctions are useful for interpreting enzyme-deficiency questions: substrate accumulation, altered redox state and diminished product formation identify a functional bottleneck but not always a unique molecular cause.

Suppose a permeabilised mitochondrial preparation converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA slowly, but addition of active PDP restores production. This suggests that inhibitory phosphorylation was contributing to the low activity. If adding PDP fails while added TPP restores activity, a cofactor limitation is more consistent with the result. Neither result is absolute proof of a genetic disorder. Carefully matched assays with substrate and cofactor availability controlled are necessary to distinguish reversible regulation from a catalytic lesion.

20. Worked quantitative reasoning: ATP, redox and carbon

Example 1. A preparation completely converts 12 μmol glucose to pyruvate. The preparatory phase spends 24 μmol ATP. Each of the two ATP-producing steps occurs 24 μmol times, so gross production is 48 μmol ATP. Subtract the cost to obtain 24 μmol net ATP. GAPDH produces 24 μmol NADH and the pathway forms 24 μmol pyruvate. If all pyruvate becomes lactate, 24 μmol NADH is reoxidised and net NADH accumulation is zero. The net ATP gain remains 24 μmol.

Example 2. A preparation makes 10 μmol glucose-derived pairs of trioses, and 30% of its 1,3-BPG molecules pass through a shunt that bypasses PGK but rejoins at 3-PG. Twenty μmol trioses are formed; 6 μmol bypass the first ATP-forming reaction. PGK now forms 14 μmol ATP and pyruvate kinase forms 20 μmol. Gross yield is 34 μmol, investment 20 μmol and net gain 14 μmol, or 1.4 ATP equivalents per glucose on average. The average may be fractional even though each individual transfer forms a whole ATP molecule. The shunt does not bypass GAPDH, so 20 μmol NADH is still generated before any regeneration.

Example 3. If 5 μmol glucose are completely converted to acetyl-CoA through glycolysis and PDH, 10 μmol pyruvate reach PDH. Glycolysis makes 10 μmol net ATP and 10 μmol cytosolic NADH. PDH produces 10 μmol acetyl-CoA, 10 μmol CO₂ and 10 μmol mitochondrial NADH. In a simplified calculation assuming a malate–aspartate shuttle and 2.5 ATP per mitochondrial NADH equivalent, these twenty μmol NADH could yield about 50 μmol ATP via oxidative phosphorylation, in addition to the 10 μmol direct ATP. With a glycerol phosphate shuttle valued at 1.5 ATP per cytosolic NADH equivalent, the estimate falls to 40 μmol oxidative ATP and 50 μmol total. Neither value includes subsequent TCA oxidation. Actual coupling yields are not fixed integers.

Example 4. A glycogen-derived glucose residue released as glucose-1-phosphate can be converted to G6P without the hexokinase expenditure. Assuming entry into classical glycolysis and complete lactate formation, only PFK-1 consumes ATP. Gross production remains four, and the net gain is three ATP per such residue. A free glucose molecule released at a glycogen branch point does require phosphorylation and gives the ordinary two. The calculation is restricted to the pre-existing glycogen residue; it does not include the earlier energy cost of glycogen synthesis.

Example 5. Suppose cytosolic free NAD⁺ is initially 60 nmol and neither respiration nor fermentation can regenerate it. Ignoring other sources and sinks, at most 60 nmol GAP can be oxidised to 1,3-BPG because the reaction consumes one NAD⁺ per GAP. This is equivalent to the payoff phase of 30 nmol glucose if both trioses are completely processed. In a real cell, the free pool cannot usually be exhausted quantitatively and other redox reactions matter. The calculation nevertheless demonstrates why a carrier pool that is small relative to nutrient supply must be recycled rather than continually consumed.

21. Interpreting experiments rather than memorising a pathway

Biochemical evidence becomes useful when a prediction distinguishes competing explanations. A classical cell-free fermentation experiment demonstrates that soluble components can carry out sugar conversion, but it does not by itself identify each intermediate. Fractionation and reconstitution can show that a missing enzyme or cofactor is required. Isotopic carbon tracing tests which bonds are broken, while metabolite assays reveal changes in the pools upstream and downstream of a block. Each method answers a different question.

Consider the following deliberately constructed teaching dataset, not measurements from a published experiment. Equal amounts of glucose and extract are incubated briefly with the indicated modification. All other conditions are held constant. The rates are reported in arbitrary matched units, and the concentration changes refer to the initial response relative to control. The purpose is to reason about location and consequences of a block, not to infer universal enzyme rates.

In condition A, G6P rises but F1,6BP falls, consistent with decreased committed entry at PFK-1 or impaired access to F6P. The specific rescue by F2,6BP in a mammalian extract with active PFK-1 would support the regulatory explanation. In condition B, 2-PG rises and PEP falls, suggesting a local block at enolase. In condition C, pyruvate and NADH initially rise while lactate falls; an LDH limitation is plausible in a fermenting preparation. Eventually insufficient NAD⁺ can slow GAPDH as well. The order of observation matters: a late sample could show reduced pyruvate formation even though LDH was the initial lesion.

A concentration change is not a direct flux measurement. For example, a small pyruvate pool could indicate rapid consumption or poor formation, while a large pool could indicate rapid production or a block in its removal. An isotope-label appearance rate or a directly measured product formation rate provides a stronger estimate of flux. An appropriate control includes equal protein, substrate, temperature, pH and measurement duration; otherwise a change in activity may reflect an unrelated condition.

NADH absorbs light near 340 nm much more strongly than NAD⁺. In a simplified optical assay with path length 1 cm and NADH molar extinction coefficient 6220 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹, a decrease in absorbance at 340 nm of 0.1244 corresponds to Δc = ΔA/(εl) = 0.000020 M = 20 μM NADH oxidised, after suitable blank correction. In a 2 mL assay this is 40 nmol. If LDH is the sole reaction consuming NADH, 40 nmol pyruvate has been reduced. In a coupled assay other reactions may produce or consume NADH, so the inference requires a controlled reaction mixture. This standard measurement provides a bridge from pathway chemistry to quantitative experimental questions.

22. Conceptual consolidation and the next metabolic stage

The route begins with two ATP investments, not with oxidation. A carbonyl rearrangement allows cleavage of a six-carbon sugar into two trioses. TPI supplies two equivalent GAP molecules, and GAPDH captures oxidation energy as NADH and acyl phosphate. PGK then repays the initial cost. Phosphate relocation and dehydration produce PEP, whose conversion to keto pyruvate drives the second substrate-level phosphorylation. Per glucose, the direct net gain is two ATP, two NADH and two pyruvate. No oxygen is consumed and no carbon dioxide is formed in these ten reactions.

Three highly forward-biased steps offer important regulatory opportunities. Hexokinase handles glucose entry, PFK-1 controls committed carbon flow and pyruvate kinase controls the final ATP-generating conversion. Their regulation is integrated with substrate transport, the NAD⁺ pool and cellular energy consumption. Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ by reduction of an organic acceptor; it does not supply extra ATP through the LDH or alcohol dehydrogenase reactions. PDH instead forms acetyl-CoA and NADH, loses carbon dioxide and links carbohydrate catabolism to oxidative metabolism. Its three catalytic components and five organic cofactors transfer carbon and electrons in a coordinated cycle.

A useful examination strategy is to write a separate carbon ledger, ATP ledger and electron ledger. Decide whether the question includes glycolysis alone, glycolysis plus fermentation, glycolysis plus PDH, or complete respiration. Then identify the relevant cellular compartment and organism. For a regulation question, distinguish an immediate allosteric response from phosphorylation or altered enzyme abundance. For a defect question, distinguish the first blocked reaction from later changes caused by feedback or loss of redox recycling. These steps make unfamiliar problems manageable even when the names of several enzymes are deliberately included as distractors.

The next chapter follows the two-carbon acetyl group into the citric acid cycle. Its central question is how a cyclic pathway oxidises acetyl groups while regenerating the acceptor oxaloacetate and providing intermediates for other cellular needs. The NADH produced here and in that cycle will then lead to electron transport and oxidative phosphorylation. No additional respiratory ATP should be counted until the route and assumptions are specified.

23. Part B — conceptual and assertion–reason practice

These original conceptual questions are designed for NET/SET preparation, not reproduced examination papers. Attempt each before opening the explanation.

Question 1. Which reaction commits F6P to classical glycolysis?

A. Hexokinase; B. PFK-1; C. PFK-2; D. PGK

Answer and reasoning

B. PFK-1 makes F1,6BP for cleavage. Hexokinase makes a branch-point metabolite; PFK-2 makes the regulatory F2,6BP; PGK recovers ATP later.

Question 2. Which glycolytic step directly consumes inorganic phosphate?

A. Hexokinase; B. PFK-1; C. GAPDH; D. Enolase

Answer and reasoning

C. GAPDH incorporates Pi into 1,3-BPG while reducing NAD⁺. Hexokinase and PFK-1 use ATP as phosphate donor; enolase removes water.

Question 3. Which enzymes generate ATP directly?

A. PFK-1 and PGK; B. PGK and pyruvate kinase; C. GAPDH and enolase; D. LDH and ADH

Answer and reasoning

B. Both transfer phosphoryl groups from metabolic intermediates to ADP. PFK-1 consumes ATP, GAPDH makes NADH, and the fermentation reductases regenerate NAD⁺.

Question 4. What best explains the high group-transfer potential of PEP?

A. Its phosphate bond releases energy when broken alone; B. Keto pyruvate is stabilised after transfer; C. Oxygen binds PEP; D. PEP directly reduces NAD⁺

Answer and reasoning

B. Product tautomerisation contributes to the favourable net reaction. A is an incorrect bond-energy explanation; C and D are not part of the reaction.

Question 5. Which feature distinguishes typical hepatic glucokinase from hexokinase I?

A. Direct G6P inhibition; B. Lower capacity; C. Higher glucose concentration needed for substantial activity; D. Direct ATP production

Answer and reasoning

C. Glucokinase has a higher S₀.₅ and high capacity; it is not directly inhibited by G6P, and both enzymes consume ATP.

Question 6. Which metabolite activates PFK-1 but is not a mandatory glycolytic intermediate?

A. F2,6BP; B. F1,6BP; C. PEP; D. Pyruvate

Answer and reasoning

A. F2,6BP is made by PFK-2 and regulates PFK-1. The other three are pathway intermediates or products.

Question 7. Which PDH cofactor is covalently attached to a lysine?

A. NAD⁺; B. CoA; C. Lipoyl group; D. TPP

Answer and reasoning

C. The lipoyl arm is tethered to E2. NAD⁺ and CoA diffuse, and TPP is enzyme-associated without this covalent lysine linkage.

Question 8. Which PDH component regenerates the oxidised lipoyl group?

A. E1; B. E2; C. E3; D. PFK-1

Answer and reasoning

C. E3 carries reducing equivalents through FAD to NAD⁺. E1 decarboxylates pyruvate and E2 transfers acetyl groups.

Question 9. Assertion: mature mammalian erythrocytes can produce lactate in oxygenated blood. Reason: they lack mitochondria.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false

Answer and reasoning

A. They cannot use mitochondrial respiration to recycle their glycolytic NADH; LDH supports continued ATP formation.

Question 10. Assertion: fermentation produces no additional ATP in the LDH step. Reason: LDH transfers electrons from NADH to pyruvate, rather than a phosphoryl group to ADP.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false

Answer and reasoning

A. LDH regenerates NAD⁺, which supports ATP production in earlier glycolytic reactions but does not phosphorylate ADP.

24. Part C — quantitative and experimental practice

These original analytical questions are designed for NET/SET preparation, not reproduced examination papers. Attempt each before opening the explanation.

Question 1. 8 mmol glucose pass entirely through glycolysis. Find gross ATP, net ATP and NADH.

A. 16,16,8; B. 32,16,16; C. 32,32,16; D. 16,8,16 mmol

Answer and reasoning

B. Two investment ATP per glucose cost 16 mmol; four gross ATP give 32 mmol; subtract to get 16. GAPDH acts twice, giving 16 mmol NADH.

Question 2. 5 mmol glucose undergo alcoholic fermentation. How much ethanol, CO₂ and net ATP result?

A. 5,10,10; B. 10,5,10; C. 10,10,10; D. 10,10,20 mmol

Answer and reasoning

C. Each glucose yields two three-carbon pyruvates. Each loses one carbon and becomes ethanol. Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ without additional ATP.

Question 3. A red cell diverts 25% of the 1,3-BPG molecules through the 2,3-BPG shunt. What is its average net ATP per glucose?

A. 0.5; B. 1; C. 1.5; D. 2.5

Answer and reasoning

C. Two trioses yield 0.5 bypass events per glucose on average. Subtract 0.5 ATP from the normal net two. GAPDH and pyruvate kinase are not bypassed.

Question 4. A mixture contains 100 μM NAD⁺ and no means to oxidise NADH. What is the ideal upper bound on fully processed glucose in the same volume?

A. 25 μM; B. 50 μM; C. 100 μM; D. Unlimited

Answer and reasoning

B. Each glucose requires two NAD⁺ in the payoff phase. Divide the initial available pool by two, neglecting other sinks and incomplete conversion.

Question 5. [3,4-¹³C₂]glucose is converted to acetyl-CoA by glycolysis and PDH. Where do the labels first leave as CO₂?

A. Hexokinase; B. Aldolase; C. PDH E1; D. Neither label leaves

Answer and reasoning

C. Glucose C3 and C4 become the carboxyl carbons of pyruvate. E1 removes these. Glycolysis has no decarboxylation.

Question 6. [1-¹³C]glucose undergoes alcoholic fermentation. Where is the label found in the final products?

A. CO₂; B. Ethanol methyl carbon; C. Ethanol hydroxymethyl carbon; D. NADH

Answer and reasoning

B. Glucose C1 becomes pyruvate C3, the methyl carbon retained in acetaldehyde and ethanol. CO₂ comes from pyruvate C1.

Question 7. An extract contains adequate NAD⁺ and ATP but lacks Pi. Which first payoff reaction is directly constrained?

A. TPI; B. GAPDH; C. Enolase; D. Pyruvate kinase

Answer and reasoning

B. Pi is a direct GAPDH substrate. Subsequent ATP-forming steps are affected indirectly because their substrates fail to be produced.

Question 8. An inhibitor causes 2-PG to rise and PEP to fall during a brief assay. Which target is most consistent?

A. Aldolase; B. PGK; C. Enolase; D. LDH

Answer and reasoning

C. The substrate and product are on opposite sides of the enolase reaction. The other targets do not directly interconvert these metabolites.

Question 9. At 310 K an isomerisation has ΔG°′ = +4.0 kJ/mol and Q = 0.10. Estimate ΔG.

A. +9.9; B. −1.9; C. +1.9; D. −9.9 kJ/mol

Answer and reasoning

B. RT ln Q = 2.577 × (−2.303) = −5.93 kJ/mol; 4.0 − 5.93 = −1.93. A positive standard value is not sufficient to infer actual direction.

Question 10. A preparation converts glucose to acetyl-CoA and all cytosolic NADH is valued at 1.5 ATP each; matrix NADH at 2.5. Exclude TCA. Estimate ATP equivalents per glucose.

A. 2; B. 7; C. 10; D. 12

Answer and reasoning

C. Direct 2 + cytosolic 2 × 1.5 + matrix 2 × 2.5 = 10. These are conditional ATP equivalents, not direct glycolytic yield.

Question 11. Glucagon activates PKA in a fasting hepatocyte. Which combination is expected for the liver isoforms?

A. F2,6BP rises and L-PK activates; B. F2,6BP falls and L-PK is inhibited; C. F2,6BP falls and L-PK activates; D. F2,6BP rises and L-PK inhibited

Answer and reasoning

B. PKA favours the phosphatase activity of hepatic PFK-2/FBPase-2 and inhibits L-pyruvate kinase via phosphorylation, conserving carbon for glucose synthesis.

Question 12. PDH E3 is inactivated while E1, E2 and all cofactors remain available. Which earliest recycling failure follows?

A. TPP can never bind pyruvate; B. Reduced lipoyl groups cannot be efficiently reoxidised; C. PFK-1 cannot bind ATP; D. CoA cannot initially accept acetyl

Answer and reasoning

B. E3 restores the oxidised lipoyl state through FAD and NAD⁺. Initial E1/E2 turnover may occur, but sustained cycling fails.

Question 13. PDH activity increases after a phosphatase is added, with substrate supply unchanged. What is the strongest inference?

A. E1 necessarily has a mutation; B. Inhibitory phosphorylation contributed to low activity; C. FAD is unnecessary; D. PDH directly generates ATP

Answer and reasoning

B. Reversible dephosphorylation can activate mammalian E1. The result does not identify a mutation or remove cofactor requirements.

Question 14. In a fixed-pH near-equilibrium LDH model, NADH/NAD⁺ doubles. What happens to lactate/pyruvate if K′ stays constant?

A. Halves; B. Doubles; C. Quadruples; D. No change

Answer and reasoning

B. Rearranging the equilibrium expression gives lactate/pyruvate = K′ × NADH/NAD⁺. This assumes near-equilibrium and comparable activities, not every whole-cell condition.

Question 15. A 1 cm assay shows a 0.0622 absorbance fall at 340 nm in 3 mL, ε = 6220 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Find NADH oxidised.

A. 10 nmol; B. 30 nmol; C. 100 nmol; D. 300 nmol

Answer and reasoning

B. Δc = 0.0622/6220 = 10⁻⁵ M = 10 μM; 10 μmol/L × 0.003 L = 0.03 μmol = 30 nmol.

Question 16. A cell consumes 4 glucose residues from pre-existing glycogen as G1P and 3 free glucose, converting all to lactate. Find net ATP.

A. 14; B. 18; C. 21; D. 28

Answer and reasoning

B. G1P entry saves the hexokinase ATP, yielding 4 × 3 = 12. Free glucose yields 3 × 2 = 6. Total 18 ATP; glycogen synthesis cost excluded.
