1. Why an organelle for processing is necessary

Imagine an enzyme that has just been synthesized in a pancreatic acinar cell. The ribosome has assembled its amino acids in the correct order, and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) has provided an environment in which the polypeptide can fold. Yet the cell has not completed its task. The enzyme must leave the ER, acquire any necessary carbohydrate modifications, be separated from proteins intended for other destinations and, in this particular cell, reach a secretory granule. The granule must remain inside the cell until an appropriate physiological signal triggers its release. Accurate synthesis alone is therefore insufficient for accurate delivery.

Now consider an acid hydrolase that will digest macromolecules within a lysosome. It also begins its secretory-pathway journey in the rough ER, but releasing most of it into the extracellular space would be wasteful. A membrane receptor presents a third problem. Its ligand-binding surface must end up on the extracellular side of the plasma membrane while its signaling domain remains cytosolic. These three proteins begin in the same general manufacturing system, yet their mature structures and destinations differ. What cellular arrangement can solve all three problems without mixing incompatible products?

The Golgi apparatus provides a physically organized sequence of chemical environments followed by a sorting system. Its membranes do not contain ribosomes for synthesizing polypeptides. Instead, the organelle receives cargo from the ER and uses resident enzymes to modify that cargo. It then directs many of the processed molecules toward other compartments. The Golgi can synthesize certain carbohydrate and lipid structures, so describing it as only a packaging organelle is equally misleading. It is better understood as a dynamic organelle that coordinates chemical maturation, membrane composition and destination selection.

To follow this chapter, recall three properties of membranes. A lipid bilayer separates two aqueous spaces; membrane proteins have an orientation, with distinct regions exposed to each side; and transport by budding and fusion preserves this orientation. These facts explain why the carbohydrate chains added to a plasma membrane receptor in the Golgi lumen eventually face the extracellular fluid. They also explain why soluble hydrolases can remain enclosed throughout a journey from the ER to a lysosome, without passing directly through a hydrophobic bilayer.

Two distinctions will guide the rest of the chapter. First, a cargo molecule travels through the pathway, whereas a resident enzyme performs work repeatedly in a particular compartment. Second, a change in the chemical structure of a cargo is not the same as a decision about its destination. Some modifications influence sorting, but many are needed for stability, molecular recognition or biological activity. By keeping these distinctions clear, the structure of the Golgi can be understood as a consequence of its function rather than as a picture to memorize.

2. The architecture and polarity of the Golgi stack

Camillo Golgi described an internal reticular structure in nerve cells in 1898 using silver-based staining. Because metallic stains reveal only selected material, early light-microscopic observations did not immediately settle whether the structure was a genuine organelle. Later electron microscopy established the presence of organized membrane-bounded compartments. A structure that appeared as a dark network at one resolution was revealed as a set of flattened membrane sacs, with associated tubules and vesicles, at another.

Each flattened sac is called a cisterna (plural cisternae). Its bounding membrane is one lipid bilayer, folded around an internal aqueous lumen. In a thin electron-microscope section the two opposing sides of a flattened cisterna can look like parallel lines. They are not two separate envelopes analogous to the inner and outer membranes of a mitochondrion. A single cisterna encloses one continuous lumen, while adjacent cisternae ordinarily have distinct lumens. Narrow intercisternal spaces contain cytosol.

A typical stack has several flattened cisternae, often expanded at their rims. Many carriers bud from or fuse near these regions. Their membrane curvature permits transitions between sheets, tubules and transport carriers. A stack is sometimes called a dictyosome, particularly in descriptions of plant cells. In many mammalian cells, numerous stacks are connected into a perinuclear Golgi ribbon near the microtubule-organizing center. In plants, individual Golgi stacks are commonly dispersed and motile. Budding yeast has unstacked Golgi cisternae, demonstrating that the fundamental ability to process cargo does not require a conspicuous stack. These organizational differences are important exceptions to any rigid textbook image.

The key architectural feature is polarity. The cis face is the receiving side associated functionally with incoming ER-derived traffic. The medial cisternae occupy an intermediate position, and the trans face lies toward the exit side. The cis-Golgi network (CGN) is an early tubular and vesicular region associated with cargo reception and retrieval. The trans-Golgi network (TGN) is a late sorting region associated with the trans side. The TGN should not simply be equated with the entire trans cisterna: although connected in functional progression, the network specializes in forming outgoing routes and receiving selected endosomal traffic. The precise structural boundaries vary by cell type.

Textbook diagrams commonly depict the cis face as convex and the trans face as concave. This is a useful representation of some stacks, not the definition of their polarity. A rotated or differently curved stack still has a receiving and a delivering side. When interpreting an unfamiliar micrograph, identify the relationship with ER exit regions, secretory carriers and compartment-specific enzyme markers rather than rely only on the direction of curvature. Similarly, a cell containing numerous small stacks need not have multiple different kinds of Golgi apparatus: each can carry out a coordinated set of early and late functions.

3. How cargo enters from the endoplasmic reticulum

The preceding ER chapter establishes the starting conditions for this organelle. Many soluble secretory proteins and integral membrane proteins enter the ER during synthesis. Folding, disulfide bond formation and early glycosylation occur in this compartment. Proteins that have failed to reach a suitable conformation are usually retained for further folding or selected for degradation rather than efficiently exported. Thus a protein arriving at the Golgi has already passed an important set of ER-dependent controls. The Golgi does not substitute for those controls, even though abnormal cargo can also be recognized at later stages.

Specialized ER exit sites generate carriers through the COPII machinery. A soluble protein may be captured by a membrane cargo receptor, or enter a carrier by bulk flow; transmembrane cargo can contain cytosolic export information. The carriers converge at early secretory intermediates. In mammalian cells the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) is a useful way of describing transport and sorting between ER and cis Golgi, whereas equivalent early traffic need not form a similarly conspicuous compartment in every organism. At the early Golgi, fusion of incoming material contributes to the formation and maintenance of cisternae.

Transport is selective but not perfectly one-way. An ER luminal protein such as BiP, carrying a C-terminal KDEL retrieval signal in mammals, can occasionally escape from the ER. If it reaches an early Golgi compartment, a KDEL receptor recognizes the signal in the more acidic environment, participates in retrograde transport and releases its ligand on return to the ER. The receptor can subsequently cycle again. Many ER membrane residents contain different retrieval information, for example a cytosolic dilysine motif. The presence of a retrieval pathway means that finding a small fraction of a resident ER protein outside the ER does not by itself establish a defect.

The Golgi must receive more than soluble cargo. Incoming carriers add membrane lipids, transmembrane proteins and transport machinery. As cargo moves onward, some of those components must return. Otherwise the ER would progressively lose membrane and the Golgi would accumulate proteins that prevent its proper operation. The COPI-dependent retrieval pathway introduced in 2.A.10 helps maintain this balance. Here the essential organellar consequence is that the ER and Golgi retain distinct molecular identities despite exchanging material.

Consider a glycosylated membrane receptor as an example. A luminal carbohydrate modification added in the ER remains luminal when an ER carrier buds, fuses with early secretory intermediates and joins a cis Golgi compartment. Its cytosolic tail remains outside the carrier lumen. The Golgi can therefore recognize the receptor in two ways: enzymes on the luminal side may modify the extracellular domain of the future receptor, while sorting machinery on the cytosolic side may read its tail. This simultaneous access to opposite domains is a major functional advantage of preserving membrane topology.

4. Evidence for the ordered secretory pathway

A static image reveals where a Golgi stack is located, but it cannot tell us whether a protein passes through it or whether the protein was synthesized there. A classic solution is to mark only a short cohort of newly synthesized molecules and observe their subsequent movement. This is the reasoning behind pulse–chase experiments. During the pulse, cells receive a labeled amino acid that becomes incorporated into newly made polypeptides. During the chase, a large excess of unlabeled amino acid minimizes further incorporation of the label. The previously labeled cohort can then be followed while other proteins continue to be synthesized.

In the 1960s, Caro, Jamieson and Palade examined pancreatic exocrine cells using radioactive amino acids, autoradiography and subcellular fractionation. Pancreatic acinar cells were particularly useful because they synthesize and export abundant digestive enzymes. In their classical studies, recently synthesized material was found first in rough ER, then in peripheral Golgi elements and later in condensing vacuoles and zymogen granules. The important result was not simply that all of these structures contained secretory protein. It was that label appeared in them in an ordered time sequence. Jamieson and Palade's 1967 studies supported a directional transport pathway linking synthesis in rough ER to packaging and subsequent secretion.

Autoradiography uses radiation-sensitive material to reveal the approximate position of radioactive molecules in a section. A silver grain marks a region where radioactive decay occurred, but it does not precisely identify a single individual protein molecule. Fractionation offers complementary evidence by separating compartments according to physical properties and measuring label in recovered fractions. Neither method is perfect: microsomal fractions can be contaminated, recovery can vary and apparent signal over a small organelle may include nearby structures. Using different methods makes the conclusion more robust.

A further experiment addressed a deeper question. Must continued protein synthesis be occurring for an already synthesized cohort to move? If translation is inhibited after a brief labeling pulse, movement of that existing cohort can still take place over a suitable observation interval. Jamieson and Palade examined this separation of transport from synthesis in 1968. The result distinguishes formation of cargo from its later delivery, not that secretion can operate indefinitely without new proteins. Transport depends on an existing functional membrane system, nucleotide metabolism and cellular energy.

Suppose a mutation prevents exit from the ER. Early label will still appear in the ER but the Golgi maximum should be reduced or absent. If the mutation blocks exit from the TGN instead, the ER may clear at approximately the normal initial rate, yet label should accumulate in late Golgi compartments. To make this distinction, one needs time-resolved measurements or separate early and late Golgi markers. Simply measuring how much protein reaches the culture medium cannot identify which step has failed. This logic will be useful when we analyze glycosylation and sorting defects later.

5. How cargo crosses the stack: two models and their evidence

Once the directional pathway was established, another problem became apparent. A cisterna receives cargo at the early side, but how does material encounter enzymes characteristic of the next cisterna? The membrane boundary that creates separate chemical compartments also prevents simple free diffusion from one lumen to the next. Two classical models offer different solutions. Their difference is not whether traffic moves from early to late; both agree that it does. The difference concerns which part of the system changes location or molecular identity.

In the stable-compartment or vesicular transport model, each cisterna maintains its identity over time. Cargo leaves one cisterna in a forward transport carrier, crosses the cytosolic gap and enters the next cisterna by fusion. The enzymes that define cis, medial and trans regions are preferentially retained in their compartments or recovered if they escape. A small secretory protein can readily be imagined fitting within such a carrier. If this model were the only mechanism, however, extremely large luminal cargo would have to be moved by suitably large carriers or a different transfer process.

The cisternal maturation model reverses the perspective. New early cisternae arise near the receiving side of the Golgi, and a cisterna containing cargo changes from early to medial to late composition. Cargo can remain within the same lumen while resident enzymes are progressively replaced. Retrieval of Golgi enzymes toward younger compartments permits reuse of early-processing machinery. The older cisterna ultimately generates outgoing carriers or contributes to late compartments. The critical step is a change of compartment identity, not simply the physical translation of a rigid disk through space.

Large procollagen assemblies provide a useful physical argument for maturation. Their dimensions can exceed those of conventional small transport vesicles, and experimental observations in mammalian cells have found large cargo within cisternae at successive Golgi stages. This is consistent with cargo traveling inside maturing cisternae. It is not a proof that small cargo never uses carriers or that all Golgi traffic follows exactly one mechanism. Unusually large cargo can motivate alternative hypotheses; direct observations are needed to distinguish them.

In 2006, independent studies by Losev and colleagues and Matsuura-Tokita and colleagues used the dispersed cisternae of budding yeast to track resident proteins labeled with different fluorescent markers. Individual cisternae that initially displayed early markers later acquired late markers while losing early ones. A permanent early compartment that only passes cargo forward would not be expected to show this systematic conversion. The observations directly supported maturation in that system. Because yeast cisternae are not tightly superimposed in a compact stack, following individual compartments was particularly informative.

Modern teaching should avoid framing the two models as mutually exclusive universal rules. Cisternal maturation provides an important framework for explaining cargo progression and resident enzyme recycling. Vesicles, tubules and other intercisternal connections can exchange selected material as well. The relative contributions can depend on cargo and organism. At NET level, distinguish the predictions of the idealized models, recognize the evidence for maturation, and remember that retrograde recycling is necessary for stable biochemical organization regardless of the appearance of the Golgi.

6. How resident enzymes and compartment identity are maintained

Different Golgi regions must expose cargo to different enzyme populations for processing to occur in the proper sequence. If all glycosidases and glycosyltransferases became uniformly mixed in every compartment, a substrate could encounter competing reactions in an unpredictable order. The same protein made at the same rate might then leave with a very different set of carbohydrate chains. Polarity is therefore biochemical, not merely geometric.

Many Golgi enzymes are integral membrane proteins whose catalytic domains face the lumen. Their transmembrane segments, cytosolic regions, association with other proteins and interactions with the retrieval system contribute to their localization. A useful physical principle is that Golgi membranes and later plasma membrane domains have different lipid compositions and average membrane thicknesses. In some cases, a short transmembrane region helps favor residence in earlier membranes rather than partition into cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich outgoing regions. This principle is not a universal address code. An enzyme's localization can involve several cooperating features, and changing the length of its membrane anchor does not necessarily relocate all Golgi proteins in the same way.

The concept of residence is dynamic. Suppose that a cisterna matures and loses an early enzyme by packaging it into retrograde carriers. When those carriers fuse with younger compartments, the enzyme again encounters early substrates. The enzyme can thus perform many rounds of catalysis without traveling all the way to the cell surface. If the retrieval mechanism is weakened, early enzymes may become depleted from their usual locations and appear too late in the pathway. Cargo can then be underprocessed even if the total amount of enzyme inside the cell remains unchanged. A measurement of protein abundance is not a measurement of enzyme distribution.

The lumen also changes in physicochemical properties along the pathway. Golgi compartments tend to become more acidic toward the trans side, although actual pH values vary among cells and compartments. V-type proton pumps move protons into the lumen; proton leaks, counterion movements and other membrane transport processes help set the steady-state pH. A membrane pump is thus part of the conditions that permit luminal enzymes and receptors to work correctly. An enzyme with an optimal pH different from its surroundings may act inefficiently even when present in its normal location.

To appreciate the scale, compare hypothetical luminal pH values of 6.7 and 6.0. Since pH equals the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen ion activity, the proton activity ratio is 10^(6.7 − 6.0), approximately 5.0. The difference is substantial even though both compartments are only mildly acidic. It does not follow that the later lumen contains exactly five times as many total protons, because compartment volume and buffering capacity matter. Free proton activity is the quantity indicated by pH.

Golgi organization also depends on structural proteins and the cytoskeleton. Golgins are elongated proteins associated with Golgi membranes that participate in tethering and organization; stacking proteins contribute to the arrangement of cisternae in many cells. Microtubules and their motors help organize the perinuclear ribbon in many animal cells, whereas actin-based motility is important for plant Golgi dynamics. Dispersing the mammalian ribbon by disrupting microtubules does not necessarily destroy every individual processing compartment. Spatial organization, cisternal identity and catalytic capacity are related but experimentally separable properties.

7. The biochemical logic of glycosylation

Many secretory and cell-surface proteins carry carbohydrates covalently attached to their polypeptide chains. Such proteins are called glycoproteins. Carbohydrate attachment can influence molecular folding, resistance to proteolysis, recognition by lectins, receptor interactions and the hydrated protective layer at the cell surface. These effects depend on the position, composition and architecture of the carbohydrate chain. A glycan is not merely an extra mass placed on a protein.

Unlike a DNA or protein sequence, the exact sequence of a glycan is not copied directly from a nucleic acid template. The genome specifies the enzymes, their localization and the pathways that supply activated sugar donors. The product emerges from a sequence of enzyme-catalyzed reactions acting on the accessible sites of a protein. Because different tissues express different combinations of enzymes and because some sites are physically shielded, molecules with the same amino acid sequence can carry different carbohydrate forms. These are often described as glycoforms. Their existence is an expected consequence of the chemistry rather than evidence that the protein sequence has changed.

Two broad kinds of enzymatic activity cooperate. Glycosidases remove particular sugars or cleave specified glycosidic linkages. Glycosyltransferases transfer sugars from activated donors onto acceptor molecules, with specificity for the acceptor and the linkage formed. Trimming can expose a site required by the next transferase; adding a branch can create new acceptors for later reactions. The product of one stage thus becomes the substrate of the next. A block in an early reaction may prevent a chain of later changes, even when the late enzymes themselves are fully active.

Most activated sugar donors used by Golgi glycosyltransferases, such as UDP-galactose and UDP-N-acetylglucosamine, are made outside the Golgi lumen and require specific nucleotide-sugar transporters to reach it. CMP-sialic acid is a notable donor synthesized in the nucleus in vertebrates before reaching the cytosolic pool and being transported into the Golgi lumen. The membrane separates catalytic reactions from the cytosol but does not cut them off from cellular metabolism. Donor transporters and luminal nucleotide metabolism sustain the availability of substrates. If a nucleotide-sugar transporter is defective, glycosyltransferases may remain catalytically normal in an isolated assay with added donor while performing poorly in intact cells.

A cell must also coordinate reaction rate with the time a cargo spends in the relevant compartment. An accessible substrate that remains long enough near an enzyme is more likely to be processed than a protected substrate or one that passes through very rapidly. Enzyme abundance, lumen pH, donor availability and residence time therefore interact. The familiar cis-to-trans order describes an organizational pattern; it does not guarantee that every protein undergoes every reaction or achieves identical final glycosylation.

8. N-linked glycan processing: from ER precursor to Golgi products

N-linked glycosylation is named for attachment of the carbohydrate chain to the amide nitrogen of an asparagine side chain. Many such sites occur in the sequence Asn–X–Ser/Thr, where X is generally not proline. The presence of this sequence is not sufficient to guarantee occupancy; accessibility and local context also influence modification. In the ER, a preassembled oligosaccharide containing glucose, mannose and N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) is transferred from a dolichol-linked precursor to a suitable asparagine. Glucose trimming and folding-related reactions begin in the ER. The detailed enzymology of this initial attachment belongs to protein processing, but its outcome is essential here: the Golgi receives a protein with a carbohydrate precursor already attached.

For many glycoproteins, early Golgi mannosidases remove selected mannose residues. The remaining structure can be acted upon by GlcNAc transferases and further mannosidases as it encounters medial processing enzymes. The sequence of additions and removals is important because enzymes recognize particular glycan configurations. Later reactions can extend branches, add galactose and, depending on the cell and substrate, add terminal sialic acid or fucose. Core fucosylation and other modifications can occur at different stages; it would be incorrect to classify every fucose addition as a late terminal reaction. The precise route depends on the organism, enzyme complement and accessibility of each glycosylation site.

Three major categories summarize mature N-glycan architectures. High-mannose glycans retain multiple mannose residues beyond the conserved core. Complex glycans have branches elaborated with sugars such as GlcNAc and galactose and may include sialic acid and fucose. Hybrid glycans combine features of high-mannose and complex structures on different branches. All share a characteristic core built from two GlcNAc and three mannose residues. The categories refer to molecular composition, not to the age of the protein. A high-mannose glycan can remain on a mature protein that has passed through the Golgi.

Why might one glycosylation site remain high-mannose while another on the same protein becomes complex? A folded protein may hide one branch from a processing enzyme while exposing another. In addition, cells differ in the enzymes they express and some cargo encounters a different set of compartments or spends less time in them. Treating the Golgi as an automatic machine that converts every chain to a single universal final form would overlook these constraints. High-mannose glycan retention must therefore not be used alone as proof that a protein is still located in the ER.

A simplified causal sequence helps organize the detail. Removal of particular mannose residues produces acceptors for branching reactions; a branching reaction supplies a substrate for further trimming and extension; and terminal modifications alter properties of the completed branch. Loss of an early mannosidase may therefore shift the distribution of glycan classes even if the abundance of later enzymes is unchanged. Loss of a terminal sialyltransferase may leave earlier trimming and branching intact but reduce sialylated products. These outcomes distinguish the location of a processing block from the location of a transport block.

An important biological effect of terminal sialic acids is the negative charge they confer on many animal cell-surface glycoconjugates. This can influence interactions with other molecules and the behavior of circulating glycoproteins. Removing a terminal sugar can expose a previously masked recognition feature, changing how a glycoprotein is cleared. The same polypeptide may therefore have different behavior in vivo without any change to its amino acid sequence. The Golgi is responsible for establishing many such chemical differences.

9. O-linked glycans and the extracellular protective layer

The best-known Golgi-initiated form of O-linked glycosylation in animal secretory proteins involves addition of N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) to the oxygen of serine or threonine side chains. In contrast with the initial N-linked reaction, this mucin-type O-linked pathway usually builds the carbohydrate by sequential addition of individual sugars rather than transfer of a large preassembled precursor. Additional transferases generate different core structures and extend or terminate their branches. There is no single universal O-glycosylation consensus sequence comparable to the common N-linked sequon.

Mucins illustrate why this pathway matters. A mucin polypeptide contains regions rich in serine and threonine, permitting dense O-glycosylation. The resulting hydrated carbohydrate chains occupy a large volume around the protein and help produce a slippery, protective mucus layer. They can also reduce access of proteases to the polypeptide. The cell does not need to synthesize an entirely different protein polymer to alter mucus properties: changing its repertoire of glycosyltransferases or availability of donors can alter the glycan coat of its existing mucins.

Other O-linked glycan types exist, including modifications initiated in the ER, and intracellular O-GlcNAc modification commonly occurs in the nucleus and cytosol rather than through Golgi secretory processing. The statement that all O-linked glycosylation happens in the Golgi is thus false. For examination questions that compare N-linked with O-linked pathways, identify the class being discussed. The standard contrast is between ER-initiated N-glycan assembly and Golgi-initiated mucin-type O-glycan assembly.

Cell-surface glycoproteins and glycolipids collectively contribute to the glycocalyx, the carbohydrate-rich environment external to the plasma membrane. A carbohydrate chain located in the Golgi lumen has access to luminal glycosyltransferases and later faces outside the cell when a carrier fuses with the plasma membrane. Carbohydrate addition to cytosolic protein domains would not produce this orientation. The same principle explains the lumen-facing carbohydrate domains of internal membrane proteins. In all these cases, topology establishes a direct relation between where processing occurs and where the finished product functions.

10. Beyond glycoproteins: Golgi synthesis and maturation of other products

The Golgi receives membrane lipids as well as proteins. The ER is a major location for synthesis of membrane phospholipids and ceramide. Ceramide delivered to Golgi membranes can be converted into sphingomyelin and a range of glycosphingolipids. Sphingomyelin has a phosphocholine-containing polar head group, whereas glycosphingolipids carry carbohydrate head groups. Their formation changes the chemical properties of membranes, particularly those of the late secretory pathway and the plasma membrane.

Some lipid modifications present an additional topological problem. In animal cells, glucosylceramide synthesis begins on the cytosolic side of Golgi membranes; further elaboration into many complex glycosphingolipids involves enzymes acting on the luminal side. The lipid precursor must therefore be redistributed to the appropriate leaflet or membrane region. The precise trafficking routes are beyond the scope of this chapter, but the basic lesson is important: formation of a chemical intermediate is not sufficient if the next enzyme lies on the opposite side of a membrane. Sphingomyelin synthesis in the Golgi generally involves the luminal-facing catalytic site of sphingomyelin synthase, and the newly made lipid can later become enriched on the external leaflet of the plasma membrane.

Sphingolipids and cholesterol influence membrane order and the thickness of later secretory membranes. Their abundance can affect how membrane proteins partition into transport domains. A protein may be sorted not just by a short sequence in its cytosolic tail but also by its ability to associate with a particular lipid environment. This explains why changes in lipid metabolism can alter protein trafficking. It does not mean that all proteins carrying a given lipid anchor follow one universal route.

Proteoglycans represent another important class of Golgi products. Their core proteins are made in the ER, while long glycosaminoglycan chains are elaborated through the secretory pathway, largely in the Golgi for examples such as heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate. These chains may be sulfated using an activated sulfate donor, contributing negative charge and water-binding properties. Proteoglycans are important components of extracellular matrices and cell-surface signaling environments. They differ from ordinary glycoproteins because their glycosaminoglycan chains can dominate their physical properties. Hyaluronan is an important exception: it is synthesized at the plasma membrane in vertebrates rather than in the Golgi as a typical core-protein-linked glycosaminoglycan.

Golgi compartments can also carry out selected phosphorylation and sulfation reactions on luminal cargo. Tyrosine sulfation is one example of a late secretory-pathway modification. Controlled proteolytic cleavage of precursor proteins is another: selected proprotein convertases act in late Golgi, endosomal or secretory compartments, depending on the precursor and cell type. For insulin, much of the final conversion of proinsulin to insulin and C-peptide occurs in immature and maturing secretory granules rather than simply inside a Golgi cisterna. The Golgi participates in getting proinsulin to the correct granule; the final maturation of the hormone illustrates how a chemical task can continue beyond the organelle.

Plant cells provide an especially clear example of Golgi biosynthetic function. Pectins and many hemicelluloses are synthesized or assembled in Golgi cisternae, then exported to the cell surface where they contribute to the matrix of the cell wall. Cellulose, in contrast, is synthesized predominantly by cellulose synthase complexes at the plasma membrane. The Golgi is involved in producing and delivering those complexes, but it is not the principal site of cellulose polymerization. The separation of matrix-polysaccharide synthesis from cellulose assembly explains why blocking Golgi function affects wall construction without requiring that every cell-wall polymer be made in the Golgi.

11. Trans-Golgi network: one input, multiple destinations

By the time cargo reaches late Golgi regions, it has encountered a succession of enzymes. The next challenge is deciding which membrane carrier should receive it. The TGN is especially suited for this task because it is a network of tubules and membrane domains that can recruit different sorting and carrier-forming machinery. A soluble protein enclosed in a TGN lumen cannot directly contact cytosolic adaptors. A transmembrane sorting receptor can bridge that gap: its luminal domain binds the soluble cargo and its cytosolic domain interacts with the machinery that selects membrane and forms a carrier.

There are several major destinations. Secretory proteins may be released outside the cell, and membrane proteins and lipids may be delivered to the plasma membrane. In specialized cells, selected luminal proteins are concentrated in secretory granules and released only in response to a signal. Newly synthesized lysosomal hydrolases are directed toward endosomal compartments, where they subsequently participate in lysosomal function. Golgi residents and cycling receptors must also be retained or returned. These are separate sorting outcomes, not necessarily four identical vesicles budding from a common point.

Constitutive secretion is the continual delivery of selected cargo to the plasma membrane or extracellular space under ordinary conditions. It replenishes membrane and allows cells to release extracellular matrix proteins and other products without waiting for a special acute stimulus. In many textbook examples, soluble cargo that lacks specific retention or diversion signals enters constitutive transport. However, constitutive does not mean indiscriminate: polarized cells may actively sort constitutively delivered proteins, and cargo can be selectively enriched or excluded.

Regulated secretion requires the formation of storage compartments, typically secretory granules, in specialized cells. At or beyond the TGN, the appropriate cargo becomes concentrated, sometimes aided by aggregation in an environment with suitable pH and calcium. Immature granules can continue to mature by changing their contents, processing precursor proteins and removing incorrectly included material. Mature granules remain available for rapid fusion in response to a signal, commonly a rise in cytosolic calcium. The defining feature is the separation in time between synthesis and release, not a universal signal sequence shared by every regulated cargo.

An acinar cell provides a concrete reason for this separation. Digestive enzymes must be available rapidly after a meal-related signal, and synthesizing them all from scratch after the stimulus would be inefficient. Storage of preformed enzyme-containing granules permits a rapid response. Endocrine cells use a similar organizational principle for peptide hormones. In contrast, fibroblasts engaged in continuous extracellular matrix deposition rely heavily on constitutive delivery.

A polarized epithelial cell has an additional sorting problem. Its apical membrane faces a lumen or external compartment, while its basolateral membrane contacts neighboring cells and underlying tissue. These domains contain different transporters and receptors. Tight junctions help maintain their separation, but separate delivery and recycling pathways are also required. TGN and endosomal sorting can route proteins to particular domains using cytosolic motifs, carbohydrate-dependent recognition or lipid association. No single rule applies to every apical or basolateral protein, and the initial destination can sometimes be corrected by transcytosis. The important consequence is that a protein may be synthesized and glycosylated normally yet appear on the wrong surface and fail to support epithelial physiology.

These pathways use vesicular and tubular transport machinery described in 2.A.10. At the organelle level, the crucial distinction is between selecting a cargo's route, physically forming the carrier and finally fusing with the target. A failure in any of these steps can reduce correct delivery, but the pattern of affected molecules differs. The next section focuses on the lysosomal route because its carbohydrate-based sorting signal provides one of the clearest experimental links between Golgi processing and destination selection.

12. Mannose-6-phosphate: a chemical address for lysosomal hydrolases

Lysosomes contain enzymes that hydrolyze proteins, polysaccharides, nucleic acids and lipids. Most soluble lysosomal acid hydrolases in mammalian cells are made in the ER and pass through the Golgi. If the TGN treated them as ordinary secretory proteins, many would be released to the extracellular space. The sorting system must distinguish these enzymes from other glycoproteins with the same general N-linked glycan precursor. The difference is a specialized modification of selected mannose residues: mannose-6-phosphate, abbreviated M6P.

The first event occurs in early Golgi compartments. A GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase recognizes structural features of the folded lysosomal enzyme, often described as a signal patch. Recognition is not based merely on the presence of a mannose-containing N-glycan, since many proteins possess such glycans. The transferase uses UDP-GlcNAc to transfer a GlcNAc-1-phosphate group onto the 6-hydroxyl group of an appropriate mannose residue. The phosphate is initially covered by GlcNAc in a phosphodiester arrangement.

A second enzyme, commonly called the uncovering enzyme, removes the outer GlcNAc, revealing a phosphate on mannose at carbon 6. This creates a recognizable ligand for M6P receptors. The sequence is worth understanding rather than memorizing as a single phosphorylation arrow: one enzyme recognizes the hydrolase and installs a covered modification; another exposes the recognition signal. A defect in either step can reduce the efficiency with which hydrolases acquire the address, although their effects on different receptor forms and cargo can differ.

At the TGN, membrane-bound M6P receptors bind the phosphorylated carbohydrate using their luminal domains. Their cytosolic tails interact with adaptors that help recruit clathrin-associated sorting machinery. Budding and fusion deliver the receptor-bound enzymes into the endosomal system. As the compartment acidifies, conditions favor dissociation of many of the enzymes from their receptors. The enzymes proceed toward lysosomes, whereas receptors are recycled to the TGN or cycle through other compartments for further rounds of transport. Release from the receptor is essential because a receptor that remained permanently attached to its cargo could not sustain repeated sorting.

Mammalian cells contain two major M6P receptors, a cation-dependent receptor and a cation-independent receptor. The latter can also function at the cell surface, where it can recapture some extracellular M6P-bearing hydrolases. Their overlapping but nonidentical functions help explain why loss of a single receptor need not eliminate all lysosomal enzyme targeting. The terminology describes requirements and properties of receptor binding; it does not imply that one receptor carries a positive charge while the other has no charge.

M6P is a targeting signal rather than an activation switch that makes every lysosomal enzyme catalytic immediately. Acid hydrolase activity depends on enzyme structure, maturation and the chemical environment. Some lysosomal enzymes are proteolytically processed later, and many act best at acidic pH. Correct sorting brings them to the appropriate system but does not replace these additional requirements. In the next node, lysosomal acidity will be examined as a mechanism for intracellular digestion.

There are important limits to the M6P rule. Lysosomal membrane proteins generally use cytosolic sorting motifs rather than an M6P carbohydrate tag. Certain soluble hydrolases, including glucocerebrosidase transported by the membrane protein LIMP-2, can use M6P-independent pathways. Plant vacuolar hydrolases have other targeting signals and receptors. Therefore, M6P should be understood as a major mammalian route for many soluble lysosomal enzymes, not as the universal signal for every hydrolytic or vacuolar protein in all eukaryotes.

13. What a sorting defect reveals: I-cell disease

A powerful way to test whether a targeting label matters is to examine cells that cannot make it. Mucolipidosis II, traditionally called I-cell disease, is a severe inherited disorder caused by deficiency of GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase, typically involving GNPTAB. Affected cells have impaired formation of M6P on numerous lysosomal hydrolases. Because many enzymes fail to receive an efficient lysosomal address, a substantial fraction is routed toward secretion instead. The extracellular activity of several hydrolases can be abnormally high even though their activity inside lysosomes is deficient.

The consequences follow from mass balance. Macromolecules enter lysosomal compartments from endocytosis and cellular turnover. When too few hydrolytic enzymes arrive, breakdown fails to keep pace with delivery. Undigested material accumulates, producing characteristic inclusions within cells. These inclusions explain the historical name. The organelle has a membrane and may receive cargo, yet its digestive function is compromised because the appropriate enzyme complement is missing.

This phenotype distinguishes the upstream labeling defect from a mutation that abolishes catalysis by one particular lysosomal hydrolase. In the latter case, the enzyme may reach the lysosome normally but fail to cleave its substrate. In I-cell disease, many enzymes can retain catalytic activity when assayed under suitable conditions, yet be distributed incorrectly. The finding of high enzyme activity in extracellular fluid together with low activity inside patient cells led to the concept that lysosomal enzymes possess a recognition marker required for targeting.

Another informative experiment uses uptake of extracellular hydrolases. Normal M6P-bearing enzymes added to cells can be captured by appropriate surface receptors and delivered into endosomal and lysosomal compartments. Adding free M6P at sufficient concentration can compete for receptor recognition and reduce receptor-mediated uptake. A corresponding unrelated sugar is a useful control. Such experiments distinguish a carbohydrate-dependent receptor mechanism from simple uptake proportional to protein concentration. Successful uptake requires functioning receptors and downstream delivery; competition alone does not prove that the hydrolase reaches a fully functional lysosome.

Not every tissue shows the same distribution of hydrolases in a patient, in part because alternative pathways can contribute to lysosomal targeting. A hydrolase that does not depend strongly on the M6P route may be relatively spared. Therefore, a statement that all lysosomal proteins are secreted in I-cell disease is too strong. The disease illustrates the organelle-specific importance of chemical recognition while also exposing the diversity of targeting mechanisms.

A general lesson follows. Whenever an enzyme appears to be absent from its working compartment, ask whether it was never synthesized, was synthesized but cannot fold, was folded but cannot be marked, was marked but cannot be recognized, or reached its destination but cannot function there. Distinguishing these alternatives requires evidence from multiple locations and from assays of both chemical modification and catalytic activity. This kind of reasoning is more useful for CSIR-NET than recalling the name of a single disorder in isolation.

14. Reading glycan states and perturbations as evidence

Because the Golgi modifies carbohydrate chains, the chemistry of an isolated glycoprotein can serve as a record of its prior exposure to processing enzymes. Endoglycosidase H (Endo H) is commonly used to distinguish sensitive high-mannose and many hybrid N-glycans from resistant complex glycans. In a standard assay, protein samples taken at different chase times are treated with Endo H and then separated by electrophoresis. Removal of a susceptible carbohydrate portion reduces the apparent molecular mass of the glycoprotein. A newly synthesized glycoprotein may therefore shift to a faster-migrating band after digestion, while a later processed form no longer shifts.

Resistance normally indicates that a suitable glycan encountered reactions characteristic of medial Golgi processing or later. It does not specify the exact current location of the protein. A receptor on the plasma membrane may retain the resistance it acquired in the Golgi; a protein can acquire resistance even in a perturbed cell if Golgi enzymes relocate to the ER. Conversely, a protected N-glycan site may remain sensitive even on a protein that reaches the surface. The most reliable experiment combines glycan analysis with independent localization or functional evidence.

PNGase F provides a useful comparison because it removes most mammalian N-linked glycans, including complex types, from appropriate substrates. It can help determine whether a mass shift reflects N-glycan attachment. It does not normally remove mucin-type O-linked glycans, and certain glycan structures, such as those bearing core alpha-1,3-fucose common in plants and some invertebrates, resist it. Endo H and PNGase F thus answer related but different questions. When a band fails to shift, consider substrate specificity, enzyme activity and sample preparation before assigning a cellular location.

Immunofluorescence gives another perspective. An antibody against GM130 can identify a cis-associated Golgi region in many mammalian cells, whereas TGN46 is commonly used as a marker of the trans-Golgi network. A labeled cargo that colocalizes first with an early marker and later with a TGN marker supports orderly progression. Optical resolution limits the interpretation: two signals within a diffraction-limited spot are not necessarily on the same molecular complex or even in the same cisterna. Electron microscopy or carefully chosen additional markers can help clarify uncertain cases.

Pharmacological perturbations provide tests of causality. Brefeldin A (BFA) interferes with susceptible ARF guanine nucleotide exchange factors, disrupting normal coat recruitment and membrane traffic. In many mammalian cells it induces extensive redistribution of cis and medial Golgi components into the ER. Some late network components behave differently. Classical experiments by Lippincott-Schwartz and colleagues and by Doms and colleagues showed that Golgi enzymes can reach the ER in treated cells. The result is evidence of membrane cycling and the dynamic nature of organelle identity, not an ordinary physiological merging of all ER and Golgi compartments.

Monensin, an ionophore that exchanges monovalent cations and protons, disrupts ion and pH regulation and can cause swelling of Golgi cisternae and defects in late processing or secretion. If a protein remains underprocessed after treatment, possibilities include impaired transport, an altered enzyme environment or both. Comparing treatment with a direct mutation of a specific glycosyltransferase can reveal whether the observed phenotype is broad or substrate-specific. Neither drug should be treated as a perfectly specific switch for one cisterna.

Temperature-sensitive secretory cargo provides an additional way to synchronize movement. A cargo that folds or exits the ER only under permissive conditions can be accumulated in the ER and released by a temperature shift. Imaging its subsequent localization while assaying glycan changes allows a researcher to compare transport timing with processing timing. Temperature itself can affect many cell functions, so appropriate controls at the same temperature are necessary. The experimental principle is the important point here, not the specialist procedure.

Together, these assays offer an approach to interpreting apparently contradictory data. A glycoprotein can be chemically mature yet remain trapped inside a TGN carrier. A glycoprotein can be chemically immature yet reach the surface when an enzyme or donor transporter is missing. A cell can even show chemical processing in the wrong compartment after an experimental redistribution of enzymes. A correct conclusion must therefore combine the location of the cargo, the state of its modifications and its ultimate biological destination.

15. Quantitative reasoning: residence time, flux and sorting efficiency

The Golgi is not usually taught through a large set of formal equations, but a few simple models help convert qualitative statements into testable predictions. Their value is in defining the quantities that are changing and making explicit what assumptions are required. The following examples use original hypothetical numbers, not universal cellular rates.

First consider an accessible glycan passing through a compartment containing a processing enzyme. If the enzyme is present at approximately constant concentration, donor is abundant and substrate is dilute relative to the relevant saturation scale, the disappearance of an unmodified substrate can be approximated as first order. Let U(t) be the number of unmodified molecules at time t, and k be an effective rate constant in min⁻¹. The rate equation is dU/dt = −kU. Separating variables gives dU/U = −k dt. Integrating from U(0) to U(t) yields ln[U(t)/U(0)] = −kt, hence U(t) = U(0)e−kt. The fraction processed by time t is 1 − e−kt, assuming conversion is irreversible and the only loss of unmodified substrate is that reaction.

For example, if k = 0.15 min⁻¹ and the cargo spends 10 min in the relevant compartment, kt = 1.5 and the predicted modified fraction is 1 − e−1.5 = 0.777, or 77.7%. If transit time is shortened to 4 min with the same enzyme activity, the fraction becomes 1 − e−0.6 = 0.451, or 45.1%. The cargo can therefore emerge from a normally equipped Golgi less completely modified simply because it spends less time near the enzyme. This model is not exact for an enzyme saturated with substrate, varying donor levels, sequential reactions or a mixture of residence times.

A second example concerns a sequence of two necessary reactions. If 80% of cargo molecules complete an early step and 75% of those successfully processed molecules complete a later step, the overall fraction completing both is 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.60. Starting with 1,000 cargo molecules, 800 undergo the first reaction and 600 complete both. There are 200 molecules that missed the first step and another 200 that completed the first but not the second. The conditional wording is essential: the later probability is defined among molecules that reached the required intermediate. It is not appropriate to multiply two unrelated marginal percentages without knowing their dependence.

Next consider a steady flux through an organelle. If J molecules per minute arrive and leave at the same average rate, and the mean residence time is τ minutes, the average number N in the organelle is Jτ. To see why, over a sufficiently long observation interval T, approximately JT molecules enter, and each contributes τ molecule-minutes on average. Dividing total molecule-minutes by T gives the average occupancy. If a cell receives 240 cargo molecules per minute and the average Golgi residence time is 12 minutes, approximately 2,880 cargo molecules are in the Golgi at a given moment under the stated steady-state conditions. If residence time doubles while throughput stays constant, the average occupancy doubles. A transient block that stops output altogether breaks the steady-state assumption, so this relation cannot be applied unchanged to the accumulating cargo.

A compartmental block can instead be understood with a conservation equation. Let N be the amount in a TGN compartment. If input J_in and output J_out are the only significant fluxes, then dN/dt = J_in − J_out. When input is 100 molecules per minute and output falls from 100 to 30 molecules per minute, the net accumulation is 70 molecules per minute. With constant rates over five minutes, the compartment gains 350 molecules. This is an initial short-time prediction; long blocks can change input, promote degradation, activate stress responses or saturate the compartment. The simple balance clarifies why accumulation provides evidence for a downstream problem only when upstream input is known.

Finally, consider sorting efficiency. A cohort of 500 hydrolase molecules enters the TGN. Suppose 90% receives a recognizable M6P tag, 80% of tagged molecules is captured and 90% of captured molecules reaches the appropriate endosomal destination. The predicted successful number is 500 × 0.90 × 0.80 × 0.90 = 324, or 64.8% of the original cohort. If the tagging efficiency is reduced to 20% while the other efficiencies remain unchanged, success becomes 72 molecules, or 14.4%. This model assumes the sequential percentages are conditional and ignores alternative routes. A measured change in extracellular activity alone could not tell which of the three efficiencies changed. Direct assays of tag, receptor interaction and destination are needed.

The models share a common theme. Enzymatic conversion depends on exposure and kinetics; distribution depends on input, output and residence; targeting depends on successive probabilities. These quantities are distinct, even though they interact in the living Golgi. A student who identifies the relevant variable before performing arithmetic is less likely to confuse the percentage of labeled molecules in a compartment with the rate at which they pass through it.

16. Golgi diversity, cell specialization and organelle dynamics

An organelle's abundance often reflects the work required of a cell. Pancreatic acinar cells possess abundant rough ER, prominent Golgi regions and numerous secretory granules. The rough ER supports high rates of polypeptide synthesis, while the Golgi supplies maturation and sorting capacity. The granules store products for regulated release. A large Golgi by itself does not imply that a cell synthesizes most of its protein there. It suggests that substantial processing and membrane traffic may be required, a hypothesis that can be examined by observing labeled cargo.

Goblet cells produce heavily glycosylated mucins and have an active Golgi associated with secretory product formation. Plasma cells secrete antibodies and possess a conspicuous secretory apparatus. Fibroblasts use the Golgi to modify and dispatch extracellular matrix molecules, including glycoproteins and proteoglycans. These different examples show that the same basic organelle performs different chemical tasks depending on the expressed enzymes, available substrates and required sorting routes.

Plant Golgi stacks also supply the growing cell wall. During cytokinesis, Golgi-derived secretory carriers and associated trafficking pathways contribute membrane and wall-building material to the developing cell plate. The phragmoplast helps guide delivery and assembly between the daughter nuclei. As the cell plate expands, it ultimately joins the existing plasma membrane and wall system. This provides a link between an organelle's biochemical output and a visible change in cellular architecture. Full regulation of cell division belongs to Subunit 2.D; the point here is that an active plant Golgi contributes both material and membrane to formation of a new partition.

The plant TGN often functions also as an early endosomal compartment, where secretory and endocytic traffic meet. Plant Golgi stacks and associated TGN structures can be mobile and can have spatial relationships different from the centralized mammalian Golgi ribbon. Thus a diagram of a mammalian cell should not be used as the only standard for identifying the plant secretory pathway. The direction of processing is conserved in principle, while the spatial arrangement and particular sorting receptors vary.

Golgi architecture changes as cells develop, alter secretion or divide. In many dividing mammalian cells the interphase Golgi ribbon fragments and its components are redistributed; reassembly follows mitosis. Golgi membranes are not destroyed in the sense that all their lipids and proteins disappear. Instead, organization is remodeled so that daughter cells can establish functional secretory systems. The ER remains an important source of newly synthesized Golgi components. Unlike mitochondria and plastids, the Golgi lacks its own genome and ribosomal protein synthesis system, and it is not explained by the standard endosymbiotic model for those organelles.

Mature mammalian erythrocytes provide an exception to the usual statement that eukaryotic cells contain a Golgi. During maturation they lose their nucleus and many organelles, including the ER and Golgi, and depend on preexisting cellular components. Prokaryotes do not possess a canonical eukaryotic Golgi apparatus, even though bacteria can synthesize and modify extracellular proteins and polysaccharides by different mechanisms. The existence of similar biochemical outcomes does not establish the existence of homologous organelles in every cell.

A comparison of ER and Golgi illustrates a recurring principle of cellular organization. The ER is a continuous network associated with initial synthesis and folding of secretory proteins, many lipids and calcium storage. The Golgi consists of functionally polarized processing compartments that receive and redistribute selected material. The TGN interfaces with endosomes and lysosomes, but Golgi cisternae are not lysosomes: their principal functions are maturation and sorting rather than bulk hydrolytic digestion. These differences explain why a defect in one organelle can alter the function of another even while both are morphologically present.

17. Diagnosing secretory defects by combining independent observations

Imagine three mutants of a cultured secretory cell that all release very little of a reporter into the medium. In the first mutant, the reporter remains in the ER and retains an early glycan pattern. This supports a defect in ER folding, export or fusion with early Golgi compartments; more precise assays are needed to choose among them. In the second mutant, the reporter reaches the TGN and acquires an Endo H-resistant glycan, but little appears outside. The block lies more plausibly in late sorting, carrier movement or fusion at the surface. In the third mutant, the reporter reaches the cell surface in almost normal quantity but its glycan structure is abnormal. This suggests a modification problem rather than a general inability to deliver cargo. If the extracellular assay measures activity rather than total protein, the apparent secretion defect in that third mutant could actually reflect altered biological activity.

The crucial experimental design principle is to separate cargo synthesis, cargo abundance, cargo location and cargo function. A total-protein immunoblot answers whether the protein is present. A digestion assay can indicate a class of glycan modification. Imaging identifies the compartments associated with the cargo. An activity assay tells whether a protein performs the expected reaction under the assay conditions. A secretion assay measures delivery into the medium and needs to distinguish actively released molecules from material leaking out of damaged cells. No single result proves the entire secretory mechanism.

Consider an experiment comparing two groups with normal total lysosomal hydrolase synthesis. Group X has little M6P tagging, abundant untagged hydrolase in the culture medium and reduced lysosomal activity. Group Y has normal M6P tagging but little receptor-mediated uptake and reduced lysosomal activity. Both groups have impaired targeting, yet the first points toward the labeling process and the second toward receptor function or its downstream traffic. A binding test with purified receptor may further distinguish failure of receptor recognition from failure of receptor recycling or carrier delivery.

Another distinction is between location and timing. A normal amount of protein in the Golgi at one time could represent normal passage through the compartment, a delayed cargo pool, or accelerated delivery balanced by accelerated exit. Measuring entry and exit across several time points resolves these possibilities. A time series should be evaluated against a control cohort labeled to a comparable extent, with comparable cell numbers and recovery. If the total label decreases, investigate degradation or release rather than renormalizing without considering the loss.

Finally, consider a test of the cisternal maturation model. Tag an early resident enzyme with one fluorescent marker and a late resident enzyme with another. Track individual cisternae rather than merely comparing their average number in a population. A systematic disappearance of early signal accompanied by appearance of late signal within the same tracked compartment supports maturation. Simply observing some green spots and some red spots at one instant does not distinguish maturation from permanent compartments. If the imaging interval is too long, two adjacent compartments may be mistaken for one moving object. Appropriate resolution and continuity of tracking are therefore part of the biological inference.

18. Integration: follow the protein, the enzyme and the membrane

The Golgi apparatus can now be understood using three simultaneous journeys. A cargo protein moves from the ER into early Golgi compartments, encounters different enzymes, and leaves through a selected route. A Golgi resident enzyme carries out repeated reactions and is retained or recovered as cisternae change. The membrane that carries both is itself being remodeled, recycled and selectively delivered. A correct model must explain all three journeys rather than only draw a single arrow from ER to cell surface.

The architecture supplies separate environments; polarity imposes an ordered sequence; enzyme distribution, donor supply and pH determine which reactions can occur; cisternal maturation and intercompartment exchange sustain progression; and the TGN connects processing to destination selection. These mechanisms provide a causal explanation for the distinctive structure that Golgi first observed. If architecture is disrupted, processing can become inaccurate. If processing fails, selected sorting signals may be lost. If sorting fails, organelles downstream can become deficient in proteins they need even though those proteins have been synthesized normally.

Several frequently confused statements now have clear corrections. Golgi cisternae are not sites of translation, but they do synthesize and modify important carbohydrate and lipid structures. N-linked glycosylation commonly begins in the ER, while mucin-type O-linked glycosylation begins mainly in the Golgi; neither generalization describes every possible modification of that linkage class. M6P directs many soluble mammalian hydrolases, but not all lysosomal proteins. Endo H resistance records a processing event rather than an exact current location. A cisterna can mature, and the Golgi can remain functionally ordered despite continual membrane exchange.

For examination reasoning, trace the earliest changed variable. A protein that never leaves the ER cannot normally receive late Golgi modifications. A protein that reaches the trans side without an expected sugar may have encountered an absent enzyme, unavailable donor, inappropriate pH or insufficient residence time. A correctly modified protein that fails to appear at its destination requires consideration of sorting and carrier fusion. A cell that secretes catalytically active lysosomal hydrolases but accumulates undigested material may have an upstream targeting defect. The different predictions allow one to interpret data instead of memorizing disconnected associations.

The next chapter turns to lysosomes. The Golgi has solved the problem of supplying digestive enzymes to the endosomal and lysosomal system; the next question is how that system produces a suitable acidic environment, confines hydrolytic reactions and returns useful breakdown products to the rest of the cell.

19. Part B — Conceptual and assertion–reason practice

Original single-correct questions. Attempt each before opening its explanation.

1. A thin-section micrograph shows four flattened sacs with separate lumens. What does each sac represent?

  1. A double-envelope organelle
  2. One cisterna enclosed by one lipid bilayer
  3. A ribosome-lined ER sheet
  4. A secretory granule with a solid lumen
Answer and reasoning

B. One cisterna enclosed by one lipid bilayer

Each cisterna has a single bounding bilayer folded around one lumen. Two opposing membrane profiles do not constitute two envelopes; lack of ribosomes and the flattened stack distinguish the other choices.

2. Which feature most reliably defines the cis face?

  1. Its curvature in every organism
  2. Its proximity to the plasma membrane
  3. Its functional association with ER-derived traffic
  4. Its exclusive synthesis of polysaccharides
Answer and reasoning

C. Its functional association with ER-derived traffic

Receiving function is the definition. Curvature and location vary, and polysaccharide assembly is not restricted to the cis face.

3. Which operation occurs principally at the rough ER rather than the Golgi?

  1. Initial polypeptide synthesis into the secretory pathway
  2. Terminal sialylation
  3. M6P receptor sorting
  4. Formation of many complex glycosphingolipids
Answer and reasoning

A. Initial polypeptide synthesis into the secretory pathway

Attached cytosolic ribosomes and ER translocation initiate the journey. The other events are associated with Golgi processing or sorting.

4. An individual yeast Golgi cisterna loses an early resident marker and gains a late marker. This most directly supports:

  1. An obligatory permanent cisterna model
  2. Cisternal maturation
  3. Protein translation in the TGN
  4. Destruction of all Golgi enzymes
Answer and reasoning

B. Cisternal maturation

The changing marker identity of the tracked compartment is the prediction of maturation. No translation or complete enzyme destruction is demonstrated.

5. Which statement about mature N-linked glycans is correct?

  1. Every mature N-glycan is complex
  2. All are transferred to serine
  3. A high-mannose glycan may persist after Golgi passage
  4. Every glycan has an identical terminal sugar
Answer and reasoning

C. A high-mannose glycan may persist after Golgi passage

Accessibility and enzyme expression vary. N-glycans attach to asparagine, and their terminal composition is variable.

6. What is the usual initial sugar of mammalian mucin-type O-glycosylation?

  1. GlcNAc on asparagine
  2. GalNAc on serine or threonine
  3. Mannose-6-phosphate on lysine
  4. Galactose on cytosolic DNA
Answer and reasoning

B. GalNAc on serine or threonine

The initiating GalNAc transferases modify hydroxyl oxygens on Ser/Thr. The first choice describes a different linkage context; the last two do not describe mucin-type glycosylation.

7. Which molecule recognizes the exposed M6P tag in the TGN?

  1. GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase
  2. A soluble acid hydrolase
  3. M6P receptor
  4. Ribosomal RNA
Answer and reasoning

C. M6P receptor

The phosphotransferase recognizes structural features of the hydrolase to establish the precursor tag; the membrane receptor recognizes the exposed carbohydrate tag and helps sort cargo.

8. A lysosomal membrane protein usually contains which kind of sorting information?

  1. An M6P tag on every domain
  2. A cytosolic sorting motif
  3. An ER ribosome in its lumen
  4. A chloroplast transit peptide
Answer and reasoning

B. A cytosolic sorting motif

Membrane-protein sorting often uses cytosolic motifs. M6P is a major route for soluble hydrolases, and the alternatives confuse different compartments.

9. Which material is principally synthesized at the plasma membrane rather than inside the plant Golgi?

  1. Pectin
  2. Many hemicelluloses
  3. Cellulose
  4. Many Golgi-derived matrix polysaccharides
Answer and reasoning

C. Cellulose

Cellulose synthase complexes operate mainly at the cell surface. The Golgi assembles pectins and many hemicelluloses.

10. Which is a defining property of regulated secretion?

  1. No cargo enters ER
  2. All secretion requires a new round of translation immediately before release
  3. Selected cargo is stored and released in response to a stimulus
  4. Only lipids enter its carriers
Answer and reasoning

C. Selected cargo is stored and released in response to a stimulus

Storage and stimulus-dependent release separate synthesis from secretion. Regulated cargo commonly includes peptide hormones and proteins entering the ER.

11. Assertion: Golgi glycosyltransferases often face the lumen. Reason: Glycan chains on secretory cargo are accessible on the non-cytosolic side.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. Assertion is true; reason is false.
  4. Assertion is false; reason is true.
Answer and reasoning

A. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.

Both statements are true and the reason explains the localization of the catalytic domains. Membrane topology brings enzyme and substrate into the same compartment.

12. Assertion: Endo H resistance proves a protein currently resides in the medial Golgi. Reason: Complex N-glycans are generally resistant to Endo H.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. Assertion is true; reason is false.
  4. Assertion is false; reason is true.
Answer and reasoning

D. Assertion is false; reason is true.

The assertion is false; a resistant protein may have moved onward, or acquired the modification elsewhere after perturbation. The reason is true.

13. Assertion: I-cell disease can increase extracellular activity of several acid hydrolases. Reason: Failure of efficient M6P labeling causes many hydrolases to be missorted toward secretion.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. Assertion is true; reason is false.
  4. Assertion is false; reason is true.
Answer and reasoning

A. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.

Both statements are true; the tagging defect accounts for redistribution. A failure of synthesis alone would not predict the same rise outside the cell.

14. Assertion: Every O-linked glycan begins in the Golgi. Reason: Mucin-type O-GalNAc glycosylation usually begins in the Golgi.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both are true but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. Assertion is true; reason is false.
  4. Assertion is false; reason is true.

The first is false because there are other O-linked pathways, including cytosolic O-GlcNAc and ER-initiated types. The reason correctly describes the mucin-type pathway.

20. Part C — Analytical and quantitative practice

Original examination-style problems. Numerical data are illustrative and do not represent measured universal values.

1. Pulse–chase: ER label is 80, 20, 5 units at 0, 20 and 50 min; Golgi label is 15, 65, 15; granule label is 5, 15, 80. Which inference is warranted?

  1. Granules synthesize the labeled polypeptide
  2. The cohort moves from ER toward Golgi and granules
  3. The Golgi uses only forward vesicles
  4. All molecules travel at the same rate
Answer and reasoning

B. The cohort moves from ER toward Golgi and granules

The peaks occur in the expected order. This is population-level transport evidence, not direct evidence of intra-Golgi mechanism or equal transit times.

2. A pulse-labeled reporter reaches TGN46-positive regions but secretion falls to 10% of control. It is Endo H resistant. What is most consistent?

  1. Defective initial ER translocation
  2. Failure of all glycosylation
  3. Defect in late delivery or fusion
  4. Absence of mRNA
Answer and reasoning

C. Defect in late delivery or fusion

The reporter exists and has undergone typical medial processing. Its late localization implicates downstream traffic; more experiments are needed to distinguish budding and fusion.

3. A drug redistributes cis/medial Golgi enzymes into the ER. An ER-trapped reporter becomes Endo H resistant. What follows?

  1. The reporter necessarily reached the TGN
  2. Processing occurred after enzyme redistribution
  3. Endo H is now a protease
  4. ER ribosomes acquired glycosyltransferase activity
Answer and reasoning

B. Processing occurred after enzyme redistribution

Exposure to redistributed enzymes can cause resistance without normal transport. The assay measures glycan state, not location.

4. With an effective first-order processing rate 0.20 min⁻¹ and residence of 5 min, what percentage is processed?

  1. 20.0%
  2. 36.8%
  3. 63.2%
  4. 100%
Answer and reasoning

C. 63.2%

kt = 1.0. Unmodified fraction e⁻¹ ≈ 0.368; processed fraction 0.632. The second choice is the remaining substrate.

5. Two obligatory stages have conditional completion rates of 75% and 60%. Out of 2,000 cargo molecules, how many complete both?

  1. 900
  2. 1,200
  3. 1,500
  4. 1,800
Answer and reasoning

A. 900

First stage: 2,000 × 0.75 = 1,500. Second: 1,500 × 0.60 = 900. The rates are conditional, so multiplication is valid.

6. At steady state a Golgi receives 180 molecules/min, mean residence is 15 min. Average cargo occupancy is:

  1. 12
  2. 180
  3. 2,700
  4. 27,000
Answer and reasoning

C. 2,700

N = Jτ = 180 molecules/min × 15 min = 2,700 molecules. This is occupancy, not a production rate.

7. For 6 min a TGN receives 75 molecules/min and releases 25 molecules/min, with no other flux. Net change?

  1. −300
  2. 150
  3. 300
  4. 450
Answer and reasoning

C. 300

Input minus output is +50 molecules/min. Multiply by 6 min to obtain +300. The fourth choice counts input without subtracting output.

8. A compartment shifts from pH 6.6 to pH 6.1. Approximate fold increase in free proton activity?

  1. 0.5
  2. 1.5
  3. 3.16
  4. 10
Answer and reasoning

C. 3.16

The ratio is 10^(6.6 − 6.1) = 10^0.5 ≈ 3.16. Total buffered proton content cannot be inferred.

9. A cohort of 800 hydrolases has 75% tagging, 80% conditional capture and 50% conditional delivery. Successful molecules?

  1. 240
  2. 300
  3. 480
  4. 600
Answer and reasoning

A. 240

800 × 0.75 = 600 tagged; 600 × 0.80 = 480 captured; half reach destination, 240. The latter values are intermediate counts.

10. Mutant A secretes hydrolases without M6P; mutant B secretes hydrolases carrying M6P. Both have normal protein synthesis. Which test best differentiates causes?

  1. Measure only total ribosomes
  2. Measure hydrolase tag and receptor binding/recycling separately
  3. Count mitochondria
  4. Measure DNA content only
Answer and reasoning

B. Measure hydrolase tag and receptor binding/recycling separately

A suggests failure of labeling, B a problem in receptor-mediated sorting or later transport. The proposed paired assays distinguish address generation from address reading.

11. A membrane nucleotide-sugar transporter fails, but isolated Golgi transferase activity is normal when donor is added. What explains the intact-cell defect?

  1. The enzyme must be absent
  2. The enzyme cannot access donor in the lumen
  3. All cargo must be trapped in nuclei
  4. Glycosylation is no longer enzyme-mediated
Answer and reasoning

B. The enzyme cannot access donor in the lumen

Substrate access is distinct from catalytic capacity. A normal in vitro assay bypasses the missing transport step.

12. A cell shows normal constitutive release but no stimulated hormone release, with abundant mature granules. Most likely affected step?

  1. All ER protein synthesis
  2. Initial N-glycosylation of every protein
  3. Stimulus-dependent granule fusion or signaling
  4. Cellulose synthesis
Answer and reasoning

C. Stimulus-dependent granule fusion or signaling

Mature stored granules and continued constitutive output indicate a selective defect after formation of regulated granules. The other options predict unrelated or broader defects.

13. A receptor has two N-glycosylation sites. One is Endo H sensitive after surface delivery, the other resistant. What explains this?

  1. The receptor never left the ER
  2. The two sites may differ in accessibility to processing enzymes
  3. The resistant site must be O-linked
  4. The receptor is synthesized twice
Answer and reasoning

B. The two sites may differ in accessibility to processing enzymes

Site-specific accessibility can yield different N-glycans on a single protein; resistance or sensitivity does not force all sites to have identical histories.

14. A plant cell can still incorporate glucose into cellulose at the surface briefly after Golgi traffic is disrupted, but new pectin delivery falls. Which conclusion is best?

  1. Both polymers are synthesized exclusively in the Golgi
  2. Existing surface cellulose synthases can function while Golgi matrix delivery is impaired
  3. Pectin is synthesized by ribosomes
  4. Golgi traffic has no effect on future cellulose synthase delivery
Answer and reasoning

B. Existing surface cellulose synthases can function while Golgi matrix delivery is impaired

The sites of polymer formation differ. Existing synthase can continue temporarily; longer disruption can impair replacement or delivery.

15. To test maturation rather than merely establish early and late markers, which observation is strongest?

  1. A single image of different green and red cisternae
  2. Total amount of late enzyme doubles
  3. Continuous tracking shows the same cisterna switching from early to late identity
  4. An ER protein has a signal sequence
Answer and reasoning

C. Continuous tracking shows the same cisterna switching from early to late identity

Tracking individual cisternae over time tests the distinguishing prediction. Static color distributions or total abundance cannot determine whether an individual compartment changes identity.

16. A hydrolase reaches lysosomes at normal concentration and has normal M6P, but substrate accumulates and enzyme activity is low in acidic lysosomal extracts. What does this favor?

  1. A defect of catalysis or maturation after delivery
  2. No ER entry
  3. Complete absence of M6P receptor binding
  4. A universal TGN secretion block
Answer and reasoning

A. A defect of catalysis or maturation after delivery

Correct delivery and tagging make upstream targeting less likely. A catalytic or post-delivery maturation defect is more consistent; tests of individual enzyme structure and activation could distinguish these.
