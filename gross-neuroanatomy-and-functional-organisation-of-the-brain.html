1. The brain as a distributed physiological system

An animal that approaches food must do much more than contract muscles. It must distinguish a useful sensory signal from background activity, combine information from the eyes and body, choose an appropriate action, coordinate the limbs, adjust breathing and circulation, and retain some record of what happened. These activities occur at the same time. The brain solves this problem by distributing different computations among connected regions. A useful anatomical map must therefore explain both specialisation and integration. Knowing that the cerebellum participates in coordination is useful, but understanding why coordination fails while a muscle can still contract requires a model of what information enters the cerebellum and what leaves it.

Gross neuroanatomy concerns the organisation of nervous tissue into visible or macroscopically identifiable structures. It differs from histology, which examines cells and their arrangements, and from electrophysiology, which measures electrical activity. The three levels support one another. A large visible pathway consists of axons whose membranes conduct action potentials, and its destination contains synapses whose properties determine how the signal affects other neurons. Knowledge of the resting potential and synaptic integration developed earlier therefore remains essential even when studying the entire brain. The anatomical name of a region does not itself identify the electrical effect of activating every neuron in it.

The central nervous system consists of the brain and spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system connects these organs with receptors, muscles, glands and other tissues. In a vertebrate, signals may pass through peripheral sensory neurons, spinal or brainstem circuits and higher centres before they influence an effector. Some adjustments can be organised locally; the brain need not consciously supervise every reflex. This arrangement permits rapid responses while allowing their strength and purpose to be modified by context. For example, a postural reaction during standing and a similar muscle response during a voluntary movement may involve overlapping spinal circuitry under different descending influences.

The word centre is often used for an anatomical region that is especially important for a function. It must not be interpreted as a claim that the function occurs in one isolated point. Breathing depends on brainstem networks, sensory feedback, respiratory muscles and chemical conditions in the blood. Memory depends on multiple neural systems, some of which participate in forming new memories and others in skills or long-term representation. When a lesion damages a particular centre, a physiological deficit may reveal a critical link in a network rather than the entire location of a mental capacity. This distinction becomes particularly important in experimental interpretation.

A second principle is that a single brain structure can support several related functions. The hypothalamus coordinates endocrine, autonomic and behavioural responses, because all three may be required to maintain an appropriate internal environment. The thalamus participates in sensory processing but also in loops involved in motor function and cognition. A structure should therefore be identified by its position, main connections and characteristic contributions, not by one word in a memorised table. These features make it possible to predict what changes when input or output is interrupted.

Anatomical terminology provides a shared system for describing these connections. In the human forebrain, anterior or rostral means toward the forehead, posterior or caudal toward the back, dorsal toward the upper surface and ventral toward the base. In the brainstem, the bend of the neuraxis changes the relationship between these terms and the body axes; dorsal is generally toward the back and ventral toward the front. Medial means nearer the midline and lateral means farther from it. Ipsilateral and contralateral identify relationships on the same and opposite sides respectively. These are relative terms, not fixed names for a particular hemisphere.

A sagittal section divides tissue into left and right portions. A midsagittal section passes through the midline, revealing structures such as the corpus callosum, third ventricle and the central relationship between the brainstem and cerebellum. A coronal section separates anterior from posterior and can reveal paired deep nuclei and their relation to internal white matter. An axial or horizontal section displays superior and inferior portions of the forebrain. Students should establish the plane and orientation before trying to identify a structure. Two images of the same brain may look very different simply because their sections pass through different levels.

Gross organisation is conserved sufficiently among vertebrates to support comparative physiological reasoning, but relative sizes and patterns of folding vary substantially. Human cortical lobes are a convenient reference for this chapter, not a universal template for every animal. A fish, bird and mammal may solve similar tasks with different relative investments in sensory processing, learning and movement. A disproportionately large visual-processing region can be associated with ecological reliance on vision, but comparison of size alone cannot establish computational ability. Homology requires evidence of developmental origin, connectivity and evolutionary relationship rather than superficial shape alone.

2. Developmental divisions and the adult brain

The earliest vertebrate central nervous system develops from a neural tube with a hollow lumen. The rostral end enlarges and becomes organised into primary brain vesicles. The terms prosencephalon, mesencephalon and rhombencephalon refer to the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain respectively. Although these are developmental terms, their relationships provide a durable framework for understanding the adult organ. The cavity does not disappear when surrounding tissue differentiates. It becomes the ventricular system, whose arrangement offers a second, independent clue to the developmental location of a brain region.

The forebrain divides into the telencephalon and diencephalon. The telencephalon forms the cerebral hemispheres, including their cortex, underlying white matter and many deep structures. The diencephalon gives rise to regions surrounding the third ventricle, prominently the thalamus and hypothalamus. The midbrain retains its name mesencephalon. The hindbrain separates into metencephalon and myelencephalon: the pons and cerebellum develop from the former, and the medulla oblongata from the latter. This mapping explains why two adult structures can share embryonic origin yet have quite different physiological responsibilities.

The term brainstem is an adult anatomical grouping that does not exactly reproduce the three primary embryonic divisions. It includes the midbrain, pons and medulla. The cerebellum develops from the hindbrain but is not part of the brainstem. The forebrain includes both cerebral hemispheres and diencephalon; the cerebrum is often used for the hemispheres themselves, although terminology varies in broader introductory texts. When an examination question specifies a developmental category, answer according to embryonic origin rather than adult functional grouping. In a question on the respiratory brainstem, use adult anatomy.

Imagine tracing the lumen from the paired cerebral hemispheres toward the spinal cord. Each hemisphere encloses a lateral ventricle. The two lateral spaces communicate separately with the single third ventricle through interventricular foramina. A narrow passage, the cerebral aqueduct, crosses the midbrain and connects the third to the fourth ventricle. The fourth ventricle lies between the pons and upper medulla anteriorly and the cerebellum posteriorly. This arrangement is useful for recognising the relative position of structures in a midsagittal drawing. It also predicts which fluid spaces may enlarge when a connection is obstructed.

The adult hemispheres extend over much of the diencephalon and midbrain. A lateral surface view therefore reveals large areas of cerebral cortex while concealing most of the thalamus, basal ganglia and hypothalamus. This is a limitation of the viewing angle, not evidence that the deeper structures are absent. To see them, one must examine a section or reconstruct their location from three-dimensional imaging. The posterior inferior cerebellum remains visible from some external views; its fine parallel surface folds contrast with the larger convolutions of the cerebrum. The brainstem descends from the diencephalic region toward the spinal cord, providing continuity between brain and spinal pathways.

The concept of a nucleus in neuroanatomy means an aggregation of neuronal cell bodies within the central nervous system. It does not mean the membrane-bound organelle containing chromosomes. A ganglion generally denotes a collection of neuronal cell bodies in the peripheral nervous system, although the historically established term basal ganglia refers to central structures. A tract is a collection of axons in the CNS; nerves are usually bundles of axons in the PNS. The optic nerve is a notable developmental and structural exception to a simplistic rule because it is formed by CNS-derived retinal ganglion-cell axons and has central myelin.

The visible differences between grey and white matter are also related to function. Grey matter contains many neuronal somata, dendrites, synapses and glial cells, as well as axons. White matter contains many myelinated axons and supporting glia, but the distinction is not absolute: both types include varied cellular elements. Grey matter is prominent in the superficial cerebral cortex and deep nuclei, whereas white matter links separated regions. In the spinal cord, the gross arrangement is different, with central grey matter surrounded by white matter. Students should avoid transferring the surface pattern of the cerebrum to every part of the nervous system.

These relationships reveal the logic of neural organisation. A large processing region must remain connected to distant regions, and physical separation introduces conduction delays. Myelination reduces those delays and permits efficient transmission over distance. Shorter local connections support recurrent circuits, whereas long pathways exchange the results of processing between systems. This division of labour does not require all connections to run upward or downward in a simple hierarchy: reciprocal connections permit feedback, modulation and prediction.

3. Cerebral hemispheres and cortical functional regions

Each cerebral hemisphere has an outer cortical sheet that is folded into gyri, the raised ridges, and sulci, the intervening grooves. Larger or deeper grooves are often called fissures. Folding allows a larger cortical surface to occupy a limited skull volume. The longitudinal fissure separates the hemispheres at the midline, although commissural axons connect them internally. The central sulcus separates frontal and parietal cortical territories and is an important landmark because regions immediately anterior and posterior to it have different principal physiological roles. The lateral sulcus marks the upper boundary of much of the temporal lobe, while the parieto-occipital sulcus is particularly evident on the medial surface.

Most of the human cerebral cortical surface is neocortex, which typically has six distinguishable layers. Their cellular density, morphology and connectivity vary according to function. Many thalamocortical sensory inputs terminate strongly in layer IV of primary sensory cortex; major descending projection neurons of motor cortex are concentrated in layer V. These tendencies help explain why a cortical region receiving sensory information may look microscopically different from one generating descending commands. The arrangement is not a fixed wiring diagram identical throughout the cortex. The hippocampal formation and some other evolutionarily older cortical regions have different lamination, so six layers must not be treated as a defining feature of every cortical structure.

The frontal lobe includes motor and premotor regions, prefrontal association cortex and other areas involved in planning, action selection and behavioural control. The primary motor cortex occupies the precentral gyrus and has an orderly representation of body regions. A body part requiring finely differentiated control tends to receive a disproportionately large cortical representation. The resulting motor homunculus is a schematic map of relative functional representation, not the anatomical size of body parts. A large hand representation supports control of independent, skilled movements; it does not imply that hand muscles are the largest muscles in the body.

The premotor and supplementary motor regions contribute to preparing and organising actions. A movement can be planned before its first measurable muscle contraction, and preparatory neural activity may occur while a subject waits for a cue. The prefrontal cortex contributes to maintaining information relevant to current goals, selecting among actions and adjusting behaviour as conditions change. It is misleading to call the frontal lobe the location of intelligence as a whole. Planning depends on sensory evidence, memory, motivation and reciprocal interactions with other cortical and subcortical regions. The detailed descending motor pathways and postural contributions will be considered in later nodes.

The primary somatosensory cortex lies mainly in the postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe. It receives organised representations of bodily sensation, including touch and proprioceptive information relayed through thalamic pathways. A sensory homunculus represents the relative cortical investment in processing different body surfaces. Regions such as the fingers and lips often occupy substantial cortical territory because they convey richly differentiated sensory information. The size of an area on the map cannot be converted directly into receptor number; peripheral innervation, central processing and experimental mapping methods all affect the representation.

Parietal association areas help combine information about body position, objects and spatial relationships. For example, reaching toward a seen target requires information about where the target lies and where the arm begins. Visual coordinates and body-centred coordinates are not identical. An integrated representation allows the nervous system to guide the arm despite changes in head position or posture. Damage to association regions can therefore impair a spatial task even when the eye, primary visual cortex and limb muscles retain substantial function. Such a result demonstrates a failure of integration, not necessarily a loss of the primary sensation.

The occipital lobe contains primary visual cortex, located mainly around the calcarine sulcus on the medial surface, and additional visual processing regions. Neural activity here preserves spatial relationships within the visual field, a principle known as retinotopy. Retinotopic representation is a mapping of visual space rather than a miniature image seen by an internal observer. Separate populations encode features and their relationships; perception emerges from distributed activity and interactions. A disruption of visual pathways or cortical processing can impair vision even when retinal photoreceptors remain responsive. The receptor mechanism of visual transduction belongs to the subsequent sensory-system subunit.

The temporal lobe includes auditory cortical regions, association regions involved in recognising complex stimuli, and medial structures that contribute to memory. Primary auditory cortex lies on the superior temporal plane, including the transverse temporal gyri. Its organisation reflects frequency relationships, called tonotopy. Tonotopic order also exists at earlier stages of the auditory pathway, so it is not invented anew by the cortex. Speech perception and recognition of environmental sounds require more than detection of individual frequencies. Temporal association networks combine patterns across time and relate them to information already acquired.

The insula lies deep within the lateral sulcus, covered by parts of the frontal, parietal and temporal lobes. It contributes to functions including interoception, taste and integration of bodily signals with emotion and behaviour. Interoception refers to sensing the internal physiological state of the body. A signal about visceral distension can acquire different behavioural significance depending on hunger, illness or recent experience. The insula provides a useful example of why the externally visible four-lobe diagram is an incomplete inventory of cortical function.

Language in most humans is strongly lateralised toward the left hemisphere, but language production and comprehension depend on distributed networks rather than two isolated boxes. Classical observations linking frontal and posterior temporal damage to different speech difficulties were foundational. Modern evidence indicates that the location and extent of damage, white-matter disconnection and individual variation influence the resulting deficit. A person may understand a request but have difficulty producing fluent speech, or produce fluent speech whose content is poorly related to the request. These patterns demonstrate partial dissociation of physiological functions without proving that each function belongs to a single sharply bounded region.

Lateralisation also does not divide people into logical left-brained and creative right-brained categories. Both hemispheres participate in most complex behaviours. Each hemisphere receives and processes some information differently, and commissural connections allow coordination. The particular balance varies between individuals and tasks. In examination reasoning, hemisphere dominance should be treated as a statistically common functional asymmetry rather than an absolute rule applying to every subject or every cognitive operation.

An important distinction runs through all cortical functions: primary regions receive relatively direct sensory information or generate major motor outputs, while association regions combine and transform information to support complex behaviours. These categories describe dominant patterns, not strictly sequential stages. Feedback can influence even early sensory processing. A subject attending to a sound may show different cortical activity from one hearing the same sound without attending. If a task changes activity in a sensory area, that result may reflect top-down modulation as well as sensory stimulation itself.

4. White matter and communication between hemispheres

Cerebral white matter contains routes that connect cortical regions to one another and to deeper or more distant structures. Association fibres link regions within the same hemisphere. Commissural fibres cross the midline to connect the hemispheres. Projection fibres connect cortex with subcortical nuclei, brainstem or spinal cord. This classification is based on the relationship between origins and destinations rather than on conduction speed or transmitter identity. A lesion affecting white matter may produce a deficit by disconnecting viable areas of grey matter.

The corpus callosum is the largest cerebral commissure. In a midsagittal section it is seen as a curved band of white matter above much of the ventricular and diencephalic region. Fibres from different cortical territories tend to occupy different parts of the callosum, supporting coordination between the hemispheres. Other commissures, including the anterior commissure, also carry information across the midline. Thus cutting the corpus callosum substantially limits cortical interhemispheric communication but does not eliminate all bilateral coordination in the organism. Both hemispheres still receive many common influences from subcortical systems and the environment.

Classical split-brain investigations exploited the distinction between sensory input and the ability to report it verbally. With fixation controlled, an object in the left visual hemifield is represented initially in the right primary visual cortex. In an individual with major callosal disconnection and typical left-sided language organisation, information about that object may be difficult to name aloud even when nonverbal responses show that the right hemisphere detected it. The interpretation requires careful control of eye movements, presentation time and response mode. An inability to name a stimulus is not equivalent to blindness. A subject may identify it by selecting a matching object with the left hand. These results support a role for commissures in transferring information needed for particular tasks.

The internal capsule is an important concentration of projection fibres between deep nuclei. Fibres carrying signals to and from cerebral cortex pass through it. Because many functionally important axons are packed into a restricted area, a relatively local interruption may have effects that extend over a substantial body territory. This illustrates an anatomical principle: the size of a lesion is not a reliable measure of the importance of the resulting deficit. Its location relative to concentrated pathways and alternative routes matters.

A gross anatomical drawing should distinguish a white-matter bridge from a deep grey-matter relay. The corpus callosum carries axons between hemispheres; the thalamus contains neuronal populations that receive, process and transmit information. Both are essential for communication, but the kinds of operation performed differ. Tracts primarily transmit axonal activity; synaptic processing occurs at their origins, destinations and collateral contacts. The distinction is useful when reasoning about drugs, lesions or recording experiments. A recording from a white-matter bundle may reveal the timing of propagated spikes without revealing the full synaptic computations that generated them.

An experiment that temporarily blocks conduction in a pathway can help distinguish communication failure from loss of a region's processing capacity. If a sensory region still responds normally to a stimulus but a connected association region no longer responds, interruption of the connection is a plausible explanation. However, the same pattern might arise from changed inhibition or insufficient stimulation. A strong experiment verifies local responses, uses appropriate control conditions and tests whether the downstream effect recovers when conduction is restored. Anatomical tracing and physiological recording together provide stronger evidence than either alone.

5. Thalamus: relay, selection and reciprocal communication

When the cerebral hemispheres are separated in a midsagittal view, the region around the third ventricle provides a useful reference for locating the diencephalon. Much of the lateral wall of the upper third ventricle is related to the thalamus. The paired thalami are substantial masses of grey matter rather than simple fibre bundles. Each contains groups of neurons with differing afferent and efferent connections. The hypothalamus occupies a more ventral position, and the epithalamus includes structures such as the pineal gland. These subdivisions support different physiological activities while remaining connected to each other.

The thalamus is often introduced as the major relay station for sensory information arriving at the cerebral cortex. The reason for a relay is not that an axon cannot conduct far enough without a new neuron. Axons can extend over great distances. A relay introduces opportunities for convergence, selection, modulation and distribution. Thalamic relay cells receive information from specific afferent pathways and send axons toward cortical territories that process that information. Their synaptic responses also depend on inhibitory input and feedback from the cortex. The output of a relay is therefore not necessarily an unchanged copy of the input.

Consider the visual pathway as an example of anatomical correspondence. Retinal ganglion-cell axons travel centrally; major populations synapse in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, which projects to primary visual cortex. This connection carries information useful for detailed visual perception. Other retinal projections reach regions concerned with different tasks, including the regulation of biological rhythms and orienting responses. A signal can reach more than one destination through separate branches and pathways, so a diagram showing one major relay should not imply that all visual processing passes through that relay.

The medial geniculate nucleus participates in auditory transmission from the midbrain to auditory cortex. The ventral posterior thalamic nuclei relay much somatosensory information toward the postcentral cortex; different subdivisions receive inputs representing the body and face. The names lateral geniculate, medial geniculate and ventral posterior are useful because they refer to distinct anatomical stations rather than arbitrary mnemonic labels. A student should associate each with the modality carried and the corresponding cortical destination. The detailed ascending routes carrying the signals will be developed later.

Olfaction is a standard exception to the statement that sensory information reaches primary cortex only after a thalamic relay. Olfactory receptor neurons synapse in the olfactory bulb, and bulb output can reach primary olfactory cortical regions without first undergoing a conventional obligatory thalamic relay. This does not mean the thalamus has no role in olfactory processing: thalamic connections participate in further processing and communication with higher cortical regions. The exception must therefore be phrased with reference to the initial cortical route, not to every subsequent stage of smell perception.

Thalamic nuclei also participate in communication that does not begin with a peripheral sense organ. Motor-related thalamic regions receive output from basal ganglia or cerebellar circuits and project to cortical areas involved in movement. Other nuclei participate in association networks and arousal. The thalamus thus provides several interfaces through which subcortical activity modifies cortical activity. When interpreting an examination question, identify the particular input and output involved before assigning a thalamic function. The word relay is not synonymous with sensory relay.

A substantial fraction of the connections between thalamus and cortex are reciprocal. Cortical neurons send axons back toward thalamic nuclei. Some of this descending influence modulates the responsiveness of ascending relay pathways. An inhibitory structure called the thalamic reticular nucleus forms a shell around much of the lateral thalamus and receives collaterals from thalamocortical and corticothalamic pathways. It sends inhibitory projections to thalamic neurons, not directly to the cerebral cortex as a conventional sensory relay. This arrangement creates opportunities to alter the gain and timing of transmission in relation to behavioural state.

The term gain describes the magnitude of a response relative to a change in input. Imagine an illustrative recording in which increasing a sensory stimulus from one to two arbitrary units changes the firing rate of a thalamic neuron from 10 to 20 spikes per second in one condition but from 10 to 14 spikes per second in another condition. The input increments are identical, but the response increments are different. In the first condition the average gain over the measured interval is 10 spikes per second per input unit; in the second it is 4. This observation suggests that the transformation of input has changed. It does not identify the cause without measurements of the input reaching the thalamus, the relay cell's membrane properties and the inhibitory or modulatory signals impinging on it.

A second distinction concerns firing mode. Many thalamic relay neurons can fire isolated spikes in a relatively tonic mode or groups of spikes in a burst mode, depending partly on membrane potential and the availability of voltage-dependent calcium channels. Hyperpolarisation can remove inactivation of low-threshold calcium channels, allowing subsequent depolarisation to trigger a calcium-dependent event associated with a burst of action potentials. The general principle follows from channel gating discussed in the preceding nodes. A burst is not merely a stronger version of a tonic response: it changes the temporal pattern that the receiving cortical cells encounter. State-dependent firing contributes to the relationship between thalamocortical activity, sensory transmission and sleep, although no single firing pattern fully explains all sleep stages.

Evidence for relay function can come from anatomical tracing, recordings and selective perturbations. If a labelled population of retinal axons ends in a thalamic region and neurons there project to visual cortex, the anatomical chain is plausible. If those neurons respond to controlled visual stimuli with appropriate spatial organisation, there is physiological support. If interrupting the relay reduces cortical responses while retinal responses remain intact, causal support is stronger. None of these experiments alone reveals every operation performed within the relay. A robust conclusion combines independent forms of evidence and avoids treating a correlation between two signals as proof of a direct connection.

A disrupted relay may alter one sensory function more strongly than another. Nevertheless, real damage can affect adjacent nuclei or passing fibres, and the thalamus also contributes to attention and arousal. A broad deficit need not correspond to one sharply isolated sensory nucleus. Gross functional reasoning starts with the expected pathway and then asks which additional connections might account for observations that the simple model cannot explain.

6. Hypothalamus and the regulation of the internal environment

The hypothalamus occupies a relatively small region at the base of the diencephalon but has extensive influence over physiology. It lies below the thalamus and contributes to the floor and lower lateral walls of the third ventricle. Its connections with the pituitary gland, brainstem autonomic centres, limbic structures and sensory pathways allow it to couple information about the internal state to endocrine, neural and behavioural outputs. The key to understanding its importance is to distinguish the size of a structure from the breadth of the systems it controls.

Homeostasis means maintaining internal conditions within ranges compatible with function, not making every physiological variable constant. Temperature, plasma osmolality and energy availability change over time, and the appropriate response depends on circumstances. Negative feedback reduces a deviation by initiating responses that oppose it. The hypothalamus participates in several feedback systems in which sensory signals, hormonal information and neural signals converge. It is therefore not simply a gland that secretes hormones or a sensory structure that detects temperature. It integrates signals and helps organise multiple kinds of response.

Suppose evaporation during exercise increases water loss. If body water falls relative to dissolved solute, plasma osmolality can rise. Osmosensitive systems in and near the hypothalamus respond to changes in the effective concentration of extracellular fluid. Neural activity influences thirst and vasopressin release. Drinking supplies water, while vasopressin promotes water conservation by the kidney through mechanisms to be developed in the excretory-system subunit. Both responses tend to reduce the original disturbance. A purely renal explanation would omit the neural coordination of intake, and a purely behavioural explanation would omit regulation of water loss.

This example also illustrates why osmolality and blood volume must be distinguished. Loss of isotonic fluid may decrease circulating volume without producing the same osmotic change as loss of pure water. Cardiovascular sensory signals and circulating hormones can modify thirst and vasopressin secretion even when osmolality is not the only relevant stimulus. The controller receives multiple forms of information about the internal environment. It does not have one sensor that reports a universal measure of hydration.

Neuroendocrine signalling allows hypothalamic activity to influence distant tissues. Certain hypothalamic neurosecretory cells synthesize vasopressin or oxytocin and transport these peptides along axons to terminals in the posterior pituitary. The supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei are major sources of these magnocellular neurosecretory neurons, with both peptides represented in these nuclei. When appropriate electrical activity reaches the terminals, the peptides are released into the circulation. The posterior pituitary is thus a neural extension and release site rather than the primary site of synthesis of these hormones.

The anterior pituitary differs in organisation. Hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting factors enter capillaries near the median eminence and reach anterior pituitary endocrine cells through the hypophyseal portal vessels. A portal system connects two capillary beds in series, allowing a locally released signal to reach its target at an effective concentration without first being widely diluted in systemic blood. The anterior pituitary cells themselves synthesize and secrete their own hormones in response to hypothalamic signals. The contrast between direct axonal transport to posterior pituitary and portal chemical regulation of anterior pituitary is particularly important for anatomical reasoning.

Consider two hypothetical interruptions. In one case, axonal transport in the hypothalamo-neurohypophyseal tract is selectively impaired. In the other, the portal vascular connection to the anterior pituitary is interrupted while the posterior pathway remains intact. The two manipulations do not predict identical endocrine effects. The former directly compromises delivery of hypothalamic peptides to posterior terminals; the latter compromises delivery of releasing and inhibiting factors to anterior cells. Secondary consequences can develop in both conditions, so the initial direct effect should be separated from later compensatory or systemic changes.

Temperature regulation demonstrates how hypothalamic pathways combine behavioural and autonomic responses. Warm-sensitive neural systems, particularly in the preoptic region, receive information relevant to body temperature. If heat production and environmental heat load raise body temperature, heat-loss responses may include changes in skin blood flow, evaporative cooling in species that use it, and a preference for cooler surroundings. Cold exposure can evoke heat conservation, increased metabolic heat production and behavioural adjustments. Which response is available depends on the species and the conditions. Sweating, for example, is not a universal vertebrate solution to heat stress.

The older description of a single heat centre opposed by a single cold centre is an oversimplification. There are multiple populations with different connections and response properties. Temperature control also depends on input from skin, deep tissues and other regions, as well as endocrine and autonomic pathways. A useful physiological model nevertheless retains the central idea that neural circuits compare or integrate information about thermal conditions and initiate responses that tend to preserve an appropriate range. The specific effector mechanisms and acclimatisation are reserved for the thermoregulation subunit.

Feeding and energy regulation similarly depend on networks rather than isolated hunger and satiety buttons. Hypothalamic regions receive information about nutrient availability, adipose energy stores, circulating hormones and neural signals originating in the digestive system. Some neuronal populations favour food intake and others suppress it under particular conditions. The arcuate nucleus is a prominent interface for metabolic signals; neighbouring and connected hypothalamic regions influence behaviour and autonomic or endocrine responses. A simple lesion may alter body mass or feeding but also change activity, energy expenditure or the interpretation of food-related cues. These effects must be separated experimentally before concluding that the manipulation specifically changed appetite.

The suprachiasmatic nucleus, positioned above the optic chiasm, is a major circadian pacemaker in mammals. It receives direct retinal input that conveys information about environmental light and helps align endogenous daily rhythms with the external light-dark cycle. The term entrainment means adjustment of the phase and period relationship of an internal oscillator to a recurring environmental signal. It is distinct from the immediate response of a physiological variable to light. If an organism shows recurring activity in constant darkness, an endogenous rhythm is implicated; if its phase adjusts when the light schedule changes, the oscillator can be entrained.

The pineal gland, associated with the epithalamus rather than the hypothalamus, secretes melatonin in a rhythm influenced by the circadian system and lighting conditions. The suprachiasmatic nucleus is not itself the principal source of circulating pineal melatonin. This distinction illustrates how a neural oscillator, an endocrine output and the target tissues responding to the output can be physically separated. A hormone rhythm may report the state of a central timing system without constituting the oscillator that originally generated that timing.

The hypothalamus also participates in the integrated expression of responses to threat, reproductive signals and changes in internal state. Its output can alter cardiovascular function via brainstem and spinal autonomic pathways and can influence endocrine responses through the pituitary. Limbic and cortical inputs allow the perceived meaning of a situation to modify physiological output. A familiar sound and a threatening sound may have similar acoustic intensity but lead to very different autonomic responses. The physical stimulus alone does not determine the entire reaction because previous experience and context are also represented in the network.

An experimental design for separating the components of a hypothalamic response might compare a controlled increase in extracellular osmolality with an independent reduction in circulating volume and measure thirst, hormone concentration and urine output. Appropriate control groups would experience the same handling and time of observation without the physiological perturbation. An experiment that measured only urine output could not establish whether a neural signal, hormone action or a change in kidney function caused the difference. Simultaneous measurements across the pathway provide a more complete explanation. This reasoning framework transfers directly to endocrine and excretory questions.

7. Basal ganglia: subcortical selection of actions

The cerebral hemispheres contain several large groups of subcortical grey matter. Among these are nuclei that participate in the basal ganglia system. The historically established name ganglia is an exception to the usual CNS terminology. The core functional circuit includes the caudate nucleus, putamen and globus pallidus in the forebrain, together with the subthalamic nucleus of the diencephalon and substantia nigra of the midbrain. Because these structures occupy different embryonic and gross anatomical locations, the basal ganglia are best understood as a connected functional system rather than a single continuous mass.

In a coronal section through the cerebral hemispheres, the caudate nucleus lies in relation to the lateral ventricle, while the putamen and globus pallidus occupy more lateral and ventral positions. The internal capsule separates important portions of these grey-matter structures. The putamen and globus pallidus together form the lentiform or lenticular nucleus in gross anatomy. In functional anatomy, the caudate and putamen form the dorsal striatum, a major input region of the basal ganglia. These different groupings illustrate why an anatomical name based on shape and a physiological name based on circuit membership need not define the same combination of nuclei.

The basal ganglia receive extensive input from cerebral cortex and influence cortical and brainstem systems through their output pathways. They contribute to selecting and regulating actions, learning habitual behaviour and aspects of motivation and cognition. They do not generally command individual skeletal muscle fibres directly. The final common output to skeletal muscle comes from lower motor neurons. Instead, basal ganglia output modifies the activity of networks that prepare and execute actions. A difficulty initiating or appropriately scaling movement can therefore occur even when the muscles remain capable of generating force.

A useful introductory distinction separates movement selection from movement coordination. If a person has difficulty initiating a desired movement or produces unwanted movements, disruption of action-selection circuits may be important. If the desired movement begins but its trajectory is poorly timed or overshoots a target, cerebellar contributions to coordination may be particularly relevant. These are broad physiological predictions, not exclusive diagnostic rules. Both systems participate in learning, and damage to one system changes the signals reaching other parts of the network.

Many basal ganglia output neurons are inhibitory and exhibit ongoing activity. This creates a counterintuitive possibility: inhibiting an inhibitory output neuron can release its downstream target from inhibition. The effect is called disinhibition. If neuron A inhibits neuron B, and neuron B inhibits neuron C, an increase in the activity of A can reduce the inhibition received by C. The sign of the final effect must be determined by following the chain rather than by assuming that every inhibitory synapse produces an overall suppression of behaviour.

In a simplified basal ganglia loop, cortical excitation reaches striatal neurons, some striatal outputs inhibit basal ganglia output nuclei, and changes in those output nuclei alter the inhibition of motor-related thalamic or brainstem targets. Dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta modulates striatal circuitry. Different pathways through the system have distinct influences on output activity; their interactions are developed in the central motor-control chapter. For gross neuroanatomy it is sufficient to understand that the striatum receives extensive input, output nuclei influence downstream targets, and dopamine modulates the transformation between input and output.

Parkinsonian movement difficulties illustrate the consequence of altered function within these circuits. The loss of dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta changes the state of basal ganglia networks and contributes to slowness and difficulty with movement initiation. Tremor, rigidity and other manifestations may also occur. This example should not be reduced to the slogan that dopamine directly contracts muscle or that all dopamine neurons are located in the substantia nigra. Dopamine is a neurotransmitter with multiple systems and effects, and the particular consequences depend on the circuit involved.

Huntington disease provides a contrasting example in which degeneration of striatal neurons is associated with disturbances including involuntary movements and cognitive or behavioural changes. The contrast between excessive unwanted movements and impaired initiation helped establish the importance of the basal ganglia in regulating which motor programmes are expressed. However, diseases affect multiple cell populations over time. They cannot be interpreted as perfectly selective experimental removal of a single synaptic connection. Gross anatomical evidence is informative when combined with cellular and circuit-level evidence.

The ventral striatum and related circuits participate in motivation and reinforcement learning. An organism learns that certain actions predict desirable outcomes and modifies future behaviour accordingly. Reward-related signals do not mean that the entire basal ganglia system is a pleasure centre. Learning involves the relationship between cues, actions, outcomes and expectations, and many connected regions contribute. In the context of this chapter, these functions show why the same broad organisation of cortical input, subcortical integration and return pathways can support both motor and nonmotor tasks.

Experimental reasoning can distinguish a deficit in action selection from loss of sensation or strength. Imagine an animal trained to choose one of two actions after a cue. If a manipulation increases inappropriate choices or delays initiation, the researcher should also test whether the animal detects the cue and can perform each movement when required in a simpler task. Preserved sensory discrimination and preserved force generation make a central selection deficit more plausible. A failure in all tasks would be less specific and could reflect reduced arousal, motivation or general motor ability.

The anatomical position of the internal capsule introduces an important experimental limitation. A lesion aimed at the putamen may also affect nearby projection fibres. If the resulting animal has weakness, it would be premature to attribute all of that weakness to loss of putaminal processing. Anatomical verification of the affected area and comparison with reversible, more selective manipulations improve the interpretation. The more concentrated the surrounding pathways, the more carefully one must distinguish destruction of local neurons from interruption of axons passing through the area.

8. Limbic structures, memory and behavioural significance

The term limbic system arose from descriptions of cortical and subcortical structures arranged around medial regions of the cerebral hemispheres. In contemporary physiology it refers loosely to interacting structures important for memory, emotion, motivation and the relationship between bodily state and behaviour. Its boundaries differ between accounts, and not every emotional response can be assigned exclusively to it. It is more productive to identify particular structures and their connections than to imagine a single enclosed emotional brain operating independently of cognition.

The hippocampal formation occupies the medial temporal lobe and has a distinctive curved arrangement. It includes the hippocampus proper, dentate gyrus and subicular region in common anatomical descriptions. Connections through nearby cortical regions bring information from widespread association areas. Outputs return information to cortical and subcortical networks. This organisation allows relations among elements of experience to be represented and helps support the formation of new declarative memories, including memories of facts and events.

Declarative memory is memory that can generally be consciously recollected and expressed as knowledge about a fact or experience. Procedural memory includes the learning of skills and habits, which may improve without a person being able to verbalise the details of what was learned. Working memory refers to the temporary availability of information for an ongoing task. The distinctions are functional rather than a claim that every memory belongs to only one anatomical site. A complex task can involve all three forms. Learning a route may require remembering landmarks, maintaining current instructions and gradually acquiring an efficient sequence of movements.

The classic observations reported by William Scoville and Brenda Milner in 1957 showed that bilateral removal of medial temporal structures could be followed by severe difficulty forming new enduring memories. The widely studied patient H.M. retained many previously acquired abilities, including aspects of immediate information retention, despite profound impairment in acquiring new declarative memories. Subsequent observations showed that some forms of skill learning could improve across practice even when explicit recollection of earlier practice was severely impaired. This dissociation helped establish that memory is not a unitary physiological capacity.

The case also illustrates the limits of inference from a lesion. The operation affected more than the hippocampus alone, and a single patient cannot establish the precise function of every cellular component of a region. The preservation of some abilities despite severe impairment in others argues against an explanation based solely on general intellectual loss or a universal inability to learn. Stronger conclusions about individual hippocampal subregions require convergent evidence from additional patients, animal studies, anatomy and physiological recording. It is not correct to claim that this case proved that all long-term memories are stored permanently in hippocampal neurons.

The fornix is a major pathway carrying fibres associated with the hippocampal formation toward other structures, including septal and hypothalamic regions. The mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus and anterior thalamic nuclei participate in interconnected memory-related networks. The cingulate cortex occupies a medial cortical position above the corpus callosum and contributes to several functions including aspects of attention, action monitoring, memory and emotional processing. These relationships explain why disruption of diencephalic structures or connections can also produce significant memory disturbances, even though the hippocampus is not itself destroyed.

The amygdala is a collection of nuclei in the anterior medial temporal region. It receives highly processed sensory information and signals from internal-state and contextual networks. Through connections with hypothalamus and brainstem, it can contribute to autonomic and behavioural responses associated with the significance of stimuli. It is involved in learning and evaluating both threatening and other biologically important events. Calling it the fear centre is convenient but misleading because threat responses depend on multiple regions, and amygdalar activity is not limited to fear.

Associative learning provides a conceptual example. A previously neutral tone may acquire predictive significance if it is reliably followed by an aversive event. After learning, the tone alone can evoke changes in behaviour and autonomic physiology. The design should compare the effect of predictive pairing with unpaired exposure to tone and event, because repeated exposure alone could alter responsiveness. A manipulation that disrupts the learned response might impair association formation, retrieval, detection of the tone, or expression of the response. Separate tests of sensory detection and unconditioned response help distinguish these alternatives.

A key distinction separates the experience or evaluation of an event from the physiological expression of that evaluation. A network can influence the hypothalamus and brainstem to change heart rate or respiration without causing the same pattern of overt behaviour in every circumstance. Conversely, a voluntary action can alter those physiological variables through cortical control. Recording a change in heart rate alone does not establish that a particular emotion was experienced. It establishes a physiological response whose significance depends on experimental context.

The limbic concept links directly to homeostasis. A hungry animal assigns different significance to food-related stimuli from a satiated animal. Neural signals representing metabolic state can influence motivational circuits, and experience can modify the behavioural responses to the same internal condition. The hypothalamus receives information relevant to energy balance; hippocampal and cortical networks contribute information about context and previous experience; amygdalar and striatal circuits contribute to the value and selection of actions. The resulting behaviour is neither a pure reflex nor a decision independent of physiology. It is the outcome of distributed interactions.

An exam question may provide two animals that both remember a simple instruction immediately but differ in later recognition of newly presented objects. The crucial issue is whether the impairment is specific to a form or stage of memory. If the animals can discriminate the objects during presentation and have similar motivation to explore them, a deficit in later recognition becomes more interpretable. If the impaired animal never examines the objects, a memory conclusion is weak. Functional neuroanatomy depends on matching each behavioural measure to the physiological process that it is intended to test.

9. Midbrain: orienting, transmission and state regulation

The midbrain occupies the upper portion of the brainstem, between the diencephalon and pons. Its central cavity is the cerebral aqueduct, a narrow channel connecting the third and fourth ventricles. In a transverse section, structures dorsal to the aqueduct form the tectum, while the tegmentum lies more ventrally. Large descending fibre bundles occupy the ventral region of the midbrain. These landmarks make it possible to distinguish local sensory and motor processing structures from long pathways passing through the brainstem. An interruption in the midbrain can affect both classes of organisation.

The tectum includes paired superior and inferior colliculi, which form prominent rounded structures on the dorsal surface. The superior colliculus participates in the orientation of eyes and head toward behaviourally relevant stimuli. It receives visual information as well as input from other sensory and cortical systems. The inferior colliculus participates in the central auditory pathway, receiving and integrating information from lower auditory regions and providing major output toward the medial geniculate nucleus. The colliculi are not equivalent to the primary visual and auditory cortices. Their characteristic roles emerge from their position within different networks.

An orienting response illustrates why a sensory signal may follow several routes. A sudden movement in the visual field can trigger a rapid shift of gaze toward the stimulus before its detailed identity is established. The superior colliculus participates in translating spatial information into commands that contribute to such shifts. Cortical networks can modify the choice of where to look, especially when an organism has a task that requires ignoring distracting stimuli. A useful target may therefore be selected even if a brighter stimulus is present elsewhere. The activity of an orienting system represents the behavioural importance and position of a potential target, not simply its brightness.

The relationship between tectal structures and ecology is especially informative in comparative neuroanatomy. In many nonmammalian vertebrates, the optic tectum is a prominent visual-processing structure with major contributions to guiding behaviour. Its evolutionary relationship to the mammalian superior colliculus is recognised from developmental and anatomical evidence. Mammalian expansion of cortical systems changes the relative contribution of different pathways without making the midbrain visual system unnecessary. An examination question comparing species should therefore distinguish evolutionary homology from identical adult functional importance.

The inferior colliculus integrates auditory information from multiple ascending pathways. Sound localisation requires comparing information across ears, such as differences in arrival time or intensity. Such computations involve several brainstem stations; the inferior colliculus participates in combining ascending information rather than independently performing every stage of auditory analysis. Because information from both ears is distributed bilaterally through central pathways, unilateral damage to one central auditory station does not necessarily produce complete deafness in the ear on the opposite side. The interpretation of a sensory lesion depends on where signals have already crossed or converged.

The substantia nigra, described in the basal ganglia section, is located in the midbrain. Its pars compacta contains important dopaminergic neurons, whereas the pars reticulata has major output functions within basal ganglia circuitry. The name refers to the dark pigmentation visible in parts of the adult human structure. The red nucleus is another midbrain structure with connections relevant to motor coordination. The relative importance of its descending pathways varies among species, and detailed motor circuitry will be addressed separately. These examples reinforce the fact that a structure located in the brainstem can belong to a functional system extending well beyond the brainstem.

The periaqueductal grey surrounds the cerebral aqueduct and contributes to the organisation of defensive responses, modulation of pain and associated autonomic changes. It provides a spatially useful landmark: the aqueduct identifies a cavity, while the surrounding grey matter contains neuronal circuits. A descending pathway influencing pain transmission is not the same thing as an ascending pathway conveying information about tissue damage. Modulation can change the response of neurons further downstream without removing the peripheral stimulus. This is an instance of the general principle that sensory experience is shaped by both input and central regulation.

Neuronal populations of the midbrain and upper brainstem also contribute to arousal and the regulation of behavioural state. Ascending neuromodulatory influences can change the responsiveness of widespread cortical and subcortical networks. They are not ordinary point-to-point sensory relays that carry a faithful spatial representation of the stimulus. Instead, they alter the conditions under which local processing occurs. A subject can receive sensory input while asleep but respond differently from when awake; changes in the state of large neural networks help explain that difference. The details of all individual transmitter nuclei are not required to understand the gross organisation.

Imagine a physiological experiment in which a visual stimulus evokes both a cortical electrical response and an orienting movement. Temporary disruption of a tectal pathway reduces the movement while leaving substantial cortical responses intact. This would support a separation between circuitry contributing to orienting and the circuitry generating the measured cortical response. It would not prove that cortical regions never contribute to gaze control. Testing voluntary orienting, responses to auditory cues and recovery after the manipulation would help clarify the extent of the deficit.

Midbrain anatomy therefore serves as a bridge between forebrain processing and lower neural control. Some signals pass through it with little local processing along the particular axon being traced; others terminate in local nuclei and are transformed before further transmission. A student interpreting a sagittal section should first identify the aqueduct and its relationship to pons and diencephalon, then use the dorsal colliculi and ventral fibre systems to establish orientation. These relationships are more reliable than memorising the colour assigned to a structure in one illustration.

10. Pons and medulla: brainstem integration and vital control

The pons lies below the midbrain and above the medulla. Its ventral surface forms a conspicuous rounded prominence in the human brainstem. The name pons means bridge, reflecting an important anatomical role in communication, particularly between cerebral cortex and cerebellum. The ventral pontine region contains pontine nuclei and transverse fibres whose pathways enter the cerebellum predominantly through the middle cerebellar peduncles. Dorsally, the pontine tegmentum contains ascending and descending pathways, cranial nerve-related nuclei and networks participating in functions such as arousal and respiratory coordination.

A motor command prepared in cerebral cortex can be sent both toward spinal motor systems and toward circuits that monitor or coordinate the developing movement. Cortical signals reaching pontine nuclei are relayed into cerebellar networks. This information helps the cerebellum acquire a representation of the intended action, which can be related to sensory feedback about the action's consequences. The pons should not be regarded merely as an inert connection between upper and lower structures. Its local neurons contribute to transforming and distributing information. A damaged relay may impair the availability of information to the cerebellum even though the cerebral cortex remains active.

The middle cerebellar peduncles are particularly associated with pontocerebellar input. The other peduncles are also important: the superior peduncle carries much cerebellar output, together with some input, and the inferior peduncle carries several types of input and output including connections with medullary and vestibular systems. Peduncles are fibre bundles linking the cerebellum to other nervous-system regions. Their names describe anatomical position rather than one universal direction of signal flow. The broad distinction is useful for identifying communication routes on gross sections without requiring memorisation of every tract.

The pons participates in respiratory control through neural circuits that influence the timing and pattern of breathing generated by distributed brainstem networks. Breathing is not produced by the pons acting alone. Medullary rhythm-generating circuitry and motor pathways, chemoreceptor signals, lung sensory feedback and higher influences contribute to the final pattern. Pontine activity can modify transitions between phases of respiration and help adapt breathing to other behaviours. The preceding respiratory chapter examined the physiological mechanisms in greater detail; here the relevant point is their location within the brainstem and integration with neighbouring systems.

The medulla oblongata forms the lower part of the brainstem and continues caudally into the spinal cord. The change from medulla to spinal cord is not a sudden disappearance of all ascending and descending fibres. Many pathways continue across the boundary, while others terminate or originate in medullary nuclei. The ventral surface includes longitudinal elevations called pyramids, which contain many descending corticospinal fibres. Near the caudal medulla, a substantial proportion of these fibres cross the midline. The pyramidal decussation is one anatomical basis for the predominantly contralateral influence of cerebral motor cortex on distal limb movement.

Not every motor fibre crosses at that location, and not every descending motor pathway belongs to the corticospinal tract. Some fibres remain uncrossed at the pyramidal decussation, and other pathways arising in different brain regions have their own patterns of termination and crossing. A statement that all left-brain signals control the right half of the body is therefore too broad. The consequence of a lesion must be predicted from the particular tract, its crossing and its target. Detailed ascending and descending spinal pathways will be considered in 7.D.06 and the central motor-control chapter.

On the ventrolateral medullary surface, the olives mark underlying inferior olivary nuclei. These structures provide important inputs to the cerebellum. Their anatomical relationship to cerebellar processing illustrates the diversity of medullary functions: the medulla is not exclusively a collection of centres for automatic visceral responses. It also contains sensory relays and motor-related connections. A sagittal diagram summarising breathing and cardiovascular functions must not conceal this broader organisation.

The medulla includes neural populations essential for automatic respiratory and cardiovascular regulation. Respiratory rhythm-generating networks in the ventrolateral medulla contribute to the recurring pattern of inspiratory motor activity. Other medullary populations receive signals related to blood gases, airway state and cardiovascular variables and interact with autonomic and respiratory pathways. The nucleus of the solitary tract is an important recipient of visceral sensory signals, including those related to arterial pressure and blood chemistry. It integrates information rather than functioning as a direct peripheral receptor.

The arterial baroreflex provides a concrete connection with cardiovascular physiology. An increase in arterial pressure stretches arterial baroreceptors, changing afferent activity. Signals enter the brainstem through appropriate cranial sensory pathways and influence medullary circuits that adjust autonomic output. The resulting changes in heart activity and vascular tone tend to oppose the pressure change. The heart and blood vessels are peripheral effectors; the medullary network provides central integration. The receptor, afferent pathway, integrative circuitry and effector must all be distinguished. Blocking the afferent input has a different primary effect from blocking autonomic output to the heart.

Protective and feeding-related behaviours also depend on medullary and neighbouring brainstem circuits. Swallowing requires carefully timed activation of several muscles and coordination with breathing. Coughing and other airway-protective responses similarly depend on integration of sensory information and organised motor output. A central pattern-generating network can produce a coordinated sequence that differs from a simple one-synapse reflex. The relevant anatomical idea is that local brainstem circuitry can organise a complex response while remaining subject to sensory and descending modulation.

The reticular formation is a distributed network of neurons extending through much of the brainstem, rather than a single sharply bounded nucleus visible on every gross section. Its populations participate in arousal, postural control, autonomic regulation and other integrated functions. Ascending influences contribute to cortical activation and the organisation of waking and sleep states; descending influences contribute to motor and autonomic control. This organisation explains how local injury to brainstem systems can have widespread functional consequences. However, wakefulness, attention and consciousness are not identical phenomena, and they cannot be reduced to a single ascending pathway.

The term cranial nerve nucleus describes a group of CNS neurons associated with cranial nerve input or output. Many such nuclei occur within the brainstem, but the exact distribution varies with the nerve. The olfactory and optic pathways are associated with forebrain structures, while the spinal component of the accessory nerve illustrates an additional exception to a simple brainstem-only classification. The brainstem also contains nuclei whose major functions are not cranial nerve functions. The detailed organisation of individual peripheral cranial nerves belongs to 7.D.07.

Experimental interpretation of automatic control often requires separating central neural drive from peripheral effectiveness. If brainstem respiratory output persists while a respiratory muscle fails to contract, the failure lies downstream of the recorded central activity or in a parallel mechanism. If the central rhythmic signal disappears despite intact muscle responsiveness, a different level of explanation becomes plausible. Recording neural activity, muscle activity and actual ventilation provides information at three distinct stages. A single measurement of airflow cannot distinguish every possible mechanism of respiratory failure.

Gross functional anatomy is especially useful when predicting the difference between cerebral and brainstem injury. Severe cerebral damage may profoundly affect voluntary behaviour and awareness while some automatic brainstem-regulated functions persist. Conversely, disruption of critical brainstem networks can compromise physiological functions necessary for survival even when portions of cerebral cortex are structurally intact. Neither statement implies a complete independence of the two regions. Cortical and subcortical interactions ordinarily contribute to both voluntary and automatic behaviour.

11. Cerebellum: prediction, coordination and error-dependent adjustment

The cerebellum lies in the posterior cranial region, behind the pons and upper medulla and beneath posterior portions of the cerebral hemispheres. Its surface is organised into narrow folds called folia, which differ in appearance from the larger gyri of the cerebrum. The midline region is called the vermis, and paired cerebellar hemispheres lie on either side. In a midsagittal section, branching white matter can form a tree-like pattern traditionally called the arbor vitae. A grey-matter cortex covers much of the surface, while deep cerebellar nuclei lie within the internal white matter.

Gross anatomical divisions include anterior and posterior lobes and the flocculonodular lobe. Functional divisions are often described as vestibulocerebellum, spinocerebellum and cerebrocerebellum. These classifications are related but not interchangeable in every detail. Functional divisions emphasise major inputs and outputs: vestibular information is especially important for balance and gaze stability, spinal and related sensory input for ongoing body and limb control, and cerebral cortical input for planning and skilled movement. The anatomical vermis and intermediate regions are particularly relevant to posture and ongoing movement, while lateral hemispheric regions participate extensively in cerebrocerebellar loops.

The cerebellum helps organise the timing, accuracy and adaptation of movements. It does not normally provide the final axon that activates a skeletal muscle fibre. Rather, its outputs influence motor systems that generate or modify descending commands. To understand why its function is distinct from that of primary motor cortex, consider reaching toward a cup. The desired action requires an initial command, a suitable sequence of muscle activity, adjustment as the limb moves, and cessation at the correct location. The cerebellum contributes to coordinating these requirements and adapting them when the physical conditions change.

Sensory feedback inevitably arrives after the events that generated it. Nerve conduction, synaptic processing and mechanical events all require time. If a fast movement depended exclusively on correction after delayed sensory feedback, substantial errors could develop before the correction took effect. Cerebellar processing can contribute to predictive control, using information about intended actions and previous experience to help anticipate their consequences. Sensory feedback remains essential because predictions can be wrong. The relationship between prediction and feedback provides a functional reason for the cerebellum to receive both cortical signals and information about current body state.

A simple analogy is a person adjusting the force needed to lift an object that looks heavy but is unexpectedly light. The initial muscle command may be larger than necessary, and the object may move too quickly. The difference between expected and actual consequences provides information useful for subsequent trials. Repetition allows the command to become better matched to the object. The cerebellum contributes to this kind of error-dependent adaptation, but not every adjustment in a complex task is necessarily cerebellar. Other learning systems, attention and peripheral mechanics also contribute.

At the cellular level, the cerebellar cortex is organised into molecular, Purkinje-cell and granular layers. Purkinje cells have large, highly branched dendritic trees and are the principal output neurons of the cerebellar cortex. Their axons are inhibitory and project to deep cerebellar nuclei or, in certain pathways, vestibular nuclei. Mossy fibres provide important input through granule cells; granule-cell axons form parallel fibres that contact Purkinje cells and other cortical neurons. Climbing fibres originate in the inferior olive and form powerful excitatory connections with Purkinje cells. These are useful basic relationships for explaining how the cortex receives and processes information without requiring advanced synaptic models.

The inhibitory character of Purkinje-cell output does not imply that the cerebellum simply inhibits all movement. Deep nuclei receive both excitatory input and inhibition from Purkinje cells, and their output influences other brain regions. Changes in the timing and rate of inhibition can therefore alter the downstream pattern of activity. The physiological effect depends on the circuit and its current state. A reduction in Purkinje-cell activity can increase activity in a target neuron if other inputs remain constant; whether the resulting motor behaviour increases or decreases cannot be determined without knowing further connections.

Climbing-fibre activity is often associated with signals useful for learning and adjustment, including errors in certain motor tasks. It would be excessive to claim that every climbing-fibre signal is a universal numerical error signal or that cerebellar learning occurs at only one synapse. Several forms of neural plasticity and multiple circuit elements participate in adaptation. For the purposes of gross functional anatomy, the key distinction is between information reaching the cerebellum, processing within its cortex and nuclei, and the resulting modification of downstream motor systems. More detailed mechanisms can be linked to basic synaptic plasticity studied earlier.

Cerebellar impairment may produce ataxia, which means a disturbance of coordination, rather than complete inability to activate muscles. Dysmetria refers to inaccurate movement amplitude, such as overshooting or undershooting a target. Intention tremor may become more evident during an attempt to approach a target. Problems with rapid alternating movements and balance may also occur. The particular pattern depends on which cerebellar regions and connections are affected. These features help distinguish coordination failure from primary weakness, but they do not replace a complete physiological assessment.

The lateralisation of cerebellar effects can surprise students who have learned that cerebral motor cortex mainly influences the opposite side of the body. Many cerebellar influences on limb movement are expressed on the same side as the affected cerebellar hemisphere. This relationship reflects the crossing patterns of incoming and outgoing pathways, including subsequent crossings of descending motor signals. Rather than memorising a contradiction, trace the net relationship between a region and its peripheral target. The same hemisphere label can have different implications at different levels of the motor system.

A useful experimental approach compares performance on an established movement with adaptation to a new perturbation. Imagine that participants move a cursor toward a target while a visual display introduces a fixed rotation between hand direction and cursor direction. With practice, many participants gradually reduce the directional error. If an experimental group can execute the original movement but adapts more slowly to the perturbation, the findings suggest that the ability to update control in response to error is impaired. The interpretation should consider whether the participants can detect the visual error, understand the task and maintain attention.

After the perturbation is removed, a temporary deviation in the opposite direction can provide evidence that the motor system acquired an altered mapping rather than merely reacting to the visible error on each trial. This is known as an aftereffect. Its magnitude depends on the task, learning strategies and measurement conditions. An aftereffect does not identify a unique cellular learning mechanism by itself. The strongest interpretation combines behaviour with selective perturbation and neural recording where appropriate.

The cerebellum also participates in nonmotor functions through connections with association cortical networks. This does not negate its well-established motor roles. It illustrates that repeated circuit arrangements may contribute to more than one kind of information processing. In a chapter on gross anatomy, it is appropriate to emphasise coordination, balance, timing and adaptation while recognising that the cerebellum is not exclusively a motor organ. The finer details of motor pathways and vestibular integration are reserved for later nodes.

The contrast among cerebral cortex, basal ganglia and cerebellum is best expressed in terms of contributions to a common task. Cortical networks help represent goals and organise commands; basal ganglia circuits help regulate the selection and expression of actions; cerebellar circuits contribute to accurate timing and adaptive coordination. All three interact, and none alone explains the full movement. A student should be able to predict which performance measures would help separate these contributions: initiation latency, force capacity, trajectory error, adaptation across trials and sensory detection provide distinct information.

12. Meninges and mechanical protection

The organisation of brain tissue creates a particular need for mechanical and chemical protection. Neurons have long processes, and normal physiological function requires preservation of membrane gradients, synaptic arrangements and blood supply. The skull offers a rigid external enclosure, but the soft brain does not simply lie in direct contact with bone. Connective-tissue coverings called meninges enclose the central nervous system and help establish compartments in which vessels and cerebrospinal fluid are arranged. The three meninges are the dura mater, arachnoid mater and pia mater, named in order from superficial to deep.

The cranial dura is a tough fibrous covering. Its periosteal layer is closely associated with the inner surface of the skull, while its meningeal layer continues as the covering of the central nervous system. In certain places the layers separate to form dural venous sinuses that receive venous blood. Folds of the meningeal dura help partition the cranial cavity: the falx cerebri projects into the longitudinal fissure between the cerebral hemispheres, and the tentorium cerebelli separates much of the posterior cerebrum from the cerebellum. These folds provide support and limit displacement but do not prevent all movement of brain tissue.

The arachnoid mater lies deep to the dura and does not closely follow every small cortical groove. Its trabeculae extend across the subarachnoid space toward the pia. The subarachnoid space is a real fluid-containing compartment through which cerebrospinal fluid circulates around the brain and spinal cord; major cerebral vessels also traverse it. Where the space enlarges, it forms cisterns. These relationships are important because fluid and vessels occupy a specific anatomical compartment, rather than lying within the cerebral cortex itself.

The pia mater is the delicate covering closely applied to the surface of the brain and spinal cord. It follows the contours of many sulci and gyri more closely than the arachnoid. In a cross-sectional drawing, the pia can therefore be identified by its direct relationship to nervous tissue, while the arachnoid tends to bridge the recesses. The two are often referred to collectively as the leptomeninges, in contrast to the tougher dura. The name does not mean that the pia and arachnoid are one membrane; they remain distinct layers enclosing the subarachnoid compartment.

In the intact adult cranium the epidural and subdural spaces are normally potential spaces rather than large open fluid-filled cavities. The cranial epidural location lies between the skull and dura, while the subdural location is at the interface between dura and arachnoid. Blood or other material can accumulate in these locations when tissue layers separate abnormally. The spinal epidural space, by contrast, is a real anatomical space containing fat and venous vessels. This is a useful example of why the same named relationship should be interpreted with reference to the particular part of the central nervous system.

A collection of blood in one meningeal compartment can compress nervous tissue without initially destroying every neuron beneath it. Because the skull is relatively rigid, addition of volume can displace fluid, blood or tissue and eventually raise intracranial pressure. The mechanical consequences depend on the rate and location of accumulation and the ability of other compartments to accommodate the change. A slowly developing increase in volume may initially produce a different pressure response from a rapid increase of similar magnitude. The anatomical compartment therefore matters to the physiological outcome.

Cerebrospinal fluid provides buoyant support. An object immersed in a fluid experiences an upward force equal to the weight of fluid displaced. The net downward force on an immersed brain is consequently less than its weight measured without fluid support. The tissue is neither weightless nor perfectly protected against acceleration. Its density differs somewhat from that of the surrounding fluid, and connections to vessels and meninges impose mechanical constraints. Nevertheless, buoyancy reduces the load borne by structures at the base of the cranial cavity. Fluid movement and the coverings also contribute to distributing mechanical forces.

A simplified calculation clarifies the physical principle without suggesting that a brain behaves exactly like an isolated rigid object. Suppose an illustrative immersed object has mass 1.4 kilograms and displaces 1.35 litres of a fluid with density 1.0 kilogram per litre. Taking gravitational acceleration as 9.8 metres per second squared, the gravitational force is 13.72 newtons and the buoyant force is 13.23 newtons. The net downward force in this idealised static model is 0.49 newtons. Real tissue is deformable and not freely floating; the numerical values are illustrative, not measurements of an individual human brain. The calculation shows why similar object and fluid densities yield substantial buoyant support.

The meninges also have biological functions beyond passive enclosure. Cells at meningeal interfaces contribute to compartment boundaries, immune surveillance and interactions with fluid drainage. The barrier separating circulating blood from brain tissue is nevertheless not identical to the dura or arachnoid. Its principal anatomical basis lies at specialised cerebral microvascular endothelial cells, considered below. A student should distinguish physical protection by the skull, mechanical and compartmental organisation by meninges, buoyancy by cerebrospinal fluid, and regulated molecular exchange by specialised barriers.

13. Ventricles, cerebrospinal fluid production and circulation

The ventricles form a connected system of cavities within the brain. Each cerebral hemisphere contains a lateral ventricle; their shapes extend into different parts of the hemispheres. The third ventricle occupies a midline position in the diencephalon. The cerebral aqueduct links it with the fourth ventricle, which lies between the dorsal brainstem and the cerebellum. These spaces contain cerebrospinal fluid, abbreviated CSF. The arrangement of the ventricles follows the development of the neural tube and provides an anatomical map through otherwise complex structures.

Most CSF is produced by the choroid plexuses, specialised vascular and epithelial structures in the lateral, third and fourth ventricles. Capillaries of the choroid plexus are relatively permeable compared with the typical cerebral capillaries that form the blood–brain barrier. The critical blood–CSF barrier is provided by tight junctions between the choroid plexus epithelial cells. These cells regulate movement of solutes and water into the ventricular fluid. It is therefore incorrect to assume that CSF is simply an unregulated filtrate of plasma passing directly through holes in the capillary wall.

Ion transport establishes conditions favouring movement of water. ATP-dependent transport and coupled movement of ions across epithelial membranes contribute to net secretion, with water following osmotic gradients. The epithelial cells are polarised, meaning that their blood-facing and ventricular-facing membrane domains differ in the transport proteins they contain. This directional organisation is necessary for regulated secretion. The composition of CSF differs from that of plasma; it is normally relatively low in protein and cells and has a regulated ionic composition. Glucose enters through transport mechanisms, and its concentration in CSF need not equal that of blood at every instant.

Fluid formed in the lateral ventricles flows through the paired interventricular foramina, also called the foramina of Monro, toward the third ventricle. It then passes through the cerebral aqueduct, also called the aqueduct of Sylvius, toward the fourth ventricle. CSF can leave the fourth ventricle through a median aperture and paired lateral apertures to enter the subarachnoid space. The median aperture is conventionally called the foramen of Magendie and the lateral apertures the foramina of Luschka. Flow continues around the brain and spinal cord. Communication with the spinal central canal exists anatomically, but this canal is not the principal route through which adult human CSF leaves the ventricular system.

The classical account of CSF return to the circulation emphasises arachnoid villi and granulations that project into dural venous sinuses. CSF drainage also involves lymphatic routes, including pathways associated with cranial and spinal outflow and meningeal lymphatic vessels. The relative contributions of drainage mechanisms vary with species and physiological conditions, and fluid motion is influenced by pulsation and pressure changes. A simplified diagram of net flow is valuable, provided that it is understood as a representation of the principal routes rather than a claim that every molecule travels continuously in one direction at the same speed.

A mass-balance equation gives a useful quantitative framework. Let V denote the volume of CSF in millilitres, Qp the production rate in millilitres per hour, and Qd the effective drainage rate in the same units. The rate of change of volume is dV/dt = Qp − Qd. This equation follows from conservation of volume when additional sources and losses are either negligible or included in the defined rates. At a steady volume, mean production equals mean drainage. Steady volume does not mean that fluid production or circulation has stopped.

A typical adult teaching estimate is a total CSF volume of roughly 150 millilitres and production of roughly 500 millilitres per day, with substantial variation. Dividing 500 by 150 gives about 3.3 nominal volume equivalents produced per day. Dividing volume by mean production rate gives a nominal residence time of about 7.2 hours under a simple steady well-mixed model. This is not the measured travel time of every fluid parcel, because real flow and mixing are spatially complex. The calculation demonstrates that a small fluid compartment can undergo substantial continuous exchange. It also explains why production and drainage must remain coordinated.

Consider a hypothetical system producing 20 millilitres per hour while drainage temporarily falls to 15 millilitres per hour. If both rates remain constant for two hours, the net accumulation is 5 millilitres per hour multiplied by two hours, or 10 millilitres. This estimate assumes that neither production nor drainage changes in response to pressure, and that there is no compensating route. In the living brain, an increase in pressure can affect fluid movement, and other intracranial compartments can initially accommodate changes. The calculation is therefore a first approximation, not a prediction of the pressure that an individual would develop.

Obstruction of a narrow connection can cause fluid to accumulate upstream. If the cerebral aqueduct is blocked while production continues and alternative routes are insufficient, the lateral and third ventricles are expected to enlarge relative to the fourth. The fourth ventricle remains connected to its outlets, but it no longer receives normal flow from the third. An obstruction of one interventricular foramen can preferentially affect the corresponding lateral ventricle. These predictions arise from the topology of the ventricular system and do not require memorising a separate response for each location.

Hydrocephalus refers to abnormal accumulation of CSF associated with ventricular enlargement. Obstructive or noncommunicating hydrocephalus involves impaired passage through a portion of the ventricular pathways. Communicating hydrocephalus refers to situations in which communication from the ventricular system to subarachnoid spaces is not blocked at the usual internal connections, but fluid dynamics or drainage is abnormal. Excess production is a less common possible mechanism. Ventricular size alone cannot establish the precise mechanism: tissue loss can also enlarge ventricular spaces, a process distinct from active fluid accumulation.

The connection between volume and pressure can be described using compliance, defined locally as a change in volume divided by the resulting change in pressure. Units might be millilitres per millimetre of mercury. In a hypothetical range where adding 4 millilitres raises pressure by 2 millimetres of mercury, compliance is 2 millilitres per millimetre of mercury. If the same increment later raises pressure by 8 millimetres of mercury, compliance in that range is only 0.5. A rigid cranial enclosure does not imply zero effective compliance because fluid and venous blood can be displaced. Once compensatory capacity is reduced, relatively small additional volumes can produce larger pressure changes. The relationship is not generally linear.

A tracer experiment can illustrate fluid connectivity. Introduce a hypothetical inert detectable marker into a lateral ventricle in an experimental model, then sample downstream fluid compartments at appropriate times. Under normal connectivity the marker can reach the third and fourth ventricles and subarachnoid compartment. If it reaches the third ventricle but fails to appear in the fourth during a sufficiently long validated observation period, an interruption between them is a plausible explanation. The conclusion depends on controls for marker stability, detection sensitivity, injection location, physiological conditions and alternative routes. Tracer arrival establishes connectivity under the tested conditions; it does not alone establish a precise net production or drainage rate.

The CSF system contributes to chemical stability, transport of substances and removal of some metabolic products, as well as mechanical support. Exchange between CSF and interstitial fluid is important, but the two compartments should not be treated as identical. Local neuronal and glial activity alters extracellular chemistry, and specialised mechanisms regulate the environment at multiple interfaces. An examination question about a ventricular obstruction concerns fluid circulation; a question about selective transfer of a blood-borne molecule into neural interstitial fluid concerns a different anatomical barrier.

14. Blood–brain barrier and neurovascular support

The brain requires continuous delivery of oxygen and metabolic substrates, yet uncontrolled exchange between blood and the neural extracellular environment would interfere with neuronal signalling. A change in extracellular potassium can alter membrane potential; a change in transmitter concentration can alter synaptic responses. Plasma composition changes with meals, exercise and many other conditions. The nervous system must receive necessary resources while restricting uncontrolled entry of substances whose concentrations in blood fluctuate. The blood–brain barrier, abbreviated BBB, is a major solution to this problem.

The principal cellular basis of the BBB is the specialised endothelium of cerebral microvessels. Endothelial cells are joined by tight junctions that restrict paracellular movement between adjacent cells. Many brain endothelial cells also show relatively low nonspecific vesicular transport across the cell. A molecule that cannot pass between the cells must use an appropriate route through them or remain excluded. Selective transport proteins, metabolic enzymes and efflux systems further regulate exchange. The BBB is therefore a set of physiological properties of an interface, rather than a separate solid membrane surrounding the entire brain.

Pericytes are associated with the microvascular wall and contribute to vascular development, stability and maintenance of barrier properties. Astrocyte endfeet closely associate with much of the cerebral microvasculature and contribute to regulation and maintenance of the neural environment. Basement membranes provide structural and signalling relationships among the vascular and neural components. The term neurovascular unit describes the functional association of these components with neurons and other cells. Astrocyte endfeet support the barrier, but the tight junctions forming the principal paracellular seal of the BBB are between endothelial cells, not between astrocytes.

Small gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide readily cross membranes by diffusion, with net movement depending on gradients and physiological conditions. Glucose, a major energy substrate, requires specialised transport across the endothelium, prominently through GLUT1-mediated facilitated transport. Specific amino-acid transport systems also enable delivery of required substrates. Other compounds may be restricted because they are large, highly polar, poorly transported or actively removed by efflux transporters. The ability of a substance to dissolve in lipid is relevant to membrane permeation but does not guarantee unrestricted entry, since several mechanisms act together.

A simplified comparison illustrates the difference between passive movement and transport-mediated movement. If a compound moves mainly by diffusion across a barrier of constant area and permeability, a larger concentration difference can produce greater net flux. If another compound depends on a limited number of carriers, increasing concentration may eventually produce a diminishing increment in transport as carrier occupancy approaches saturation. Experimental measurements can distinguish a broadly linear concentration dependence over a tested range from a saturating response. However, a limited experimental range may not reveal saturation, and multiple pathways can operate simultaneously.

Not every blood-brain interface has identical permeability. Circumventricular organs include specialised regions with vascular properties that allow access to information about circulating substances. The area postrema and certain hypothalamus-associated regions are familiar examples. These locations support physiological detection or release functions while additional cellular arrangements help limit uncontrolled spread into neighbouring tissue. This is not equivalent to the entire brain being unprotected. The exception makes sense when related to the specific requirement to detect circulating chemical signals or release neuroendocrine substances.

The BBB and the blood–CSF barrier must be distinguished by location and cell type. In ordinary brain tissue, specialised capillary endothelial cells form the major barrier between blood and brain interstitial fluid. In the choroid plexus, epithelial tight junctions are central to the barrier between blood-derived material and ventricular CSF. The underlying capillaries of the plexus are not identical to typical BBB-forming microvessels. A tracer excluded from neural interstitial tissue but present in choroid plexus stroma would therefore not demonstrate an absence of all CNS barriers.

Classical studies of dye distribution helped reveal that blood and neural tissue do not exchange substances in the same way as many other tissues. A dye introduced into the circulation may stain peripheral tissues while leaving much of the brain relatively unstained, whereas direct introduction into CSF can produce a different distribution. Such observations support selective compartmentalisation. They do not alone establish whether the restriction occurs at vascular endothelium, at associated glial processes or through another mechanism. Microscopic localisation of markers and ultrastructural study of junctions provide complementary evidence. The review by Daneman and Prat describes how anatomical and physiological studies identify specialised endothelial barrier properties and their interactions with neighbouring cells.

A modern conceptual experiment could compare the brain accumulation of two labelled compounds that have similar circulating concentrations but differ in their ability to use a transport pathway. Appropriate measurements would distinguish marker remaining in blood vessels from marker that has reached neural tissue. The investigator would control dose, exposure time, systemic clearance and the stability of the label. A higher measured amount of one marker in brain does not automatically establish higher endothelial permeability if that marker remains bound to vascular surfaces or accumulates in a different compartment. The interpretation depends on the anatomical location of the detected material.

Selective increases in barrier permeability can alter neural function even without direct destruction of neurons. Entry of inappropriate solutes or proteins, changes in ion regulation and fluid movement, and inflammatory responses can affect the extracellular environment. Conversely, low delivery of essential substrates can impair energy metabolism and the ATP-dependent processes that maintain ionic gradients. Both excessive entry and insufficient delivery can be harmful. A well-functioning neurovascular interface must therefore combine selectivity with sufficient transport capacity.

The internal carotid and vertebral arterial systems provide major routes for blood delivery to the human brain. The vertebral arteries join to form the basilar artery, and arterial connections at the base of the brain form the circle of Willis. These anastomoses can provide alternative routes under some conditions but do not guarantee adequate collateral delivery after every obstruction. Vascular anatomy varies among individuals, and small downstream territories may have limited alternative supply. The anatomical map helps explain why a vascular interruption can affect a particular cortical or subcortical region rather than producing a perfectly uniform reduction in function.

Local neural activity is associated with changes in local metabolic demand. Neurovascular coupling describes mechanisms through which active neural networks influence local blood flow. Neuronal and glial signals act on vascular elements, changing resistance and delivery of blood. Functional magnetic resonance imaging often uses the blood oxygenation level-dependent signal, which reflects haemodynamic and oxygenation changes related to neural activity. The signal is indirect: it is not a direct count of action potentials, and its timing and magnitude depend on vascular as well as neural factors. Comparing groups with different vascular responses requires particular care.

Cerebral autoregulation describes the tendency of cerebral blood flow to remain relatively stable over a range of perfusion pressures. Vascular resistance changes help compensate for variations in driving pressure. This regulation is limited, differs among regions and conditions, and is influenced by factors such as carbon dioxide. A useful approximation for cerebral perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure when intracranial pressure is the relevant downstream pressure. For illustrative values of 90 and 15 millimetres of mercury respectively, the estimated driving pressure is 75 millimetres of mercury. If intracranial pressure rises to 25 at unchanged arterial pressure, the estimate falls to 65. Flow also depends on resistance, so the pressure change alone does not determine the exact flow reduction.

The quantitative relationship connects the protective systems considered in this chapter. Mechanical accumulation of fluid or tissue volume can alter intracranial pressure; pressure influences the driving force for cerebral circulation; blood flow supports metabolic delivery; and regulated exchange preserves the environment needed for neuronal excitability. A defect in a structure that is not itself a neuron can therefore compromise neuronal computation. The nervous system must be studied as an organ supported by vascular, epithelial, glial and connective-tissue components, not only as a network of electrical cells.

15. Reading anatomical and experimental evidence

A reliable approach to a gross neuroanatomical question begins by identifying the plane, orientation and scale of the evidence. In a lateral drawing of a cerebral hemisphere, the central and lateral sulci help define cortical territories. In a midsagittal drawing, the corpus callosum, diencephalic region, aqueduct and cerebellum provide landmarks. In a coronal section, paired nuclei and white-matter pathways can be identified relative to the ventricles and internal capsule. The same name should not be assigned to two similarly coloured shapes without considering these spatial relationships.

Structural imaging and physiological methods answer different questions. Conventional magnetic resonance imaging can reveal differences in tissue structure and fluid compartments. Diffusion-sensitive imaging can provide evidence about the organisation of white-matter pathways, but inferred fibre orientations do not necessarily establish a direct synaptic connection or direction of signalling. Electrophysiological recordings reveal the timing of electrical responses in sampled neurons or populations. Functional imaging reports signals related to neural or metabolic activity at a different spatial and temporal scale. A complete interpretation recognises the limitations of each method.

An anatomical connection is a necessary condition for direct axonal communication, but it does not prove that the connection is responsible for a particular behaviour. A recording showing activity during a task establishes association with that task; it may reflect sensory input, movement, feedback or another correlated event. Selective perturbation can provide evidence for necessity or contribution, especially when the effect is reversible and unrelated functions remain intact. Stimulation can provide evidence that activation is capable of influencing an outcome under the tested conditions. Neither necessity nor sufficiency in an experimental context implies that the entire behaviour normally originates in one structure.

Suppose a hypothetical study compares two groups in a reaching task. One group shows a long delay before initiating the movement but relatively accurate trajectories after initiation. The other starts promptly but overshoots the target and shows little improvement over repeated trials with altered feedback. Both groups have similar maximal grip force and can detect the visual target. The first pattern is more consistent with disrupted action selection or initiation, and the second with disrupted coordination and adaptation. The data do not uniquely identify basal ganglia or cerebellar lesions; they guide a hypothesis that requires anatomical and physiological confirmation.

A useful test for specificity is a double dissociation: one manipulation impairs task A while relatively preserving task B, and another impairs B while relatively preserving A. In the memory context, separate tests of new declarative learning and skill improvement can expose distinct contributions of different systems. In the movement context, separate measures of initiation and adaptation can reveal different physiological constraints. A double dissociation is stronger than observing that one difficult task deteriorates after a lesion, because differences in general task difficulty alone become less persuasive as an explanation. It still requires carefully matched controls and a clear operational definition of each task.

The chapter's regional map is a foundation for the next levels of nervous-system physiology. The cerebral cortex receives and transforms sensory information and helps organise actions. Deep forebrain structures participate in selecting, regulating and remembering behaviour. The diencephalon links cortical processing to sensory, endocrine and internal-state information. The brainstem coordinates essential functions and connects higher regions with spinal and peripheral pathways. The cerebellum contributes to the timing and adaptive accuracy of behaviour. Protective interfaces maintain the environment in which these circuits can operate. The spinal cord chapter will extend this map to the pathways and local circuits through which many central decisions become sensory-motor responses.
