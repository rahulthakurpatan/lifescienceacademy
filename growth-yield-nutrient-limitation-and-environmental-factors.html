1. Why growth requires more than a supply of food

Two microbial cultures receive identical amounts of glucose. One begins to grow rapidly, becomes cloudy within a few hours and eventually stops increasing in biomass. The other grows more slowly but produces more dry cell material by the time its glucose supply is exhausted. Neither observation is contradictory. The first culture has a higher rate of increase during the measured interval; the second may be more efficient at converting the available substrate into new cell material. These are different properties of living populations. Understanding the distinction is necessary before attempting to explain why a microorganism grows well in one habitat but poorly in another.

The preceding chapters described growth curves and the rate of population increase. In the ideal exponential phase, the number of cells increases in proportion to the number already present, provided that the conditions permit the same average rate of division. That mathematical description leaves a biological question unanswered: where do the atoms and usable energy come from to construct each daughter cell? An equation describing doubling does not guarantee that enough material exists for another doubling. A population cannot continue increasing indefinitely in a closed vessel, however rapidly its cells are able to divide initially.

Each cell contains a membrane, proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrate-containing structures and small metabolites. To increase the amount of any of these components, the cell must obtain their constituent elements from somewhere. To combine those elements into a highly organized system, it must also couple energetically unfavorable biosynthetic reactions to favorable processes. A bacterium incorporating carbon from a sugar into amino acids, for example, does not simply retain intact sugar molecules. It transforms the substrate into intermediates, transfers nitrogen to carbon skeletons, adjusts the oxidation states of intermediates and uses activated carriers to make peptide bonds.

A substrate is a substance acted upon in a metabolic reaction or, in the broader context of microbial growth, a resource consumed by the organism. Glucose is a convenient example because many familiar organisms use it as both a carbon and an energy source. But the same definition does not require all microorganisms to use glucose. An organism that fixes carbon dioxide may use an inorganic electron donor and an entirely different energy source. When reading a growth experiment, the identity of the measured substrate is therefore as important as its concentration.

The term biomass means the amount of cellular material in a population. In many quantitative studies the operational measurement is dry mass rather than wet mass, because water content changes with osmotic conditions and cellular composition. A gram of wet cells may contain substantially different amounts of cellular solids in two different environments. Dry mass reduces this ambiguity, although it still includes storage polymers and may include material from cells that are no longer viable. Biomass is not itself a measure of the number of living individuals.

The connection between number and mass can be written as X = N times the average cell dry mass, when X represents total dry mass and N the number of individual cells. If N is expressed per litre instead, X must also be expressed per litre. This relationship is a useful accounting identity, not a biological assertion that every cell is identical. In a population containing long filaments, small newborn cells and cells accumulating storage material, the average mass can change markedly. A culture may gain mass before a new division occurs. Conversely, daughter cells can separate while the mass of the population changes only slightly over that short period. Cell number, biomass and metabolic activity are therefore complementary observations.

Growth yield relates the material gained to the resource used. A yield of 0.5 grams of dry biomass per gram of substrate consumed means that a particular net biomass increment of one gram was associated with disappearance of two grams of the measured substrate under specified conditions. It does not mean that half of every individual substrate molecule entered the cell unchanged. Some molecules can be oxidized completely, others rearranged into precursor molecules and others released in metabolic products. Moreover, nitrogen, hydrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and other atoms enter biomass from sources other than the measured substrate. The yield is an operational relationship between measurable amounts.

Two independent questions arise from this accounting. First, what is the potential growth rate when a particular cell has access to its required resources under suitable conditions? Second, what total amount of biomass can be supported by the available supply? A nutrient can influence both answers, but it need not influence both in the same direction or by the same proportion. The remainder of this chapter builds the explanation from the chemical requirements of biomass through the energetic costs of building and maintaining cells, the meaning of nutrient limitation, and the environmental conditions that determine whether these processes are possible.

2. What a growing microorganism must obtain

The periodic table contains far more elements than a cell requires for ordinary growth. The essential elements are those for which the organism cannot complete its normal growth and reproduction without a supply or a usable substitute in a particular physiological context. Essentiality does not mean that the element must be present at a high concentration. Iron, for instance, is needed in relatively small amounts in many organisms, yet the lack of iron can disable an electron transfer system and stop growth despite abundant carbon.

Carbon forms the framework of most biological organic molecules. Its ability to form stable bonds with itself and other elements allows carbohydrates, membrane lipids, proteins and nucleic acids to possess a remarkable range of structures. A cell needs carbon in a form it can assimilate. Assimilation means the incorporation of a nutrient into cellular material through metabolism. Carbon in a chemically inaccessible polymer may be present in large total amounts without being available to an organism that lacks the enzymes needed to break the polymer down. Even soluble carbon compounds differ in accessibility when transport proteins or metabolic pathways are absent.

Hydrogen and oxygen occur throughout biomolecules and are also supplied by water and other compounds. Water is the solvent in which most metabolic transformations occur. It permits diffusion, stabilizes macromolecular structures and participates in hydrolysis and other reactions. The elemental requirement for oxygen in cellular material should not be confused with the physiological requirement for gaseous molecular oxygen. An obligate anaerobe still contains oxygen atoms in its DNA, proteins and carbohydrates. Its inability to tolerate molecular oxygen does not eliminate its need for oxygen-containing molecules.

Nitrogen is required for amino groups in amino acids and for nitrogenous bases in nucleic acids. When carbon is plentiful but assimilable nitrogen is absent, the organism may obtain energy from the carbon source yet be unable to sustain the production of new proteins and nucleic acids. Ammonium is a readily usable nitrogen source for many organisms because it supplies reduced nitrogen. Nitrate can supply nitrogen to organisms capable of assimilatory reduction, which uses reducing equivalents to convert nitrate through intermediates to ammonium before incorporation. Molecular nitrogen is available directly as a nitrogen source only to organisms with nitrogen-fixing machinery. The fact that nitrogen gas is abundant in the atmosphere does not mean that all organisms have unrestricted access to biologically usable nitrogen.

The nitrogen incorporated into biomass must be distributed among many products. A central example is the formation of glutamate and glutamine, which serve as important donors of amino nitrogen or amide nitrogen in biosynthesis. Many metabolic reactions then transfer that nitrogen to other carbon skeletons. If the cell lacks a sufficient nitrogen supply, carbon skeletons alone cannot complete those reactions. The balance of carbon and nitrogen is therefore not merely an ecological statistic: it reflects the biochemical need to bring different chemical building blocks together.

Phosphorus is needed in nucleic acids, phospholipids, ATP and many phosphorylated metabolic intermediates. Its absence affects both structural synthesis and energy-related reactions. Inorganic phosphate is commonly the form supplied in a defined growth medium. Some microorganisms can release phosphate from certain organic compounds or mobilize stored polyphosphate, but these capabilities differ. Total phosphorus measured after chemically digesting an environmental sample may thus overestimate the immediate nutritional phosphate supply to a particular organism.

Sulfur is present in cysteine and methionine, sulfur-containing cofactors and iron-sulfur clusters. A sulfate-assimilating organism can reduce sulfate and incorporate the reduced sulfur into organic molecules. This reduction requires energy and electrons. An organism capable of using a reduced organic sulfur compound may avoid some of that expenditure. Assimilatory sulfate reduction should be distinguished from dissimilatory sulfate reduction, in which sulfate serves in energy metabolism as a terminal electron acceptor and much of its reduced product is released. One chemical species can have different physiological roles.

Potassium contributes to ionic homeostasis, enzyme function and the maintenance of appropriate intracellular conditions. Magnesium helps stabilize ribosomes and nucleic acids and is frequently associated with ATP in enzyme reactions. The negatively charged phosphate groups of ATP interact with magnesium ions; an enzyme may recognize a magnesium-ATP complex rather than free ATP. Removing available magnesium can therefore affect both the protein-synthesizing machinery and reactions that consume ATP. Calcium has organism-dependent roles, including stabilization of some structures, and is associated with certain differentiated cell states such as bacterial endospores.

Trace elements are often required as components of metalloproteins or cofactors. Iron participates in hemes and iron-sulfur proteins. Zinc can stabilize particular protein structures and contribute to catalytic sites. Molybdenum is important in several enzyme systems, including common forms of nitrate reductase and nitrogenase. Nickel, cobalt, manganese and copper also have roles in selected microbial pathways. These examples should be interpreted by reference to the organism's metabolic capacities rather than as a claim that every species needs every metal in equal proportions.

The usefulness of a metal depends on chemical form. Iron may be present in a medium yet remain poorly soluble. Some microorganisms synthesize siderophores, molecules that bind iron strongly and facilitate its acquisition. This can improve availability under iron-limited conditions. A high total metal concentration, in contrast, may become inhibitory when excess reactive metal accumulates or occupies inappropriate binding sites in proteins. The biological response is often therefore nonmonotonic: deficiency at one end, an adequate interval, and toxicity at the other.

Macronutrients and micronutrients are distinguished by the quantities required, not by their importance to the cell. A small supply of a micronutrient may support a considerable amount of biomass because it is needed in only a few molecular sites per cell. However, if that supply becomes insufficient for the next generation of enzyme molecules, metabolism can fail. Essential nutrient requirements can be understood as a set of material constraints, with the cell's biochemical machinery determining the accessible chemical forms and the quantities needed.

3. Carbon sources, energy sources and electron donors are separate classifications

The words autotroph and heterotroph describe how cellular carbon is acquired. Autotrophs obtain most of their biomass carbon from inorganic carbon, commonly carbon dioxide or bicarbonate. Heterotrophs obtain carbon principally from organic compounds. This classification addresses construction material; it does not specify the source of energy for constructing that material. Carbon dioxide is already highly oxidized, and reducing it into organic carbon requires both energy and electrons. An autotroph therefore needs mechanisms that supply these resources.

Phototrophs obtain usable energy from light. Chemotrophs obtain usable energy from chemical reactions. In a phototroph, absorbed light can initiate electron transfer and the generation of a membrane electrochemical gradient. In a chemotroph, energy is released by chemical transformations such as oxidation of an electron donor coupled to reduction of an electron acceptor. These alternatives are independent of the carbon-source distinction: a phototroph may be autotrophic or heterotrophic, and so may a chemotroph.

A further distinction identifies the source of reducing electrons. Lithotrophs obtain electrons from inorganic donors, such as hydrogen, reduced sulfur compounds or ferrous iron, depending on their metabolic machinery. Organotrophs obtain electrons from organic donors. The donor is oxidized in the relevant reaction. The acceptor is reduced. Electrons transferred from the donor may eventually help conserve energy or may be diverted to reductive biosynthetic reactions. The exact route varies, but the need to obtain and appropriately dispose of electrons follows from chemical conservation and redox balance.

Many familiar bacteria and fungi are chemoorganoheterotrophs. In an organism growing on glucose, carbon atoms may enter biosynthesis through glycolytic or related intermediates. The oxidation of another fraction of the glucose supplies energy and reducing equivalents. Glucose consequently serves several roles in this example. That overlap is a property of the specific nutrient and organism, not a universal rule. If the organism grows with carbon dioxide as its main carbon source and hydrogen as an energy-yielding electron donor, an increase in hydrogen supply can enhance carbon fixation without hydrogen supplying the carbon atoms.

Cyanobacteria provide a familiar example of oxygenic phototrophy, with light supplying energy, water supplying electrons and inorganic carbon supplying biomass carbon. Their requirements also include nitrogen, phosphorus, trace metals and other essential resources. If phosphate is limiting, greater light intensity cannot replace the missing phosphorus in nucleotides. A photosynthetic cell may continue absorbing light without increasing biomass. This illustrates why the terms energy limitation and nutrient limitation cannot always be treated as synonyms.

Some purple nonsulfur bacteria exhibit photoheterotrophic growth in appropriate conditions, combining light-derived energy with organic carbon sources. Their existence makes it clear that light utilization does not automatically imply autotrophic carbon fixation. Nitrifying microorganisms illustrate another combination: organisms that oxidize reduced inorganic nitrogen compounds can conserve chemical energy and use it for carbon assimilation, when growing autotrophically. Their nitrogen compounds may therefore participate in energy metabolism in addition to, or separately from, the nitrogen incorporated into biomass.

A common misconception is that an inorganic energy source makes the cell independent of organic compounds altogether. The cell itself must still manufacture organic compounds. What differs is whether those compounds need to be supplied externally as the main carbon source. A second misconception is that all microorganisms that consume organic carbon release the same amount of energy from it. Different pathways extract different fractions of the available free energy; the nature of the electron acceptor is often decisive. The yield per gram of organic substrate can therefore vary strongly even when the substrate is unchanged.

To read a question involving these categories, identify three properties separately: the material contributing most of the biomass carbon, the process supplying energy, and the source of electrons. If the question specifies oxygen, decide whether it is acting as a terminal electron acceptor rather than incorrectly assigning it as the electron donor. If it specifies light, do not assume that light contributes carbon atoms. If it specifies ammonia, determine from context whether ammonia is being assimilated, oxidized for energy, or both. This disciplined classification prevents confusion when the same nutrient appears in multiple roles.

4. From nutritional requirements to a growth medium

A growth medium is the physical and chemical environment supplying nutrients and permitting an organism to grow. The same nominal carbon concentration can produce very different outcomes when the other constituents of the medium differ. To attribute an observed change to a particular nutrient, scientists need to know what other sources of carbon, nitrogen, energy and growth factors are present. This requirement explains why chemically defined and complex media serve different experimental purposes.

A chemically defined medium has a known chemical composition in which the identity and amount of each supplied constituent are specified. A simple defined medium for an organism capable of synthesizing its own essential organic compounds can include a suitable carbon and energy source, inorganic nitrogen, phosphate, sulfur and mineral salts. The precise requirements differ among organisms. A medium that supports one organism may be incomplete for another, even when both are grown at the same temperature and external pH.

A complex medium contains ingredients whose molecular composition is not completely specified, such as yeast extract or protein digests. These ingredients can supply many amino acids, peptides, vitamins and other substances in addition to the nominal major substrate. Complex media are useful for supporting organisms with demanding nutritional requirements. They are less suitable for interpreting an exact yield on a single supplied carbon source unless the contributions of the other ingredients are accounted for. Otherwise the measured biomass may partly derive from carbon and energy that were omitted from the calculation.

Growth factors are organic compounds required in small amounts by organisms that cannot synthesize sufficient quantities for their needs. A vitamin used as the precursor of a coenzyme is a typical example. Some cells require particular amino acids or nucleotide precursors from their surroundings because a biosynthetic pathway is missing or nonfunctional. An organism that can make a particular required compound from the ingredients of a suitable minimal medium is described as prototrophic with respect to that nutritional requirement. A mutant unable to make the compound and requiring it externally is described as auxotrophic for that compound.

Consider two otherwise similar strains supplied with glucose, mineral salts and inorganic nitrogen. One synthesizes all required amino acids. The other has lost an enzyme essential for synthesizing tryptophan. The second strain may import glucose, perform energy metabolism and make many cellular intermediates, but it cannot sustain ordinary protein synthesis without the missing amino acid. Adding tryptophan can restore growth if no other requirement is missing. Adding more glucose does not repair the missing reaction. The experiment is informative because the added nutrient specifically bypasses the biochemical defect rather than merely increasing the general richness of the medium.

A useful control is the parental strain in the same unsupplemented medium. If neither strain grows, the missing tryptophan pathway cannot by itself explain the result. A supplemented condition for each strain can establish that the medium otherwise supports growth. A cell-free medium control helps identify changes in optical readings due to precipitation or chemical reactions. These controls are logical comparisons, not specialist cultivation protocols. Their purpose is to distinguish an organism-specific requirement from an environmental defect shared by all cultures.

The ability of a nutrient to support growth also depends on transport. A large extracellular concentration cannot guarantee a large intracellular supply if the cell lacks a suitable uptake system or the transported chemical form is inappropriate. Transport proteins may require ATP directly or exploit an ion gradient. Accordingly, a defect in energy conservation can appear experimentally as a nutritional limitation even though the chemical composition outside the cell is adequate. Earlier chapters on membrane transport provide the explanation: concentration outside the cell and access inside the cell are not equivalent.

Defined composition, nutrient accessibility and organism-specific requirements therefore determine whether a resource can be used at all. Once the resources enter the metabolic network, a different question arises: how much of the consumed material is converted into new biomass? Answering that question requires a quantitative definition.

5. Deriving and interpreting the biomass yield coefficient

Consider a closed culture containing a known quantity of a particular substrate. Let X₀ denote the initial dry biomass concentration and X₁ the biomass concentration at a later sampling time. Let S₀ and S₁ be the initial and later concentrations of the substrate, expressed in grams per litre. We assume that the liquid volume is effectively constant, that no additional substrate has been introduced between the observations, and that the measured disappearance represents consumption rather than an unaccounted physical loss. The net biomass produced is X₁ − X₀. The amount of substrate consumed is S₀ − S₁. The observed biomass yield on substrate is defined by their ratio.

Yₓ/ₛ = (X₁ − X₀)/(S₀ − S₁) = ΔX/(−ΔS).

The sign convention matters. If the substrate is consumed, its change ΔS = S₁ − S₀ is negative. The minus sign converts that negative change into a positive amount of substrate consumed. For a culture that has increased in mass, ΔX is positive. Thus the usual growth yield is positive. A negative value calculated from two data points may be meaningful as a net loss of biomass while the substrate is disappearing, but it does not represent ordinary positive biomass synthesis. It demands interpretation of death, lysis, measurement error or the physiological state of the cells.

The dimensions of Yₓ/ₛ are obtained by dividing grams of dry biomass per litre by grams of substrate per litre. The volume units cancel, leaving grams of dry biomass per gram of substrate. It is good practice to retain the notation g biomass/g substrate even though a ratio of two masses is formally dimensionless. The labels identify which materials are being compared. If one measurement is in milligrams per litre and the other is in grams per litre, they must first be expressed on compatible bases. A numerical value without that conversion can be wrong by a factor of one thousand.

The definition uses changes rather than final concentrations. The inoculum, meaning the starting microbial population added to the medium, already contains biomass before the substrate under study has been consumed. That pre-existing material is not a product of the measured growth interval. Similarly, the original substrate concentration is not necessarily the amount that has been consumed. Some substrate may remain in the medium after growth stops, perhaps because another nutrient has become limiting. Using X₁/S₀ automatically assumes both a negligible inoculum and complete substrate utilization; neither assumption is justified unless stated or established.

Worked example 1. A hypothetical closed culture begins with 0.30 g/L of dry biomass and 12.0 g/L of a measured organic substrate. When sampled later, the biomass is 3.90 g/L and the residual substrate is 4.0 g/L. The net biomass increment is 3.90 − 0.30 = 3.60 g/L. The amount consumed is 12.0 − 4.0 = 8.0 g/L. Division gives Yₓ/ₛ = 3.60/8.0 = 0.45 g/g. If the student instead divides the final biomass by the initial substrate, the result is 3.90/12.0 = 0.325 g/g. That answer describes a different ratio and underestimates the observed growth yield here.

The yield expression can be rearranged when the yield is known and approximately constant over the interval: ΔX = Yₓ/ₛ(S₀ − S₁). Adding the initial biomass gives X₁ = X₀ + Yₓ/ₛ(S₀ − S₁). This predicts the final net biomass for the specified consumption. It is not a time-dependent growth law. It contains no duration or specific growth rate. A slow culture and a fast culture can reach the same predicted endpoint if they have the same initial biomass, consume the same amount of substrate and maintain the same observed yield. The rearranged expression is valid only when the assumed yield represents the interval of interest.

Worked example 2. A culture starts with 0.15 g/L of biomass. It consumes 6.0 g/L of substrate and its observed yield is 0.40 g/g. The predicted increment is 0.40 × 6.0 = 2.40 g/L. The final biomass concentration is 2.55 g/L. In a constant 0.50 L culture this corresponds to 2.55 × 0.50 = 1.275 g of total dry material, of which 1.20 g was gained during the interval. The calculation illustrates why the units must specify whether the question is about total mass or concentration.

If the culture volume does change, the simple concentration difference is not generally a valid mass difference. Suppose the biomass concentration remains constant while water evaporates and no biomass is produced or destroyed. In fact a constant concentration in a shrinking volume would imply a reduction in total biomass; a culture in which biomass is conserved would instead show a rising concentration. The point is that concentrations depend on both amount and volume. With different initial and final volumes, the net biomass change must be calculated from X₁V₁ − X₀V₀. For substrate, S₀V₀ − S₁V₁ is a net disappearance and can be called consumption only if feed, sampling, leakage and other flows or losses are absent or accounted for. The detailed treatment of flowing cultures belongs to bioprocess engineering; here the necessary principle is conservation of mass.

Worked example 3. An illustrative culture begins at 1.0 L with X₀ = 0.20 g/L and S₀ = 8.0 g/L. It ends at 0.80 L with X₁ = 3.0 g/L and S₁ = 2.5 g/L. Assume water alone was lost, with no material removed in samples. Biomass amounts are 0.20 g initially and 2.40 g finally, so the gain is 2.20 g. Substrate amounts are 8.0 g and 2.0 g, so consumption is 6.0 g. The mass-based yield is 2.20/6.0 = 0.367 g/g, approximately. The concentration-only ratio 2.8/5.5 = 0.509 g/g is incorrect for this changing volume. Such a difference is a measurement-basis problem, not evidence that evaporation improved the true physiology.

A yield is usually specified with respect to one substrate. It may be a mass yield, a molar yield or a yield expressed per amount of an element such as carbon. These quantities must not be interchanged without a conversion. For glucose, whose molar mass is approximately 180 g/mol, a mass yield of 0.40 g biomass per gram of glucose corresponds to 72 g biomass per mole of glucose. Biomass itself is a mixture, not a compound with a single universally defined molecular weight. Writing moles of biomass requires a stated empirical formula or another explicitly defined basis; grams of dry material avoid that difficulty.

The yield coefficient is an observed physiological property for specified conditions, rather than a fixed constant belonging permanently to a species. The substrate identity, energy-conserving pathway, nutrient supply, growth rate, temperature, pH and period of measurement may all influence it. The measured value is a net outcome; it includes effects of any biomass losses that occurred during that interval. One must therefore state whether the measurement was made during active growth, across the entire batch growth period, or after prolonged starvation.

6. Where does the consumed substrate go?

A cell converts imported nutrients through networks of enzyme-catalysed reactions. Some reactions provide intermediates for biosynthesis, some conserve energy, and others dispose of products that cannot be used further under the prevailing conditions. The distinction between assimilation and dissimilation helps organize these events. Assimilation retains material within the organism as cellular constituents. Dissimilation refers broadly to breakdown processes serving metabolic energy production rather than retention of all substrate material as biomass. The same substrate can contribute to both.

Glucose metabolism provides a familiar illustration. A glucose molecule entering a heterotrophic cell can be phosphorylated and transformed into smaller intermediates. Some intermediates supply carbon skeletons for amino acids, fatty acids or nucleotide precursors. Other intermediates undergo further oxidation. The electrons released during oxidation can reduce carriers, and the resulting reduced carriers may participate in respiration or be reoxidized during fermentation. The cell must distribute substrate carbon and electrons among these alternative destinations while maintaining a supply of ATP and biosynthetic precursors.

The dry biomass formed is not chemically identical to the original substrate. A protein synthesized from glucose-derived carbon also contains nitrogen, and some amino acids contain sulfur. Nucleic acids include nitrogen and phosphorus. Biomass formation therefore depends on incorporation of material from several sources. In aerobic metabolism, oxygen is involved in electron acceptance and may end up in water; carbon dioxide can carry away carbon that was initially present in the substrate. Fermentative pathways can release organic products that still contain substantial chemical energy and substrate carbon. Neither a lower mass yield nor a higher mass yield alone identifies the exact pathway responsible.

A carbon balance offers a more precise way to reason about these destinations. If the only new carbon entering a closed culture is a measured organic substrate, and there is no net inorganic carbon assimilation or unmeasured carbon input, the carbon removed from that substrate must be accounted for in newly accumulated biomass carbon, released carbon dioxide and other carbon-containing products, together with any changes in unmeasured pools. Carbon conservation is not the same as whole-mass conservation because other elements and water enter and leave the biomass. A comparison of dry biomass mass with glucose mass does not directly determine the fraction of glucose carbon retained.

Worked example 4. Suppose 10.0 g of glucose is consumed and 4.0 g of new dry biomass is produced. Assume, for this hypothetical calculation, that glucose contains 40% carbon by mass and the new biomass contains 50% carbon by mass. The glucose supplies 4.0 g of carbon, while biomass retains 2.0 g of carbon. The mass yield is 4.0/10.0 = 0.40 g/g, whereas the fraction of substrate carbon retained in biomass is 2.0/4.0 = 0.50, or 50%. The remaining 2.0 g of carbon must be accounted for elsewhere if the stated assumptions hold. It cannot be assigned exclusively to carbon dioxide unless other carbon-containing products and pools have been measured or excluded.

A useful extension of this example shows why measuring one metabolic product may be insufficient. Suppose the same system releases 4.4 g of carbon dioxide. With molar masses of 12 g/mol for carbon and 44 g/mol for carbon dioxide, the carbon in that gas is 4.4 × 12/44 = 1.2 g. This leaves 0.8 g of the original carbon unaccounted for after subtracting biomass carbon and measured gas carbon. The missing carbon could be present in soluble organic products or other pools. It is not legitimate to claim that all of the glucose was fully oxidized merely because carbon dioxide was detected. The balance instead identifies an additional measurement required for a convincing interpretation.

Substrate disappearance should not automatically be equated with oxidation. A cell can take up a molecule and store it in a polymer without immediately oxidizing it. Alternatively, the substrate may be converted to a secreted product while producing some ATP. A chemical assay measures how much of a specified compound remains, not all of the pathways through which it has passed. If the question asks why the biomass yield fell although the rate of substrate disappearance increased, consider whether more carbon was directed toward secreted products, whether the energy cost of maintenance increased, or whether biomass loss increased. These mechanisms make different predictions and require different observations to distinguish them.

The use of an uninoculated control becomes important when the substrate is unstable, adsorbs to vessel surfaces or changes chemically under the test conditions. Substrate disappearing in that control was not consumed by living cells. Similarly, the biomass measurement should exclude residual medium solids when possible. If extracellular polymeric material is included with the cells, the operational definition of biomass changes. Such considerations do not invalidate yield measurements; they establish what the measurements actually represent.

7. How energy conservation influences biomass production

The synthesis of a cell requires more than a collection of appropriate atoms. Chemical reactions must be directed toward the formation of organized products. Polymer synthesis, active transport, maintenance of ion gradients and molecular repair consume usable energy. ATP provides a familiar means of coupling cellular reactions, while reduced cofactors supply electrons for biosynthesis. The metabolic pathway by which a cell obtains these resources influences the amount of substrate needed to construct a particular amount of biomass.

Substrate-level phosphorylation produces ATP by transferring a phosphoryl group from a suitable metabolic intermediate to ADP. This is a direct chemical coupling. In oxidative phosphorylation, electron transfer through a membrane-associated respiratory system helps establish an electrochemical ion gradient. ATP synthase couples ion movement down that gradient to ATP formation. Many bacteria use the cytoplasmic membrane for these processes, rather than the inner mitochondrial membrane of eukaryotes. The underlying bioenergetic principle is the same: an energetically favorable flow is coupled to an otherwise unfavorable synthesis.

During aerobic respiration, oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor. The transfer of electrons from suitable donors to oxygen can provide a substantial free-energy decrease, allowing many organisms to conserve considerable energy from the oxidation of organic substrates. Under oxygen limitation, an organism capable of alternative respiratory pathways may use nitrate, fumarate or another appropriate acceptor if available. The energy conserved depends on the redox chemistry and the way the electron transfer system is coupled to ion translocation; there is no single ATP yield that describes all microbes or all acceptors.

Fermentation provides a different means of maintaining redox balance. In a standard example, glucose is converted to pyruvate through glycolysis, producing a net gain of two ATP molecules per glucose and reduced NADH. If NADH is not reoxidized to NAD⁺, the reactions requiring NAD⁺ cannot continue indefinitely. In lactic fermentation, electrons from NADH are transferred to pyruvate, generating lactate and regenerating NAD⁺. In alcoholic fermentation, a pyruvate-derived intermediate accepts electrons and ethanol is formed. Other fermentations have different products and ATP balances. The defining point for these textbook examples is that internally generated organic molecules accept electrons and extensive energy remains in the released products.

The metabolic comparison leads to a conditional prediction. If two cultures of a facultative organism consume the same quantity of an organic substrate, and other growth conditions and cell composition are comparable, efficient respiration may support a greater biomass yield than fermentation because more usable energy is conserved per substrate molecule. This is an expectation, not an absolute law. Some organisms use mixed pathways, have different maintenance demands, or divert resources into products even in the presence of oxygen. Oxygen availability alone does not establish the exact energy-conservation mechanism.

The historical work of Bauchop and Elsden in 1960 helped make the connection between energy supply and microbial growth experimentally explicit. They compared dry biomass yields in microorganisms using different energy-yielding substrates and related them to estimated ATP production. Their study encouraged researchers to express biomass formation per amount of ATP generated rather than considering substrate mass alone. It also highlighted the need to know the metabolic products and reactions contributing to energy conservation. ATP production in such an analysis is inferred from pathway stoichiometry; a bulk cellular ATP concentration is not the amount of ATP produced over the entire growth period.

Why is this distinction so important? ATP can be synthesized, consumed and regenerated many times. A cell containing a relatively constant amount of ATP during exponential growth may be making and hydrolysing ATP rapidly. Measuring ATP at the beginning and end of an experiment may show little net change even when much energy has passed through the ATP pool. Similarly, a culture with a larger ATP pool does not necessarily produce more biomass from the same amount of glucose. What matters is the balance between the rate of production, the rate of consumption and the way ATP-consuming reactions contribute to net biomass formation.

Bauchop and Elsden's comparisons helped establish the value of relating energy metabolism to dry-mass yield, but the resulting yield per mole of ATP should not be treated as a universal biological constant. Different organisms and growth conditions require different amounts of energy for nutrient assimilation, macromolecular synthesis and cellular upkeep. The amount of ATP inferred from a pathway may also depend on assumptions about coupling efficiency. The historical evidence supports an energetic framework, not a fixed numerical conversion applicable to every microorganism.

To interpret a simple experiment comparing respiration and fermentation, one should ideally measure both the substrate used and biomass formed. Measurements of released products or oxygen uptake can support a pathway-based explanation. A higher final biomass in the oxygenated culture is not by itself evidence of a greater yield unless the amount consumed is also known. Conversely, a lower biomass yield in a fermentative condition need not imply that the cells were dead or that glycolysis failed. It may reflect a functioning pathway in which a large fraction of substrate energy remains in products.

Energy conservation provides an important bridge from cell physiology to microbial ecology. Environments differ in their supply of electron donors and acceptors as well as in their nutrient content. A microorganism with access to an appropriate acceptor can occupy a different energetic niche from an organism relying on fermentation. But the consequences for growth must be understood in relation to the resources limiting biosynthesis. Even a highly favorable energy-yielding reaction cannot supply phosphorus if the environment lacks accessible phosphorus.

8. Growth-associated energy expenditure and maintenance

A cell must expend energy even when its mass is not increasing. Membranes are not perfectly impermeable; passive ion movements can oppose the gradients required for active transport and homeostasis. Proteins and other cellular constituents can be damaged or degraded and need replacement. Some cells maintain motility or other functions that consume energy without directly creating new biomass. The collective energetic demands that sustain existing cellular functions are called maintenance requirements. They should be distinguished from the energetic costs directly associated with constructing additional biomass.

Imagine a population in which the quantity of substrate used per gram of biomass formed would be constant if there were no maintenance requirement. If the population grows rapidly, a particular amount of existing biomass is maintained for a comparatively short time while new biomass is being formed. If it grows slowly, that biomass may need support for a much longer time before an equivalent net increment is achieved. Additional substrate can then be consumed without a proportional gain in biomass. Under the stated assumptions, the observed yield decreases as maintenance becomes a larger fraction of the total substrate expenditure.

Pirt's 1965 analysis formalized this idea by distinguishing the substrate use associated with growth from an additional expenditure for maintenance. The important conceptual achievement was to explain why the observed yield need not be fixed even when the biosynthetic cost of making a unit of new biomass is assumed constant. This model is especially useful for interpreting slow growth, but its assumptions must remain visible. Maintenance demand can itself change with environmental conditions or metabolic state; cell composition and the pathways used to conserve energy can also change. Therefore a difference in observed yield does not prove that maintenance alone has changed.

A simple accounting example illustrates the idea without introducing a full continuous-culture model. Suppose an idealized organism would need 2.0 g of substrate to construct 1.0 g of net new biomass, excluding additional maintenance costs. Culture A consumes a further 0.5 g of substrate for maintenance over the measurement interval, while culture B consumes a further 2.0 g. Both cultures produce exactly 1.0 g of new biomass. The observed yields are 1.0/2.5 = 0.40 g/g and 1.0/4.0 = 0.25 g/g respectively. The second culture has the same assumed biosynthetic cost but a smaller observed yield because more substrate is allocated to upkeep.

This is an illustrative budget, not a measurement of the maintenance costs of a real microorganism. If cells also die during the interval, net biomass accumulation can be smaller even when the surviving cells are synthesizing material efficiently. If a metabolic shift changes the amount of ATP conserved per gram of substrate, a lower yield may instead result from less efficient energy conservation. If the cells produce extracellular polysaccharides not included in the biomass measurement, an apparent decrease in yield can occur because carbon is redirected outside the measured cell fraction.

Maintenance is one reason why the physiological meaning of stationary phase is not simply inactivity. A cell that no longer contributes to net population growth may continue importing nutrients, consuming stored material and carrying out repair. The total biomass concentration may appear approximately constant because synthesis and losses balance at the population level, or because little net mass change occurs even while metabolism continues. Such a culture can exhibit little or no positive net growth yield over a particular interval. It would be incorrect to calculate a doubling time from its consumption of substrate alone.

Environmental conditions can modify this expenditure. A cell growing outside its favorable pH range may need more work to maintain its internal ionic environment. High temperature can increase molecular damage and the demand for repair, even while some individual enzyme reactions become faster. These effects can reduce net biomass obtained from a given amount of substrate. Yet it is also possible for a moderately warmer condition to accelerate growth enough that time-dependent maintenance consumes a smaller share. The final yield depends on the combined balance, not on a single universally directed effect of temperature.

The energy budget is thus a way to organize alternative hypotheses. It separates material retained as new biomass from substrate consumed for useful activities not directly reflected in that biomass. It explains why an experimental yield is conditional and why the same organism can exhibit different growth efficiencies. To proceed from this idea to population data, it is necessary to make an equally careful distinction between yield and growth rate.

9. Growth rate and growth yield answer different questions

Specific growth rate is the rate of biomass or population increase relative to the amount already present. During balanced exponential growth, when the average dry mass of a cell is approximately constant, the specific growth rate calculated from biomass agrees with that calculated from cell number. If X is biomass concentration, the differential form is dX/dt = μX. The specific rate μ is therefore (1/X)(dX/dt) and has units of reciprocal time, for example h⁻¹. The derivation in the preceding growth-kinetics chapter leads to X₁ = X₀ exp(μt) for a constant μ. Its logarithmic form is μ = ln(X₁/X₀)/t.

This equation describes how quickly biomass increases, not how much substrate was used to achieve that increase. Its variables include time, but no substrate concentration. The yield coefficient, in contrast, compares biomass increase with substrate consumption and contains no explicit time variable. If the time taken to produce an amount of biomass is cut in half, the yield does not necessarily double. If the amount of substrate consumed is cut in half while the net biomass gain remains fixed, the yield does double. These statements follow directly from the denominators of the two definitions.

The relationship between the two quantities can be made clearer using rates. During a short interval of constant volume and positive growth, define the specific substrate-consumption rate qₛ as −(1/X)(dS/dt). Its units are grams of substrate consumed per gram of biomass per hour. Then the instantaneous biomass yield is the ratio of the biomass accumulation rate to the positive substrate-consumption rate. Substituting dX/dt = μX and −dS/dt = qₛX gives Yₓ/ₛ = μ/qₛ, or μ = Yₓ/ₛ qₛ. This is simply an accounting identity for the instantaneous net rates on matching bases, not an additional growth law or a claim that any of these quantities is constant. It does not apply to finite intervals by substituting arbitrarily averaged values.

The cancellation of X has an important biological interpretation. One gram of existing biomass may take up substrate quickly but convert only a small amount of that substrate into new biomass. Another may take up substrate more slowly but convert a greater fraction into new biomass. The net specific biomass growth rate is determined by both uptake and conversion. Growth rate can thus be high even when yield is low, provided that consumption per unit existing biomass is sufficiently high. Conversely, an efficient organism may grow slowly if its substrate uptake rate is low. The two properties can also increase together if an improved condition increases efficiency and accelerates uptake.

Worked example 5. In two hypothetical cultures during a short balanced growth interval, culture A has qₛ = 2.0 g substrate g biomass⁻¹ h⁻¹ and μ = 0.60 h⁻¹. Culture B has qₛ = 0.80 g substrate g biomass⁻¹ h⁻¹ and μ = 0.40 h⁻¹. Their instantaneous yields are 0.60/2.0 = 0.30 g/g and 0.40/0.80 = 0.50 g/g. Culture A has the higher specific growth rate, but B has the higher yield. Assuming these growth rates remain constant during exponential growth, their biomass doubling times are ln(2)/0.60 = 1.155 h and ln(2)/0.40 = 1.733 h, respectively. The numerical comparison establishes exactly which culture is faster and which is more economical with respect to substrate.

There is also a distinction between specific growth rate and absolute biomass production per litre per hour. If two cultures have identical μ but different amounts of existing biomass, the denser culture will add more biomass per hour at that moment. A culture at X = 4 g/L and μ = 0.2 h⁻¹ accumulates 0.8 g/L/h, while another at X = 1 g/L and μ = 0.4 h⁻¹ accumulates 0.4 g/L/h. The second culture has the higher rate relative to its own biomass but the first has the greater absolute biomass increase per unit volume at that moment. The comparison requires no assumption about which will have the higher endpoint.

An experimental growth curve makes these distinctions visible. Suppose two cultures begin with identical inocula. Culture A has a steep straight segment on a graph of ln X against time, followed by a plateau at 2 g/L. Culture B has a shallower exponential segment and a plateau at 4 g/L. If both consumed exactly the same quantity of substrate and began with equal biomass, B obtained more biomass per unit substrate. But without the substrate data the endpoint comparison cannot determine yield. A may have stopped because nitrogen was exhausted while glucose remained, whereas B may have consumed all of the available glucose. The graph alone establishes an endpoint difference and a rate difference, not their biochemical cause.

Microbial growth strategies have ecological consequences. A rapidly growing organism may gain a temporary numerical advantage when a fresh resource becomes available. A slower but more efficient organism may produce more biomass from a fixed resource supply, particularly when the opportunity to use that resource is prolonged. However, neither strategy universally guarantees competitive success. Resource uptake at low concentration, survival during starvation, interactions with neighboring organisms and the timing of resource availability may matter more than either isolated parameter. Within this chapter the useful lesson is that growth rate and biomass yield are distinct measurable properties, not mutually exclusive classifications of organisms.

10. Limiting nutrients: the resource that constrains further biomass formation

A microorganism requires a set of nutrients, not simply an adequate total mass of dissolved substances. Consider a medium containing abundant glucose, nitrogen and minerals but only a small quantity of accessible phosphate. During growth, the population incorporates phosphate into nucleic acids and other cellular constituents. If all accessible phosphate is used and no additional source is available, continued production of ordinary new cells eventually becomes impossible. Additional glucose cannot supply the missing phosphorus. In this condition phosphate constrains the amount of biomass that can be formed.

A limiting nutrient is a resource whose availability constrains a specified growth outcome under the existing conditions. It is not necessarily the nutrient present at the lowest measured concentration. Different cellular constituents are required in very different quantities. A small concentration of an essential trace element can be sufficient for the biomass supported by a much larger concentration of carbon. Conversely, the nutrient at the highest concentration might still be insufficient if the organism requires it in a large quantity or cannot access most of it. One must compare the usable amount of each nutrient with the organism's requirement for that nutrient.

This principle can be expressed using a simple biomass-composition model. Let a nutrient element E form a fraction fₑ of newly synthesized dry biomass by mass. If Mₑ grams of that element are accessible for net incorporation, a maximum biomass increment allowed by that supply is Mₑ/fₑ grams, assuming fixed composition, no relevant initial reserve, and complete incorporation of the accessible element. For example, if cells require 0.10 g of nitrogen per gram of new dry biomass, 0.40 g of assimilable nitrogen can support at most 4.0 g of such biomass. The model accounts for material incorporated into the biomass; for a dual-purpose resource such as carbon, some of the consumed element can also leave as metabolic products, so the calculation is an upper bound, not automatically a realistic observed yield.

This is the logic behind the traditional law of the minimum, commonly associated with mineral nutrition and often applied as a conceptual model for microbial resource limitation. Each essential resource places an upper bound on growth. If all bounds are independent and the cell composition and resource-use efficiencies are fixed, the smallest bound sets the maximum biomass. More of a resource whose bound already exceeds that minimum cannot increase the maximum until the actual limiting resource is increased. The organism cannot substitute a surplus of one element for a chemically indispensable different element.

Worked example 6. In a hypothetical one-litre medium, a supplied carbon substrate can support a net biomass increment of 5.0 g on the basis of its measured yield in these conditions. The accessible nitrogen pool contains 0.30 g of nitrogen and the cells are assumed to require 10% nitrogen by dry mass, so the nitrogen-supported increment is 0.30/0.10 = 3.0 g. The accessible phosphorus pool contains 0.08 g and the biomass is assumed to contain 2% phosphorus, so the phosphorus-supported increment is 0.08/0.02 = 4.0 g. With all other requirements satisfied, the smallest bound is 3.0 g and nitrogen is the predicted limiting resource. If the initial biomass was 0.20 g, the predicted final mass is 3.20 g, not 3.0 g.

Suppose an additional 0.20 g of nitrogen is made accessible without altering the other conditions. The new nitrogen bound is 0.50/0.10 = 5.0 g. The phosphorus bound remains 4.0 g and now constrains the predicted maximum. The attainable biomass increases by only 1.0 g, despite the extra nitrogen theoretically supporting 2.0 g of additional material. The limiting nutrient has shifted. Further nitrogen addition will not increase the predicted endpoint unless the phosphorus constraint is also relieved.

Real cells can modify their biomass composition. Under some nutrient limitations, they accumulate carbon-rich storage material or alter the relative abundance of proteins, RNA and other constituents. A strict assumption of fixed composition can therefore fail, particularly near stationary phase. Cells may also contain intracellular stores inherited from the inoculum. These reserves temporarily support reactions even when the extracellular supply has been exhausted. The law of the minimum is useful as an idealized starting point, but empirical data are needed when the assumptions do not hold.

The word limiting can refer to the growth rate during a particular interval or to the maximum biomass at a later time. For instance, a very low extracellular substrate concentration can restrict transport and reduce the specific growth rate while a large total quantity of the substrate becomes available gradually. Conversely, a finite quantity of a nutrient can permit a high initial rate but cap the final biomass. The distinction depends on whether the question concerns immediate supply per unit time or total accessible supply. To avoid ambiguity, specify what was measured and over what interval.

Nutrients may jointly constrain growth. If two resources are both present in quantities close to their requirements, adding one alone may have little effect because the other remains limiting. A combined addition may produce a large increase. A simple minimum model predicts this possibility when two bounds are equal, but actual joint limitation may also arise from interactions between uptake and metabolism. For example, synthesis of a transport protein itself requires nitrogen and energy. Carbon and nitrogen supply can therefore affect each other's utilization. A single limiting-resource label is appropriate only when supported by the conditions and measurements.

11. Nutrient availability, saturation and excess

When a required substrate is scarce, a small increase in its accessible concentration may produce a large increase in growth rate. One reason is that uptake systems encounter substrate molecules more frequently. Enzyme reactions consuming the resulting intermediates can also become less constrained. At higher concentrations, however, the rate of uptake or metabolism approaches a capacity determined by the number and activity of proteins participating in the pathway and by the availability of other resources. The growth rate cannot increase without bound merely because the external substrate concentration continues to rise.

A qualitative nutrient-response curve often rises steeply from low substrate concentration, then increases more gradually before approaching a plateau. The low-concentration region describes conditions in which small changes in substrate availability have a relatively large effect on the measured growth rate. The plateau describes an interval over which increasing that substrate further provides little additional acceleration under the particular conditions. It does not mean that the substrate has ceased to be necessary. Removing it altogether would still affect the organism if it were an essential resource.

It is important not to identify this curve automatically with the velocity curve of a purified enzyme. Microbial growth is a population-level outcome involving transport, energy conservation, biosynthesis and cell division. Its substrate dependence may resemble saturation in a single enzymatic reaction, but several processes may determine the observed shape. Detailed mathematical models of substrate-dependent growth, including the Monod equation and its application to continuous culture, are treated in biochemical engineering. At this stage, the appropriate objective is to understand the biological meaning of a qualitative growth response.

Saturation of growth rate must also be distinguished from saturation of biomass yield or endpoint. Suppose a defined medium with 1 g/L of usable substrate already permits the maximum observed exponential growth rate. Increasing the initial substrate to 2 g/L may not make the logarithmic phase steeper, yet it can prolong that phase and increase the biomass produced before substrate exhaustion. The two cultures can have similar initial growth rates but different durations of growth. This difference is expected if the substrate remains the resource limiting the eventual biomass and the observed yield stays approximately constant.

If, instead, the biomass supported by another nutrient is reached at 1 g/L of substrate, increasing the substrate concentration to 2 g/L may not improve either the maximum growth rate or the endpoint. The excess substrate remains or is consumed for non-growth purposes. Consequently, an endpoint-versus-initial-substrate graph can plateau for a different reason than a specific-growth-rate-versus-substrate graph. In the first case the plateau may arise because another resource limits total biomass; in the second, because the capacity for growth at that instant has been approached.

In some cases, excessive substrate concentration inhibits growth. A high solute concentration may lower water activity. A compound that is readily used at moderate concentrations may perturb membranes or inhibit metabolic reactions at much higher concentrations. Rapid conversion into inhibitory products can also make a high initial nutrient concentration unfavorable later in the growth period. A declining growth response at high concentration should therefore not be confused with simple saturation, which predicts a leveling off rather than a fall.

Suppose increasing glucose concentration from 1 to 4 g/L increases the exponential growth rate, whereas increasing it from 4 to 8 g/L gives little additional increase. At 40 g/L the observed rate is lower. The data are compatible with saturation followed by inhibition, but the mechanism of the inhibition has not been established. A control in which a different, nonmetabolized solute creates comparable osmotic conditions could help test an osmotic explanation. Measuring the external pH and metabolic products could test a product-accumulation explanation. These tests are based on isolating causal variables rather than assigning a mechanism from curve shape alone.

The ability of cells to regulate uptake also complicates simple expectations. When a nutrient is scarce, synthesis of high-affinity uptake systems can improve access to that nutrient. High affinity describes the ability of a system to operate effectively at low substrate concentration; it does not guarantee a large maximal transport capacity. A system effective at scavenging sparse nutrient molecules can differ from one that permits rapid uptake at high concentrations. The overall outcome depends on the transport and metabolic machinery expressed by cells during the observation interval.

A culture transferred from a rich medium to a poor medium may not immediately behave like cells that have been growing in the poor medium for many generations. Existing enzymes, cellular reserves and gene expression patterns can transiently influence the result. Therefore the culture's previous environment is a potential confounder. The same nutrient concentration can produce different early growth responses if the cells enter the experiment in different physiological states. This connects nutrient-response experiments to the lag phase of batch growth.

12. How nutrient limitation can be established experimentally

The observation that growth has stopped while some glucose remains is informative, but it does not by itself identify the cause. A nutrient other than glucose may have been exhausted. The pH may have moved outside the favorable range, a necessary electron acceptor may have become scarce, or an inhibitory metabolic product may have accumulated. Distinguishing these possibilities requires an intervention designed to change one relevant condition while keeping others comparable.

An elementary approach is a nutrient-addition experiment. Samples of the same culture, or matched cultures of the same physiological history, are compared after adding individual candidate resources. One condition receives no additional resource, serving as a comparison for changes that would occur without the addition. If phosphate addition reproducibly permits further biomass increase while adding glucose or nitrogen does not, the result supports phosphate limitation under those test conditions. However, the added compound may have altered pH, ionic strength or the availability of another nutrient. Suitable matched conditions are needed before attributing the result exclusively to the phosphorus supply.

A convincing interpretation is strengthened when the added nutrient was independently shown to have become depleted or inaccessible, and when biomass formation resumes in a manner consistent with the extra supply. The result does not prove that phosphate was the only limitation throughout the previous growth period. It identifies a constraint operating when the intervention was performed. Earlier in the batch, another factor may have influenced the specific growth rate. As biomass accumulates again, a different constraint may become decisive.

Hypothetical data illustrate the distinction between single and joint limitations. Assume a culture reaches 1.5 g/L and then shows no further net growth. After an appropriate observation period, the untreated condition remains at 1.5 g/L. Additional carbon alone gives 1.5 g/L, nitrogen alone gives 1.6 g/L, and phosphate alone gives 1.5 g/L. The combined nitrogen-plus-phosphate condition gives 3.0 g/L. The difference between 1.5 and 1.6 g/L may be within experimental variability; the large combined response suggests that both nutrients constrained further growth or that their availabilities interacted. It would be unjustified to classify nitrogen as the sole limiting nutrient from the small single-addition response without considering replication and measurement uncertainty.

If no addition restores growth, several hypotheses remain possible. The added resource may not be in a usable form. Cells may have lost viability. Oxygen or another electron acceptor may be limiting. The spent medium may contain a substance that inhibits metabolism. Transferring a matched sample to a suitable fresh medium, and independently assessing viability, helps distinguish irreversible loss from a reversible environmental constraint. A restoration of growth in fresh medium shows that at least some cells retained the capacity to reproduce, but does not identify which specific property of the original medium prevented growth.

A second approach is to compare biomass endpoints across several initial concentrations of one resource while other essential nutrients are kept adequate. If net biomass rises in approximate proportion to the initial supply and little of that resource remains, the slope can provide an estimate of its observed biomass yield, provided the resource is the one actually consumed and the other conditions are comparable. If the graph begins at a positive biomass intercept, that intercept may reflect inoculum biomass or growth supported by other medium constituents. The slope, rather than a ratio from the origin, is then the relevant estimate over the linear region.

The question of measurement method remains important. An increase in optical density after nutrient addition might reflect more cells, larger cells, stored material, cell aggregates or chemical precipitation. Combining a dry-mass measurement with an independent measure of viable population or cell count strengthens interpretation. If the exam presents only optical density, make conclusions conditional on the assumption that the reading is a valid biomass proxy. Evidence becomes more persuasive when different measurements support the same interpretation.
