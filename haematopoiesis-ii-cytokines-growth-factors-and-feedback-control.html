1. The biological problem: keeping a changing blood supply functional

Blood is a renewing tissue rather than a permanent collection of cells. Erythrocytes transport respiratory gases, platelets participate in the formation of haemostatic plugs, and several classes of leucocytes help defend tissues. Their rates of disappearance and the circumstances under which they are needed are different. An erythrocyte normally circulates for months in humans, while platelets turn over over a period of days. Many neutrophils are comparatively short lived, and the lifetime of a lymphocyte depends strongly on its type and state. Consequently, a single undifferentiated stimulus for all marrow cells would not be an adequate solution to the problem of replacement. The organism must regulate several outputs with partially independent signals.

The distinction between a stock and a flow is fundamental to understanding this regulation. A blood sample contains a stock of cells that have arrived from production sites and have not yet been removed from the sampled compartment. Production is a flow, expressed as cells generated per unit time. Removal is also a flow. A stable stock can coexist with a substantial production rate when production and loss are balanced. Conversely, a normal concentration measured on one day does not prove that production is normal. A temporary reduction in production may be concealed by the cells already present, whereas a sudden redistribution can alter a sampled count long before progenitors have divided.

Haematopoiesis is the formation of blood cells from stem cells and their descendants. Haematopoietic stem cells, abbreviated HSCs, possess long-term regenerative capacity and the ability to produce different blood lineages. Their progeny include progenitors, which have more restricted developmental possibilities and often considerable capacity for proliferation. Differentiation changes cellular identity, whereas proliferation changes cell number. Maturation establishes the structural and functional features needed by the resulting cell. These processes often overlap but are not interchangeable. A signal that prevents a progenitor from dying can increase the final output without increasing the probability that each surviving cell adopts a particular fate.

In adult mammals the marrow is an important site of formation of blood cells, but the need for those cells is determined in many tissues. A reduction in oxygen supply may be detected by cells in the kidney, an infection may generate inflammatory signals outside the marrow, and the liver participates in control of platelet production. All these organs communicate with progenitors that are physically distant. The marrow in turn provides local signals needed to retain, nourish and regulate those cells. The chapter therefore connects endocrine communication, paracrine communication, cell adhesion, regulated gene expression and feedback between organs.

Consider two disturbances with superficially similar outcomes: blood loss and accelerated destruction of circulating erythrocytes. Both can reduce effective oxygen delivery, even though they originate in different mechanisms. In the absence of a functioning renal oxygen-sensing system, however, the marrow may not receive the appropriate endocrine signal. If the renal response is intact but the marrow lacks responsive progenitors, the signal may be abundant while replacement remains inadequate. A causal analysis thus asks separately whether the disturbance is detected, whether a signal reaches its target, whether the target can respond, and whether its response restores the original physiological function.

Homeostasis does not require the instantaneous return of every measurement to a fixed numerical value. Responses have delays, available nutrients may be limited, and rates of removal can remain elevated while a disturbance continues. Nevertheless, feedback generally reduces the mismatch between demand and supply. The balance can also shift deliberately during inflammation or development. The difference between adaptive change and dysregulation depends on whether the change serves an appropriate biological need, remains within physiological constraints, and diminishes when the initiating condition resolves.

2. The stages at which growth factors act

A cytokine is an extracellular signaling protein that influences the activity of target cells. Growth factor is a functional term for a signal that promotes processes such as survival, proliferation or growth. Many haematopoietic regulators fall into both categories. Some have predominantly endocrine actions, whereas others act primarily over short distances. A colony-stimulating factor received its name from the capacity to support colonies in an appropriate experimental culture, not because its only biological function is to increase cell division. A chemokine is a cytokine particularly associated with directed cell movement and localization. CXCL12 illustrates how location itself can be an important component of blood production.

The earliest decision about whether a stem cell enters a cycle is distinct from the later decision about how many mature erythrocytes its descendants produce. Quiescent cells are not inactive in the sense of being dead or incapable of metabolism. They maintain themselves while avoiding continuous progression through the cell cycle. A stimulus that pushes all HSCs into rapid division might briefly increase downstream supply but compromise long-term regeneration if self-renewal does not keep pace. The marrow therefore maintains a pool whose output can be increased without necessarily using up its entire regenerative capacity.

Consider a precursor already restricted to erythroid development. If that cell receives a survival signal, it may complete its existing differentiation programme instead of undergoing apoptosis. The observed increase in erythrocyte formation does not imply that the factor converted unrelated lymphoid precursors into erythroid cells. This illustrates a permissive action: a signal makes an existing developmental possibility more likely to succeed. In other situations extracellular inputs influence the probability of commitment to particular states. An instructive effect is harder to establish experimentally because survival of a pre-existing responsive subpopulation can mimic a change of fate.

Expansion occurs when precursor divisions generate additional descendants. The number of divisions is not itself a reliable measurement of useful output. In a symmetric division producing two equally viable progenitors, cell number doubles, but in a division generating one self-renewing cell and one differentiating descendant the stem-cell pool need not increase. Furthermore, progenitors may stop dividing as they mature. A comparison between two samples should establish whether the counted cells are stem cells, lineage-restricted progenitors or terminal products, and whether they possess the functional characteristics appropriate to their stage.

Survival signals often regulate the probability of programmed cell death. If a trophic factor is withdrawn, pro-apoptotic pathways may dominate, reducing the number of cells available to enter subsequent stages. The precise consequences depend on developmental stage, receptor expression, and other available signals. In some cultures, adding a second growth factor does little by itself but permits the action of a first factor to become apparent. Such cooperation can result from complementary survival and proliferation effects, even if the pathways are not directly connected at the receptor.

Release into the circulation is a separate target of regulation. A precursor or mature cell may be retained by an adhesive interaction, guided by chemokine concentration differences, or confined by the organization of tissue vessels. A regulator that changes these constraints can increase the number of cells in blood without immediately increasing total formation. This distinction is particularly important for neutrophils and haematopoietic progenitor mobilization. It also connects this chapter to integrins and extracellular matrix signals: cellular communication can regulate physical position, not merely transcription.

3. How experimental evidence revealed soluble regulators

The idea that blood-cell formation depends on factors outside the developing cells could not be established simply by looking at marrow sections. A tissue section reveals neighbouring cells and structural organization, but it cannot determine whether a diffusible molecule is necessary for a particular progenitor to survive. It also cannot show whether an apparently adjacent cell is providing an essential factor or merely sharing the same environment. A combination of physiological manipulation, culture experiments and later genetic studies made it possible to distinguish these explanations.

Experiments using transplanted marrow provided evidence for clonogenic blood-forming precursors. The classic studies of Till and McCulloch showed that transplanted marrow cells could give rise to discrete colonies in the spleens of irradiated recipient mice. Later work helped establish clonal origin and regenerative properties. Spleen colony-forming activity is not synonymous with the complete modern definition of a long-term HSC; the assay detects a population capable of producing colonies in a particular host environment. Its important contribution was to make the regenerative potential of a starting cell experimentally accessible.

Semisolid culture subsequently allowed researchers to investigate what soluble materials would permit myeloid progenitors to generate colonies outside the body. Work by Bradley and Metcalf and independently by Pluznik and Sachs in the 1960s was central to this development. In a semisolid matrix, descendants are less able to disperse than in liquid medium. A local cluster of cells can therefore provide information about proliferative capacity, provided the starting density and culture conditions support a clonal interpretation. The need for a stimulatory source made the culture a bioassay in which biological activity could be measured while candidate factors were purified.

A bioassay differs from an assay that merely recognizes a molecular structure. An antibody may detect a protein fragment even when that fragment no longer has normal biological activity. A colony assay tests the ability of the sample to support a particular outcome in a population of living cells. Neither approach is universally superior: molecular assays are valuable for identity and concentration, while functional assays connect the molecule to a response. Combining the two helps distinguish an abundant but inactive protein from a biologically effective signal.

The purification of human erythropoietin by Miyake, Kung and Goldwasser in 1977 provided a landmark example of converting physiological evidence into molecular evidence. The isolation of an active material made biochemical characterization and later molecular cloning possible. It was not necessary to assume that kidney cells physically contacted marrow erythroid progenitors. A circulating protein could account for the communication between organs. The purification history also shows why a molecule may have physiological importance at concentrations too small to isolate readily from ordinary samples.

The discovery and eventual molecular identification of colony-stimulating factors and thrombopoietin established that separate extracellular signals could regulate partly distinct haematopoietic outputs. The history should not be interpreted as discovery of a perfectly exclusive one-factor-one-cell rule. The activity of a purified factor depends on the cells tested, the other growth factors available and the endpoint measured. Evidence for a factor that generates granulocyte colonies does not exclude additional effects on the function or survival of mature granulocytes. Similarly, a factor that is central to platelet production can also influence the stem-cell compartment.

A modern experimental comparison can test whether a cytokine is necessary, sufficient, or both in a specified context. If a culture produces colonies with a particular factor but not without it, the factor may be necessary under those conditions. If its addition restores colonies after neutralization of the endogenous signal, the restoration strengthens the causal inference. If additional factors are present throughout the experiment, the result does not prove that the molecule alone is sufficient. These distinctions underlie many analytical questions about cytokine action.

4. Receptor selectivity and the capacity of a cell to respond

A soluble protein reaches many cells, but it affects them selectively because their receptor complements and intracellular states differ. Erythropoietin is a glycoprotein that binds the erythropoietin receptor, EPOR. Thrombopoietin binds MPL, a receptor expressed in important haematopoietic populations including megakaryocytic cells and HSCs. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor binds CSF3R, commonly called the G-CSF receptor. These receptors connect extracellular ligand recognition with intracellular changes, but the downstream biological outcome depends on the type of responding cell.

The membrane is an important physical boundary because large hydrophilic proteins do not ordinarily cross the lipid bilayer freely. The extracellular region of a receptor provides binding specificity; the membrane-spanning region anchors the protein; and the cytoplasmic region couples binding to enzymes or adaptor proteins. EPO and TPO receptors belong to the cytokine receptor family that commonly uses associated Janus kinases rather than intrinsic receptor kinase activity. Their ligand-dependent organization activates associated signaling proteins. The ligand itself need not enter the nucleus.

A useful principle from cell signaling is that phosphorylation changes protein interactions and activities. Kinases transfer phosphate groups using nucleotide substrates, and phosphatases remove them. Activated cytokine receptors can recruit signaling intermediates, including STAT proteins, and influence survival, metabolic activity and gene transcription. Erythroid EPOR signaling includes JAK2 and STAT5, while MPL uses JAK2 and can activate several downstream pathways. A detailed reconstruction of every phosphorylation step is not required to reason about the haematopoietic response, since the decisive question here is which cells can respond to a ligand and what output their response produces.

Other haematopoietic signals illustrate different receptor families. SCF binds KIT, which has intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase activity. CXCL12 binds the chemokine receptor CXCR4, a G-protein-coupled receptor. These differences are important because neither extracellular protein signaling nor control of haematopoiesis implies a universal JAK–STAT mechanism. The response to SCF may involve survival and maintenance, whereas CXCL12-dependent guidance and retention can change the exposure of a cell to neighbouring signals. The receptor family specifies how a message is converted into intracellular activity, not all of its physiological consequences.

Receptor abundance can change as a cell matures. A ligand may be present in the marrow continuously, yet only a particular developmental stage remains strongly dependent on it. A receptor-deficient precursor and a ligand-deficient environment can generate superficially similar phenotypes. Supplying the missing ligand should help the latter if other necessary components remain functional, but it cannot restore a completely missing receptor in the former. An activating receptor mutation can have the opposite pattern: the downstream response may persist at low ligand concentration, disconnecting the target from normal feedback.

A signaling pathway is also shaped by termination. Ligand binding is reversible, receptors can be internalized, phosphorylated intermediates can be dephosphorylated, and inhibitors induced by the initial response can limit further activation. These changes prevent a temporary signal from necessarily becoming a permanent instruction. They also help distinguish two dose-response experiments: a cell exposed briefly to a strong stimulus and a cell exposed continuously to a weaker stimulus may attain similar instantaneous activity but different cumulative biological responses.

The final phenotype must be interpreted in the context of the cell's existing transcriptional programme. A kinase may be activated in multiple lineages while each lineage expresses a different set of genes in response. A STAT-dependent survival programme in an erythroid precursor need not reproduce the fate of a lymphoid precursor. Thus receptor identity, developmental competence, transcriptional context and the microenvironment form a connected system. This is why a simple list matching a growth factor with a mature blood cell is useful as a starting point but insufficient for mechanistic examination questions.

5. Erythropoietin: the kidney-to-marrow communication axis

The physiological variable relevant to erythropoietin production is the oxygen environment experienced by the oxygen-sensing cells, not the numerical concentration of erythrocytes alone. Haemoglobin inside erythrocytes binds oxygen reversibly and carries most of the oxygen in arterial blood. Changes in haemoglobin concentration, oxygen saturation, blood flow or local oxygen consumption can alter tissue oxygenation. The kidney integrates several of these influences because it receives blood and consumes oxygen while carrying out its transport and metabolic functions.

In postnatal mammals, specialized renal interstitial fibroblast-like cells are the principal physiological source of circulating erythropoietin. The liver has a prominent role in fetal life and can contribute in adults. The hormone released into blood reaches the marrow, where erythroid progenitors express EPOR during important stages of their development. Mature mammalian erythrocytes have lost their nuclei and cannot divide in response to a transcriptional growth signal. EPO therefore increases erythrocyte output through precursors rather than by causing circulating erythrocytes to reproduce.

Reduced oxygen availability stimulates EPO production in a functioning renal oxygen-sensing system. Increased EPO supports survival and maturation of responsive erythroid cells, allowing a larger proportion to develop towards reticulocytes and mature erythrocytes. This sequence changes oxygen-carrying capacity only after a delay because cellular differentiation, haemoglobin synthesis and release require time. The delay prevents the endocrine response from being confused with an immediate physical restoration of oxygen in the affected tissue.

The feedback loop has an overall negative sign. Low local oxygen promotes production of a hormone whose downstream action tends to improve oxygen delivery. When oxygenation improves, the original stimulus to EPO synthesis diminishes. Negative feedback describes the effect of completing the loop, not the sign of every individual step. EPO positively influences responsive progenitors, and erythrocyte output positively influences oxygen-carrying capacity; the final reduction in the original stimulus determines the overall feedback sign.

Altitude provides a familiar physiological example. At sufficiently high altitude, lower inspired oxygen partial pressure can reduce arterial oxygenation and stimulate the renal endocrine response. Adaptation includes several physiological processes, only one of which is increased erythropoiesis. The magnitude and timing of the EPO response depend on the degree and duration of hypoxia and other physiological adjustments. An isolated value measured after acclimatization need not remain as high as a value obtained during the initial response.

Erythrocyte loss and reduced renal EPO synthesis can each result in low erythrocyte numbers but produce different regulatory patterns. In a simplified setting with intact oxygen sensing and a responsive kidney, low oxygen delivery after red-cell loss tends to raise EPO. In a setting in which the renal cells cannot produce adequate EPO, the signal may be inappropriately low relative to the degree of reduced oxygen delivery. These are mechanistic comparisons, not stand-alone diagnostic rules. Other processes affect oxygen supply, hormone clearance and marrow response.

6. How oxygen becomes a transcriptional signal

The renal oxygen-sensing system can be understood as a regulated balance between the synthesis and degradation of a transcriptional regulator. Hypoxia-inducible factors, abbreviated HIFs, contain an oxygen-regulated alpha subunit and a partner beta subunit. For physiological regulation of renal erythropoietin, HIF-2 alpha has a particularly important role. It is not necessary for oxygen molecules to bind directly to the EPO receptor. Oxygen is detected upstream in the cell that produces the hormone, whereas the hormone receptor is used downstream by the target cell in the marrow.

Under sufficiently oxygenated conditions, prolyl hydroxylase domain enzymes use molecular oxygen, iron in the ferrous state and 2-oxoglutarate in a reaction that hydroxylates particular proline residues of HIF-alpha. The enzyme is a dioxygenase, and conversion of 2-oxoglutarate is associated with production of succinate and carbon dioxide. The resulting hydroxylated protein can be recognized by the von Hippel–Lindau protein, abbreviated VHL, within an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. Ubiquitin-dependent targeting promotes proteasomal degradation of the alpha subunit. Even if HIF-alpha is being synthesized, rapid destruction limits its accumulation.

When oxygen availability becomes sufficiently low, hydroxylation can become less effective. The alpha subunit escapes the same degree of degradation and accumulates. It associates with HIF-beta, also called ARNT, to form a transcriptionally active complex under appropriate cellular conditions. Binding to hypoxia-responsive regulatory DNA and recruitment of transcriptional machinery can increase EPO gene expression in cells equipped for that response. Production and release of the resulting hormone connect a change in oxygen-dependent chemistry to regulation at a distant tissue.

Notice that oxygen participates as a substrate in the hydroxylation reaction. This chemical feature provides a means by which changes in oxygen availability can alter protein stability without requiring the cell to synthesize a different oxygen sensor for each disturbance. However, the effective enzyme activity is not controlled by oxygen alone. Cofactor availability, enzyme abundance and cellular metabolic state can influence the same reaction. A stable HIF-alpha protein is evidence that its rate of synthesis and removal has changed relative to one another; it is not by itself a quantitative measurement of tissue oxygen partial pressure.

A simplified genetic perturbation clarifies the causal sequence. Suppose VHL-dependent recognition of hydroxylated HIF-alpha fails, while the transcription factor remains functional. The presence of oxygen and successful hydroxylation may no longer ensure normal destruction. HIF activity can consequently become inappropriate for the prevailing oxygen environment. Conversely, loss of functional HIF-2 alpha in an EPO-producing cell may diminish its ability to increase EPO transcription during hypoxia. These opposite perturbations distinguish the inhibitory degradation machinery from the positively acting transcriptional regulator.

It is useful to compare the locations of two reactions that are easily confused. The oxygen-dependent hydroxylation of HIF is part of signal generation in the kidney. JAK2-dependent signaling downstream of EPOR occurs in responsive erythroid cells. Inhibition at the first location may stabilize a regulator of hormone production; inhibition at the second location may reduce responsiveness to the hormone already produced. Therefore, identifying the molecular target of a perturbation is essential before predicting whether circulating EPO and erythrocyte output will change in the same direction.

The EPO gene is regulated in a cell-specific manner. HIF activation in a different tissue can influence genes associated with metabolism, vascular responses or other aspects of oxygen adaptation without producing a major circulating EPO response. The expression of an oxygen-sensitive transcription factor does not establish that every HIF-responsive cell secretes erythropoietin. Chromatin accessibility, tissue-specific regulatory elements and other components of transcriptional control limit which genes respond in each cell population.

7. EPO-dependent survival and the production of functional erythrocytes

The transition from an early erythroid progenitor to a circulating red cell involves several stages. Burst-forming unit erythroid, or BFU-E, and colony-forming unit erythroid, or CFU-E, are terms originally grounded in the colony-forming properties of precursors in suitable culture assays. The later CFU-E and early erythroblast stages are particularly sensitive to EPO-dependent survival. Earlier precursors can also be influenced by other factors such as SCF. These dependencies overlap, and the names of culture-defined populations should not be mistaken for a sharp universal boundary in a continuous differentiation process.

In the absence of adequate EPO, a substantial proportion of susceptible erythroid progenitors undergo apoptosis. The cells do not simply stop their differentiation programme in an otherwise indefinitely viable state. In a classic study reported in 1990, Koury and Bondurant examined DNA fragmentation and cell death in erythroid precursors deprived of EPO. The results supported the conclusion that EPO prevents programmed death in such cells. This evidence helped establish that regulation of precursor survival is central to regulation of red-cell output.

Binding of EPO to its receptor promotes signaling through associated JAK2 and downstream pathways, including STAT5-dependent gene regulation. Among the relevant responses are changes in the balance of proteins that favour survival or apoptosis. BCL-xL provides an important example of a survival-associated protein in erythroid maturation. These signaling effects permit cells to progress through a developmental programme in which haemoglobin accumulates, nuclear organization changes and, in mammals, the nucleus is eventually expelled. The receptor does not perform haemoglobin synthesis or nuclear expulsion itself; it contributes to the conditions that allow those processes to occur.

Reticulocytes are immature erythroid products that retain residual RNA and complete maturation after nuclear extrusion. Their appearance provides a useful indicator of recent erythropoietic output because it precedes the full contribution of those cells to the mature red-cell population. A sample containing a relatively high reticulocyte fraction may indicate a compensatory response, but the fraction must be interpreted alongside the total number of red cells. A high percentage calculated from a small denominator can correspond to a modest absolute number of new cells.

Suppose two cultures begin with the same number of erythroid precursors. In one, half of the cells die before dividing; in the other, only one tenth die. If surviving cells subsequently undergo the same number of divisions, the final populations differ even though their division rates are identical. A measurement of final cell number alone cannot resolve whether EPO increased the number of divisions per survivor or reduced cell death before those divisions occurred. Simultaneous observation of cell-cycle progression and viability makes the mechanism more interpretable.

A favourable endocrine signal is also insufficient when substrates required for functional maturation are unavailable. Haemoglobin synthesis requires iron and appropriate globin expression, and cell division depends on nucleotide synthesis and general nutrient availability. A precursor may receive an effective survival signal but still be unable to generate a normal mature product. This distinction explains why hormone concentration, progenitor abundance and oxygen-carrying capacity should not be treated as interchangeable measures of the response.

Erythroid precursors communicate with iron-regulating tissues as well. EPO-stimulated erythroblasts can produce erythroferrone, which contributes to suppression of hepcidin under increased erythropoietic demand. Hepcidin normally limits iron export through ferroportin; reducing hepcidin can improve availability of iron for erythropoiesis when appropriate. This additional feedback is a useful example of coordination between material supply and a growth signal. It does not mean that erythroferrone replaces EPO as the principal endocrine regulator of erythroid precursor survival.

8. The quantitative meaning of oxygen-dependent feedback

A useful physiological approximation separates oxygen content from oxygen partial pressure. Arterial oxygen content can be estimated as the sum of haemoglobin-bound oxygen and oxygen physically dissolved in plasma and the aqueous phase of blood. For ordinary teaching calculations, the relation is written CaO2 = 1.34 × Hb × SaO2 + 0.003 × PaO2. Here CaO2 is millilitres of oxygen per decilitre of blood, Hb is grams of haemoglobin per decilitre, SaO2 is the fractional arterial oxygen saturation, and PaO2 is arterial oxygen partial pressure in millimetres of mercury. The coefficients are approximate physiological conversion factors with the units required to make both terms quantities of oxygen per decilitre.

For hypothetical blood containing 15 grams of haemoglobin per decilitre, saturation 0.98 and PaO2 of 100 millimetres of mercury, the bound component is 1.34 × 15 × 0.98, or 19.698 millilitres per decilitre. The dissolved component is 0.3 millilitres per decilitre. The resulting estimate is approximately 20.0 millilitres per decilitre. If haemoglobin falls to 10 grams per decilitre with saturation and partial pressure unchanged, the estimate becomes 13.432 millilitres per decilitre. Thus a large reduction in oxygen content can occur even though PaO2 remains unchanged. The calculation is illustrative, not a diagnostic threshold.

Oxygen delivery to a tissue also depends on blood flow. If flow is expressed in decilitres per minute, multiplying by oxygen content in millilitres per decilitre yields oxygen supplied in millilitres per minute. A decrease in oxygen content can be partly compensated by increased flow, whereas impaired perfusion can reduce delivery despite normal blood oxygen content. Oxygen extraction and local metabolic demand then influence tissue oxygen availability. For this reason there is no universal linear conversion from a peripheral haemoglobin concentration to a renal EPO concentration.

To reason about the temporal response, let N be the total number of circulating red cells, R their effective appearance rate, and k a constant fractional removal rate. A simplified balance is dN/dt = R − kN. R has units of cells per day and k has units of inverse days. At a steady state the derivative is zero, giving N* = R/k. Increasing production raises the predicted equilibrium if removal remains unchanged. If the removal rate also increases, the effect of increased production can be cancelled partly or completely.

This model assumes a well-mixed compartment and a removal process that is proportional to the current number of cells. Actual erythrocytes age and are removed through processes that depend on cell condition, so a constant fractional hazard is not an exact description of their lifespan distribution. It nevertheless provides a transparent comparison of production and loss. In a hypothetical population with R = 100 arbitrary cell units per day and k = 0.01 per day, the equilibrium is 10,000 units. Doubling R and k together leaves this equilibrium unchanged, despite doubling the turnover.

The model also shows why a count changes gradually after production increases. A simple first-order system starting at N0 and subjected to a new fixed R approaches N* as N(t) = N* + (N0 − N*)exp(−kt). The exponential is dimensionless because k and t have reciprocal units. If a system starts at 10,000 units and its new equilibrium is 15,000 units, after one characteristic time 1/k it has completed about 63 percent of the difference. A real EPO response adds developmental delays and changes in the hormone itself, so a more realistic trajectory need not be exactly exponential.

At the endocrine level the feedback signal changes production rather than instantaneously changing the stock. One can imagine a time-dependent function R(E), where E is the concentration of biologically active EPO and the function increases within a responsive range. Kidney oxygenation influences E, while N contributes to oxygen delivery. The resulting closed system is qualitatively different from a culture exposed to a fixed external EPO concentration. A culture dose-response curve measures an open-loop response; it cannot by itself demonstrate whole-organism homeostatic feedback.

9. Thrombopoietin: regulation of megakaryocytes and platelet output

Platelets are small, anucleate cytoplasmic elements derived from megakaryocytes in mammals. They contribute to haemostasis, and their number depends on the balance between generation and clearance. Megakaryocytes are nucleated marrow cells with an unusual maturation programme. They acquire extensive cytoplasm and frequently become highly polyploid through modified cell cycles. Cytoplasmic processes extending into marrow vascular spaces contribute to platelet release. Thrombopoietin, abbreviated TPO, is the principal physiological regulator of this production system, acting through its receptor MPL.

The liver is the major source of circulating TPO, with additional production in other tissues including the kidney. This endocrine source is easily confused with the source of platelets themselves. Hepatocytes generate a regulatory signal; the marrow contains megakaryocytic precursors that respond to it. A rise in TPO concentration does not imply that hepatocytes have begun to make platelets. As in the EPO system, separation of source and target permits an organ to communicate with a specialized production tissue.

TPO acts on megakaryocytic progenitors and other responsive haematopoietic cells. MPL signaling influences survival, expansion and maturation of cells capable of contributing to platelet production. It also has important functions in the maintenance of HSCs. The second action prevents a simplistic rule that TPO can affect only cells which are already producing platelets. A disturbance of MPL signaling may affect both a particular mature output and the earlier regenerative capacity of the marrow, although the magnitude of each effect depends on the context and duration of the disturbance.

Megakaryocyte polyploidization is a distinct process from ordinary multiplication of cell number. During endomitosis, DNA is replicated in cycles that do not culminate in complete cell division. A cell with more copies of its genome can enlarge and develop extensive cytoplasm while remaining one megakaryocyte. Doubling its DNA content does not double the number of megakaryocytes in the tissue. In an experimental comparison, the proportion of highly polyploid megakaryocytes, the total number of megakaryocytes and the number of newly generated platelets are therefore separate measurements.

An increase in megakaryocyte number is not automatically accompanied by a proportional increase in circulating platelet count. Maturation and release must occur, and newly generated platelets may be consumed or cleared rapidly. For example, a hypothetical change that preserves progenitor survival but prevents terminal cytoplasmic organization could increase megakaryocyte abundance while leaving platelet output low. Conversely, more efficient platelet release might increase output without an equally large change in the number of recognizable megakaryocytes.

The origin of TPO as a recognized growth factor was established after experimental systems for several other haematopoietic cytokines had been developed. Molecular identification in the 1990s established the connection between the ligand and MPL. Historical accounts of the discovery highlight how a factor could be physiologically important even while its purification and identification were difficult. The decisive biological question was whether the candidate ligand could account for regulated megakaryocyte and platelet production, not whether its name resembled those of other factors.

Loss of functional TPO or MPL would be expected to impair normal thrombopoiesis. If MPL is absent, adding extra TPO cannot replace the receptor. A response in the opposite direction may arise from an activating receptor or downstream signaling mutation that permits abnormal proliferation or survival at reduced ligand concentrations. Such perturbations illustrate the same signal-source, receptor and output distinction encountered in the erythroid system. Disease classification and detailed treatment are not needed to explain the general principle.

10. Platelet feedback: regulation of hormone clearance and production

A classical model of TPO homeostasis starts with the observation that circulating platelets and megakaryocytes express MPL and can bind and remove the hormone from circulation. If there are fewer receptor-bearing cells, the total capacity to remove TPO may decrease. Under an otherwise constant secretion rate, the concentration of unbound hormone can rise. Increased availability then supports production by responsive precursors. As the receptor-bearing cell mass recovers, hormone removal can increase. This provides a feedback mechanism in which changing hormone clearance contributes to changing hormone concentration.

The logic is easier to see by considering a water tank with a constant inflow and an outlet whose capacity changes. A reduction in drainage raises the amount of water in the tank without requiring any increase in inflow. TPO is not literally water, and its binding, distribution and degradation are regulated processes rather than a simple open pipe. The analogy isolates a critical distinction: high hormone concentration can arise from reduced clearance, increased production or both. Measuring the concentration alone cannot establish which process changed.

The classical clearance model is not the entire explanation of TPO homeostasis. In addition to MPL-dependent removal of hormone, changes in the surface glycans of ageing platelets can influence hepatic hormone synthesis. Terminal sialic acid residues on some platelet glycoproteins decline as platelets age or undergo particular alterations. Exposed underlying sugars can be recognized by the hepatic Ashwell–Morell receptor, which participates in removal of desialylated platelets. Studies by Grozovsky and colleagues linked this recognition and clearance to increased expression of TPO in the liver through JAK2–STAT3-dependent signaling.

This mechanism does not contradict receptor-mediated clearance of circulating TPO. One pathway concerns recognition and disposal of particular platelets, with consequences for hepatic TPO synthesis. The other concerns binding and removal of TPO by receptor-bearing blood and marrow cells. Their regulatory signs and biological variables are different. The first can couple loss of ageing platelets to replacement; the second can alter free hormone availability according to the size and receptor status of the target population. Both mechanisms may operate in the same organism.

A reduction in circulating platelet count can result from several causes. Reduced production may leave fewer megakaryocytes and platelets capable of removing TPO, whereas rapid peripheral platelet loss may coexist with an expanded megakaryocyte population. The latter can still provide considerable hormone-removal capacity. Accordingly, platelet concentration and free TPO concentration do not obey a simple universal inverse proportionality. The route of platelet clearance, the size of the megakaryocyte compartment, hepatic production and receptor expression all matter.

The difference between the EPO and TPO systems can be expressed by asking what directly influences hormone abundance. In the EPO system, oxygen-dependent regulation of gene expression in renal cells is central. In the TPO system, hepatic production and receptor-mediated clearance both contribute, with platelet disposal pathways providing additional feedback. Both systems support a negative homeostatic relationship at the level of mature-cell replacement, but the molecular implementation is not identical. There is no reason for two organ-to-marrow signals to use the same method of detecting demand.

A useful experiment would distinguish hormone removal from hormone synthesis. In a hypothetical comparison, hepatocytes remain unchanged while the number of MPL-bearing cells exposed to a fixed amount of TPO is varied. A difference in the rate of hormone disappearance supports an effect on removal. In another experiment, hepatocytes are exposed to differently treated platelets and THPO messenger RNA is measured. A change in transcript abundance provides evidence about hepatic regulation, although changes in hormone secretion and receptor-specific signaling would require their own measurements. The two experimental designs ask different questions about the same circulating ligand.
