1. Why a heartbeat is not the same as effective circulation

A heart can produce a regular rhythm without providing an adequate supply of blood to tissues. The electrical rhythm determines when contraction should begin, but it does not specify the amount of blood that each contraction will deliver. A ventricle that fills with very little blood cannot eject a large volume even if its muscle develops considerable tension. A ventricle that fills normally but encounters a large opposing arterial load may also deliver a small volume. Conversely, a strong ventricular contraction may eject a large proportion of a relatively small initial volume. The central physiological problem is to explain the amount of blood transported per unit time rather than merely to count beats.

The mammalian heart contains two pumps connected through two vascular circuits. Blood leaving the right ventricle passes through the pulmonary vessels and returns to the left atrium. Blood leaving the left ventricle passes through the systemic circulation before returning to the right atrium. The pumps differ in the pressure they ordinarily develop because the systemic vascular circuit offers much more resistance than the pulmonary circuit. Nevertheless, over a sufficiently long interval in a stable circulation, their effective outputs must match. If the right ventricle continuously ejected more blood than the left, pulmonary blood volume would rise without limit. That cannot represent a steady state. Short transient inequalities can occur as vascular and cardiac compartments temporarily store blood, especially during changes in breathing and posture.

Pressure, volume and flow describe distinct properties. A sealed chamber can sustain pressure without delivering flow through an outlet. Flow requires a route and a pressure gradient across it. The ventricular wall converts chemical energy into mechanical work; some of that work is transmitted to arterial blood, enabling flow through the vasculature. A pressure measurement alone cannot establish that the amount of flow is sufficient. The relation between pressure and flow depends on the mechanical properties of the vascular system. For this reason, a reduction in arterial pressure may result from a reduction in cardiac output, a reduction in vascular resistance, or a combination of both.

The previous chapter described the sequence of valve movements and the changing pressure and volume within a cardiac cycle. Here the question shifts from the sequence of events to their quantitative consequences. We will first measure the volume expelled in one beat and then examine how filling, arterial load and the state of the muscle alter that volume. Finally we will connect the pump to the returning blood and to whole-body oxygen consumption. This integrated viewpoint is necessary to understand why an isolated ventricular preparation and an intact animal may respond differently to the same intervention.

2. Stroke volume, ejection fraction and cardiac output

At the end of ventricular filling, immediately before systolic ejection, the chamber contains its end-diastolic volume (EDV). At the end of systole, the remaining chamber volume is called the end-systolic volume (ESV). The amount by which ventricular volume decreases during ejection is its stroke volume (SV). For a normally functioning heart without appreciable valve leakage, this is also the volume that passes forward through the corresponding semilunar valve. With a leaky atrioventricular valve, part of the volume decrease can instead be directed backward, so total ventricular stroke volume and effective forward stroke volume must be distinguished. The simple chamber-volume difference is still meaningful, but it is no longer sufficient to quantify systemic delivery.

The ejection fraction (EF) divides stroke volume by the volume initially available for ejection. It is a dimensionless fraction, usually expressed as a percentage. For EDV of 120 mL and ESV of 50 mL, stroke volume is 70 mL and ejection fraction is 70/120, or 58.3%. ESV is not an abnormal residue that must disappear for the heart to function efficiently; the ventricle normally retains blood after systole. Ejection fraction is useful because it places the expelled volume in relation to chamber size, but it is not a direct measurement of intrinsic muscle contractility. Changing either the initial filling or the opposing load can alter EF without changing the biochemical state of the myocardium.

Consider two hypothetical hearts. Heart A fills to 100 mL and retains 40 mL; heart B fills to 180 mL and retains 120 mL. Both eject 60 mL, although their ejection fractions are 60% and 33.3% respectively. If they beat at the same rate and have competent valves, their forward cardiac outputs are equal. This example demonstrates that a percentage cannot be interpreted as an absolute volume. Conversely, a small ventricle that ejects 50% of 70 mL delivers only 35 mL, whereas another ventricle with the same EF and an EDV of 140 mL delivers twice that amount.

Cardiac output (CO) is the volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per unit time. When stroke volume is stable over the counting interval, CO equals the product of heart rate and stroke volume. A rate of 75 beats per minute and an SV of 70 mL per beat give 5250 mL/min, or 5.25 L/min. The beat unit cancels in the multiplication. When individual beats differ substantially, use their actual summed forward volumes over the observation interval; multiplying the number of beats per minute by their mean stroke volume gives the same result if the mean uses those same beats.

A common error is to add right and left ventricular output. In a stable mammal without shunts, each ventricle pumps essentially the same volume per minute. Adding both counts the same circulating blood twice. In contrast, the flows to organs arranged in parallel in the systemic circuit can be added to obtain systemic flow. Cardiac index is cardiac output divided by body surface area, usually expressed in L/min/m², and allows a limited comparison between humans of different size. It does not remove every influence of body composition, age, activity or temperature. A numerical value of cardiac output is therefore best interpreted together with its measurement conditions and metabolic context.

SV = EDV − ESV; EF (%) = 100 × SV / EDV; CO = HR × SV

3. Preload: the starting mechanical state

A muscle does not begin every contraction from the same initial length. Its force-generating machinery is organized into sarcomeres, and the dimensions of the sarcomeres immediately before activation influence subsequent force development. In ventricular physiology the term preload refers to the mechanical loading or stretch present before contraction. End-diastolic wall stress is a chamber-level representation of that load. EDV is a convenient index of stretch because filling enlarges the chamber, but a given volume need not generate identical wall stress in differently shaped or thick-walled ventricles.

End-diastolic pressure is another practical index. To understand its limitations, imagine two ventricular chambers receiving the same additional amount of fluid. A compliant chamber accommodates the extra volume with a small pressure rise, whereas a stiff chamber experiences a larger pressure rise. Compliance is the local change in volume divided by change in pressure. It is not necessarily constant over the filling range. A typical passive ventricular pressure–volume relation becomes progressively steeper at large volumes because the passive structural elements resist further expansion. Thus a high end-diastolic pressure does not prove that the ventricular volume is large, and a single pressure reading cannot independently specify myocardial fibre length.

The pressure distending a chamber is the difference between pressure inside the chamber and pressure outside it. For example, a rise in intrathoracic pressure may raise the measured intracardiac pressure without raising the distending pressure. This distinction becomes important when interpreting heart–lung interactions. A high right atrial pressure measured relative to atmosphere is not automatically evidence of increased right ventricular preload if the surrounding pressure rises at the same time. Pericardial restraint and the volume of the neighbouring ventricle also influence the relationship between pressure and volume.

Venous return is an important determinant of filling, but it is a flow rather than a mechanical load. The blood entering the atria during a given interval must either move onward or temporarily increase the blood stored in cardiac and nearby vascular compartments. A rise in venous return tends to increase end-diastolic volume when ventricular rate and compliance are held constant. In an intact animal, however, those other quantities may change concurrently. A large rise in heart rate can reduce filling per beat despite an increase in venous return per minute. Careful experiments therefore distinguish the input flow, ventricular volume, filling pressure and muscle length instead of treating them as synonyms.

The atrium assists ventricular filling near the end of diastole when the atrioventricular valve remains open. The significance of this contribution depends on relaxation, compliance and the time available for filling. With a compliant, slowly beating heart, much filling occurs passively before atrial contraction. A stiff or rapidly beating ventricle may depend more on a well-timed atrial contribution. Loss of that contribution need not stop ejection, but it can reduce the volume available for the next systole. The effect on cardiac output depends on what happens to rate, remaining filling and vascular return.

Compliance = ΔV/ΔP; transmural pressure = internal pressure − external pressure

4. Frank–Starling behaviour and the sarcomere

The Frank–Starling mechanism describes the tendency of a heart to develop a stronger contraction and eject more blood when its initial filling increases within the physiological range, at otherwise comparable contractile state and load. The name combines the contributions of Otto Frank, who examined mechanical relationships of the heart, and Ernest Starling and colleagues, who studied the relationship between filling and cardiac performance in heart–lung preparations. Their experimental approach allowed the mechanical input and output of the pump to be considered separately rather than inferred solely from arterial pressure in an intact animal.

The importance of the relationship is evident in a thought experiment. Suppose blood arriving at the right ventricle increases for several beats while its intrinsic contractile state and rate remain unchanged. More blood tends to remain available at the end of filling. Within the functional range, this increases initial muscle length and facilitates a more forceful subsequent contraction, so the ventricle tends to deliver more blood. The pulmonary circulation then receives more flow, and the left ventricle can respond to the resulting increase in filling. This intrinsic response helps match the two pumps without requiring a neural command that specifies the required stroke volume of every individual beat.

At the molecular level, ventricular contraction begins when cytosolic calcium activates the thin filament. Calcium binding to troponin C changes the regulatory state of the troponin–tropomyosin complex, allowing force-generating interactions between myosin heads and actin. Length-dependent activation means that at longer physiological sarcomere lengths the myofilaments produce greater force for a given level of activating calcium. This cannot be explained exclusively as a change in actin–myosin overlap. Changes in calcium sensitivity, the structural arrangement and recruitment of force-generating myosin heads, and titin-associated mechanical effects contribute. The relative contributions vary with preparation and experimental conditions; it is not necessary to assume a single universal molecular explanation.

An experiment on a permeabilised cardiac muscle preparation can separate calcium sensitivity from calcium delivery. The researcher imposes different initial lengths, controls the bathing calcium activity and measures developed force. If force is greater at the longer length at the same calcium activity, the difference does not require a larger calcium transient generated by a living membrane. A separate experiment in an intact beating preparation can measure both cytosolic calcium and force after a length change. Such comparisons are important because a slower change in force after stretch may involve changes in calcium handling in addition to the immediate length-dependent response.

A ventricular function curve can display stroke volume on the vertical axis and end-diastolic volume or an appropriate filling-pressure index on the horizontal axis. Under a fixed contractile state and afterload, increasing filling moves the operating point along the curve. The increase in stroke volume is not indefinitely proportional to filling. At high distending pressures or volumes, additional stretch may produce little additional output while passive tension rises greatly. A markedly weakened heart may operate on a flatter portion of its function curve. A diagram extending indefinitely as a straight ascending line is therefore a poor representation of biological performance.

The response to filling must not be confused with an increase in inotropy. If the initial end-diastolic volume is kept the same and a positive inotropic stimulus enables greater ejection against the same load, the ventricular function curve has changed. An increase in filling, in contrast, selects a different position on an existing curve. This distinction is operational: compare controlled conditions rather than using stronger contraction as the sole criterion. In an intact animal, an intervention such as exercise can increase both filling and inotropy, making the observed change in stroke volume a combined result.

5. Afterload and the opposition to ejection

Afterload is the mechanical opposition against which an activated ventricle develops force and shortens. In a simplified description of the left ventricle, the pressure of blood in the aorta is an important component of the load. The aortic valve does not open merely because electrical excitation occurs. Ventricular pressure must first exceed aortic pressure. A rise in the pressure of the aorta therefore raises the pressure the ventricle must generate before ejection can begin, all else being equal.

Afterload is more precisely related to wall stress during contraction and ejection, not merely to a single measurement of arterial pressure. In a thin-walled spherical approximation, circumferential stress is pressure difference multiplied by radius and divided by twice wall thickness. This simplified Laplace relation helps explain why a large chamber can experience greater wall stress at the same pressure, and why wall thickening can reduce stress for a given pressure and radius. The real ventricle has a thick, curved and actively contracting wall with a complex shape; the formula is a qualitative guide rather than an exact calculation of myocardial stress in every region.

A larger load generally reduces the amount and velocity of shortening achieved at a fixed activation state and initial length. In an idealized immediate increase in left ventricular afterload with EDV and inotropy held constant, ejection decreases, leaving a larger ESV. The volume difference EDV minus ESV therefore falls. This effect can be followed by a change in the starting volume of the next beat because additional residual blood remains in the chamber. The next beat is not necessarily identical to the first; this is why an immediate mechanical prediction should be distinguished from a later integrated response.

Systemic vascular resistance contributes to the arterial pressure and hence to ventricular loading, but resistance and afterload are not identical. The compliance of large arteries, characteristic impedance of the outflow tract, the timing of reflected pressure waves and valve obstruction can all influence the load encountered during ejection. Two ventricles with the same mean arterial pressure need not have identical systolic wall stresses or pressure waveforms. For the right ventricle, the relevant arterial circuit is the pulmonary circulation, so a selective rise in pulmonary vascular resistance may alter its output even if systemic arterial pressure initially remains unchanged.

The load-dependent relationship also explains why it is misleading to call a low EF proof of weak muscle. If the heart suddenly has to work against a greater opposing pressure, it may retain more blood after systole while its intrinsic ability to generate active tension remains unchanged. The observation that ejection improved after the opposing load was lowered likewise does not, on its own, prove that the myofilaments became stronger. A scientifically valid interpretation requires some control or measurement of the initial loading state.

Thin spherical wall approximation: σ ≈ ΔP × r/(2h)

6. Contractility and calcium-dependent force generation

Contractility, or inotropic state, refers to the capacity of the activated myocardium to generate force and perform shortening at comparable initial length and external load. Unlike preload, it is not defined by the mechanical starting point. Unlike afterload, it is not defined by the external opposition to shortening. Operationally, a change in inotropy is inferred from a change in the force or ejection obtained under controlled mechanical conditions. No simple whole-heart measurement is perfectly independent of all loading and rate effects.

Sympathetic stimulation provides a familiar physiological example. Activation of cardiac beta-1 adrenergic receptors can stimulate the Gs–adenylyl cyclase pathway, raising cyclic AMP and protein kinase A activity. Phosphorylation of regulatory targets increases the calcium available to activate contraction and facilitates calcium cycling. Enhanced L-type calcium current promotes calcium-induced calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Phosphorylation of phospholamban relieves inhibition of the sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium pump SERCA, facilitating calcium reuptake and helping subsequent calcium loading. Phosphorylation of other targets, including troponin I, contributes to faster relaxation. These actions allow the ventricle to produce stronger contractions and relax effectively during a shorter interval.

The distinction between inotropy and lusitropy is useful. Inotropy describes the force-generating state, whereas lusitropy describes relaxation. Faster relaxation helps prepare the ventricle for the next filling phase by lowering its pressure earlier in diastole. Chronotropy denotes an effect on heart rate, and dromotropy denotes an effect on conduction. A single sympathetic stimulus can influence all four properties, but they are not interchangeable definitions. An isolated ventricular muscle experiment paced at a constant rate can help separate inotropic changes from changes in frequency.

If the initial volume and arterial load are held constant, a positive inotropic change generally reduces the end-systolic volume. The resulting rise in stroke volume is produced by more complete ejection from the same starting volume. On a pressure–volume diagram this can be visualized as a change in the end-systolic pressure–volume relation. In the useful linear approximation over a limited loading range, the slope is called end-systolic elastance. It is a relatively load-insensitive index of ventricular contractile state compared with ejection fraction, but it still depends on the model, chamber geometry, measurement method and physiological conditions. An elastance is a pressure change per unit volume change, not a pressure or a dimensionless fraction.

Ees ≈ ΔPes/ΔVes (linear approximation)

7. The pressure–volume loop as a perturbation experiment

A ventricular pressure–volume loop summarizes one beat as a closed path in the plane of ventricular pressure and volume. Starting at the low-pressure, end-systolic volume point, filling moves the trace to the right; isovolumetric contraction then raises pressure with little change in volume; ejection decreases volume; isovolumetric relaxation returns pressure toward its starting level. The loop width is the difference between end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes. The area enclosed by the loop represents external stroke work in pressure–volume units. With pressure in mmHg and volume in mL, multiplication by approximately 0.000133 converts mmHg·mL into joules.

When preload is selectively increased, the right boundary of the loop generally moves toward larger volumes. Under a simplified fixed afterload and fixed end-systolic relation, ESV may change little, and the loop becomes wider. When afterload is increased abruptly at the same EDV and contractile state, a higher pressure is needed for ejection. More blood remains at end systole, moving the left boundary to the right and narrowing the loop. When positive inotropy increases at the same starting volume and arterial load, the end-systolic boundary can move leftward and widen the loop. These idealized comparisons are valuable because they isolate variables that may otherwise change together.

An actual arterial system is not a fixed pressure reservoir. Increased stroke volume can raise arterial pressure, and a change in arterial pressure can feed back on the ventricle. The end-systolic point therefore reflects an intersection between myocardial properties and the vascular load. Similarly, an altered heart rate changes the time available for relaxation and filling and may change contractile state. The idealized pressure–volume predictions describe the controlled initial comparison, not necessarily the final steady-state result. A student interpreting a figure must identify which variables were held fixed and which were allowed to vary.

A useful teaching calculation compares three hypothetical conditions at EDV 120 mL. Baseline ESV is 50 mL, so SV is 70 mL and EF is 58.3%. After an abrupt increase in arterial load, ESV rises to 70 mL and SV falls to 50 mL, giving EF of 41.7%. After a separate positive inotropic intervention under the baseline arterial load, ESV is 35 mL and SV becomes 85 mL, giving EF of 70.8%. These values are illustrative rather than measurements from a published experiment. They show why the direction of ESV change is informative only when the EDV and mechanical conditions are known.

8. The venous side of the circuit

Veins contain a large fraction of the blood volume and can accommodate substantial volume changes at relatively small pressure changes. This property makes the venous system important in determining how much blood is available for cardiac filling. During quiet upright standing, gravity changes the pressure distribution in the lower body. Some blood accumulates in compliant dependent veins, reducing central blood volume compared with the supine condition. If reflex adjustments were prevented, the resulting reduction in ventricular filling would tend to reduce stroke volume.

The skeletal muscle pump uses external compression of veins by contracting muscles. Venous valves help prevent backward movement, so repeated compression and relaxation can favour net movement toward the heart. The valves are passive structures responding to pressure differences; they are not tiny independent muscular pumps. During sustained isometric contraction, compression may impede flow instead of providing the repeated emptying and refilling that characterizes an effective muscle pump. An intact valve system and an available return path are necessary for the usual beneficial effect.

Spontaneous inspiration typically lowers intrathoracic pressure and can increase abdominal pressure as the diaphragm descends. The resulting redistribution of pressures favours systemic venous flow toward the right atrium under many ordinary conditions. However, respiratory changes also influence pulmonary vascular blood storage, ventricular interaction and left ventricular afterload. Right and left ventricular stroke volume may therefore change in different phases of the respiratory cycle. During positive-pressure inspiration, the pressure changes differ from those during spontaneous inspiration; an increase in intrathoracic pressure can reduce the gradient available for venous return. The actual effect depends on blood volume, vascular pressures, resistance and surrounding tissue pressure.

Venoconstriction can shift blood from relatively unstressed volume, which fills vessels without developing substantial recoil pressure, into stressed volume, which distends the vessels and contributes to pressure. This does not create new blood. It alters the volume–pressure relationship of the circulation. A rise in stressed volume can increase the equilibrium pressure associated with the systemic vascular compartment and promote flow toward the heart in a suitable model. Conversely, venodilation can increase capacitance and reduce the pressure associated with a given volume. The concepts of stressed and unstressed volume are idealizations of a nonlinear vascular system, not two physically separated reservoirs of blood.

9. Cardiac function, venous-return curves and steady state

The classical Guyton framework relates systemic venous return to a pressure difference and an effective resistance. Mean systemic filling pressure (Pmsf) is the equilibrium pressure in the systemic vascular compartment under no-flow conditions when pressures have had an opportunity to redistribute under the specified vascular state. It is not mean arterial pressure and is not the pressure at every point of an actively circulating system. Right atrial pressure (Pra) is the downstream pressure in the simplified model, and resistance to venous return (Rvr) represents the effective resistance linking the distributed systemic vascular compartments to the right atrium.

Within the linear range of the model, venous return equals (Pmsf minus Pra) divided by Rvr. If pressures are given in mmHg and resistance in mmHg per (L/min), the result is L/min. At a Pmsf of 8 mmHg, right atrial pressure of 3 mmHg and Rvr of 1 mmHg per (L/min), predicted venous return is 5 L/min. At Pra equal to Pmsf, the model predicts zero flow. At very low right atrial pressures, collapsible veins can limit further flow, producing a plateau instead of the indefinite increase predicted by extending the straight line.

A cardiac function curve plots output against a filling-pressure index, often right atrial pressure in the combined systemic model. When cardiac pump function improves, it may deliver greater flow at a given right atrial pressure. The venous-return curve generally slopes downward with increasing right atrial pressure within its linear range. The intersection gives the equilibrium flow and right atrial pressure under the specified conditions. Neither curve independently determines both variables. A positive inotropic shift can raise flow and reduce right atrial pressure at the new operating point; a volume-induced increase in Pmsf can raise flow and right atrial pressure. These are conditional predictions for the simple model.

Conservation of blood volume is the reason the intersection matters. For a control volume containing the central heart and pulmonary vascular compartments, its rate of storage equals inflow minus outflow. If systemic venous return exceeds left ventricular forward flow, blood accumulates within these compartments. If left ventricular forward flow exceeds systemic venous return, the stored volume decreases. Such changes alter filling pressures and volumes, shifting the operating state. In a stable closed circulation without shunts, net accumulation approaches zero over an appropriate averaging period. The equality of flows is therefore a mass-balance requirement, not a claim that their values are identical at every instant.

The Guyton diagram is an experimentally useful graphical representation of a coupled system, but students should avoid interpreting its axes as a demonstration of a unique causal direction. Right atrial pressure is itself affected by cardiac function and the vascular state. Mean systemic filling pressure is a no-flow reference, not a continuously operating auxiliary pump. The heart supplies the mechanical energy needed to maintain circulation, while vascular recoil and resistance influence the distribution and return of blood. The simplified relation is most informative when its assumptions and the interventions used to construct a curve are stated.

VR = (Pmsf − Pra)/Rvr; at steady state, VR = CO

10. Fick principle: measuring flow by conservation of oxygen

A direct measure of every volume ejected by a beating heart is not always convenient. Adolf Fick proposed a different approach in 1870: infer flow from the rate at which a substance is exchanged and the difference in its content between entering and leaving blood. Oxygen is useful because the body removes it from systemic blood at a measurable rate, and the lungs restore oxygen to the blood. The underlying principle is conservation of matter, independent of the particular shape of the ventricular chamber.

Consider the whole systemic vascular bed in a stable subject. During each minute, the entering arterial blood carries a quantity of oxygen equal to blood flow multiplied by arterial oxygen content. Blood returning through the systemic veins carries an amount equal to the same flow multiplied by mixed venous oxygen content. The difference is the net oxygen transferred to tissues per minute. If there is negligible net storage of oxygen during the measurement interval and no unaccounted exchange route, this equals whole-body oxygen consumption. Rearranging gives cardiac output equal to oxygen consumption per minute divided by the arterial–mixed venous oxygen content difference.

The word content is essential. Oxygen content is the amount of oxygen carried per volume of blood; partial pressure is not a quantity of oxygen per volume of whole blood, and percentage haemoglobin saturation is a fraction of binding sites occupied. Most blood oxygen is transported bound to haemoglobin, with a smaller dissolved component. Two samples with the same oxygen saturation but different haemoglobin concentrations can carry quite different quantities of oxygen. Subtracting arterial and venous partial pressures directly in the Fick equation would be dimensionally and physiologically wrong.

For a hypothetical resting subject, oxygen consumption is 250 mL O₂/min, arterial oxygen content is 20 mL O₂/dL and mixed venous oxygen content is 15 mL O₂/dL. The difference is 5 mL O₂/dL. Dividing 250 by 5 gives 50 dL/min, which is 5 L/min because ten decilitres make one litre. If the same oxygen consumption is accompanied by a content difference of 10 mL O₂/dL, the inferred flow is 2.5 L/min. The direction is inverse because a larger amount extracted from each unit of blood allows a specified total consumption to be met with fewer units of flowing blood.

Mixed venous blood is sampled after systemic venous contributions have mixed, ordinarily using pulmonary arterial blood in the classical direct oxygen Fick method. A single peripheral vein reflects the metabolism and flow of a local region and may not represent whole-body extraction. Measurement of oxygen uptake must also correspond to the blood sampling interval; using a resting assumed oxygen consumption during exercise introduces an error. Shunts and rapidly changing oxygen stores complicate the assignment of pulmonary and systemic flows. The direct Fick approach is conceptually powerful precisely because its assumptions can be identified from the conservation equation.

The Fick relationship can also be used to understand why oxygen delivery differs from oxygen consumption. Delivery to the systemic circulation equals cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content. At 5 L/min and 200 mL O₂/L, delivery is 1000 mL O₂/min. If consumption is 250 mL/min, the extraction fraction is 25%. During exercise, both flow and extraction can increase, permitting a large rise in consumption. Reduced haemoglobin can reduce delivery even when CO remains unchanged. A flow measurement therefore cannot by itself establish adequacy of tissue oxygenation.

CO = V̇O₂/(CaO₂ − CvO₂); oxygen delivery = CO × CaO₂

11. Heart rate, cardiac reserve and exercise

Cardiac reserve describes the capacity to raise cardiac output above a resting level. It can be expressed as the difference between maximal and resting cardiac output, or as a percentage of resting output, provided the convention is stated. An individual with resting output of 5 L/min and a maximal output of 20 L/min has an absolute reserve of 15 L/min and a relative reserve of 300% above rest. Maximal output itself is 400% of resting output. These two percentage statements have different numerators, so an examination problem should state whether it asks for maximum relative to rest or the increase above rest.

An increase in rate increases the number of opportunities to eject blood per minute. If stroke volume were fixed, the product HR times SV would increase linearly with rate. Stroke volume is not fixed across all physiological rates. At higher rates, diastole becomes disproportionately shorter, potentially limiting ventricular filling. The effect depends on relaxation, venous return, contractility and rhythm. During physiological exercise, enhanced relaxation and sympathetic support can maintain or increase stroke volume over a substantial range of rates. Beyond a limiting rate, further shortening of filling time can reduce SV sufficiently that cardiac output plateaus or even declines.

Consider an illustrative comparison: at 70 beats/min and SV 75 mL, CO is 5.25 L/min. At 140 beats/min and SV 90 mL, CO is 12.6 L/min. At an excessive rate of 200 beats/min with SV reduced to 45 mL, CO is only 9 L/min. The fastest rate in this example does not provide the largest flow. These hypothetical values isolate the arithmetic of a physiological trade-off; they are not proposed universal rate thresholds or measured exercise results.

Exercise increases muscle oxygen demand and requires coordinated adjustments. Rhythmic muscle contraction supports venous return; local vascular responses help direct blood toward metabolically active tissues; sympathetic stimulation can increase heart rate, contractility and venous tone. The precise balance depends on the intensity and type of exercise, body position, training and environmental conditions. Cardiac output and venous return rise together at the resulting steady state, while arterial–venous oxygen difference may also rise. Detailed local and neural regulation belongs to the subsequent cardiovascular nodes, but the pump mechanism explains how the required increased flow becomes mechanically possible.

12. How the pump was separated experimentally from its circulation

The central difficulty in identifying the determinants of cardiac output is that the variables in a living circulation are interdependent. A rise in arterial pressure increases the pressure against which the left ventricle ejects, but it may also change the autonomic signals reaching the heart. A rise in venous return increases ventricular filling, but it can change arterial pressure after several beats. The observer who measures only output before and after an intervention may know that something changed, but cannot necessarily identify the mechanism responsible. Much of the history of cardiac physiology involves developing ways to isolate these variables without losing the functional properties of the heart.

An isolated ventricular muscle preparation offers one solution. The tissue can be stimulated electrically at a defined frequency, maintained at a controlled temperature and supplied with a solution that supports its metabolism. A mechanical apparatus sets the initial length of the muscle and measures its developed tension. If the muscle ends are held fixed during stimulation, the preparation contracts isometrically: its overall length remains constant while tension changes. If it is allowed to shorten against a defined load, the shortening distance and velocity can instead be measured. A comparison of contractions at different initial lengths is meaningful only when the stimulation frequency, external ionic conditions and metabolic support are comparable.

An isolated heart provides a second level of experimental organization. The ventricular muscle remains arranged around a chamber, so investigators can relate force generation to pressure and volume. In an isovolumetric preparation, a fluid-filled balloon inside the ventricle may be connected to a pressure transducer. Changing balloon volume changes initial chamber distension, and recording the pressure transient reveals the mechanical response. The relevant quantity is often developed pressure, the difference between systolic and diastolic pressure, because a distended chamber may already exhibit substantial passive pressure before active contraction. The balloon preparation generates pressure but does not eject blood in the normal manner; consequently, it cannot directly establish a forward stroke volume from the pressure record alone.

A working heart preparation permits ejection through an outlet connected to an adjustable load. The researcher may vary the inflow reservoir pressure while maintaining a comparable outflow load and stimulation rate, then measure the volume emerging from the outlet during a known time. This arrangement brings the pump closer to its physiological task. If increased inflow pressure raises EDV and output without any change in stimulation, the finding supports an intrinsic filling-dependent response. A preparation of this kind still differs from an intact animal: neural feedback, hormone release, whole-body metabolic demands and blood redistribution are absent or altered.

Starling and colleagues used heart–lung preparations to investigate the performance of the circulation while controlling important aspects of venous input and arterial resistance. The continued involvement of the lungs helped support gas exchange while the systemic circulation could be represented by an adjustable experimental circuit. Such preparations were particularly valuable for distinguishing intrinsic mechanical adjustment from changes initiated by the nervous system. The historical conclusion was not that nerves and hormones are unimportant, but that increased filling can elicit stronger mechanical performance without a new external neural command.

The distinction between input and output is illustrated by an elementary reservoir experiment. Imagine a pump supplied from an upstream container whose height can be changed. Increasing the height raises the hydrostatic pressure available to deliver fluid toward the pump. If the pump ejects more per cycle in response, one must determine whether the change came from increased initial chamber volume, higher contraction frequency, altered opposition to outflow or a direct change in the pump's motor. A physiological preparation adds these measurements rather than treating reservoir height itself as a complete explanation. Reservoir height is an intervention; ventricular preload is the mechanical condition that may follow.

There is an important difference between controlling a variable and merely recording it. If an isolated heart is paced electrically at 75 beats/min, a subsequent change in output cannot be attributed to an increase in the imposed pacing rate, although changes in electrical conduction or contraction reliability still require attention. If rate is only recorded, and it changes when inflow increases, the observed output change has more than one potential explanation. A strong experimental design explicitly states which quantity is experimentally fixed and which quantity is an observed response.

Temperature is an example of a potentially hidden variable. Changes in temperature affect enzyme activity, membrane currents and the rates of cross-bridge cycling. A progressive decline in muscle performance could arise from deteriorating oxygen supply or temperature regulation instead of the experimental change in length. Returning periodically to a baseline condition helps establish whether the preparation remains stable. Randomizing the order of comparable interventions, where appropriate, reduces confusion between treatment effects and time-dependent deterioration. Repeated measurements in separate preparations are necessary before a general biological inference can be made.

A further difficulty is the difference between a measurement taken immediately after an intervention and one taken after the system has reached a new equilibrium. Changing aortic pressure can alter ESV in the first beat. Residual volume then changes the initial filling condition of the next beat, and a continuing sequence of adjustments follows. In an intact animal, reflex changes may also develop. A recording that averages many minutes cannot automatically reveal the first-beat mechanical effect. Time-resolved pressure and volume measurements allow a student to distinguish immediate load dependence from secondary responses.

The classical preparations remain conceptually valuable because their limitations are visible. Isolated muscles help identify cellular mechanisms but lack chamber geometry. Isovolumetric hearts demonstrate pressure generation but cannot directly measure normal ejection. Working hearts permit flow measurement but simplify the vascular circuit. Intact-animal studies reveal integrated regulation but introduce interdependent variables. The strength of physiological knowledge comes from consistent evidence across these levels, not from assuming that one arrangement reproduces every feature of an animal.

13. Pressure generation, passive tension and the force–length experiment

It is possible for cardiac muscle to exhibit a larger total force at a greater initial length even before the mechanisms of active contraction are considered. A stretched, unactivated muscle resists elongation because its structural elements have elastic properties. This force is called passive tension. When electrical excitation activates the contractile machinery, an additional active component develops. The force recorded by a transducer during a contraction includes the passive force already present and the contribution from active contraction. Consequently, one cannot compare active force simply by comparing two peak total force readings without accounting for the baseline.

Imagine an illustrative preparation in which passive force at a short initial length is 1 mN and peak total force during stimulation is 6 mN. The developed active force is 5 mN. At a greater length, passive force increases to 4 mN and peak total force reaches 12 mN; developed active force is 8 mN. Both passive tension and the active response have increased. At an even greater initial length, passive force might rise to 9 mN while peak total force reaches 16 mN. Although total peak force is greatest, developed force is now 7 mN, less than at the intermediate length. These values are hypothetical and demonstrate why the baseline correction is indispensable.

A force–length graph should therefore distinguish passive force, active developed force and total force. The total force curve is not necessarily shaped like the active force curve. The active force generated by striated muscle depends on molecular interactions between thick and thin filaments, on the availability of activating calcium and on the sensitivity of the contractile apparatus to that calcium. The passive force depends on structures including titin within sarcomeres and extracellular connective tissue. Both influence the mechanical properties of the intact ventricular wall.

Sarcomere length is the distance between successive Z discs. As sarcomeres lengthen within the physiological range of cardiac muscle, the molecular arrangement changes in several ways. In a simplified cross-bridge picture, activated myosin can bind actin and generate force only where the filaments can interact. At extremely long sarcomere lengths, reduced overlap limits the number of possible force-generating interactions. At very short lengths, crowding and unfavorable filament arrangements may interfere with efficient force development. Nevertheless, filament overlap alone is not sufficient to explain the steep dependence of cardiac active tension on length within the ordinary range of ventricular operation.

A calcium–force experiment adds a second axis to the analysis. A muscle can be held at a given length and exposed to a range of calcium activities under conditions that permit calcium access to its contractile apparatus. The measured force usually rises from a low value toward a maximum as activating calcium increases. The calcium concentration needed to produce half the maximum force is a useful operational index of sensitivity. If a longer sarcomere length allows half-maximal activation at a lower calcium concentration, the preparation has become more sensitive to calcium. When calcium is expressed on a negative logarithmic pCa scale, lower concentration corresponds to higher pCa; the direction of a graphical shift must therefore be interpreted from the actual axis labels.

The magnitude of force at a given calcium activity can change for two reasons. The maximum force-generating capacity might change, or the fraction of that capacity activated by the selected calcium level might change. Normalizing each curve to its own maximum helps compare relative sensitivity, while plotting absolute force reveals differences in the maximum itself. Neither representation is universally preferable. A student should determine what question the experiment addresses before deciding whether normalized or absolute data are appropriate.

Permeabilised preparations provide a useful way to examine the contractile apparatus because the investigator can control the surrounding calcium activity more directly than in a normally excitable cell. However, permeabilisation disrupts normal membrane control of calcium and may alter the physical environment of the myofilaments. The experiment establishes properties under specified experimental conditions; it does not prove that the relative contributions of all mechanisms are identical in an intact ventricle during exercise. In an intact muscle, the amplitude and time course of the calcium transient, the rate of calcium removal and the mechanical state of the tissue all influence the contraction.

Titin is a large sarcomeric protein extending through part of the myofibril and contributing to passive elasticity and structural organization. When a sarcomere is stretched, titin develops tension and can influence the spatial and mechanical arrangement of the filaments. These effects are relevant to length-dependent activation. Evidence also supports changes in thin-filament regulation and thick-filament recruitment. For NET and SET reasoning the important conclusion is not that one protein alone explains the entire Starling law, but that the initial length modifies the response of the contractile apparatus to activation through several interacting molecular mechanisms.

Consider a hypothetical comparison of two muscles with identical calcium transients after a rapid stretch. If one muscle develops more force at its new length, a greater calcium transient is not necessary to explain the increased force. Conversely, if the calcium transient grows substantially after several minutes at the longer length, part of the later force response may reflect altered calcium handling. The appropriate conclusion depends on the temporal resolution of the experiment. A rapid intrinsic length-dependent response and a slower secondary response need not have the same mechanism.

The Frank–Starling mechanism is often described as a change in force caused by filling, but at the organ level the outcome of interest is frequently stroke volume or stroke work. An isometric force measurement cannot be substituted directly for ejected volume. Greater capacity to develop force can permit ejection against an opposing load, but the actual volume expelled depends on how long the valve remains open, the changing ventricular and arterial pressures and the geometry of the chamber. The sarcomere experiment explains a mechanism; the working heart experiment establishes how the mechanism contributes to pumping.

14. Relating myocardial mechanics to end-systolic and diastolic pressure–volume relations

The pressure–volume loop becomes a more powerful analytical tool when a set of loops is obtained under several loading conditions rather than a single beat being examined in isolation. If a brief intervention changes ventricular filling while contractile state remains approximately stable, the end-systolic points of successive loops can be plotted together. Over a limited range, these points may be represented by an approximately linear end-systolic pressure–volume relation. Its slope is commonly denoted Ees, the end-systolic elastance. The intercept with the volume axis is denoted V0 in the linear model.

The equation Pes = Ees(Ves − V0) describes the model. Pes is end-systolic pressure in mmHg, Ves is end-systolic volume in mL and V0 is a model intercept in mL. Ees therefore has units of mmHg/mL. It would be incorrect to assign Ees units of mL/mmHg, because those would describe a compliance-like quantity. The intercept is an extrapolated parameter; it need not correspond to a physically empty ventricular chamber or a directly observed volume in a living animal. The pressure–volume relation also need not be exactly linear over very large volume ranges.

To see how the relation constrains ejection, suppose the linear model has Ees equal to 2 mmHg/mL and V0 equal to 10 mL. If end-systolic pressure is 100 mmHg, the corresponding end-systolic volume is 100/2 + 10 = 60 mL. If the same ventricle develops an increased inotropic state represented by an Ees of 4 mmHg/mL, and if end-systolic pressure and V0 remain the same in the simplified comparison, the predicted ESV is 35 mL. With a common EDV of 120 mL, the respective stroke volumes are 60 and 85 mL. This hypothetical calculation shows why an increased slope can correspond to more complete ejection without any initial volume change.

The end-diastolic pressure–volume relation represents a different property. It describes the pressure needed to distend the relaxed ventricular chamber to different volumes under specified external conditions. It usually has a nonlinear shape, because passive stiffness increases as the chamber becomes more distended. For a small region of the curve, diastolic stiffness can be represented by the slope dP/dV. Compliance is the reciprocal of this local slope if the relation is differentiable and the slope is not zero. A steeper diastolic relation means that a given increment of volume produces a larger pressure increment. This does not imply that the end-systolic elastance has increased; the two relations refer to different mechanical states.

An important analytical consequence is that diastolic and systolic dysfunction can influence output in different ways. A chamber with impaired relaxation or increased passive stiffness may fill to a smaller volume at the same atrial and surrounding pressures, even when its ejection fraction is not reduced. A chamber with impaired systolic force generation may retain a larger ESV despite substantial filling. The pressure–volume diagram helps separate these mechanisms because it shows both the lower filling trajectory and the upper ejection trajectory. In real physiology, changes in both properties can coexist.

The work performed on the blood during one beat can be estimated from the area enclosed by the pressure–volume loop. This is not generally equal to the product of peak pressure and stroke volume because pressure varies during ejection and filling. A rough rectangular approximation may be suitable for a teaching problem if explicitly stated. Suppose effective ejection occurs at an average pressure of 95 mmHg and filling at an average pressure of 8 mmHg, with stroke volume of 70 mL. An approximate net work is (95 − 8) × 70 = 6090 mmHg·mL. Since one mmHg is approximately 133.322 Pa and one mL is 10⁻⁶ m³, the work is about 0.812 J. At 75 beats/min, the corresponding external mechanical power is about 60.9 J/min, or 1.015 W. This calculation excludes the right ventricle and does not measure the chemical energy expenditure of the myocardium.

Mechanical efficiency compares useful external mechanical work with the metabolic energy consumed. The ventricular wall uses ATP for active tension generation, calcium cycling, ionic homeostasis and other functions. Even during an isovolumetric contraction that performs little or no external chamber-volume work, active tension and cellular energy expenditure can be substantial. A heart that generates high pressure against a closed valve is not energetically inactive simply because there is no ejection. For this reason, neither stroke volume nor external stroke work alone describes the complete metabolic cost of pumping.

The left and right ventricles usually deliver similar effective mean volumes, but their pressure–volume loops differ because the pulmonary vascular circuit ordinarily presents a smaller pressure load. The left ventricle generally performs more external pressure–volume work per beat. A rise in pulmonary arterial load can increase the mechanical burden of the right ventricle and reduce its output. Through the series connection, this can reduce subsequent filling of the left heart. The detailed responses are affected by ventricular interaction, pericardial constraints and the time required for blood redistribution.

When using pressure–volume curves in experimental questions, identify the measured axes before interpreting the trace. A horizontal displacement on a pressure–volume diagram represents a volume change; a vertical displacement represents a pressure change. A horizontal displacement on a graph of output against filling pressure instead represents a change in filling pressure, not directly a chamber volume. A slope on an end-systolic pressure–volume graph has a different physical meaning from a slope on a cardiac function graph. Similar visual forms can conceal different underlying variables.

A controlled loading intervention is especially informative when both pressure and volume are recorded continuously. If arterial pressure rises and ESV increases while EDV remains nearly constant for the first beat, the immediate result is consistent with greater afterload. If another intervention produces lower ESV at comparable EDV and pressure, a positive inotropic change is one plausible interpretation. If EDV increases while the end-systolic relation is stable, increased preload is a plausible contributor. These conclusions remain conditional on the experimental measurements; one should not claim that a single loop by itself uniquely identifies a change in intrinsic contractile state.

15. Resistance to venous return, stressed volume and the meaning of equilibrium

A vein is not simply an artery operating at lower pressure. Its wall structure and surrounding tissue allow the vessel to change its volume greatly as distending pressure changes. The systematic effect is that many venous segments act as reservoirs as well as conduits. A reservoir can release or retain fluid as its recoil pressure changes; a conduit permits flow when a pressure gradient is maintained. The same anatomical segment can perform both roles. This dual role is central to understanding why cardiac output depends on the circulation beyond the myocardium.

A simple vascular compartment model expresses the volume above an idealized unstressed reference as stressed volume. If a constant compliance C were assumed over a small range, the increase in pressure would be approximately ΔV/C. Suppose the effective compliance of an illustrative systemic compartment is 100 mL/mmHg. An additional 200 mL of stressed volume would increase the equilibrium pressure by about 2 mmHg in that simplified range. If venoconstriction instead decreases the unstressed reference volume by 200 mL without changing total volume, a comparable increase in stressed volume could occur. Real compliance and the reference volume can change simultaneously, so this arithmetic is an explanatory approximation rather than a universal prediction.

The equilibrium pressure after cessation of flow depends on what vascular territories are included in the definition and on whether the system has had enough time to redistribute blood. Mean circulatory filling pressure refers to a broader circulation-wide equilibrium, whereas mean systemic filling pressure is defined for the systemic compartment. These are closely related because much of the vascular compliance resides in systemic veins, but they are conceptually distinct quantities. An experimental estimate based on pressures measured during a transient interruption of circulation is not necessarily identical to a model-derived estimate from active circulation.

Consider a venous-return line with a Pmsf of 9 mmHg and a resistance of 1.5 mmHg per (L/min). Its slope when venous return is plotted against right atrial pressure is −1/1.5, or approximately −0.667 L/min/mmHg. When Pra is 3 mmHg, flow is 4 L/min. When Pra is 6 mmHg, predicted flow is 2 L/min. The intercept on the pressure axis is 9 mmHg, because that is where the model's pressure difference becomes zero. The intercept on the flow axis is 6 L/min if the linear relation applies at Pra equal to zero. A negative predicted flow beyond the zero-flow point would require an assessment of whether the physical assumptions remain applicable, rather than automatic extrapolation to an intact physiological system.

A change in the effective resistance to venous return alters the slope of this graph. If Pmsf stays constant while Rvr increases, the pressure-axis intercept remains the same, but the line becomes less steep in magnitude. At a fixed right atrial pressure below Pmsf, flow is lower. If stressed volume increases Pmsf at the same resistance, the pressure-axis intercept moves to the right, and the line shifts in parallel within the linear region. These graphical distinctions are particularly useful in examinations because a change in slope and a change in intercept carry different information about the model parameters.

Resistance to venous return is not the same quantity as total systemic vascular resistance. Total systemic resistance relates the pressure difference between the arterial inflow and venous outflow of the systemic circuit to total systemic flow. Rvr in the classical lumped model incorporates the distributed resistance and compliance of vascular segments in a way that determines the relation between right atrial pressure and return. Students should not substitute one resistance value for the other merely because both have the same units. A redistribution of resistance between compliant arterial and venous compartments can alter the venous-return relation even when the total systemic resistance does not change in a simple proportion.

The cardiac function curve can also change shape. At low filling, the output of the ventricle generally rises as filling increases. As the ventricle approaches its mechanical capacity at a particular rate and contractile state, additional filling may produce a much smaller increase in flow. A cardiac function curve with a flat upper portion represents this limitation. It is incorrect to extend a steep low-filling relationship indefinitely and predict that an arbitrary rise in filling pressure can produce arbitrarily high output. Large pressures may instead signify poor accommodation of additional blood.

An equilibrium calculation makes the interaction between curves explicit. Suppose a hypothetical cardiac function relation is CO = 1 + Pra, with CO in L/min and Pra in mmHg, and assume the numerical slope is 1 L/min/mmHg. Suppose the venous-return relation is VR = 8 − Pra, using consistent units. Setting the equations equal gives 1 + Pra = 8 − Pra, so Pra = 3.5 mmHg and flow is 4.5 L/min. Now represent improved pumping by CO = 3 + Pra while keeping the venous relation unchanged. The new solution is Pra = 2.5 mmHg and flow = 5.5 L/min. This teaching model illustrates an increase in flow accompanied by a decrease in right atrial pressure rather than an increase.

A different perturbation raises Pmsf from 8 to 10 mmHg while the original cardiac function relation CO = 1 + Pra remains fixed. Now VR = 10 − Pra, and equality gives Pra = 4.5 mmHg with flow 5.5 L/min. The flow rises by the same amount as in the preceding inotropy example, but the atrial pressure changes in the opposite direction. If an experiment reports only flow, the two interventions might appear identical. Recording filling pressure and understanding the assumptions of the curves help differentiate the mechanisms. In intact circulation, other changes could modify these patterns, so the calculation should not be used as an unconditional diagnostic rule.

Equilibrium in a physiological system does not mean that the heart has stopped or that pressure is uniform. In a steady state, blood continues to circulate and energy continues to be supplied by the heart. The term refers to the stability of averaged volumes and flows. An equilibrium of intersecting function curves is therefore different from the no-flow pressure equilibrium used to define filling pressure. Confusing the two leads to a common conceptual mistake: a student may conclude that because Pmsf is defined at zero flow, it cannot appear as a parameter in a model of flowing circulation. A reference state can characterize a system even when the system normally operates away from that state.

16. Oxygen transport measurements: from local balances to whole-animal flow

The Fick principle can be applied to a particular organ as well as to the complete systemic circulation, provided the boundary of the exchange region is defined correctly. For a muscle supplied by a known arterial inflow and drained by a sampled venous outflow, net oxygen uptake equals organ blood flow multiplied by the oxygen content difference across that organ under steady conditions. If multiple veins drain the tissue, a single venous sample may not represent the weighted mixture. The relevant outgoing content is the flow-weighted average of the oxygen contents in all drainage routes.

For example, consider two hypothetical organs supplied by the same arterial blood containing 200 mL O₂/L. Organ A receives 1 L/min and its venous blood contains 150 mL O₂/L. Its uptake is 50 mL O₂/min. Organ B receives 2 L/min and its venous blood contains 100 mL O₂/L. Its uptake is 200 mL O₂/min. The combined flow is 3 L/min and combined uptake is 250 mL O₂/min. The mixed venous oxygen content is (1 × 150 + 2 × 100)/3 = 116.7 mL O₂/L. It is not the arithmetic average of 150 and 100 because the organs contribute different blood volumes. Using the mixed value gives 3 × (200 − 116.7) ≈ 250 mL O₂/min, reproducing the total balance.

This calculation explains the physiological meaning of mixed venous oxygen content. A low value can arise when tissues extract a large amount of oxygen per volume of blood. It does not identify which organ extracted the oxygen, and it is not a pure indicator of cardiac performance. At the same metabolic consumption, lower flow tends to increase the required extraction per unit blood. At the same flow, increased metabolic demand can also increase extraction. A fall in arterial oxygen content further complicates the relationship. The mixed venous measurement must be interpreted together with arterial content, consumption and flow.

The haemoglobin-bound contribution to oxygen content can be approximated by multiplying haemoglobin concentration in g/dL by its effective oxygen-binding capacity in mL O₂/g and the fractional saturation. A commonly used physiological approximation for binding capacity is around 1.34 mL O₂/g haemoglobin; the exact coefficient depends on conventions and functional haemoglobin. A small dissolved contribution can be estimated from the solubility coefficient times oxygen partial pressure. With haemoglobin 15 g/dL, saturation 0.98 and arterial oxygen partial pressure 100 mmHg, the bound contribution is about 19.7 mL O₂/dL and the dissolved contribution is about 0.3 mL O₂/dL using approximately 0.003 mL O₂/dL/mmHg. The resulting content is near 20 mL O₂/dL. The details of haemoglobin saturation curves belong to the respiratory node; here the calculation establishes why content has the units required by Fick's equation.

Suppose a second individual has haemoglobin of 10 g/dL but the same fractional saturation and arterial partial pressure. The bound contribution is approximately 13.1 mL O₂/dL, and with the same dissolved contribution the total is near 13.4 mL O₂/dL. Equal saturation therefore does not imply equal arterial oxygen content. If both individuals have the same cardiac output, the second has lower oxygen delivery. Changes in tissue extraction or flow may compensate to some extent. The example illustrates an interaction between the blood properties studied in 7.A and the pumping function studied here.

The direct oxygen Fick method requires measurement of whole-body oxygen consumption and appropriate arterial and mixed venous oxygen contents. The word direct refers to the actual measurement of oxygen uptake instead of substituting an assumed value based on body size or other predictors. The latter approach is sometimes called indirect or estimated Fick. It uses the same conservation principle but adds uncertainty from the assumed consumption. A difference of 20% in the assumed uptake produces a 20% difference in the inferred cardiac output if the measured content difference remains fixed.

The denominator introduces an additional sensitivity to measurement error. In a hypothetical subject, actual arterial content is 20 mL/dL and mixed venous content is 15 mL/dL. At an oxygen uptake of 250 mL/min, the inferred flow is 5 L/min. If venous content is overestimated as 16 mL/dL, the difference becomes 4 mL/dL, and the calculated flow increases to 6.25 L/min. The 1 mL/dL error changes the flow estimate by 25%. When the arteriovenous content difference is small, a modest absolute error in either content measurement can produce a large relative error in the inferred flow.

Under steady conditions, the pulmonary oxygen uptake measured at the respiratory surface can represent whole-body oxygen consumption. During rapid transitions in exercise or ventilation, however, the amount of oxygen stored in lungs, blood and tissues may change. The instantaneous uptake from the environment may temporarily differ from the rate of cellular consumption. A Fick estimate based on mismatched sampling times can consequently be misleading. Averaging over a sufficiently stable period and pairing measurements appropriately are essential assumptions of the simple equation.

Alternative measurements of flow can provide useful comparisons. Doppler ultrasound estimates blood velocity in an outflow tract; multiplying the velocity integrated over the ejection period by the cross-sectional area gives an estimated stroke volume under suitable assumptions about the velocity profile and measurement location. Repeating or averaging over beats permits calculation of cardiac output. If the outflow diameter is measured inaccurately, the cross-sectional area is affected by the square of the diameter, so the resulting error can be amplified. The method measures a mechanical consequence of ejection rather than an oxygen balance.

Indicator dilution methods use a known amount of an indicator introduced upstream and measure its downstream concentration over time. In the ideal first-pass situation, flow is related to the amount introduced divided by the area under the concentration–time curve. Mixing, loss of indicator, recirculation and the stability of flow affect the estimate. This is the same broad conservation idea as Fick measurement, but the indicator is introduced deliberately rather than using ongoing metabolic oxygen removal. Agreement between different techniques under controlled conditions strengthens confidence; disagreement prompts investigation of the assumptions and measurement errors of each method.

17. Cardiac reserve as an integrated limit rather than a fixed number

The amount of blood pumped at rest is not the maximum amount that the heart can pump. A resting animal maintains adequate circulation while leaving a capacity to respond to increased demand. That reserve is important during locomotion, temperature regulation and other changes in metabolic activity. An estimate of reserve requires both a defined baseline and a defined condition for maximum or peak output. A measured peak during one exercise test is not necessarily an absolute mechanical maximum under every possible physiological condition. The term should therefore be interpreted with attention to how the measurements were obtained.

Cardiac output changes during exercise because the product of heart rate and stroke volume changes. Consider an illustrative subject at rest with a rate of 65 beats/min and SV of 80 mL. Output is 5.2 L/min. During moderate activity, a rate of 110 beats/min and SV of 105 mL produce 11.55 L/min. During intense activity, a rate of 170 beats/min and SV of 110 mL produce 18.7 L/min. The rise from rest to moderate activity involves increases in both variables. From moderate to intense activity, the change in rate contributes more than the comparatively small further rise in SV. These are hypothetical data, not a claim that every person exhibits a plateau at the same exercise intensity.

To quantify the contributions of rate and stroke volume, write the ratio of the two output measurements as CO₂/CO₁ = (HR₂/HR₁)(SV₂/SV₁). In the resting and intense examples, the rate ratio is approximately 2.615 and the stroke volume ratio is 1.375. Their product is approximately 3.596, consistent with 18.7 divided by 5.2. This representation prevents a common arithmetic error: percentage changes in the two factors cannot generally be added to obtain the total percentage change when the changes are large. The product includes their interaction.

The relation between rate and output can be examined by considering what happens when SV changes with HR. If rate increases by a factor of two but SV decreases by half, CO stays constant. If SV falls by more than half, CO declines. In terms of small proportional changes, the proportional change in output is approximately the sum of the proportional changes in rate and stroke volume. This approximation is useful when the changes are small, but the exact product should be used for larger changes. Physiologically, the response depends on whether the ventricle receives and accommodates enough blood during the shorter diastolic interval.

A graph of output against imposed rate in an isolated heart may show a rise followed by a plateau or decline. The position of the plateau depends on the preparation. Increasing available venous inflow, enhancing relaxation or modifying the inotropic state can alter the response. A rate that impairs filling in one preparation may be tolerated in another with different chamber properties. Thus there is no universal heart-rate value above which cardiac output must decrease. In an intact subject, sympathetic activation and altered venous return accompany physiological tachycardia, whereas an experimentally imposed rapid rhythm may occur without those adjustments.

A training-associated increase in cardiac reserve can involve changes in the size and filling characteristics of the heart, the achievable stroke volume and the capacity of muscles to utilize delivered oxygen. In a simplified comparison between two individuals performing the same submaximal task, one may achieve the required flow using a larger SV and a lower HR, while another uses a smaller SV and a higher HR. The heart rate alone cannot establish which individual has a larger maximal output or which has the greater oxygen consumption. The required metabolic work and the distribution and extraction of oxygen must also be considered.

At a very low exercise intensity, an increase in oxygen consumption may be accommodated partly by greater extraction of oxygen from blood, in addition to an increase in flow. At higher metabolic demands, the ability to further increase extraction is limited by the oxygen available in arterial blood and by tissue exchange conditions. The maximum rate of oxygen utilization therefore reflects the combined performance of ventilation, pulmonary exchange, blood oxygen carrying capacity, cardiac output, local perfusion and cellular metabolism. The pump is a central component of this chain but is not the only component.

Cardiac reserve also differs among animal species. A small mammal and a large mammal may have very different absolute stroke volumes and rates, in part because their hearts and metabolic demands differ in scale. A heart with a much larger absolute output does not necessarily supply more blood per unit body mass. When comparing species, define whether the relevant quantity is absolute flow, mass-specific flow or flow relative to the metabolic oxygen demand. Body surface area indexing is commonly used in human physiology, but it should not be applied uncritically to all animals as a universal scaling law.

A change in body temperature can further influence the mechanical and metabolic context of pumping. In an ectothermic vertebrate, cooling may slow pacemaker activity and the kinetics of contraction and relaxation while also reducing metabolic oxygen demand. The measured change in cardiac output cannot be interpreted without considering both the mechanical response and the demand for circulation. In an endotherm, changes in thermal environment may alter the distribution of systemic flow, particularly to skin, and thereby change the vascular conditions against which the heart operates. These examples show why a reserve value measured under one set of environmental conditions need not apply under another.

The mechanical work of exercise cannot be equated directly with the mechanical work of the heart. Skeletal muscles perform locomotor work, whereas the heart sustains the pressure and flow needed for transport. A person producing a certain external cycling power does not require the heart itself to produce that same external power. Much of the metabolic energy used by tissues is converted to heat, and the cardiovascular response reflects oxygen and nutrient transport requirements. A calculation of cardiac mechanical power from a pressure–volume loop therefore describes the pump's work on blood, not total exercise energy expenditure.

18. Integrated perturbations: posture, volume redistribution and ventricular interaction

When a person moves from lying down to upright standing, the gravitational pressure distribution changes. In an ideal static column of fluid, the pressure difference between two levels is proportional to fluid density, gravitational acceleration and vertical separation. The circulation is not a static column, but the hydrostatic relation explains why dependent vascular regions experience a change in pressure when their height relative to the heart changes. The compliant veins in the legs and abdomen can accommodate additional blood as their distending pressure rises. This can reduce the central volume available for ventricular filling.

The early mechanical response to standing can be distinguished from the compensatory response. Before full reflex compensation, reduced central filling tends to reduce EDV and SV. A decrease in arterial pressure may then recruit mechanisms that increase cardiac rate, contractile state and vascular tone. These responses are studied in the next neural regulation chapter. Here the important connection is that a decrease in output does not initially require a primary defect in the heart. A normally contracting myocardium can eject less simply because less blood reaches its chamber during diastole.

An illustrative sequence begins with EDV 125 mL and ESV 50 mL, so SV is 75 mL. If central filling falls and EDV decreases to 100 mL while ESV remains approximately 50 mL in a simplified early comparison, SV is 50 mL. At an unchanged rate of 70 beats/min, output decreases from 5.25 to 3.5 L/min. A later rise in rate to 90 beats/min with an SV of 60 mL gives 5.4 L/min. The later output may be restored even though the SV has not completely returned to baseline. These values are hypothetical and demonstrate the difference between compensating a product and restoring each factor independently.

Rhythmic leg movement changes the mechanical conditions of the venous system. Contraction increases pressure around portions of deep veins and may move blood toward the heart when appropriately oriented valves permit forward flow. During relaxation, the compressed portions can refill from other segments. The net effect over repeated cycles depends on the pressure pattern, venous valves and the ability of arterial inflow to replenish the vascular bed. A sustained contraction may instead obstruct some vessels. The distinction between intermittent compression and persistent compression is important when predicting the direction of a flow change.

Respiratory pressure changes introduce a different kind of perturbation because the external pressure around the heart and intrathoracic veins changes during breathing. If a spontaneously breathing subject inspires and intrathoracic pressure falls, the pressure gradient from extrathoracic veins toward the right atrium may increase. The measured right atrial pressure relative to atmosphere may fall even while the right ventricular filling volume rises. If one assumes that a lower measured pressure always means lower preload, this result appears contradictory. It becomes understandable when external pressure and distending pressure are distinguished.

The left ventricle does not necessarily exhibit the same immediate stroke-volume change as the right ventricle during a respiratory cycle. The pulmonary vascular compartment can temporarily accommodate a different amount of blood, and the septum and pericardium couple the mechanics of the two ventricles. A change in right ventricular filling can modify the geometry and available filling space of the left ventricle. The time required for increased right ventricular output to pass through the pulmonary circulation also matters. This is one reason why instantaneous ventricular outputs can differ without violating conservation of blood volume over an appropriate interval.

An experimentally imposed increase in pulmonary vascular load may initially reduce the amount ejected by the right ventricle. The resulting reduction in onward pulmonary flow can, after an interval, reduce the volume returning to the left atrium. A later decrease in left ventricular SV in this situation is therefore not necessarily evidence of a new primary left ventricular contractility defect. It can be a consequence of the series arrangement of the two pumps. A useful examination technique is to trace the direction of blood flow through the circuit before assigning a change to one particular chamber.

Volume redistribution must also be distinguished from changes in the total amount of circulating blood. A shift of blood from dependent veins toward the thorax may increase central filling without adding fluid to the circulation. Conversely, a reduction in total blood volume can decrease stressed volume and the pressure available to sustain venous return. Venoconstriction can compensate partly by changing the distribution between unstressed and stressed volume. These are distinct mechanisms and need not have equal effects on total peripheral resistance or arterial pressure.

Consider a pair of hypothetical steady states with the same output of 4 L/min but different central pressures. In one state, a healthy heart receives reduced venous filling because circulating blood volume has fallen. In another, the heart has reduced pump function and blood accumulates upstream, resulting in a higher right atrial pressure. The same output does not establish the same mechanism. The conceptual cardiac and venous-return curves can explain how two combinations of pump function and vascular properties yield similar flow at different pressures. Real-world interpretation would require more information than this single comparison.

Mechanical obstruction of ventricular outflow is another instructive example. Even if myocardial activation remains unchanged, an increased obstruction can raise the pressure the ventricle must develop to eject blood. A pressure measured within the ventricle may therefore increase while effective forward flow decreases. The work required to sustain a given flow also changes. The observation that the heart is generating a high pressure cannot be interpreted as proof that circulation is adequate; pressure generation and delivery of volume are different tasks.

The general lesson from posture, respiration, volume changes and outflow perturbations is that the determinants of pumping operate together. A student should first identify the site of the initial intervention, then trace how that intervention changes pressure, volume and flow, and finally consider the secondary adjustments that restore or modify the operating point. This causal order is more reliable than memorizing a fixed direction for every variable without regard to time, mechanical constraints or the particular vascular territory affected.

19. Reading pump data: distinguishing observation, hypothesis and conclusion

A common examination format presents a table of experimental measurements and asks which physiological mechanism is most consistent with the data. The first step is to determine what each measurement directly represents. Heart rate is a frequency; EDV and ESV are chamber volumes; arterial pressure is a mechanical condition of the outflow circuit; venous pressure is a pressure measured at a specified site. Cardiac output and ejection fraction can be calculated from some of these quantities. Neither a direct measurement nor a derived value automatically identifies the underlying molecular cause.

Consider three hypothetical experiments in a paced isolated working heart. In the control condition, EDV is 120 mL, ESV is 50 mL and the rate is 75 beats/min. In intervention A, EDV becomes 145 mL while ESV remains 50 mL and the imposed outflow pressure is unchanged. In intervention B, EDV remains 120 mL while ESV rises to 75 mL and outflow pressure rises. In intervention C, EDV remains 120 mL while ESV decreases to 30 mL at comparable outflow pressure. The calculated stroke volumes are 70, 95, 45 and 90 mL respectively. The corresponding flows are 5.25, 7.125, 3.375 and 6.75 L/min. Because pacing is controlled, the change in flow is explained arithmetically by changes in stroke volume.

Intervention A is consistent with increased filling and a Starling response, although the experiment must verify that inotropy did not independently change. Intervention B is consistent with increased afterload. Intervention C is consistent with enhanced inotropy if the mechanical conditions are adequately matched. The wording "consistent with" is important: the numerical observations do not directly measure molecular calcium signaling or prove that no other variable changed. A follow-up experiment could measure developed tension at a controlled initial length or construct end-systolic pressure–volume relations under different loads to obtain stronger evidence.

An experimental comparison may also reveal a change in output without a change in ejection fraction. Suppose EDV and ESV are 100 and 40 mL in one condition and 150 and 60 mL in another. Both have EF of 60%, but stroke volume increases from 60 to 90 mL. If rate remains unchanged, cardiac output rises by 50%. Conversely, EF may increase even though forward output declines when chamber filling decreases sufficiently. These patterns show why reporting a derived ratio without its constituent measurements can conceal important physiological changes.

A second examination format provides curves rather than tables. The student should read the units, identify whether the axes describe pressure and volume or flow and filling pressure, and locate the relevant points before interpreting a change. On a graph of ventricular pressure versus volume, the rightmost boundary identifies EDV and the leftmost boundary identifies ESV. On a venous-return graph, the horizontal intercept identifies the model's no-flow pressure, not the amount of blood in the ventricle. On a calcium–force graph, the direction of a sensitivity shift depends on whether calcium is displayed as concentration or negative logarithm. Correct axis interpretation is a prerequisite for meaningful reasoning.

A third format tests experimental controls. Suppose an investigator reports that stretching ventricular muscle increased its peak developed force, but the muscle was also stimulated at a higher frequency in the stretched condition. The result cannot isolate the effect of length because rate may affect the calcium available for contraction. Repeating the comparison at the same stimulation frequency and solution conditions would help establish the length dependence. If the question is specifically about myofilament calcium sensitivity, a permeabilised preparation at controlled calcium activity is more informative than peak force in an intact electrically stimulated muscle alone.

In a flow measurement problem, the first question is whether the values form a valid mass balance. An arterial oxygen content expressed in mL/dL and a venous oxygen content expressed in mL/L cannot be subtracted without conversion. If the denominator is an arterial–venous saturation difference, the problem lacks oxygen content unless additional information permits conversion. If the oxygen consumption is supplied in mL/min and the content difference in mL/L, division directly produces L/min. If it is supplied in mL/dL, the quotient is dL/min. Writing the units at each step is a simple way of detecting errors that might otherwise look plausible.

Measurement uncertainty can also be assessed by comparing independent estimates of output. Imagine a Doppler estimate of 5.4 L/min and a direct oxygen Fick estimate of 5.0 L/min obtained under comparable stable conditions. The difference need not imply that either physiological principle is wrong. Doppler estimates depend on outflow area and velocity measurements, whereas Fick estimates depend on oxygen consumption and blood content measurements. A repeat comparison under carefully matched conditions can help distinguish systematic bias from normal variability. Without knowing the uncertainty of the measurements, one cannot confidently conclude that the physiological output changed.

A final form of reasoning asks what would happen if a single variable changed while all others remained fixed. Such questions describe controlled counterfactual conditions, not complete accounts of intact physiology. An isolated increase in afterload at fixed EDV and inotropy tends to increase ESV. An isolated increase in preload at fixed load and inotropy tends to increase SV within the useful range. A positive inotropic change at fixed EDV and load tends to reduce ESV. In the intact animal, these responses can be followed by changes in other variables. The most accurate answer states the initial prediction and recognizes the assumptions under which it applies.

The conceptual structure of this chapter can now be condensed into a sequence of relationships. Electrical and calcium activation make active force generation possible. Initial muscle length and contractile state influence the force available to eject blood. Opposing load and the timing of ejection determine how much volume leaves the chamber. The heart rate determines how often that volume is delivered. The returning blood and the pump together establish the average flow through the closed circuit, and oxygen transport measurements provide an independent way of estimating that flow. The subsequent chapter will examine the properties of the vessels that translate pressure differences into the distribution of flow among tissues.
