1. Temperature as a biological constraint

A plant rooted in a field cannot move its developing leaves into shade or carry its roots into a warmer chamber when the weather changes. Yet the reactions that supply its ATP, build cell walls, copy DNA and make seeds depend on temperature. This creates a conflict between an environment that varies over hours and seasons and a collection of cellular processes that operate efficiently over limited ranges. The first task of thermal stress physiology is therefore to explain how a cell remains organized when the physical conditions supporting that organization change. A second task is to explain why a plant that survives a particular temperature on one day can be killed by the same temperature on another day.

Temperature is a measure related to the average kinetic energy of particles. Raising temperature increases molecular motion and alters the rates at which molecules collide and rearrange. Cooling has the opposite immediate effect on motion. Neither change is automatically injurious. An enzyme catalyzing a reaction in a leaf that has cooled slightly may simply carry out fewer reactions per second. If the leaf is rewarmed and function returns, the reduced rate was reversible. Injury is a different phenomenon: it indicates damage to structures or loss of functions that cannot immediately resume when the external environment is restored. A plant may have a reversible depression of photosynthesis long before appreciable tissue death appears.

The distinction can be illustrated by two experiments. In the first, a leaf initially at 25 °C is briefly transferred to 15 °C, and carbon dioxide uptake decreases. When the leaf returns to 25 °C, uptake resumes close to the original value. In the second experiment, a sensitive leaf experiences prolonged exposure to a much lower nonfreezing temperature, and assimilation remains impaired for several days after rewarming. The first result does not establish structural injury; the second raises a question about damage to membranes, chloroplasts, enzymes, transport processes or combinations of these targets. Measurements of function during exposure and during recovery are both essential.

Optima, thresholds and exposure history

An optimum is a temperature at which a specified biological process performs best under specified conditions. It is not an intrinsic temperature for the entire plant. The temperature maximizing net carbon assimilation can differ from that maximizing root elongation, pollen germination or seed production. The optimum can shift when light, atmospheric carbon dioxide, water availability or the developmental stage changes. A thermal response curve obtained for a mature, watered leaf cannot automatically be applied to a flower in dry air. This is especially important when examination questions compare growth, photosynthesis and survival; each endpoint integrates a different set of processes.

Thermal limits are equally conditional. An exposure that a young seedling survives for five minutes may be lethal when maintained for several hours. A hydrated bud and a dry seed can have very different susceptibility even on the same plant. Temperate perennial species may tolerate cold during winter after gradual acclimation, while actively growing spring tissues are vulnerable after the loss of that protection. Hence a statement such as a plant survives a temperature of minus ten degrees is incomplete unless the tissue, duration, thermal history and method of measuring survival are specified.

Three categories organize the mechanisms in this chapter. Heat stress results when elevated temperature threatens normal physiological functions. Chilling stress refers to harmful low-temperature exposure without tissue freezing; sensitive tropical and subtropical plants can be injured at temperatures above zero degrees Celsius. Freezing stress involves the formation of ice or the consequences of being exposed to subzero conditions under which ice can form. The terms distinguish physical events, not three universal numerical intervals applicable to every species. Supercooled liquid water may remain unfrozen below its equilibrium freezing point. Conversely, frost on the exterior of a leaf does not establish that ice has entered the living cells.

Avoidance, tolerance and recovery

A helpful approach is to classify a thermal response as avoidance, tolerance or recovery. Avoidance changes the exposure of sensitive tissues to a damaging condition. For example, evaporative cooling can keep leaves below ambient air temperature when water is available; deep supercooling can delay ice nucleation in certain tissues. Tolerance enables cells to withstand a stress that they actually experience, for example surviving dehydration caused by extracellular ice. Recovery restores function after the stress is removed through the repair and replacement of damaged molecules. The categories can coexist. A plant can avoid some thermal exposure, tolerate the remainder and repair residual damage afterward.

The temperature of the plant itself is often more relevant than that reported by a weather station. Leaves absorb radiation, lose heat by convection and emit thermal radiation. Water evaporation removes energy from the leaf surface. Under strong sunlight, a leaf may become warmer than the air if its cooling capacity is limited. During a clear night, radiative energy loss can cause plant surfaces to become colder than surrounding air. Soil temperature varies more slowly with depth than air temperature, so roots and shoots need not experience the same thermal history. An experiment that records only ambient air temperature may therefore mischaracterize the stress delivered to its biological subject.

Even when all tissues reach the same temperature, the consequences need not be identical. A leaf contains specialized photosynthetic membranes, abundant chlorophyll and an electron transport chain driven by light. A root depends on a different pattern of respiration and ion uptake. A pollen grain has a small, developmentally specialized set of physiological resources. Changes to enzyme activity, membrane properties and water distribution occur in all these organs, but the failure of different critical processes determines their sensitivity. For this reason a species that retains green leaves after a heat wave may nevertheless have suffered a substantial reproductive loss.

Thermal stress also changes the balance between processes. Suppose cooling reduces an enzyme's catalytic rate twofold while membrane transport declines fivefold. The product of one pathway may then accumulate because its use or export is impaired more than its formation. Similarly, a heat-stressed chloroplast can absorb light even when carbon fixation and repair of photosynthetic proteins cannot use or manage the supplied excitation effectively. It is not sufficient to say that heat accelerates metabolism and cold slows it. Cellular survival depends on whether the constituent reactions, transport systems and protective mechanisms continue to operate together at compatible rates.

Acclimation and adaptation

Acclimation is a change of physiological state produced by environmental experience within an individual's lifetime. A mild heat exposure may improve tolerance of a later severe heat exposure. A period of nonfreezing cold may improve the capacity of a temperate plant to survive extracellular ice. Such changes usually involve altered gene expression, protein abundance, metabolite composition or membrane organization, and are often reversible. Adaptation in its evolutionary sense refers to heritable changes in populations over generations. A plant that increases freezing tolerance over several days has acclimated; the inherited capacity to perform that acclimation may itself be an evolutionary adaptation.

The distinction between baseline capacity and induced capacity gives a coherent structure to thermal experiments. Genetically different plants may differ before exposure, after exposure, or both. A comparison made only after pretreatment conflates these possibilities. A comparison made only without pretreatment overlooks inducible defenses. The strongest simple design measures each genotype both with and without conditioning, and measures recovery under matched conditions. Later sections apply this principle to heat shock proteins, cold-responsive transcription factors and membrane injury assays.

A final distinction concerns thermal signals and thermal lesions. A temperature-induced change in a membrane or in intracellular calcium concentration may help initiate a protective response at a time when the cell is still viable. The same category of disturbance, if sufficiently large or prolonged, may cause ion imbalance and contribute to injury. A molecule such as hydrogen peroxide can participate in a signaling response when its concentration, localization and duration are controlled, yet damage proteins and membrane lipids when its production overwhelms scavenging. This dual nature of stress-related changes explains why complete suppression of all stress signals is not necessarily a beneficial strategy.

2. How elevated temperature injures proteins and membranes

Proteins are central targets of heat stress because their function depends on the three-dimensional arrangement of amino acid chains. The peptide bonds give a protein its covalent backbone, but the arrangement of that backbone into a functional structure depends on many noncovalent interactions. Hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic attractions and van der Waals contacts help stabilize the folded conformation. Heating changes the balance of these interactions and increases the motion of the molecule. At sufficiently high temperature, a protein may populate partially unfolded states more frequently. Most physiological heat injury does not require hydrolysis of the protein's peptide bonds.

Protein denaturation is loss of the native structure and function without necessarily destroying the primary sequence. It may be reversible if the amino acid chain remains available to fold again and conditions become favorable. However, partial unfolding can expose nonpolar surfaces that are normally buried inside globular proteins. Such surfaces can associate with similar exposed surfaces on other molecules, producing aggregates. Once molecules become trapped in aggregates, spontaneous refolding may be inefficient even after temperature falls. Protein aggregation is thus a mechanism by which a temporary physical disturbance becomes a persistent biological defect.

A functional enzyme can be lost without a measurable reduction in the total amount of its polypeptide. Imagine measuring protein concentration with an antibody that recognizes an accessible part of the chain. A heated extract and an unheated extract might yield the same antibody signal, yet the heated extract may have much lower catalytic activity because many enzyme molecules have lost their proper conformation. To determine whether heat lowers expression, promotes degradation or simply inactivates existing enzymes, abundance and activity must be measured independently. Soluble and insoluble protein fractions can provide further evidence of aggregation.

In the simplest model, a protein exists in folded and unfolded states, F and U. Their equilibrium ratio depends on the free energy difference between these states. A folded state can be favored at one temperature and less favored at another. Living cells are not closed equilibrium systems, however. ATP-dependent chaperones repair damaged molecules, new proteins are synthesized, and irreparable molecules are removed. Consequently the fraction of active enzyme in a stressed cell depends on the rates of injury and repair as well as on equilibrium folding stability. The properties of purified proteins are valuable but do not fully determine cellular heat tolerance.

Membrane integrity and ion gradients

The membrane is another target. A biological membrane consists of a lipid bilayer and associated proteins. Its hydrophobic interior separates aqueous compartments, allowing cells to maintain unequal concentrations of ions and metabolites. Lipid molecules move within the membrane, and their acyl chains exhibit thermal motion. Warming generally increases bilayer fluidity; excessive warming can disrupt packing and increase permeability. Membrane-associated proteins may also lose activity or alter conformation, so a loss of selective transport can occur even before a membrane becomes grossly disrupted.

Consider the plasma membrane proton pump, which uses ATP to export protons and helps establish the electrochemical gradients that support plant transport. If heat reduces ATP production, destabilizes the pump or increases the passive return of ions across the membrane, maintenance of these gradients becomes more costly. Several transport processes that rely on the proton motive force can then become impaired simultaneously. The direct target of heat may be a membrane protein or a lipid structure; the downstream effects extend to nutrient acquisition, cytoplasmic pH, electrical potential and water balance.

A vacuole stores a substantial fraction of the solutes and water of a mature plant cell. Its membrane, the tonoplast, is therefore as relevant to physiological stability as the plasma membrane. If tonoplast selectivity deteriorates, compartmentalized solutes may redistribute. Such redistribution changes the concentrations of ions and metabolites in the cytoplasm and may disrupt enzyme function. Membrane injury also affects the ability of chloroplasts and mitochondria to sustain proton gradients. Energy production and membrane integrity therefore depend on each other, making severe thermal damage capable of generating a self-amplifying decline.

A modest increase in membrane fluidity can be a useful signal rather than a lesion. Altered bilayer properties can affect the activity of membrane-associated proteins and ion channels, including processes that change the cytosolic calcium concentration. Calcium is normally maintained at a much lower free concentration in the cytosol than in several other cellular compartments or extracellular spaces. A transient increase can activate calcium-sensitive proteins and downstream signaling events. The crucial issue is the magnitude, timing and location of the change. Persistent uncontrolled calcium entry following extensive membrane failure is not equivalent to a regulated transient.

Redox consequences and secondary injury

Heat can also increase oxidative stress. Electron transfer pathways contain reduced intermediates that can react with oxygen under unfavorable circumstances. If utilization of absorbed excitation or respiratory reducing equivalents becomes restricted, some electrons may be diverted into reactions that generate reactive oxygen species. These species have different chemical properties: superoxide is a radical anion, hydrogen peroxide is relatively more stable and can function in signaling, while hydroxyl radicals react rapidly near their site of generation. Excess reactive oxygen species can oxidize proteins, nucleic acids and membrane lipids, aggravating the original thermal disturbance.

Lipid peroxidation illustrates the connection between heat, oxidative stress and membrane injury. Unsaturated acyl chains contain positions susceptible to radical attack. Oxidative reactions can generate lipid hydroperoxides and further breakdown products, changing lipid packing and membrane properties. A measured increase in lipid oxidation is evidence that an oxidative process has occurred, but it does not prove that oxidation initiated all observed injury. Protein unfolding, direct changes in membrane fluidity and metabolic imbalance may precede or accompany lipid oxidation. Experiments intended to establish causal order need time-resolved measurements and perturbations rather than a single endpoint.

Water relations and proteostasis

At the level of a whole plant, heat can also be an indirect water stress. At a higher air temperature, saturation vapor pressure is higher. If relative humidity remains comparable, the vapor pressure difference between the leaf interior and the atmosphere tends to increase. This can raise the potential rate of transpiration, provided stomata and hydraulic supply allow it. Increased evaporation can cool the leaf, but it can also lower plant water potential if uptake cannot meet water loss. Stomatal closure may conserve water while reducing evaporative cooling and carbon dioxide supply. The same protective response against dehydration can therefore increase the thermal burden on a sunlit leaf.

The consequences of heat must be interpreted with these interacting mechanisms in mind. A decline in growth could reflect loss of enzyme function, insufficient carbon assimilation, a disrupted membrane gradient, impaired water uptake, altered developmental signaling or a combination of these factors. An experiment that keeps soil moist and humidity controlled helps isolate direct temperature effects from drought-related secondary effects. Nevertheless, direct and indirect stresses are not biologically independent in field conditions, and a useful interpretation must recognize that protection of one process can change exposure of another.

Cells maintain proteostasis, the balance of protein synthesis, folding, function and removal. Under normal conditions new proteins are assisted by molecular chaperones, while damaged proteins are cleared. Heat raises the demand on this system. A chaperone that normally assists folding of newly synthesized polypeptides may be recruited to an unfolded pre-existing protein. Some translation is curtailed as resources are redistributed, and specific stress-related proteins continue to be produced. If the rate of damage exceeds folding and degradation capacity, aggregates accumulate and essential enzymes cease to function. The heat-shock response, considered next, provides an inducible way to expand protective capacity rather than simply allowing every thermally sensitive process to fail.

3. Photosynthesis, water relations and reproductive heat injury

A green leaf has two different tasks in photosynthesis. Its light reactions capture excitation energy and drive electron transfer and the formation of ATP and reducing equivalents. Its carbon reactions use energy and reducing power to assimilate carbon dioxide into organic compounds. Net carbon exchange reflects the combined operation of these reactions, carbon dioxide movement through the leaf and carbon loss through respiratory processes. Heating can influence each component, so a decrease in net carbon assimilation is not in itself evidence that the photosystem reaction centers have been destroyed.

The thylakoid membrane is particularly important because it houses the photosynthetic electron transport chain. Its architecture positions pigment-protein complexes, electron carriers and ATP synthase in an organized bilayer. Heat can alter the structural organization of this system and interfere with electron transfer. Photosystem II is often an important heat-sensitive target, including its water-oxidizing apparatus and the proteins associated with its reaction center. The exact sequence of injury depends on the stress conditions. A short moderate heat treatment may depress carbon metabolism without severe persistent damage to photosystem II, whereas more severe heating may damage photosynthetic membranes and reaction center function.

Carbon fixation and Rubisco activation

The Calvin–Benson cycle can be limited by the regulation of Rubisco as well as by the availability of ATP, NADPH and carbon dioxide. Rubisco is the enzyme that catalyzes the addition of carbon dioxide to ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. Its activity in vivo depends on the chemical state of its active sites and on removal of certain inhibitory sugar phosphates. Rubisco activase, an ATP-dependent protein that facilitates removal of inhibitory molecules from Rubisco active sites, is heat-sensitive in many plants. Therefore a decline in Rubisco activation can be important at moderately elevated temperatures even while the amount of Rubisco protein itself remains comparatively stable.

The distinction between the amount of Rubisco and its activation state is useful experimentally. An investigator can assay initial activity under conditions that preserve the enzyme's state at extraction and compare it with activity after appropriate activation in vitro. A decrease in initial activity relative to fully activated activity suggests a reduced activation state. It does not necessarily mean that every Rubisco molecule is permanently damaged. A change in activase function is one possible explanation, which can be evaluated with independent measurements of activase abundance and activity or with genetically distinct activase variants.

In C3 plants, photorespiration also contributes to the temperature response of net assimilation. Rubisco catalyzes both carboxylation and oxygenation of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate. With warming, the relative conditions favoring oxygenation can increase, including changes in Rubisco specificity and the relative solubility of carbon dioxide and oxygen in water. Photorespiratory recycling consumes energy and releases some previously fixed carbon dioxide. Thus net assimilation may decline even if certain catalytic rates have increased. C4 carbon concentration can reduce the oxygenation burden, but C4 plants are not universally protected from high temperature; their membranes, enzymes, reproduction and water relations remain vulnerable.

Separating stomatal, biochemical and photochemical limitations

A simple gas exchange experiment distinguishes several hypotheses. Measure carbon assimilation rate, stomatal conductance and estimated intercellular carbon dioxide concentration before and during moderate warming. If stomatal conductance falls and the intercellular carbon dioxide concentration also falls, limited carbon dioxide supply is plausible. If assimilation falls while intercellular carbon dioxide remains high or rises, biochemical limitations become more plausible because the leaf is not consuming the available carbon dioxide as effectively. These patterns are not absolute diagnoses: mesophyll conductance, respiration and the assumptions of gas exchange calculations can alter the relationship. The interpretation is strongest when gas exchange is combined with chlorophyll fluorescence or independent biochemical measurements.

Chlorophyll fluorescence is emitted when excited chlorophyll loses excitation energy radiatively. The fraction of absorbed excitation used in photochemistry, released as heat and emitted as fluorescence depends on the state of the photosynthetic apparatus. Dark-adapted measurements of minimal fluorescence, F₀, and maximal fluorescence, Fₘ, allow calculation of Fᵥ/Fₘ, where Fᵥ equals Fₘ minus F₀. The ratio estimates the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II photochemistry under the measurement conditions. A substantial persistent decline after stress can indicate impaired photosystem II performance. It does not directly measure the carbon assimilation rate, oxygen evolution rate of an intact plant or the viability of all cells.

A leaf with normal Fᵥ/Fₘ but impaired net assimilation may have a limitation downstream of photosystem II or restricted carbon dioxide entry. A leaf with depressed Fᵥ/Fₘ can have an additional photochemical constraint. Repeated dark-adapted measurements during recovery help distinguish a temporary response from persistent damage, although fluorescence alone cannot identify the affected protein. Light-adapted measures such as effective photosystem II quantum yield answer related but different questions. The ability to choose an appropriate fluorescence measurement is more valuable than memorizing a single normal value for every plant species.

Respiration introduces a second carbon cost. The immediate catalytic rates of many respiratory processes increase with moderate warming if substrate and oxygen are sufficient. As heat becomes severe, respiratory enzymes, membranes and coupling mechanisms can lose function. Over longer periods plants may acclimate their respiratory capacity. A leaf's net carbon gain during a warm day therefore reflects daytime carbon fixation minus associated losses and respiratory costs during subsequent periods. It cannot be predicted from a single temperature coefficient measured at a lower, noninjurious temperature.

The water relations of a heated plant further complicate interpretation. Leaves experiencing high vapor pressure deficit may lose water quickly while evaporative cooling lowers leaf temperature. If root water supply is insufficient, leaf water potential decreases, ABA-dependent stomatal responses can conserve water, and carbon fixation may fall. A well-watered plant under the same air temperature may maintain lower leaf temperature through sustained transpiration. Comparing these individuals as though the actual heat exposures were identical can confuse water availability with intrinsic cellular heat tolerance. Measuring leaf temperature, soil water status and gas exchange together clarifies the difference.

Reproductive sensitivity and reciprocal pollination

Reproduction is often a particularly sensitive phase. Flower formation, meiosis, pollen development, pollen release, pollen germination, pollen tube growth, fertilization and early embryo development involve distinct processes that must be synchronized. In many crop plants, high temperature during a short reproductive window can reduce seed set even if vegetative tissues survive and resume growth. Developing male reproductive tissues are frequently sensitive, but female tissues and postfertilization development can also be affected. It is therefore incorrect to treat viable leaves or green stems as direct evidence that reproductive performance was protected.

The anther provides a useful example of developmental dependence. Developing microspores receive support from surrounding sporophytic tissues, including the tapetum, which contributes nutrients and materials needed for pollen maturation. Heat can disturb the timing of tapetal development and the supply of carbohydrates or other resources to the developing pollen. Mature pollen grains may then have reduced viability or impaired capacity to germinate. Other lesions can arise during pollen tube growth, a highly polarized process requiring controlled calcium gradients, secretion and cytoskeletal organization. The failure of any necessary step can reduce fertilization.

A reciprocal pollination design separates some male and female contributions. Subject one group of plants to heat during flower development and maintain a control group at a permissive temperature. Transfer pollen from heated plants to unheated receptive flowers, and pollen from unheated plants to heated receptive flowers; include control-by-control and heat-by-heat pollinations. If seed set is depressed primarily when heated pollen is used, male function is implicated under those conditions. If seed set is depressed when heated recipients receive healthy pollen, female or maternal functions are implicated. If both crosses are affected, both components may contribute. Controls for pollination timing, stigma receptivity and later fruit development are necessary to avoid overinterpretation.

Agricultural thermal tolerance should consequently be specified in terms of the process one hopes to protect. A genotype that survives a short seedling heat shock may be useful for studying cellular protein repair, but its value for maintaining grain production during warm nights cannot be established from seedling survival alone. Selection for reproductive heat tolerance requires measurements at relevant developmental stages. Studies of photosynthesis, leaf cooling and molecular protection remain important because they explain how stress is experienced, but yield is an integrated outcome rather than an interchangeable proxy for any one of these physiological measurements.

4. Heat perception, HSFs, chaperones and thermotolerance

The existence of a heat-shock response becomes understandable once the problem of protein unfolding is recognized. A cell can survive moderate protein damage if it temporarily increases its capacity to maintain or restore protein function. Heat-shock proteins, abbreviated HSPs, are named for their characteristic induction by elevated temperature, although several members of these protein families also have constitutive functions or respond to other stresses. Heat-shock factors, abbreviated HSFs, are transcriptional regulators that control the expression of many heat-responsive genes. The two abbreviations refer to different molecular roles and should never be used interchangeably.

An HSF is a protein capable of binding appropriate regulatory DNA sequences and altering transcription. A heat-shock element, HSE, is a cis-regulatory DNA sequence typically built from appropriately arranged nGAAn-related motifs. Many heat-inducible promoters contain HSEs recognized by HSFs. Heat-dependent activation of HSFs helps increase transcription of genes encoding molecular chaperones and other protective proteins. HSF expression, activity, protein interactions, subcellular localization and promoter accessibility contribute to regulation; the exact control varies among family members and plant species.

In Arabidopsis, members of the HSFA1 group act as important early regulators of heat responses. HSFA2 is an inducible factor that contributes to sustained expression of some heat-responsive genes and prolonged protection following conditioning. These assignments are useful examples, not universal functions of every HSF. Plant HSFs form diverse families with regulatory interactions. Describing the entire network as a single protein moving from the cytoplasm into the nucleus would conceal the contributions of multiple transcription factors, chaperones and promoter contexts.

One useful conceptual model begins with the stress-induced accumulation of abnormal proteins. Molecular chaperones that interact with regulatory proteins in unstressed cells may be recruited toward unfolded substrates as protein-folding demand increases. This can contribute to altered activity of heat-responsive regulators, including HSFs. Heat-dependent signals from membranes, calcium and redox changes can also feed into heat-responsive transcription. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and need not operate identically in all tissues. Heat perception is a distributed problem involving changes in several cellular structures rather than a single universal thermometer molecule.

Timing of heat-induced gene expression

The transcriptional sequence has consequences for experimental timing. A cytosolic calcium transient can occur before a measurable increase in newly synthesized HSP protein. HSF binding or activation may precede accumulation of HSP mRNA, and translation of that mRNA and accumulation of the protein occur later. A sample taken immediately after warming is therefore suitable for investigating early signaling but may not detect an induced chaperone pool. A sample taken several hours later may contain abundant HSPs while the initiating calcium signal has already disappeared. A single measurement made at one time cannot distinguish the absence of a response from the choice of an inappropriate sampling time.

Chaperone families and energy requirements

Chaperones assist protein folding without becoming permanent components of their substrates. Different chaperone families solve distinct aspects of protein stress. Small HSPs often form oligomeric complexes and act as ATP-independent holdases, binding exposed surfaces of damaged proteins and reducing uncontrolled aggregation. They do not generally perform the complete ATP-driven refolding cycle alone. HSP70 family members use ATP-regulated substrate binding and release, with help from cochaperones such as J-domain proteins and nucleotide exchange factors, to support folding or refolding. HSP90 participates in the maturation and regulation of numerous client proteins and can influence signaling networks.

HSP60-family chaperonins provide another solution: a protected environment in which suitable substrate proteins can fold. In plants, related organellar chaperonins are important in chloroplasts and mitochondria. HSP101 is a member of the HSP100/ClpB-type ATP-dependent disaggregase family. In cooperation with other chaperones, it helps recover proteins trapped in aggregates. The division of labor matters because preventing aggregation and reversing established aggregation are not equivalent tasks. Small HSP abundance may decrease aggregation, but if aggregates form nevertheless, disaggregases and downstream folding machinery can determine the extent of recovery.

ATP is a central constraint in several of these processes. A cell severely damaged by heat may lack the ATP necessary to operate chaperone cycles, protein turnover and restoration of ion gradients. Increasing chaperone abundance does not eliminate this dependence on metabolism. Thus a plant with adequate chaperone capacity can still fail when mitochondrial function and photosynthetic energy supply are compromised. The survival of a mildly damaged cell depends on coordinated repair of its energy-producing, transport and protein-folding systems.

Damaged molecules that cannot be restored need to be removed. Cellular protein quality control can target abnormal proteins for degradation, including through ubiquitin-dependent proteasomal pathways in appropriate compartments and through other proteases or autophagy-associated processes. Removal can be protective because persistent abnormal proteins may interfere with other cellular processes. It also creates a requirement for new protein synthesis to replace lost components. Protein homeostasis during recovery is a balance between preservation, repair, removal and replacement, not a single reaction carried out by one chaperone.

Basal versus acquired thermotolerance

Acquired thermotolerance is the increase in the capacity to withstand a later otherwise damaging heat treatment as a result of a prior nonlethal conditioning exposure. Basal thermotolerance is the capacity to withstand a heat treatment without that specific conditioning. A mild conditioning temperature must be sufficient to initiate protection but not so severe that it imposes overwhelming damage. The interval between conditioning and challenge permits protective changes to develop. The duration of the acquired state is limited by the persistence of proteins, transcripts and other cellular changes and by the rate at which they are reversed.

A standard conceptual design includes four groups: an untreated control, a conditioning-only group, a severe-challenge-only group and a conditioned-plus-challenged group. If the conditioning-only group remains healthy, and the conditioned-plus-challenged group survives better than the challenge-only group, the result supports acquired thermotolerance. The conditioning-only group is necessary because a damaging pretreatment can distort the apparent benefit. The untreated control establishes baseline development and viability. All groups should be assessed at comparable recovery times because delayed damage or differences in growth rate can otherwise alter the outcome.

In a landmark Arabidopsis study published in 2000, Queitsch and colleagues investigated HSP101 using plants with altered levels of the protein. Suppressing its accumulation compromised the capacity to acquire thermotolerance, whereas increasing its abundance under conditions where it would otherwise be low improved baseline heat tolerance. This manipulation of a proposed protective factor is stronger evidence of a causal role than the observation that HSP101 accumulates during heat alone. It also demonstrates the importance of recovery: a protein can be essential for restoring cellular function after a severe stress without necessarily preventing the initial physical disturbance.

The inference has boundaries. HSP101 is not the only determinant of heat survival, and the Arabidopsis result does not establish the same numerical effect in every species, organ or temperature regime. A loss-of-function or reduced-expression phenotype should be interpreted alongside normal-temperature development and conditioning-only controls. A restoration of protection upon reintroducing a functional gene would further strengthen a gene-specific conclusion. Measurements of aggregate burden and protein activity during recovery can connect the survival phenotype to protein quality control. These distinctions are central to interpreting genetic data in Part C questions.

Protection is not free. Maintaining high levels of protective proteins, changing metabolism and allocating resources to repair can impose costs, particularly when no stressful temperature is present. Inducible responses enable a plant to deploy protective capacity when the environmental cue makes it advantageous. Heat acclimation also includes physiological changes outside the HSF–HSP system, such as redox adjustment and membrane remodeling. It is therefore important to distinguish a prominent protective molecular mechanism from the entire acclimation phenotype. The next step is to compare the very different physical constraints imposed by low temperature.

5. Chilling injury without ice formation

Low temperature presents a problem that differs fundamentally from the immediate acceleration of molecular motion caused by warming. Cooling slows thermal movement, changes the organization of membranes and affects the rates of enzymatic reactions. When these changes are moderate and reversible, they need not be pathological. A plant growing in a cool environment may simply have a lower growth rate than a similar plant at its optimum temperature. Chilling injury occurs when exposure to a low but nonfreezing temperature causes loss of function or damage beyond this reversible rate reduction. The temperatures that produce such injury differ considerably among species and developmental stages.

Many plants native to warm climates have comparatively limited capacity to tolerate prolonged chilling. Some fruits, for instance, develop surface pitting, discoloration or abnormal ripening when stored at temperatures that do not freeze the tissue. Symptoms may appear during storage or become conspicuous only after transfer to a warmer environment. A delayed symptom is consistent with damage accumulated during cooling and expressed when metabolism resumes. It does not establish the precise molecular cause, because injury to membranes, oxidative imbalance, disturbed metabolism and subsequent repair failures can all contribute. Chilling sensitivity should therefore be diagnosed under specified temperature and storage-duration conditions, rather than from the visual symptom alone.

Lipid phase behavior and unsaturation

A major physical effect of cooling is increased order in membrane lipids. Acyl chains can pack more closely as thermal motion decreases. In a simplified bilayer containing a single phospholipid species, cooling through a characteristic transition temperature can convert the bilayer from a relatively fluid liquid-crystalline state to a more ordered gel state. Biological membranes contain many lipid classes, sterols and proteins, so their transitions tend to be more complex than those of a pure lipid preparation. Nevertheless, the simplified model explains why the composition of a membrane affects its behavior at low temperature.

A cis double bond introduces a bend into an unsaturated fatty acyl chain and generally makes regular packing less favorable than for a comparable saturated chain. Increasing the proportion of suitable unsaturated lipids can lower the temperature range over which strong ordering occurs and help preserve membrane dynamics during cooling. Chain length and headgroup chemistry also influence membrane organization. Sterols can buffer changes in membrane order in context-dependent ways; a simplistic rule that they always increase or always decrease fluidity is not adequate. A plant can alter several aspects of lipid composition during acclimation, and the resulting change must be evaluated in the particular membrane involved.

The importance of fluidity is not merely that lipids must remain mobile. Many transporters and enzymes embedded in membranes require a suitable local lipid environment to change conformation and interact with other components. When a membrane becomes excessively ordered, the movement of embedded molecules and the conformational transitions associated with transport may become impaired. The relationship is not necessarily linear. In some systems, coexistence of domains with different physical states can alter permeability or protein distribution. A chilling-sensitive membrane can therefore lose normal selectivity even if the low temperature initially decreases thermal motion.

This reasoning helps distinguish the superficially contradictory observations that both excessive warming and cooling can increase electrolyte leakage. Heating can disrupt a membrane by excessive disorder and protein injury. Cooling can produce abnormal domain organization, permeability changes and secondary oxidative damage, and freezing can impose mechanical and dehydration stresses. All three can compromise the same endpoint, the membrane's ability to retain solutes, through different initial mechanisms. The common endpoint does not mean that the primary physical changes were identical.

Plants can modify fatty acid unsaturation through desaturase enzymes. Such enzymes introduce double bonds into suitable fatty acid substrates in specific cellular locations and biochemical contexts. In photosynthetic tissues, plastid lipid composition is especially relevant to the thylakoid system. Changes in desaturase activity or expression can influence the properties of chloroplast and other cellular membranes. It would be incorrect, however, to assume that one desaturase changes every membrane equally, because lipid synthesis, modification and trafficking are compartmentalized. A mutation affecting one lipid pathway may therefore produce an organelle-specific defect.

A useful experimental approach compares plants with different levels of a lipid desaturase under warm and chilling conditions. If a mutant grows normally in warmth but shows stronger inhibition of photosynthesis or greater injury in cold, a role for the affected lipid pathway in chilling tolerance is plausible. Lipid composition measurements establish whether the expected biochemical difference is present. Restoration of the phenotype by genetic complementation provides additional evidence. Comparing leaf temperature, age and light exposure controls for alternative explanations. The result is much stronger if both the physical membrane phenotype and the physiological outcome are measured.

Metabolic and hydraulic imbalance

At a nonfreezing low temperature, different cellular reactions slow by different amounts. Enzymatic rates often decline as temperature decreases, but changes in enzyme conformation, regulation, substrate concentration and membrane transport can produce departures from a simple temperature-rate relationship. Suppose nitrate uptake declines sharply while a pre-existing amino acid pool is still available. Growth may temporarily continue despite reduced nitrogen uptake. If the exposure persists, the pool becomes depleted and nitrogen-dependent metabolism becomes constrained. The outcome depends on the duration of the imbalance as much as on the immediate rate of each process.

Root chilling illustrates this principle particularly clearly. The roots may experience a different temperature from the aerial tissues. Lower root temperature can restrict hydraulic conductivity, membrane transport and metabolic energy supply. A shoot receiving bright sunlight may continue to lose water while the root system supplies water less rapidly. The shoot can therefore experience dehydration-like symptoms even when the soil is moist. This is a hydraulic limitation, not evidence that soil water is necessarily unavailable. Measurements of root-zone temperature, water uptake and leaf water status are required to separate chilling-induced transport constraints from actual drought.

Photoinhibition and recovery

Photosynthesis at low temperature is vulnerable because its component processes have unequal temperature sensitivities. A cold leaf in bright sunlight can continue to absorb excitation energy while the use of ATP and reducing equivalents by carbon metabolism is restricted. This produces an imbalance between excitation input and productive electron use. Photoprotective processes, including regulated dissipation of excitation energy, can limit the damage. If excitation pressure remains too high, reactive oxygen species may accumulate and photosystem II function may become impaired. The stress therefore depends on the combination of light and temperature rather than on either variable alone.

Photosystem II damage must also be considered alongside repair. Reaction center proteins, including D1, undergo turnover in illuminated leaves even under nonstressful conditions. If a low temperature slows the synthesis and replacement of damaged components more than it slows the processes causing damage, the amount of functional photosystem II can decline. A reduction in photochemical performance is then an outcome of both damage and insufficient repair. A plant grown at low light may escape much of this problem because less excitation arrives at its photosynthetic apparatus, even though its temperature is identical to that of a plant receiving intense light.

This mechanism can be tested through a factorial experiment involving warm or cold conditions combined with low or high light. If low temperature alone reduces carbon assimilation reversibly but the combination of low temperature and high light causes persistent fluorescence changes, the interaction between excitation and low-temperature metabolism becomes evident. A warm high-light control is needed because high light alone may also cause photoinhibition. If the combined treatment produces greater injury than expected from the separate effects, a synergistic interaction is plausible, although formal assessment depends on the response scale and statistical design.

Chilling can alter carbohydrate distribution. Sugar synthesis, utilization, conversion to storage compounds and transport through the phloem respond differently to low temperature. A leaf sugar pool may rise because export or consumption slows, even if photosynthetic production declines. This is a direct application of the distinction between pool size and metabolic flux. Accumulated sugars can also contribute to protection in acclimating plants. However, observing a high sugar concentration in cold-treated tissues does not establish that sugar synthesis increased or that the sugar caused improved survival. Time-resolved flux, metabolite amounts normalized appropriately and experimental manipulation are needed for those conclusions.

An important consequence of chilling is that injury may become evident during rewarming. Metabolic rates and electron transport can accelerate before all membranes, enzymes and antioxidant systems have recovered their coordinated functions. A tissue with reduced capacity for redox control may experience oxidative stress when metabolic activity resumes. The correct comparison is not simply warm versus cold during exposure. A recovery time course can reveal whether the tissue resumes normal function, whether damage continues to develop and whether protective acclimation improves restoration of activity. This transition from reversible suppression to persistent injury prepares the distinction between chilling and freezing.

6. Ice formation, supercooling and freeze-induced dehydration

Water is the dominant solvent in living plant tissues, but its phase behavior changes the nature of low-temperature stress. During chilling, the cellular water remains liquid. When ice forms, water is transferred from the liquid phase into a crystalline phase. This changes both the availability of liquid water and the physical arrangement of tissues. A plant cell can therefore be severely dehydrated even when the surrounding tissue contains abundant frozen water. The key to understanding freezing injury is to follow where the ice forms, where the remaining liquid water resides and how water moves between those locations.

The equilibrium freezing point of pure water at ordinary atmospheric pressure is approximately zero degrees Celsius. Plant cell sap contains dissolved substances, and dissolved solutes lower the equilibrium freezing point relative to pure water. In addition, ice formation requires nucleation, the initial formation of a sufficiently stable ice crystal or an interaction with a surface that facilitates crystal formation. Liquid water can persist below its equilibrium freezing point if suitable nucleation is delayed. This state is called supercooling. The degree and persistence of supercooling depend on physical conditions and the presence of nucleation sites.

Homogeneous nucleation refers to ice initiation without a foreign nucleating surface. Heterogeneous nucleation occurs when a surface, particle, existing ice crystal or biological material facilitates the process. Natural plant tissues provide many potential nucleation sites. Ice-nucleating microorganisms on plant surfaces can also influence the onset of freezing in some systems. The temperature at which a tissue actually begins to freeze is consequently not determined solely by the equilibrium freezing point calculated from its solute concentration. A comparison of plants cooled in the presence or absence of external ice must account for this difference.

Where ice forms and why cells dehydrate

The location of ice is especially significant. The apoplast comprises cell walls and extracellular spaces outside the plasma membrane; the symplast comprises interconnected living cytoplasms. In many freezing-tolerant tissues, ice forms initially in extracellular regions rather than inside the cytoplasm. The plasma membrane still separates the remaining intracellular liquid from the extracellular environment. Once extracellular ice forms, the chemical potential of water in equilibrium with that ice becomes lower as temperature declines. Water can leave the cells and join the external ice or redistribute into the remaining extracellular liquid. Intracellular solutes become more concentrated as water is lost.

Extracellular ice is often more survivable than intracellular ice because the growing crystals need not disrupt the cytoplasmic organization directly. Yet extracellular freezing is not harmless. Loss of cellular water shrinks the protoplast and reduces turgor. The concentrations of salts, sugars and other soluble components increase, potentially disturbing protein stability and ionic balance. Membranes that were previously separated may come into close contact as the cell volume decreases. If the plasma membrane or tonoplast cannot accommodate the changing geometry, membrane structures may become unstable and lose integrity.

The process has a clear connection with the drought and salinity chapters. Each condition can reduce the availability of water to a plant cell by lowering the water potential of the external environment. Drought lowers soil water potential in part through matric effects; dissolved salts lower the solute potential of soil solution; extracellular freezing creates a water potential difference associated with the phase equilibrium of water and ice. All three can produce cellular dehydration, but only freezing introduces the special physical constraints associated with ice nucleation, ice growth and melting. Salt stress also includes specific ion toxicity, which is not an obligatory feature of freezing.

Water potential, concentration and membrane strain

The water potential framework allows a useful approximate quantitative description of the dehydrating power of ice. Near zero degrees Celsius, the water potential of pure ice in equilibrium with liquid water at atmospheric reference pressure becomes more negative by about 1.2 megapascals for every degree Celsius below zero. This approximation follows from the chemical potential difference between liquid water and ice. It expresses the reduction in water potential needed for liquid water to be in equilibrium with ice at a given subzero temperature; it is not a statement that every frozen plant cell has exactly that measured water potential at all times.

For example, at minus three degrees Celsius the approximate equilibrium water potential associated with external ice is minus 3.6 MPa. At minus six degrees Celsius it is approximately minus 7.2 MPa. These values are much more negative than the water potential of many well-hydrated leaves. If the intracellular water initially has a higher potential, water movement out of the cells is favored. The actual rate depends on temperature, membrane water permeability, distance and tissue structure, and the cell may not reach equilibrium during a rapid cooling event. The calculation therefore gives the direction and potential magnitude of the dehydration driving force, not the exact amount of water lost.

Water loss changes intracellular concentration even when no solute has been synthesized. Consider a hypothetical cell that initially contains 1 nanomole of an effectively retained solute in 1 nanoliter of liquid water. Its concentration is 1 molar. If the water volume falls to 0.5 nanoliter while the solute amount remains unchanged, the concentration becomes 2 molar. The example is intentionally simple and not meant to represent a particular cellular solute concentration. It shows why measurements expressed per unit liquid volume can increase during dehydration without net accumulation of solute. A protective increase in total solute amount must be distinguished from passive concentration of an existing pool.

A membrane can be injured during the shrinkage phase or during subsequent rehydration. When the cell loses water, its surface area and volume change in different proportions. Membrane material may be redistributed into folds, invaginations or other configurations. If membrane organization has been irreversibly altered during dehydration, the cell may not be able to restore the appropriate surface area as water reenters after thawing. Loss of semipermeability or rupture can then become apparent on rehydration. It follows that a membrane injury measured after thawing may have originated during cooling, during the frozen period, during thawing or through interactions among these stages.

At sufficiently severe dehydration, lipid bilayers can undergo changes other than a simple transition from fluid to gel. Close membrane contact and altered hydration can favor nonbilayer structures in susceptible membranes. Such changes can interfere with the membrane's function as a continuous selective barrier. Cold acclimation can alter lipid composition and associated protective molecules so that the same dehydration produces less irreversible structural change. A student should retain the mechanistic principle that freezing tolerance depends on stability during both water removal and water return, rather than merely the fluidity of a fully hydrated bilayer at the final temperature.

Intracellular ice, cooling rate and avoidance

Intracellular ice is generally much more damaging to living plant cells. Crystals forming inside the cytoplasm or organelles can disrupt membranes and the spatial organization of macromolecules. Rapid cooling may leave insufficient time for water to migrate out of cells before intracellular ice nucleates. In many plant tissues, controlled slower freezing favors water loss to extracellular ice and reduces the probability of intracellular freezing. This is not an unlimited benefit: prolonged extracellular dehydration can itself cause severe injury. Nor should a statement about cooling rate be applied indiscriminately to specialized cryopreservation conditions or to every plant organ.

Different plants and tissues employ different strategies. Some permit extracellular ice formation and tolerate the resulting dehydration. Others delay ice entry or nucleation and survive through supercooling. Certain woody plant buds or xylem-associated living cells can exhibit substantial supercooling, depending on their structure and seasonal state. Avoidance through supercooling does not mean that the tissue could tolerate intracellular ice if its supercooling capacity were exceeded. The eventual onset of freezing in a supercooled, sensitive compartment may cause rapid injury. Tissue architecture and barriers to ice propagation therefore matter alongside cellular chemistry.

The release of latent heat during ice formation can be detected as a thermal event. An appropriately sensitive temperature measurement may show a transient deviation from the cooling curve when water crystallizes. Such an exotherm can provide evidence that freezing has occurred. It does not automatically identify whether the ice originated inside or outside individual cells; spatial information or comparison with additional methods is needed. Similarly, the absence of a clearly detectable exotherm does not prove that no microscopic ice formed if the mass of frozen water or sensitivity of the instrument is insufficient.

The degree of protection required also depends on the geometry of plant tissues. A leaf blade, a growing meristem, a woody stem and a reproductive bud have different extracellular spaces and pathways for the movement of water and ice. Ice can propagate through connected extracellular regions while being delayed by structural barriers in other locations. A frozen leaf may contain both ice-bearing and supercooled regions. Observing that a plant has experienced a subzero air temperature does not tell us which tissues froze or how much cellular dehydration occurred. Studies using controlled nucleation and tissue-level thermal measurements are designed to resolve this uncertainty.

Thawing and recovery

After thawing, a successfully tolerant plant must restore water balance, selective transport, metabolism and growth. Some leaves may appear water-soaked because membranes have lost their capacity to separate solutes and water properly. Discoloration or necrosis may become visible later. Other tissues may survive even when a portion of the leaf is damaged, allowing the plant to regenerate from protected meristems. Whole-plant survival and the percentage of injured leaf cells therefore answer different questions. Both are informative when defined clearly, and neither should be silently substituted for the other in experimental conclusions.

The three major concepts can now be connected. Chilling involves low-temperature disruption without ice. Freeze avoidance delays the formation of ice in susceptible tissues. Freezing tolerance allows cells or tissues to survive ice-associated disturbances, often including strong extracellular-ice-induced dehydration. The same plant can possess combinations of these capacities. A plant with high chilling tolerance is not necessarily highly freezing tolerant, because the latter requires protection against additional phase-change-related stresses. Cold acclimation enhances several of these capacities, depending on the organism and treatment, through coordinated physiological and molecular changes.

7. Cold acclimation and the CBF–COR regulatory network

A temperate plant that experiences several cool autumn days may survive a frost that would have damaged it during active summer growth. The useful environmental information arrives before the most damaging physical event. Exposure to low but nonfreezing temperature can induce a state in which subsequent freezing produces less injury. This process is called cold acclimation. It is distinct from the immediate slowing of metabolic reactions during cooling and from the inherited difference between species. It involves the regulated alteration of cellular composition and function over time.

The adaptive value of this sequence becomes clear when the energetic costs of protection are considered. A plant does not necessarily benefit from maintaining maximal freezing protection throughout a warm growing season. Protective metabolites, proteins and changes in developmental state may require resources or constrain rapid growth. Temperature and, in some species, seasonal information such as day length help coordinate preparation for winter. The particular cues and response times vary among species. A controlled laboratory experiment can isolate the effect of low nonfreezing temperature, while a field plant integrates multiple environmental signals.

The first stages of cold signaling arise when cellular structures experience a temperature change. Cooling alters membrane physical properties and may affect membrane-associated channels, the cytoskeleton, metabolic rates and cellular redox state. Changes in cytosolic calcium can act as early signaling events, with calcium-sensitive proteins and kinase pathways contributing to downstream responses. As in heat signaling, no single universal cold receptor accounts for all responses in all plant cells. The aim at this level is to identify the physiological perturbation, the signaling intermediates and the transcriptional outputs, without assuming that every arrow in a simplified pathway is a direct physical interaction.

The expression of cold-responsive genes can be examined by comparing RNA abundance before and after a controlled temperature change. Some genes respond rapidly and transiently, while others accumulate transcripts later or maintain expression during prolonged cold. A transcription factor induced early may regulate a set of downstream genes whose products build protective capacity. This temporal ordering is a useful hypothesis generator. Nevertheless, an early increase in one transcript followed by a later increase in another does not by itself prove that the first gene directly controls the second. Both could be influenced by a shared upstream signal.

The CBF/DREB1–COR regulon

CBF stands for C-repeat binding factor. The corresponding Arabidopsis transcription factors are also known as DREB1 proteins, meaning dehydration-responsive element binding protein 1. In the widely used Arabidopsis nomenclature, CBF1, CBF2 and CBF3 correspond to DREB1B, DREB1C and DREB1A, respectively. They belong to the AP2/ERF family of transcription factors and bind appropriate C-repeat/dehydration-responsive elements, abbreviated CRT/DRE, in regulatory DNA. A representative core motif contains CCGAC. Binding and transcriptional activation depend on the sequence and promoter context; the presence of a short matching motif alone does not prove that a particular gene is a functional CBF target.

COR genes are cold-regulated genes. The term describes a response to cold rather than a single protein family. A subset of cold-regulated genes is controlled by CBF transcription factors, and the set of genes regulated by these factors is called the CBF regulon. The regulon contains genes whose products contribute to protective functions and physiological reorganization. Other cold-responsive genes are regulated partly or independently of CBFs. Thus it is accurate to describe CBF–COR regulation as a major cold-acclimation mechanism in Arabidopsis, but not to equate every COR gene or every cold-induced physiological change with direct CBF regulation.

A central question in this pathway is whether increasing CBF activity can induce cold-protective functions when plants are not experiencing low temperature. In a landmark study published in 1998, Jaglo-Ottosen and colleagues expressed Arabidopsis CBF1 constitutively. The resulting plants expressed cold-regulated genes under conditions in which these genes would normally be much less active and displayed enhanced freezing tolerance. The experiment provides evidence that increasing the abundance of a transcriptional regulator can be sufficient to activate an important part of the protective program. It does not establish that every cold-induced gene is CBF dependent or that the complete natural acclimation response can be replaced without physiological costs.

A useful distinction is that sufficiency and necessity are different experimental claims. A constitutive CBF transgene that improves survival in an otherwise normal plant supports sufficiency of that intervention for improving the measured phenotype. A loss-of-function experiment tests whether the normal factor is necessary for a response under the conditions used. Because related CBF proteins can have overlapping functions, a mutation of one gene may yield a smaller phenotype than combined loss of several related genes. Conversely, a strong overexpression phenotype does not guarantee that a single corresponding gene is indispensable in its native expression pattern.

The classical ICE framework and its evidential limits

ICE1, inducer of CBF expression 1, is a basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor that has been included in the classical upstream framework of cold-regulated gene expression. In the original 2003 study by Chinnusamy and colleagues, a reporter driven by the CBF3 promoter was used to identify a mutant with impaired cold-induced reporter activity. This approach was attractive because it connected a visible biochemical signal to the regulation of a specific promoter. Subsequent work implicated ICE-related regulatory processes in cold responses and proposed mechanisms through which post-translational modifications influence transcription factor activity.

Scientific interpretation of the original ICE1 result has become more nuanced. Kidokoro and colleagues reported in 2020 that repression of DREB1A/CBF3 in the original ice1-1 genetic background was associated with transgene-induced DNA methylation and gene silencing, rather than being attributable simply to the ICE1 amino acid substitution. The classical diagram should therefore be understood as an organizing framework rather than definitive proof that ICE1 is the unique obligatory regulator of CBF3. Other findings continue to support contributions of ICE proteins in cold responses, but the original reporter phenotype alone cannot establish the simple linear causal claim. For examination purposes, recognize the classical ICE–CBF–COR model and the much stronger evidence for CBF-mediated downstream cold protection, while distinguishing the confidence levels of different regulatory links.

This historical correction illustrates a general problem with reporter-based genetics. A reporter is a gene whose measurable product is used as an indicator of regulatory activity. In a promoter–luciferase construct, the production of light reflects a combination of promoter activity, reporter expression and the conditions required for luciferase function. A mutation that reduces reporter output might impair transcription of the endogenous gene, but it might also affect the reporter locus, its epigenetic state or the chemistry of the measurement. Endogenous transcript analysis, segregation of genetic elements and independent alleles can distinguish these alternatives. A reporter phenotype is an entry point into mechanism, not a guarantee that the initially proposed molecular explanation is correct.

The timing of CBF expression also matters. Low temperature can produce a rapid increase in CBF transcripts, followed by accumulation of downstream protective products. The responses can be influenced by circadian timing, developmental state and the previous environmental history. Two plants subjected to cold at different phases of their internal daily cycle may not exhibit identical transcript induction. A well-controlled comparison matches sampling time, light history and developmental state, rather than assuming that the temperature treatment is the only difference between groups.

Other pathways, growth and seasonal reversal

Hormone-dependent and hormone-independent responses cooperate during acclimation. Abscisic acid is important in many dehydration-related responses and can influence expression of several protective genes. Some cold-responsive pathways are partly independent of ABA signaling. Thus an ABA-responsive protective protein is not automatically a direct target of the CBF pathway, and an intact CBF response does not imply that all ABA-dependent functions are present. The shared dehydration component of freezing, drought and salt stress explains part of this overlap. Differences in the initiating physical stresses and in regulatory context explain why their transcriptional programs are not identical.

Cold acclimation can also affect growth. In some systems, strong or prolonged expression of protective transcription factors slows expansion or development. The mechanism may involve altered hormonal regulation and allocation of metabolic resources. Growth reduction can be part of a protective seasonal transition, but it is not itself a reliable measure of freezing tolerance. A small plant may survive because its cells are better protected, because its tissues are at a different developmental stage, or because its morphology changes exposure. To establish cellular tolerance, comparisons should use appropriate tissue and developmental controls.

A central prediction follows from the transcriptional model. If the first few minutes of cold exposure alter calcium signaling but there is insufficient time for new proteins and membrane changes to develop, the increase in freezing tolerance may be limited. If the plant remains in a permissive nonfreezing cold environment for longer, downstream changes can accumulate, and protection may increase. The increase is not necessarily monotonic without limit; prolonged chilling can also injure sensitive tissues, and acclimation has physiological ceilings. Measurements across the conditioning interval are more informative than a single arbitrary time.

Cold acclimation is reversible. When the environment becomes warm, many cold-induced physiological changes are gradually lost, a process called deacclimation. Growth may resume, the expression of protective genes may decline, and tissue freezing tolerance may decrease. The rates of loss of different protective components can differ. A warm spell followed by frost may therefore be especially damaging when previously acclimated plants have already lost substantial protection. Reacclimation is possible in some circumstances, but its extent depends on species, season, developmental stage and the intervening warming period. This phenomenon shows why environmental history is as important as instantaneous temperature.

8. Protective proteins, sugars, membrane remodeling and ice-binding proteins

The transcriptional changes described in the preceding section are valuable only if they lead to changes that protect cellular functions. Cold acclimation creates a multicomponent state rather than a single molecular shield. Relevant outputs include membrane lipid remodeling, changes in soluble carbohydrate and other compatible solute pools, accumulation of dehydration-associated protective proteins, reinforcement of antioxidant systems and, in selected plants, accumulation of extracellular ice-binding proteins. These outputs address different parts of the physical problem. To understand a freezing-tolerant cell, one must relate each output to the particular lesion it can limit.

Membrane lipid remodeling helps cells deal with cooling and dehydration. Increasing suitable unsaturated fatty acyl chains can preserve membrane dynamics at low temperatures. Changes in the relative abundance of lipid headgroups and membrane-associated proteins can also alter how bilayers behave during dehydration and rehydration. The protective effect depends on lipid class, membrane compartment and the physical conditions. It is therefore possible for a plant to alter plasma membrane and chloroplast lipid composition differently during cold acclimation. Bulk leaf lipid composition provides useful information but may mask these compartment-specific changes.

Greater unsaturation is not universally beneficial under every stress. Unsaturated lipids can be susceptible to oxidative attack, and the ideal physical state of a membrane depends on temperature. A membrane composition advantageous during chilling may be less suitable during severe warming. Protection therefore requires coordination of lipid composition and antioxidant capacity. This is a general example of a physiological tradeoff: a property cannot be judged by a single desirable effect divorced from the environmental context and from other cellular functions.

Compatible solutes and sugar protection

Compatible solutes are substances that can accumulate to appreciable concentrations while generally interfering less with normal biochemical functions than many inorganic ions would at comparable concentrations. Soluble sugars, certain amino acids such as proline and other small organic molecules can contribute to water relations and stabilization of cellular components in stress contexts. The identity and importance of individual solutes vary across plant species, tissues and stresses. Sucrose and raffinose-family oligosaccharides are important examples in cold acclimation. Their roles may include osmotic effects, stabilization of membranes or proteins and provision of carbon resources during recovery.

Osmotic adjustment helps maintain a favorable water potential relationship, but it does not completely prevent dehydration when extracellular ice imposes a very negative water potential. To appreciate why, compare the osmotic effect of a modest increase in solute concentration with the equilibrium driving force associated with ice at several degrees below zero. Physiologically plausible changes in intracellular solute content can alter water relations but may be insufficient to balance the entire external freezing-induced gradient without substantial water loss. Freezing tolerance therefore requires the capacity to withstand dehydration and restore membrane function, not just the accumulation of solutes.

Sugars can also affect the stability of macromolecules through interactions with water and with membrane or protein surfaces. Under dehydrating conditions, some sugars help maintain a favorable environment around polar groups and may reduce disruptive structural changes. Their effect depends on the chemical identity, concentration, hydration and surrounding molecular system. It is not accurate to claim that every sugar simply coats every membrane or that a single mechanism explains all observed protection. A sugar may be protective in a model liposome experiment while its contribution to an intact plant depends on transport, metabolism and localization.

The distinction between solute amount and concentration is again important. If a cold-treated leaf has twice the sucrose concentration per gram of fresh mass but has lost half its water, the concentration change may not indicate a twofold increase in total sucrose. Measurements expressed relative to dry mass, total organ amount or cell water volume can answer different questions. A scientist investigating sugar-dependent protection should quantify the relevant pool, compare water status, and ideally perturb sugar metabolism or supply while accounting for secondary changes. Correlation with cold tolerance is useful evidence for choosing a hypothesis, not conclusive evidence for causation.

Dehydrins and cold-regulated protective proteins

Dehydrins are a group of highly hydrophilic proteins within the late embryogenesis abundant protein superfamily, usually classified as group II LEA proteins. Their accumulation is associated with dehydration-related stresses, including cold acclimation in many plants, and with certain stages of seed development. Several dehydrins are intrinsically disordered under physiological aqueous conditions, meaning that they do not adopt a single stable compact fold in isolation. Their flexibility and hydrophilic composition can support interactions with cellular molecules or surfaces under stressful conditions. Different dehydrins have different binding properties and protective effects.

A dehydrin can be considered a protective component rather than a catalyst carrying out a fixed chemical reaction on a substrate. In model systems, particular dehydrins can help limit aggregation or stabilize membranes during dehydration-related challenges. Their effects may depend on interactions with lipids, proteins or ions. It would be misleading to assign all dehydrins identical mechanisms or to claim that they function exactly like ATP-dependent HSP70 or HSP101. The major distinction is that the latter have well-defined energy-dependent chaperone activities, whereas dehydrins commonly contribute to protection associated with water deficit and macromolecular stability.

The chloroplast-localized Arabidopsis COR15A protein is a useful example of a cold-regulated protective protein that is not simply an upstream transcription factor. Studies of COR15A and related proteins have helped establish connections between cold-induced protein accumulation and membrane stabilization under freezing-associated dehydration. The location of a protective protein matters because the structures it can influence depend on the compartment in which it accumulates. A chloroplast-protective protein may improve the performance of photosynthetic membranes without directly stabilizing every plasma membrane or every tissue in a plant.

Ice-binding and recrystallization inhibition

Ice-binding proteins provide a different kind of protection. In selected plants, including some winter cereals and other cold-acclimating species, proteins in extracellular spaces interact with ice surfaces. A major function of many plant ice-binding proteins is inhibition of ice recrystallization, the process by which larger ice crystals grow at the expense of smaller ones over time. Binding to ice surfaces can alter crystal growth and limit the formation of large damaging crystals. Some ice-binding proteins also produce thermal hysteresis, a difference between melting and nonequilibrium freezing temperatures associated with ice growth inhibition, although many plant proteins show relatively modest thermal hysteresis compared with some animal antifreeze proteins.

Ice recrystallization inhibition must be distinguished from simple freezing point depression. A dissolved small solute lowers the equilibrium freezing point in a colligative manner that depends mainly on particle concentration in an ideal dilute solution. An ice-binding protein can act at ice surfaces in ways not explained by the number of dissolved particles alone. Its ability to alter crystal growth can be substantial at concentrations too small to account for a large colligative effect. Consequently the name antifreeze protein should not be interpreted as implying that the entire plant remains liquid at all subzero temperatures.

The extracellular location of some plant ice-binding proteins is mechanistically appropriate because extracellular ice is a major feature of freezing in tolerant tissues. A protein present in apoplastic fluid can interact with growing crystals in the region where the ice forms. A protective effect on ice growth can indirectly reduce physical injury to nearby cells. However, limiting ice recrystallization does not remove the water potential gradient between ice and intracellular liquid. The plant still needs to manage dehydration and rehydration. Ice-binding proteins therefore complement other defenses rather than replacing osmotic, membrane and macromolecular protection.

Integration of protective processes

Cold-responsive antioxidant systems address another component of the injury. During chilling and freezing recovery, imbalances in photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport can promote reactive oxygen production. Superoxide dismutase converts superoxide to hydrogen peroxide, which is then processed through appropriate peroxide-removing enzymes and associated reductant systems. Catalase and ascorbate peroxidase are representative enzymes, acting in different compartments and physiological contexts. Nonenzymatic antioxidants and the regeneration of their reduced forms help maintain cellular redox balance. Increased activity of one enzyme may not improve protection if downstream removal of its products becomes limiting.

Protection is a coordinated state. A cell with highly unsaturated membranes but limited antioxidant capacity may suffer oxidative damage; a cell with abundant protective sugars but impaired ATP production may fail to repair proteins; a cell that successfully prevents intracellular ice may still lose membrane integrity through extracellular dehydration. Cold acclimation can enhance multiple capacities together. The measured tolerance is an emergent property of these capacities plus the physical location of ice, cooling history and recovery conditions. This is why selecting a single transcript or metabolite as a universal biomarker of freezing tolerance is rarely justified.

From an applied perspective, breeders and physiologists compare genotypes under realistic thermal histories. A genotype showing strong induction of cold-protective genes after several days of cool exposure may perform well in environments with predictable autumn cooling but may be vulnerable to abrupt temperature drops if acclimation is slow. Another genotype may possess substantial baseline protection but have a smaller inducible increase. Neither category is universally superior. Their relative value depends on the timing of stress, developmental stage, water availability, production goals and the energetic consequences of maintaining protection. These considerations connect the cell-level mechanisms to the design of meaningful tolerance experiments.

9. Quantitative temperature responses and what their measurements mean

Thermal experiments are most useful when the measurements correspond to the physical or physiological quantity under discussion. Temperature, reaction rate, membrane permeability, survival and productivity are distinct variables. Mathematical relationships help us move between measured values and their interpretation, but each relationship relies on assumptions. The aim of this section is to develop a small set of calculations that can be used to reason about plant thermal stress without treating empirical coefficients as universal biological laws.

Temperature coefficient Q10

A convenient description of the effect of moderate temperature change on a reaction rate is the temperature coefficient Q10. If rates R1 and R2 are measured at temperatures T1 and T2, respectively, the coefficient is Q10 = (R2/R1) raised to the power 10/(T2 − T1), where the temperature difference is expressed in degrees Celsius or equivalently kelvins. The rate ratio is dimensionless, so Q10 is dimensionless. When the temperature difference is exactly ten degrees, the coefficient is simply the ratio of the two rates. The formula rescales an observed rate ratio to a ten-degree interval under the assumption of an approximately constant exponential temperature dependence over the range concerned.

Suppose oxygen consumption by an unstressed plant tissue is 6 micromoles per gram dry mass per hour at 15 °C and 12 in the same units at 25 °C. Its apparent Q10 over this interval is 12/6 = 2. If a different interval from 15 to 20 °C gives rates of 6 and 9, Q10 is (9/6) squared, or 2.25. The difference between the coefficients need not imply an error; it may reflect a departure from constant exponential behavior, different regulatory conditions or measurement variation. All rates used in the calculation must refer to the same defined process and normalization.

The use of Q10 becomes particularly hazardous near a damage threshold. Suppose the rate rises from 6 to 12 between 15 and 25 °C but falls to 4 at 40 °C. Extrapolating the initial Q10 would predict a continuing increase, whereas the observed rate has decreased. This may happen because the activity of enzymes, transport or energy coupling has become impaired. The coefficient measured in a noninjurious interval cannot represent the physiological consequences of severe warming. Even within a moderate interval, a coefficient measured immediately after temperature transfer can differ from one measured after days of acclimation.

Colligative freezing point depression

The freezing point depression of an ideal dilute solution provides a second calculation. The magnitude of the depression, ΔTf, equals iKf m, where i is the effective van't Hoff factor, Kf is the cryoscopic constant of the solvent and m is molality, expressed as moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. For water, Kf is approximately 1.86 kelvin kilogram per mole. For an ideal dilute nonionizing solute such as sucrose, i is approximately one. The equilibrium freezing temperature relative to pure water is lowered by ΔTf. This relationship predicts an equilibrium property, not the actual time or temperature at which ice nucleation occurs in a living plant.

For a 0.20 molal ideal sucrose solution, ΔTf equals 1 × 1.86 × 0.20 = 0.372 K. A solution initially expected to freeze at zero degrees would have an equilibrium freezing point near minus 0.37 °C under these assumptions. The modest numerical change helps explain why physiological sugar accumulation cannot usually prevent all ice formation during a severe frost through colligative action alone. Sugars can still have considerable protective effects through water relations and macromolecular stabilization. In a concentrated, multicomponent cell solution, ideal behavior is an approximation, and the use of molarity in place of molality without justification introduces an additional error.

Thermodynamic origin of ice-induced dehydration

The equilibrium relation for the water potential of ice can be motivated from thermodynamics. Let Tm denote the melting temperature of pure water in kelvins, and let ΔT denote a small positive depression below Tm. The molar entropy of fusion is approximately the molar enthalpy of fusion divided by Tm. Near the melting point the chemical potential of liquid water exceeds that of ice by approximately this entropy difference multiplied by ΔT. Expressing the chemical potential difference as energy per unit volume gives a pressure equivalent. Using a latent heat of fusion near 333.5 kilojoules per kilogram, water density near 1000 kilograms per cubic meter and Tm near 273.15 K gives approximately 1.22 million pascals per kelvin. Thus the water potential of liquid in equilibrium with pure ice is approximately minus 1.22 MPa for each degree below zero.

This derivation assumes a small temperature range, reference pressure near atmospheric pressure and an approximate constant latent heat and density. It establishes a physical scale, not an exact model of every tissue. At minus four degrees Celsius the pressure equivalent is about minus 4.9 MPa. A cell initially at a water potential of minus 0.7 MPa has a higher water potential than the external ice-equilibrated environment. Water movement out of the cell is favored if a liquid pathway exists. Subtracting the initial values gives an approximate initial potential difference of 4.2 MPa, but this difference changes as cellular solutes concentrate and cell pressure changes. A constant water loss rate cannot be calculated from that initial difference alone.

Conductivity, injury and dose-response curves

Electrolyte leakage assays provide a practical measure of the consequences of thermal membrane damage. A leaf disc or other defined tissue sample is incubated in a suitable aqueous medium after treatment, and the electrical conductivity of the medium is measured. Ions released from cells contribute to conductivity. To normalize against the total releasable electrolyte pool, the same sample is subsequently subjected to a treatment that releases essentially all releasable electrolytes, and conductivity is measured again. With blank correction, relative leakage can be calculated as 100 × (C1 − Cblank)/(Ctotal − Cblank), where C1 is the conductivity after the defined incubation and Ctotal is the conductivity after total release. Both conductivities must be measured in the same units and under comparable measurement-temperature conditions.

Consider a hypothetical sample with an initial incubation conductivity of 210 microsiemens per centimeter, a total conductivity of 610 and a blank of 10. Relative leakage equals 100 × 200/600 = 33.3 percent. If a second sample has an initial conductivity of 310 and total conductivity of 1010 with the same blank, the relative leakage is 30 percent. The second sample released a greater absolute conductivity but a smaller proportion of its available electrolyte pool. Raw conductivity values are therefore not interchangeable with normalized leakage, particularly when tissue size, ion content or treatment conditions differ.

Leakage is an index of the ability to retain ions. It is not a direct measurement of the percentage of individual cells that have died. A living cell can exhibit temporary permeability changes, and different cells can contribute unequal quantities of ions. Cutting tissue can also release electrolytes from damaged edges. Matched disc dimensions, appropriate rinsing, incubation time, solution volume, blank controls and a standardized total-release procedure are important. The temperature dependence of the conductivity measurement itself must be controlled or compensated. Combining leakage with regrowth, viability or photosynthetic measurements provides a more complete picture.

Relative injury may be estimated after accounting for leakage in unfrozen control tissue. If normalized leakage is Lc in the control and Lt in the stressed sample, a commonly used correction is 100 × (Lt − Lc)/(100 − Lc), when leakage is expressed in percentage units. This formula rescales the observed increase to the range between control leakage and complete release. A control leakage of 10 percent and treated leakage of 55 percent yield corrected injury of 100 × 45/90 = 50 percent. The correction assumes the control is an appropriate baseline and that the scale of leakage is meaningful for the tissue under comparison. An injury index remains an assay-derived quantity rather than a direct cell count.

Freezing tolerance is often summarized by a temperature associated with a specified level of injury or mortality. LT50 commonly denotes a temperature causing 50 percent lethality under defined conditions. When an electrolyte leakage assay is used to estimate a midpoint, the reported temperature may be called an injury-based LT50 or an electrolyte-leakage midpoint; the endpoint must be stated explicitly. If corrected injury is 40 percent at minus four degrees and 60 percent at minus six degrees, linear interpolation gives a 50 percent injury midpoint near minus five degrees. A more negative midpoint generally indicates better freezing tolerance under an identical protocol. The interpolation assumes a locally linear response; a full dose-response fit is preferable when sufficient observations are available.

Chlorophyll fluorescence calculations

Chlorophyll fluorescence adds a complementary functional measurement. Using dark-adapted minimal fluorescence F0 and maximal fluorescence Fm, the variable fluorescence Fv equals Fm − F0 and Fv/Fm equals (Fm − F0)/Fm. Suppose F0 is 200 arbitrary units and Fm is 1000 before treatment. The ratio is 0.80. After treatment F0 is 300 and Fm is 750, giving a ratio of 0.60. The ratio has decreased by 0.20, or 25 percent relative to its original value. These are different ways of expressing the change: a decrease of 0.20 in the dimensionless ratio is not the same numerical statement as a 20 percent relative reduction.

When comparing fluorescence, the dark adaptation conditions and measuring instrument must be matched. A reduction in Fv/Fm can support the conclusion that maximum photosystem II photochemical efficiency has been impaired, but it does not establish the identity of a molecular lesion or the extent of membrane injury elsewhere. Conversely, normal Fv/Fm does not guarantee normal carbon fixation. A well-designed study compares several endpoints and asks which hypotheses remain consistent with the entire pattern.

10. Designing experiments that distinguish damage, protection and recovery

The most direct way to study acquired thermal tolerance is to make the conditioning history an explicit experimental variable. Compare genetically matched plants subjected to a mild heat treatment or maintained at a permissive temperature. After a defined interval, expose a portion of each group to the same severe challenge. Include both unchallenged groups. This design distinguishes constitutive differences from inducible changes and ensures that a conditioning treatment is not incorrectly classified as beneficial simply because only its surviving individuals were analyzed.

In a hypothetical heat experiment, unconditioned wild-type seedlings show 25 percent survival after severe challenge, whereas conditioned wild-type seedlings show 85 percent survival. A mutant impaired in an ATP-dependent disaggregase shows 20 percent survival without conditioning and 30 percent after conditioning. Both genotypes show nearly complete survival in the untreated and conditioning-only groups. The key pattern is not merely that the mutant has lower survival after heat. The change associated with conditioning is 60 percentage points in wild type and 10 in the mutant. The contrast supports a specific role for the affected process in acquired protection, subject to the limits of the genotype and treatment. A genetic complementation test and direct measures of aggregate clearance would strengthen the mechanistic inference.

Controlled nucleation and acclimation

The same experimental logic applies to cold acclimation. One group is maintained at a permissive temperature, and another is exposed to low nonfreezing temperature for a defined period. Both are challenged across a range of subzero temperatures using a standardized cooling and nucleation protocol. The response curves can be compared rather than relying on one arbitrarily chosen temperature. A shift in the injury midpoint toward colder temperatures suggests an improvement in freezing tolerance. Differences in baseline leakage, tissue hydration and developmental state must be examined so that apparent protection is not merely an artifact of the measurement.

Ice nucleation is a particularly important experimental control. If one genotype supercools more readily than another, cooling both to a nominal air temperature without controlling freezing onset can compare avoidance with tolerance unintentionally. For a study of tolerance to extracellular ice, inducing freezing consistently under appropriate conditions and verifying the freezing event makes the physical challenge more comparable. For a study of freeze avoidance, the spontaneous onset temperature and ice propagation are themselves informative endpoints. The experimental design should follow the question rather than treating the two traits as identical.

A second hypothetical experiment compares two groups at minus six degrees Celsius. The nonacclimated group has corrected electrolyte injury of 75 percent and the acclimated group 30 percent. After thawing, photosystem II maximum quantum efficiency is 0.40 in the first group and 0.72 in the second. These measurements support protection of membrane integrity and photochemical function in the acclimated group, but do not identify the responsible molecule. The researcher might measure membrane lipid composition, sugar amounts and COR protein abundance, then use targeted genetic or biochemical perturbations. The mechanistic claim must become more specific only as the evidence becomes more specific.

Genetic and molecular evidence

In an experiment using a CBF overexpression line, a cold-responsive transcript is elevated even without chilling, and the plants survive a more severe freezing challenge than the control. If the transgenic line also grows more slowly under normal conditions, that difference deserves attention. Slower growth may affect developmental stage, cell composition or stress exposure. Comparing plants of similar developmental stage, examining several independent transgenic lines and using a suitable expression control would help exclude incidental effects of transformation or development. The overexpression phenotype supports a causal contribution of increased CBF activity but does not prove that the endogenous cold response is fully CBF dependent.

Direct target regulation can be tested at several levels. A cold-induced transcription factor may bind a sequence within the promoter of a protective gene in a DNA-binding assay. A reporter construct with that sequence mutated can show whether the sequence contributes to transcriptional activation in the assay system. Endogenous transcript measurements in appropriate genetic backgrounds establish whether the regulation matters in plants. Physiological challenge experiments then test the consequence for tolerance. Each approach answers a distinct question: physical binding, regulatory function, genetic dependence and biological outcome. Agreement among these approaches strengthens the mechanistic model.

A protein accumulation experiment should likewise distinguish association from necessity. High amounts of a dehydrin in a freezing-tolerant plant suggest a candidate protective function. If loss of the corresponding gene increases freezing injury and a functional transgene restores protection, the evidence for a causal contribution becomes stronger. However, redundancy among protective proteins can mask loss of one gene, and an overexpression experiment can produce effects not normally achieved by endogenous expression. In vitro stabilization of membranes by a purified protein supports a possible molecular mechanism, but cellular localization and physiological relevance still require separate evidence.

Factorial design and independent replication

Temperature treatments can interact with water status and light. To determine whether chilling-related photoinhibition depends on excess excitation, use a warm-low-light group, a warm-high-light group, a cold-low-light group and a cold-high-light group. Measure dark-adapted fluorescence before exposure and after a common recovery period. If the cold-high-light group alone exhibits large persistent impairment, a temperature-by-light interaction is suggested. Repeating the experiment with appropriate biological replicates, controlling actual leaf temperature and using comparable dark adaptation reduces the chance of attributing incidental differences to the treatment.

Statistical replication should be considered before interpreting a difference between treatments. Multiple discs cut from one leaf may be useful technical or subsampling replicates, but they do not necessarily represent independent plants. If all plants of one treatment are grown in one chamber and all controls in another, a chamber effect may be confounded with temperature. Replicating treatments across independent experimental units or appropriately rotating treatments helps reduce that problem. An observed difference in two group means alone does not establish its precision, reproducibility or cause.

Thermal protection must ultimately be connected to recovery and continued biological function. An intact membrane immediately after thawing is important, but the plant must also restore metabolism and developmental activity. Survival scored after a suitable recovery period, renewed leaf expansion, root growth, photosynthetic performance and reproductive success can reveal different aspects of tolerance. A plant with damaged leaves but a viable meristem can recover; a plant whose leaves appear healthy may fail to produce viable seeds. Examination problems often require choosing the endpoint that actually addresses the stated biological question.

The broader significance of thermal stress lies in its interaction with other stresses. A heat episode often coincides with high evaporative demand, while freezing produces a form of dehydration. Reactive oxygen species and hormonal pathways participate in several stress responses. The presence of shared protective processes does not erase the distinct initiating mechanisms: excessive thermal disorder and protein instability in heat, low-temperature metabolic and membrane disturbances in chilling, and ice-associated dehydration and mechanical effects in freezing. When interpreting a complex stress phenotype, first establish the physical cause, then identify vulnerable structures, then ask which responses prevent damage or promote recovery. This causal approach prepares the next chapter, in which oxygen limitation during flooding introduces another distinct physical constraint on plant metabolism.

Part B — conceptual questions

1. Which observation most directly distinguishes reversible cooling from chilling injury?

  1. Lower assimilation during exposure
  2. Persistent impairment after standardized rewarming
  3. Reduced thermal molecular motion
  4. A temperature below the growth optimum
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. Persistent impairment after recovery supports injury rather than a reversible kinetic effect. The other observations can occur in healthy tissues exposed to noninjurious cooling.

2. Which event defines freezing rather than chilling?

  1. Lower enzyme activity
  2. Increased membrane order
  3. Induction of a cold-responsive transcript
  4. Formation of ice
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Ice formation is the distinguishing physical event. Changes in enzyme activity, membrane order and gene expression can occur without ice.

3. During physiological heat denaturation, which protein property is most directly compromised?

  1. Native three-dimensional conformation
  2. Every peptide bond
  3. The genetic code
  4. All amino acid identities
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Loss of folded organization impairs activity without requiring hydrolysis of the peptide backbone. The sequence and the code need not change.

4. Which protein family is commonly described as an ATP-independent holdase?

  1. HSP101
  2. HSP70
  3. Small HSP
  4. HSP90
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Small HSPs bind vulnerable substrates and limit aggregation. HSP101, HSP70 and HSP90 have ATP-dependent cycles.

5. The most characteristic role of HSP101 in heat recovery is

  1. ATP-dependent disaggregation with partner chaperones
  2. Binding the HSE promoter as a transcription factor
  3. Catalyzing lipid desaturation
  4. Direct fixation of CO₂
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. HSP101 belongs to the HSP100/ClpB-type disaggregase family. HSFs bind HSEs, desaturases modify lipids, and Rubisco catalyzes carboxylation.

6. Which is a cis-regulatory DNA element recognized by many HSFs?

  1. HSP70
  2. HSE
  3. COR15A
  4. Rubisco activase
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. An HSE is a promoter sequence; the remaining choices are proteins or genes encoding proteins, not this heat-responsive DNA motif.

7. Basal thermotolerance is best defined as

  1. Survival of severe heat following a conditioning treatment
  2. Survival exclusively by evaporation
  3. Permanent genetic adaptation within one day
  4. Tolerance without the particular heat pretreatment
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Basal capacity is measured without specific conditioning. Prior nonlethal heat defines acquired protection; evaporation is one possible avoidance mechanism, not the definition.

8. Which change commonly helps maintain membrane fluidity during cooling?

  1. Replacing all lipids with proteins
  2. Eliminating all sterols in every membrane
  3. Increasing cis-unsaturated acyl chains
  4. Hydrolyzing all phospholipids
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Cis double bonds tend to frustrate tight packing. Sterol effects depend on context, and destruction of the lipid bilayer would not preserve function.

9. Extracellular ice initially tends to cause

  1. Net water entry into the cytoplasm at all temperatures
  2. Immediate synthesis of additional water
  3. Water movement out of cells when the external water potential is lower
  4. Loss of the cell wall by peptide hydrolysis
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Ice lowers the chemical potential of extracellular water. Cellular water moves outward if the gradient and liquid pathway permit it. The alternative direction reverses the gradient; synthesis of water and cell-wall hydrolysis are unrelated.

10. Supercooling describes

  1. Water remaining liquid below its equilibrium freezing point
  2. Melting ice above its normal melting point
  3. All cold-induced gene expression
  4. Cell dehydration following salt uptake
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Supercooling is a metastable liquid condition before ice nucleation. It is a form of freeze avoidance when it delays tissue freezing.

11. In Arabidopsis, CBF/DREB1 proteins belong to which transcription-factor family?

  1. HSP100
  2. MADS-box only
  3. ATP synthase
  4. AP2/ERF
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. CBFs are AP2/ERF transcription factors; HSP100 proteins are chaperones, MADS proteins form a different regulatory family, and ATP synthase is an enzyme complex.

12. The CRT/DRE sequence is principally relevant to

  1. ATP hydrolysis by HSP101
  2. CBF-mediated regulation of target genes
  3. Nucleation of ice on a crystal
  4. Rubisco oxygenation chemistry
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. CBF proteins bind suitable CRT/DRE motifs in regulatory DNA. The other processes are mechanistically distinct.

13. Which statement about COR genes is correct?

  1. All COR genes encode identical dehydrins
  2. Cold-regulated genes include CBF-dependent and other genes
  3. All are necessarily direct targets of ICE1
  4. COR genes cannot be expressed at warm temperatures
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. COR describes cold-regulated expression, not a unique structural family or regulatory mechanism. Some genes can be expressed outside cold conditions.

14. Dehydrins are generally classified as

  1. Photosystem reaction centers
  2. CBF DNA-binding motifs
  3. Group II LEA proteins
  4. Membrane sterols
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Dehydrins belong to the LEA protein family and are associated with dehydration-related protection. The other options denote different biological entities.

15. Many extracellular plant ice-binding proteins primarily contribute to

  1. Inhibition of ice recrystallization
  2. Elimination of all ice from the plant
  3. Hydrolysis of ATP to refold aggregates
  4. CO₂ pumping into bundle sheath cells
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Many plant ice-binding proteins inhibit crystal coarsening. They do not necessarily prevent initial freezing or remove the dehydration gradient.

16. Which molecule is often a heat-sensitive contributor to loss of Rubisco activation?

  1. Cellulose only
  2. Chlorophyll as an enzyme
  3. An extracellular ice crystal
  4. Rubisco activase
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Rubisco activase helps maintain active Rubisco sites and can be heat-sensitive. Cellulose, chlorophyll and ice do not perform that activation function.

17. A major reason bright light aggravates chilling injury is that

  1. All chlorophyll becomes nonfunctional immediately
  2. Cold converts ATP into DNA directly
  3. Photons eliminate membrane dehydration
  4. Light absorption can continue while metabolic use and repair are restricted
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Mismatch between excitation and productive metabolism or repair increases photoinhibition risk. It is not necessary for all chlorophyll to lose function.

18. Cold acclimation is an example of

  1. A reversible physiological change within an individual
  2. An obligatory change in DNA sequence
  3. A definition of intracellular ice
  4. Only a change in soil salinity
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Acclimation reflects environmental conditioning of physiological state. Evolutionary adaptation concerns heritable population changes; the other options are separate stresses.

19. Which measurement specifically estimates maximum PSII photochemical efficiency after dark adaptation?

  1. Total leaf fresh mass
  2. Electrolyte conductivity alone
  3. Fv/Fm
  4. Pollen count alone
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Fv/Fm is a fluorescence-derived PSII efficiency index, not a measure of total carbon assimilation or tissue death.

20. For two injury curves obtained by identical protocols, the line with a more negative 50% freezing-injury temperature is generally

  1. Less tolerant
  2. More tolerant
  3. Necessarily more heat tolerant
  4. Necessarily incapable of acclimation
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. A more negative injury midpoint means colder exposure is needed for the same injury. It does not establish heat tolerance or identify the acclimation mechanism.

21. Which combination identifies a heat-avoidance response rather than direct cellular heat tolerance?

  1. HSP101-mediated disaggregation at equal tissue temperature
  2. COR15A-induced membrane protection during freezing
  3. Leaf evaporative cooling and lower tissue temperature
  4. Recovery of enzyme function after heat
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: C. Evaporation reduces the experienced thermal exposure. Disaggregation and enzyme recovery address tolerance or repair, whereas COR15A is cold-related protection.

22. Which statement best describes deacclimation?

  1. Increasing permanent cold tolerance through mutation
  2. Initial ice nucleation inside a cell
  3. Constitutive expression of every HSP
  4. Loss of previously induced cold protection during warming
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: D. Deacclimation is the decline of acquired protection after conditions become warmer; it can increase vulnerability to subsequent frost.

23. Which comparison distinguishes an HSF from an HSP?

  1. Both are necessarily DNA-binding transcription factors
  2. HSF regulates transcription; HSP generally participates in protective protein functions
  3. HSF is a DNA motif; HSP is an ice crystal
  4. HSF denotes any solute; HSP denotes a hormone
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: B. HSFs are regulatory proteins, whereas many HSPs act in proteostasis. The HSE, not HSF, is a DNA motif.

24. A chilling-sensitive plant can be injured at +5 °C because

  1. Membrane and metabolic disturbances can occur in liquid water
  2. Intracellular ice must always form above zero
  3. Every plant has an identical threshold
  4. Low temperature destroys all covalent bonds
Answer and explanation

Correct answer: A. Chilling is nonfreezing injury; sensitivity varies with species, tissue and duration. Ice and widespread covalent bond cleavage are not required.

Part C — analytical reasoning

Write a reasoned response or calculation before revealing the model answer.

1. Wild type survives heat challenge at 25% without pretreatment and 85% after conditioning. A disaggregase mutant survives at 20% and 30%, respectively; both have nearly complete survival in controls. Calculate the conditioning gains and interpret.

Answer and explanation

Wild type: 60 percentage points; mutant: 10. The difference in gains is 50 points, indicating that the mutant has a much smaller acquired response under these conditions. Similar unconditioned survival argues against interpreting the difference solely as low basal tolerance. Complementation and aggregate-clearance data would strengthen a disaggregase-specific causal conclusion.

2. An extract has 100 units of immunodetectable enzyme before heat and 98 after heat, but activity declines from 100 to 35 units. Is reduced protein abundance a sufficient explanation? Propose a follow-up.

Answer and explanation

No. Nearly unchanged abundance is inconsistent with abundance loss alone explaining a 65% activity decline. Conformational inactivation, inhibitory regulation or aggregation are plausible. Assay soluble versus insoluble protein and activity following controlled recovery; measure an appropriate activity standard. Immunoreactivity alone does not establish a functional fold.

3. At 15 °C and 20 °C, respiratory rates are 6 and 9 µmol g⁻¹ dry mass h⁻¹. Compute Q10 and explain why extrapolation to lethal heat is inappropriate.

Answer and explanation

Q10 = (9/6)^(10/5) = 2.25, dimensionless. This is an apparent coefficient over the measured interval. At injurious temperatures enzymes and membranes may fail; acclimation and substrate limitation can also change the response. Extrapolation assumes unchanged exponential dependence, which the experiment does not establish.

4. Leaf A has C1 = 210 and Ctotal = 610; leaf B has C1 = 310 and Ctotal = 1010 µS cm⁻¹. Blank = 10. Which has greater normalized leakage?

Answer and explanation

A = 100 × 200/600 = 33.3%; B = 100 × 300/1000 = 30%. A has higher normalized leakage even though B's raw conductivity is greater. Different total electrolyte pools make direct comparison of unnormalized conductivity misleading.

5. A control has 10% leakage and a frozen sample has 55% leakage. Calculate corrected injury and state what it does not establish.

Answer and explanation

Corrected injury = 100 × (55−10)/(100−10) = 50%. It is a normalized assay endpoint, not direct proof that exactly half the cells died; unequal electrolyte content, reversible permeability and cutting injury can influence the measurement.

6. A freezing injury curve gives 40% at −4 °C and 60% at −6 °C. Estimate the 50% midpoint. A second line reaches 50% at −7 °C. Which is more tolerant under matched conditions?

Answer and explanation

Linear interpolation gives −5 °C for the first line. The second line has the more negative midpoint and thus better freezing tolerance by this endpoint. Interpolation assumes local linearity; neither result establishes the specific protective mechanism.

7. F0/Fm values are 200/1000 before heating and 300/750 afterward. Calculate Fv/Fm for each and interpret the change.

Answer and explanation

Before: (1000−200)/1000 = 0.80. After: (750−300)/750 = 0.60. The decrease is 0.20 absolute or 25% relative to baseline. Under matched dark adaptation it supports impaired maximum PSII efficiency, not direct quantification of whole-leaf assimilation.

8. Net assimilation falls by half under moderate heating, but Fv/Fm remains near the control value and intercellular CO₂ increases. Which hypothesis is favored and what test is useful?

Answer and explanation

A downstream biochemical limitation is plausible, rather than a purely stomatal limitation or demonstrated severe PSII injury. Measure Rubisco activation state and activase activity, alongside gas exchange and light-adapted fluorescence. Intercellular CO₂ estimates and normal Fv/Fm do not rule out all other limitations.

9. Four light-temperature combinations yield post-recovery Fv/Fm values 0.82 (warm-low), 0.79 (warm-high), 0.77 (cold-low), 0.40 (cold-high). Interpret the interaction.

Answer and explanation

High light reduces the ratio by 0.03 in warmth and 0.37 in cold; the effect is much larger in cold. This suggests a light-by-temperature interaction consistent with excitation pressure and impaired repair. Replicate independent units and test the interaction statistically; the means alone do not identify the molecular lesion.

10. A mutant has fewer cis-unsaturated chloroplast lipids and normal growth in warmth but persistent PSII impairment in cold. What distinguishes correlation from a gene-specific effect?

Answer and explanation

Confirm lipid composition and compare independent mutants or complemented plants under matched light, leaf temperature and developmental conditions. Restoration of lipid composition and phenotype by functional complementation supports causation. Changes in other membrane components or developmental traits remain possible alternatives without those controls.

11. At −5 °C, estimate the equilibrium water potential associated with pure external ice using −1.22 MPa per degree. A cell starts at −0.8 MPa. Predict initial water movement.

Answer and explanation

Ice-associated potential ≈ −6.10 MPa. The cell initially has higher potential, so water tends to exit if connected by a permeable liquid pathway. The initial potential difference is about 5.3 MPa; it changes during dehydration and cannot alone specify the final water content.

12. An ideal dilute 0.30 molal sucrose solution has Kf = 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹. Calculate its equilibrium freezing point depression. Does this predict absence of ice at −5 °C?

Answer and explanation

ΔTf = 1 × 1.86 × 0.30 = 0.558 K, approximately 0.56 °C. This colligative effect alone does not prevent equilibrium freezing at −5 °C. Actual nucleation may be delayed, and sugars may also protect structures by noncolligative mechanisms.

13. An extracellularly frozen cell retains 3 nmol of solute. Its liquid water volume falls from 6 nL to 2 nL. Calculate concentrations and infer whether new synthesis is necessary.

Answer and explanation

Initial concentration = 3/6 = 0.5 M; final = 3/2 = 1.5 M. The concentration triples solely through water loss; no increase in solute amount is required. Quantify total solute or use appropriate dry-mass normalization before attributing accumulation to synthesis.

14. Line A remains supercooled at −7 °C whereas line B forms extracellular ice at −3 °C and survives −7 °C. Which line has demonstrated freezing tolerance?

Answer and explanation

B has demonstrated survival after ice formation, consistent with freezing tolerance. A has demonstrated freeze avoidance under the given conditions; its ability to survive ice formation remains untested. Standardized nucleation can compare their tolerance directly.

15. Two freezing assays cool plants at different rates. The rapidly cooled group shows greater intracellular ice and mortality. Explain the observation and a limitation of generalizing it.

Answer and explanation

Rapid cooling may leave insufficient time for water to leave cells toward extracellular ice, increasing the likelihood of intracellular freezing. However, slower cooling may also prolong extracellular dehydration, and tissue-specific nucleation and permeability matter. One protocol cannot establish that the slowest possible cooling is always safest.

16. CBF1 overexpression activates COR genes in warm plants and increases freezing survival. What claim is supported, and what is not established?

Answer and explanation

Increasing CBF1 is sufficient to activate part of the cold-protective program and improve survival in the tested context. It does not demonstrate that every COR gene is directly regulated by CBF1, that CBF1 alone is necessary, or that all aspects of acclimation are reproduced.

17. A single cbf mutant shows a weak phenotype but a multiple cbf mutant is severely freezing sensitive. Explain.

Answer and explanation

Related CBF proteins can have overlapping regulatory functions, so loss of one may be partially compensated. Combined loss can reveal the contribution of the family. Confirm that the genotypes and developmental backgrounds are matched and check target expression to connect genotype and mechanism.

18. A promoter-luciferase line loses cold-induced light emission, but endogenous transcript abundance is unchanged. What can and cannot be concluded?

Answer and explanation

The reporter signal is reduced, but repression of the endogenous gene is not demonstrated. Altered reporter locus regulation, silencing, luciferase chemistry or expression could explain the difference. Use independent reporters, endogenous transcript assays and genetic segregation.

19. A purified protein binds a CRT/DRE-containing DNA fragment, but mutating the motif does not change gene expression in intact leaves. Does the binding result establish functional regulation?

Answer and explanation

No. Binding supports physical interaction under assay conditions. Redundant promoter elements, chromatin context or missing regulatory partners can change the in vivo consequence. Measure endogenous transcription and test regulatory dependence before making a direct functional claim.

20. A cold-acclimated line has twice the dehydrin abundance and improved survival. Which experiment best tests causality?

Answer and explanation

Compare a loss-of-function line and a complemented line in a matched background and test freezing injury and recovery. Direct membrane or protein stabilization tests can support mechanism. Correlation between protein abundance and survival alone does not show necessity or sufficiency.

21. An ice-binding protein reduces mean ice crystal size during thawing, but measured equilibrium freezing point remains nearly unchanged. Is the result contradictory?

Answer and explanation

No. Ice recrystallization inhibition reflects interactions with ice surfaces and need not produce substantial colligative freezing point depression. The protein can restrict crystal coarsening even when extracellular ice forms and dehydration remains possible.

22. After a warm spell, two previously acclimated lines have identical CBF transcript abundance, but different freezing injury. Give two plausible explanations.

Answer and explanation

Protective proteins and metabolites may persist for different durations, and membrane remodeling or developmental state may differ. A single transcript snapshot does not measure the full induced physiological state. Compare proteins, sugars, lipid composition and standardized injury across a deacclimation time course.

23. Heated pollen on control recipients yields 25% seed set, control pollen on heated recipients 85%, and control crosses 90%. What is the most defensible inference?

Answer and explanation

Male reproductive function is strongly implicated under these conditions, because the major reduction accompanies heated pollen. The smaller difference in heated recipients requires replication. The data do not establish a specific tapetal lesion or exclude every maternal contribution.

24. Two genotypes survive heat equally well, but one has lower leaf temperature because of greater transpiration. How would you distinguish heat avoidance from intrinsic tolerance?

Answer and explanation

Measure tissue temperature and water status, then compare physiological injury and recovery at matched tissue temperatures and comparable hydration. Lower leaf temperature demonstrates reduced exposure, not automatically improved cellular tolerance at the same temperature.

State SET and mixed-format practice

1. Assertion: extracellular freezing can dehydrate living cells. Reason: ice-equilibrated extracellular water has a lower chemical potential at subzero temperature.

Answer and explanation

Both statements are true and the reason explains the assertion, provided the cell initially has higher water potential and a pathway for water movement.

2. Assertion: HSFs and HSPs are interchangeable terms. Reason: many HSP genes contain heat-responsive regulatory elements.

Answer and explanation

The assertion is false; the reason is true. HSFs are regulators and HSPs are protective proteins. The presence of HSEs does not make the proteins identical.

3. Assertion: a plant with high chilling tolerance must survive intracellular ice. Reason: chilling injury occurs without tissue freezing.

Answer and explanation

The assertion is false; the reason is true. Nonfreezing protection need not protect the living compartment from ice crystals.

4. An untreated group and a conditioned-only group each survive at 100%; challenge-only survival is 30% and conditioned-plus-challenge survival is 70%. What improvement is observed?

Answer and explanation

The gain is 40 percentage points (70−30); the relative increase over challenge-only survival is 40/30 × 100 ≈ 133%. The conditioning-only control shows that conditioning was not lethal under the measured conditions.

5. Which technique provides direct evidence of a thermal event consistent with ice nucleation: a transcript assay, a detectable freezing exotherm, or pollen count?

Answer and explanation

A freezing exotherm is consistent with release of latent heat as water crystallizes. Transcript and pollen assays report different biological endpoints; an exotherm alone does not localize ice.

6. Why is one chamber per treatment with ten plants in each potentially confounded?

Answer and explanation

The chamber itself can differ in light, humidity or airflow. Plants within a chamber may not be independent treatment-level replicates. Independent chamber replicates or an appropriate randomized/rotated design are needed to separate chamber and treatment effects.

Revision, active recall and mastery

Conceptual summary

Thermal stress changes the rates and structural stability of interacting biological processes. Heat promotes protein unfolding, aggregation and excessive membrane disorder; HSFs regulate protective HSPs. Chilling causes nonfreezing metabolic imbalance and altered membrane order. Freezing adds ice-induced water redistribution, cellular dehydration and possible intracellular crystal damage. Acclimation changes the state of the plant before a subsequent challenge, whereas recovery restores surviving cells afterward.

Cold protection involves CBF-dependent and independent transcription, suitable membrane lipid remodeling, protective solutes and proteins, antioxidant functions and, in selected plants, extracellular ice-binding proteins. No individual component guarantees survival. Interpret tissue-specific endpoints in relation to exposure, pretreatment, developmental state and recovery.

Essential terminology and high-yield relationships

HSF: heat-shock transcription factor. HSE: cis-regulatory heat-shock element. HSP: heat-shock protein. CBF/DREB1: AP2/ERF transcription factor recognizing CRT/DRE. COR: cold-regulated gene. Dehydrin: group II LEA protein associated with dehydration-related protection. Supercooling: liquid persistence below the equilibrium freezing temperature. Deacclimation: decline of previously induced protective capacity.

Key sequences: heat → proteostasis and other signals → HSF → HSE-containing promoter → HSP → protein quality control; cold → signaling and multiple transcriptional inputs → CBF → CRT/DRE → subset of COR genes → protective products; extracellular ice → reduced external water potential → water loss → shrinkage and dehydration injury or tolerance.
