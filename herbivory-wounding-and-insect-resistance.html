1. Herbivory as a physiological problem

A leaf is simultaneously a photosynthetic surface, a hydraulic interface and a source of carbon and nitrogen for the rest of the plant. When a caterpillar removes a portion of that leaf, the damage is not limited to the missing tissue. Cells bordering the wound lose their membrane integrity, water and solutes escape, and surviving cells are exposed to substances that normally remain inside living cells. The plant also loses a portion of its future carbon assimilation. An effective response must protect the surviving tissue while maintaining sufficient metabolism for growth and reproduction.

Herbivory is the consumption of living plant material by animals. Insects differ enormously in the tissues they exploit and the mechanical and biochemical processes through which they feed. A chewing larva removes pieces of epidermis and mesophyll with mandibles. A piercing-sucking insect such as an aphid inserts slender stylets through tissues and may establish prolonged feeding from a phloem sieve element. A leaf miner consumes internal tissues while leaving portions of the outer epidermis intact. These are not merely differences in visible injury. Each route creates a different combination of cellular disruption, altered transport, insect-derived chemical inputs and changes in plant resource distribution.

A chewing insect can rapidly remove a large fraction of photosynthetic area. The surviving leaf may still have functional vascular connections, and some remaining tissue can increase photosynthetic activity if resource conditions permit. By contrast, a phloem feeder can divert sugars and amino acids without removing much leaf area. An apparently intact leaf is therefore not necessarily physiologically undamaged. In addition, certain piercing insects transmit plant viruses, creating a second, biologically distinct stress. A study that measures only missing leaf area will underestimate some forms of herbivore attack.

Plants cannot solve the immediate problem by moving away from the insect. They can, however, make a tissue difficult to locate, enter, digest or use for growth, and they can alter the behavior of organisms that attack the herbivore. They may also replace lost tissue or redirect resources so that reproductive performance remains relatively stable. The first group of effects constitutes resistance, whereas the ability to maintain fitness despite a given amount of damage is tolerance. A resistant plant experiences less damage or reduces herbivore success; a tolerant plant may still be eaten extensively.

Constitutive defenses exist before an attacker arrives. Induced defenses change in abundance, activity or distribution after a relevant cue. These categories are not mutually exclusive at the level of a molecule or structure. A leaf may already contain a basal concentration of a defensive metabolite and synthesize additional quantities after attack. Similarly, a genetically determined capacity to produce trichomes can be modified in newly developing leaves following damage. The timescale of the response is important: a structural hair cannot be erected instantaneously on an already mature leaf merely because a caterpillar has taken a bite.

The usefulness of induction arises partly from resource economics. A compound that is unnecessary in the absence of attack can consume carbon skeletons, ATP, reducing equivalents or nitrogen if produced continuously. However, waiting until tissue has been damaged may also be costly. Constitutive and inducible traits therefore solve different parts of the same problem. The balance depends on herbivore predictability, developmental stage, rate of attack, the vulnerability of particular organs and the capacity for recovery. An induced defense is not automatically less expensive when the plant spends much of its life under attack.

At the cellular level, a successful response does not mean that every defense-related gene increases. Rather, information about the disturbance is translated into a specific pattern of physiological changes. Wound signals, hormone pathways, biosynthesis of protective chemicals and resource allocation are connected parts of that response. The distinctions between damage, detection, signaling, resistance and tolerance will guide the subsequent analysis.

Feeding site and the type of physiological loss

To understand insect damage, it is useful to begin with the organization of the leaf rather than with the hormone pathway. The upper and lower epidermis protect the underlying photosynthetic tissue, regulate exchange with the atmosphere through stomata, and present a surface over which a feeding insect must move. A bite through this boundary exposes mesophyll cells. Ruptured cells cannot restore their own turgor because their semipermeable membrane no longer encloses the contents. Surviving cells surrounding them may retain their membranes but experience changes in hydration and the chemical composition of the apoplast. This distinction between dead cells and responding living cells is important: the wound signal is frequently produced by the destruction of some cells and interpreted by others that remain functional.

In a sieve tube, the immediate physical problem is different. Mature sieve elements form a pressure-driven transport system supplied and maintained by associated companion cells. A phloem-feeding insect can withdraw the transported solution from this system. The plant may attempt to restrict solute loss by occluding affected sieve elements, including callose deposition and, in suitable species, changes involving sieve-element proteins. The consequences depend on whether the obstruction is effective, how rapidly the insect establishes feeding, and how much of the network remains functional. A persistent block to transport can also interfere with the plant's own delivery of assimilates. An apparently protective response may therefore have a physiological cost even when the damaged site is small.

Other insect feeding modes produce different disruptions. Thrips commonly damage cells and consume their contents, so a relatively shallow feeding lesion may injure many cells without behaving exactly like a large caterpillar bite. Leaf miners remove internal tissue while preserving parts of the leaf surface; a patch that appears discolored or translucent may have lost functional photosynthetic machinery beneath the epidermis. A gall-forming insect can modify the development of the host tissue so that resources are redirected into a structure that supports the insect. Such a gall is not simply missing leaf area. It represents altered cellular growth and resource allocation. These examples explain why a single injury score cannot capture all dimensions of herbivore stress.

A photosynthetic penalty can be estimated initially by considering leaf area and assimilation per unit area. If all portions of a leaf originally assimilate at a uniform rate of 12 micromoles of carbon dioxide per square meter per second and the functional leaf area decreases from 0.020 to 0.015 square meters, whole-leaf net assimilation would fall from 0.24 to 0.18 micromoles per second under the uniform-rate assumption. The missing area is 25 percent, and the estimated decline in whole-leaf assimilation is also 25 percent. This calculation isolates geometry; it does not model the actual remaining tissue after injury. Stomatal changes, local damage, altered illumination and compensatory photosynthesis can cause the measured decline to differ from the estimate.

Suppose gas exchange measurements subsequently show that the remaining 0.015 square meters assimilates at 14 micromoles per square meter per second. Estimated whole-leaf assimilation is then 0.21 micromoles per second, or 87.5 percent of the original rate. Increased assimilation per remaining area offsets some, but not all, of the missing area. The example demonstrates the difference between a rate normalized by leaf area and the total carbon gain of an organ. It is hypothetical and neglects changes in respiration of other plant parts. Whole-plant growth cannot be predicted from this leaf measurement alone, since carbon supply, other source leaves, storage and sinks also contribute.

Herbivore pressure also differs across developmental stages. An early seedling may possess limited reserves and only a few leaves; losing one leaf can remove a large fraction of its current assimilatory capacity. A mature plant may have more source leaves and established stores, yet damage to a reproductive organ can directly reduce the number of potential seeds. A leaf feeding trial and a flower feeding trial therefore need not measure equivalent biological risks even if the dry mass of removed tissue is identical. Both the location of damage and the opportunity for subsequent development must be incorporated into the interpretation of plant performance.

In crop improvement, resistance is often evaluated relative to a particular herbivore and the developmental stage that is most vulnerable to attack. A trait effective against a first-instar chewing larva may not impede a larger insect whose mandibles and movement differ. A plant capable of recovering from early vegetative feeding may not compensate for later loss of reproductive structures. General physiological principles help explain these differences, but a resistance claim remains conditional on the organism, plant genotype and environment examined.

2. Mechanical injury and biological recognition

Consider two genetically identical plants. A hole is made in a leaf of the first plant with a sterile mechanical instrument, and a caterpillar removes approximately the same area from the second. Both treatments rupture cells, but the caterpillar introduces oral secretions, repeatedly bites tissue over time and can alter the composition of its secretions as it feeds. If defense gene expression differs between the two treatments, the observation raises a question: does the difference result from the insect's chemical contribution or from the physical pattern of injury? A properly designed experiment must separate these factors.

A damage-associated molecular pattern, abbreviated DAMP, is a molecule derived from the plant itself that becomes informative of injury or tissue disruption. Cell wall fragments and extracellularly released endogenous molecules can contribute to danger signaling. Their significance is not simply their chemical identity. Their appearance in an unusual extracellular compartment, concentration or context makes them informative. A cell wall fragment present after degradation does not convey the same information as an intact structural polysaccharide in a healthy wall.

Oligogalacturonides, fragments derived from pectin, illustrate the logic. Pectin contributes to the organization and mechanical properties of primary cell walls. Its breakdown can produce molecular fragments that stimulate defense-associated responses. The plant does not need to identify the animal species in order to detect that a protective boundary has been disturbed. Extracellular ATP and glutamate can also act as damage-related cues. These molecules have ordinary intracellular metabolic or signaling roles; a sudden extracellular appearance changes the biological context in which neighboring cells encounter them.

A herbivore-associated molecular pattern, abbreviated HAMP, originates from the herbivore or is generated in the context of its interaction with plant material. Examples include certain fatty acid–amino acid conjugates in insect oral secretions and particular herbivory-associated peptides. Volicitin, a fatty acid–amino acid conjugate originally characterized in caterpillar oral secretions, is a classic illustration of an elicitor associated with herbivore feeding. Its capacity to induce volatile responses depends on the plant and experimental context. It should not be presented as a universal signal recognized equally by every plant.

The contrast between DAMPs and HAMPs is based primarily on biological origin, not on the downstream response. Both can stimulate changes in cytosolic calcium, reactive oxygen species and hormone accumulation. Overlapping outputs do not erase the difference between their origins. Conversely, different HAMPs need not produce identical responses. A signal may be effective in one plant lineage but weak or undetectable in another because the necessary recognition system is absent or because other components modify the response.

Some insect-derived molecules reduce rather than stimulate particular plant defenses. Saliva is a complex biological secretion, and an herbivore that gains access to nutritious tissues can benefit from suppressing responses that would obstruct feeding. An oral secretion should therefore never be described as uniformly eliciting or uniformly inhibitory. The net response depends on the simultaneous actions of elicitors, suppressors, physical injury and the genetic composition of both organisms.

A useful four-treatment design includes intact leaves, mechanically wounded leaves with a matched buffer application, equally wounded leaves with oral secretion or a defined candidate elicitor, and intact leaves exposed to the same substance where feasible. The last treatment can help identify whether tissue disruption is required for access or responsiveness, but failure on intact tissue may also reflect poor penetration. Measurements at multiple times distinguish transient signals from later changes. Tissue damage, developmental stage and sampling location should be controlled independently of the candidate chemical. This design is more informative than comparing only an attacked plant with an untouched plant.

Evidence that plants distinguish feeding from simple injury

Early experiments on herbivory raised an important question: can plants identify a feature of insect feeding that is not provided by simple mechanical injury? Researchers studying caterpillar-induced odors in maize found that treating damaged tissue with material associated with insect oral secretions could reproduce important features of the volatile response. Subsequent chemical characterization identified volicitin in beet armyworm oral secretions. The compound contains a fatty acid-derived moiety linked to glutamine. Its activity made it possible to test a defined chemical contribution to the herbivory response rather than treating every component of a complex secretion as equally informative.

The chemical identity of volicitin is particularly instructive because its fatty acid portion can ultimately be related to plant material that the insect has consumed and modified. This illustrates why a herbivore-associated cue is not necessarily a completely foreign chemical element synthesized from unrelated ingredients. Biological origin is understood in the context of the interaction: modification during insect feeding creates a distinctive molecular feature encountered by the plant. The concept parallels the distinction between intact structural material and fragments generated by damage. Information resides in the molecular structure, compartment and biological circumstances of its appearance.

A persuasive elicitor experiment asks whether the candidate is sufficient to produce a defined response in a suitable test system. A defined substance added to equally wounded tissue can be compared with a matched solvent or buffer. If the candidate reproduces a response that was previously observed with oral secretion, it supports sufficiency under those conditions. To establish whether the compound is necessary for the response to complete secretion, one would need to reduce or remove that component without introducing unrelated changes, then examine whether restoring it also restores the response. A sufficiency experiment alone cannot establish necessity, especially when oral secretions contain multiple elicitors.

In cowpea and some other legumes, inceptin peptides illustrate a different form of herbivore-associated information. The relevant peptides arise through processing of plant-derived protein material during caterpillar digestion. They are returned to plant tissue during feeding and can be recognized by appropriate plant genotypes. This case reinforces two distinctions. First, the term HAMP is not limited to a molecule synthesized entirely by the insect. Second, the ability of a receptor to recognize a cue depends on the recipient plant. A positive result in a responsive legume cannot be generalized directly to maize or Arabidopsis.

Perception should be distinguished from downstream signaling through the design of genetic experiments. If a mutant does not respond to a candidate elicitor but also does not respond to mechanically released endogenous cues, the mutated component may participate in a signaling process shared by several perception routes. If the mutant responds normally to other cues but selectively loses responsiveness to the candidate, it becomes a stronger candidate for a specific recognition component. Restoration of the response by introducing a functional copy of the gene supports the connection. Direct physical interaction between a receptor and ligand supplies another kind of evidence, which is not automatically demonstrated by a genetic phenotype.

Dose–response analysis provides additional clues. At low doses of a candidate compound the response may be weak; at higher doses the response may approach a maximum. Saturation can reflect a limited number of receptors or capacity elsewhere in the signaling network, but it does not uniquely demonstrate direct receptor binding. A damaging high concentration may provoke responses through nonspecific tissue injury. Controls should therefore distinguish physiological concentrations from concentrations that independently injure tissue. A structurally related but biologically inactive compound can help test chemical selectivity without assuming that every negative result proves a particular binding mechanism.

Timing also influences the distinction between a wound response and herbivore recognition. A short mechanical stimulus can produce a rapid but transient calcium response, whereas repeated feeding can sustain or repeatedly trigger signaling. If the experimental goal is to compare the presence of a candidate elicitor at a fixed physical stimulus, the wound pattern, duration and delivery schedule must be matched. If the goal is instead to compare the total natural responses to a mechanical event and insect feeding, those differences are biologically relevant and should not be artificially removed. The appropriate control follows from the causal question being asked.

Oviposition provides a useful extension of the same recognition principle. In some plants, insect egg deposition or materials associated with eggs can induce local defense responses before the larvae begin feeding. Some responses alter the surface environment or the behavior of natural enemies. This is not the same as a response to the mechanical loss of leaf area. It demonstrates that plant recognition can act on several stages of an insect's association with a host, while also showing why insect resistance must be analyzed over a life cycle rather than only during a feeding trial.

3. Early calcium, electrical and redox signals

Living cells maintain gradients of ions across the plasma membrane and intracellular membranes. The cytosolic concentration of free calcium is normally held low relative to major extracellular and intracellular stores. An increase in calcium permeability can therefore produce a rapid change in cytosolic calcium without waiting for a new protein to be synthesized. Calcium-binding proteins then translate this change into altered enzymatic activity. The term calcium signature refers to the temporal and spatial features of the change rather than just its peak height.

A wound immediately changes the extracellular environment and mechanical conditions at the border of surviving tissue. Endogenous ligands, mechanically influenced channels and receptor-dependent signaling can contribute to ion movement. The electrical potential across the membrane also changes when charge-carrying ions move. Depolarization means that the membrane potential becomes less negative or more positive relative to its earlier value; it is not a synonym for calcium entry. Several ion species, including anions and potassium, can contribute to electrical changes. Pumps and transporters eventually help restore the gradients.

Plant glutamate receptor-like channels, commonly abbreviated GLRs, are particularly relevant to rapid wound signaling. In Arabidopsis, members including GLR3.3 and GLR3.6 contribute to the propagation of wound-induced signals. Glutamate released following injury can influence calcium-related signaling through these systems. This is a plant mechanism and must not be equated with an animal synapse. The name reflects molecular relationships and functional properties, not the existence of neurons in a plant.

Toyota and colleagues used calcium imaging in Arabidopsis to investigate rapid signaling from wounds to distant tissues. Genetically encoded fluorescent indicators allowed changes in intracellular calcium to be followed in living plants, while genetic perturbation of GLR-dependent signaling linked these proteins to the propagation process. The evidence connected extracellular glutamate with long-distance calcium-associated defense signaling. It did not establish that a single freely diffusing glutamate molecule travels to every remote leaf or that all forms of systemic wound response use only one route.

Electrical and calcium changes can interact with redox signaling. Respiratory burst oxidase homologs, plant NADPH oxidases located in the plasma membrane, can produce superoxide on the extracellular side of the membrane. Superoxide can be converted into hydrogen peroxide, which can influence additional signaling responses. Calcium-dependent regulation of oxidases and redox-dependent changes in channel activity can produce feedback. Such feedback is useful for amplification but requires regulation to avoid sustained cellular disturbance. ROS production is not always synonymous with oxidative injury; the amount, location and duration are decisive.

Rapid changes can precede substantial transcriptional reprogramming. A fluorescence response seconds or minutes after injury is therefore a plausible early marker, whereas accumulation of a protease inhibitor generally requires more time for changes in gene expression and protein synthesis. The temporal relationship supports a signaling hypothesis, but by itself it does not demonstrate causation. A second intervention is needed to test whether blocking the early process also changes the later response. A mutant with altered development may additionally differ in hormone production for indirect reasons, so genetic complementation and appropriately matched controls strengthen interpretation.

Rapid systemic communication is physiologically valuable because an insect that feeds on one leaf may subsequently move to another. Distal tissues can begin producing defensive outputs before they suffer equivalent local damage. However, distal responses depend on vascular connectivity, organ identity, stimulus intensity and the signaling competence of receiving tissues. A calcium signal detected in a remote leaf establishes a change in that leaf, not automatically the identity of all mobile molecules that contributed to it.

Reading the kinetics of a wound signal

An ion channel is a membrane protein that allows selected ions to move along an electrochemical gradient. The direction of a particular ion's movement depends on both the concentration difference and the electrical potential across the membrane. At room temperature, the Nernst equation for calcium can be written ECa = (RT/2F) ln(aout/ain), where ECa is the equilibrium potential, R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, F is the Faraday constant, and aout and ain are the activities of extracellular and cytosolic free calcium. The factor two represents the positive charge of calcium. If activities are approximated by concentrations, the equation provides a useful conceptual estimate.

For a hypothetical extracellular free calcium concentration of 1 millimolar and cytosolic free calcium of 0.1 micromolar, the ratio is ten thousand because one millimolar equals one thousand micromolar. At about 298 kelvin, RT/F is approximately 25.7 millivolts. The natural logarithm of ten thousand is approximately 9.21, yielding ECa ≈ 25.7 × 9.21/2 ≈ +118 millivolts, using the convention that membrane potential is inside relative to outside. If the cell membrane potential is negative, opening a calcium-permeable channel under these conditions strongly favors inward calcium movement. This is an idealized equilibrium calculation, not a measurement of actual calcium currents in a wounded leaf.

The Nernst potential is not the same as the measured membrane potential. A living plant cell contains many permeant ion species and actively maintains gradients through transporters and pumps. A wound may alter the conductances of calcium, potassium and anions as well as change proton pumping. Therefore, observing depolarization cannot identify a single responsible channel. To test whether a particular ion contributes to the electrical event, an experiment must combine electrical measurements with manipulations that address its movement or the activity of the relevant channel. Such evidence may still be complicated by compensatory changes in other conductances.

Calcium signals can be measured with fluorescent reporters whose emission changes with calcium binding. A reporter may provide excellent information about where and when a calcium-associated response occurs, but raw fluorescence also depends on reporter abundance, tissue thickness, optical properties, illumination and the chemical environment of the fluorophore. Normalizing to a prestimulus baseline is one way to reduce variation. If baseline fluorescence F0 is 100 arbitrary units and peak fluorescence F is 160, the relative increase (F − F0)/F0 is 0.60, or 60 percent. This calculation does not demonstrate that free calcium concentration rose by exactly 60 percent; a calibration model is required to convert reporter behavior into concentration.

A time-resolved image can distinguish a sequential propagation pattern from a simultaneous whole-plant response. If a reporter first increases near the wound and then in successively more distant connected tissue, the sequence is consistent with a spreading process. In an illustrative analysis, if the signal reaches a region 30 millimeters away after 45 seconds, and a region 10 millimeters away after 15 seconds, the apparent average speed between these two positions is (30 − 10)/(45 − 15), or approximately 0.67 millimeters per second. This estimate assumes that distance represents the actual propagation route and that timing has been measured using the same response threshold. Neither assumption is always satisfied by a simple straight-line measurement across a leaf.

When a signal passes through a vascular network, its path length may exceed the distance measured directly between two leaves. Different tissues may also require different local thresholds before a reporter becomes detectably active. Apparent propagation speed is therefore not necessarily the molecular velocity of a transported ligand. A self-propagating process can travel faster than an individual molecule diffuses over the entire distance if successive cells regenerate the signal. Conversely, fluid transport can move a compound between organs without requiring a similar activation event in every intervening cell. The experimental distinction requires spatial observations and selective perturbations rather than speed alone.

Calcium-dependent protein kinases can provide a biochemical bridge between an ion signal and later protein activity. Binding of calcium alters the regulation of these kinases, allowing phosphorylation of suitable substrates. Calcium-binding proteins such as calmodulin also relay information to other components. The specificity of the response arises from the combination of the calcium pattern, local concentrations of decoder proteins, availability of substrates and simultaneous signals. The same increase in cytosolic calcium could therefore contribute to different responses in epidermis, mesophyll and vascular-associated tissue.

A rapid ROS increase can be interpreted in the same framework. The signal may help activate neighboring cells or modulate cellular proteins, while prolonged accumulation can damage cellular components. Measurements of a fluorescent oxidation reporter should not be treated as an unambiguous chemical identification of every reactive species. A complementary enzyme or genetic intervention and suitable controls strengthen a mechanistic conclusion. The antioxidant system described in the oxidative stress chapter contributes to limiting the duration and consequences of the response; here its importance is to preserve the distinction between signaling and injury during herbivory.

4. From membrane lipids to bioactive jasmonate

Jasmonates are a family of lipid-derived plant regulators involved in development and responses to injury and biological attack. Jasmonic acid, abbreviated JA, is one member of this family. Jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine, abbreviated JA-Ile, is an important bioactive conjugate in the canonical COI1–JAZ signaling pathway. The distinction matters because a chemical measurement of total JA is not equivalent to a measurement of receptor-active JA-Ile. A plant can accumulate the precursor hormone but remain defective in its conjugation or perception.

The canonical biosynthetic route begins with alpha-linolenic acid, an eighteen-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acid abundant in chloroplast membrane lipids. Mobilization of fatty acid substrates and subsequent enzymatic oxygenation connect the state of cellular membranes with oxylipin production. Lipoxygenase acts on the fatty acid to generate a hydroperoxide; for the jasmonate route, a 13-lipoxygenase supplies the relevant oxygenated intermediate. Allene oxide synthase and allene oxide cyclase then produce 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, commonly abbreviated OPDA. This cyclic compound retains the eighteen-carbon skeleton.

OPDA and related intermediates undergo further processing involving the peroxisome. In the canonical pathway, OPDA reductase and three cycles of beta-oxidation lead to jasmonic acid formation. Beta-oxidation shortens an acyl chain by two carbon atoms per cycle, accounting for the transition from an eighteen-carbon precursor to the twelve-carbon jasmonate skeleton. The simplified sequence illustrates the cooperation between chloroplast and peroxisomal metabolism. It is not an assertion that all jasmonate production in every species proceeds exclusively through a single reductase or an identical transport route.

JA can subsequently be joined to the amino acid L-isoleucine through the activity of JAR1 and related conjugating capacity. Formation of the conjugate changes the molecular features recognized by the canonical jasmonate perception machinery. JA can also enter other metabolic routes, including methylation and hydroxylation. Methyl jasmonate is an important biologically active treatment and volatile derivative, but the effects of applying it often involve metabolic conversion. It should not be substituted for JA-Ile when describing ligand-dependent interaction of COI1 with JAZ proteins.

A pathway diagram is best read in terms of compartment, substrate and function. The chloroplast converts a membrane-derived fatty acid into an oxylipin intermediate; the peroxisomal reactions transform and shorten the carbon skeleton; conjugation produces a signal capable of promoting the canonical receptor complex. Inhibition at an early step affects multiple downstream compounds. A lesion in JAR1 primarily changes the availability of the conjugated ligand, whereas a lesion in COI1 affects perception. These defects can generate superficially similar defense phenotypes through different biochemical causes.

OPDA is not merely an inert intermediate. It can contribute to signaling responses that are not completely explained by JA-Ile-dependent perception. This provides an important limitation to simple mutant rescue experiments. Supplying JA-Ile to a biosynthesis mutant may restore a particular COI1-dependent output but need not recreate every biological consequence of restoring the entire oxylipin pathway. The experimental question must therefore specify the measured endpoint, rather than treating all wound responses as identical.

The concentration of an active hormone is dynamic. Synthesis, transport, conjugation, oxidation, hydrolysis and other inactivation mechanisms can change its abundance. A short-lived pulse can trigger defense gene expression and then diminish as the metabolic and regulatory systems reset. Sustained accumulation is not necessarily more useful, since excessive or prolonged defense signaling may restrict growth or alter reproduction. A complete account of the pathway includes both the production of the ligand and the mechanisms that limit the duration of its action.

Biochemical logic of the octadecanoid pathway

The membrane origin of the jasmonate pathway explains why tissue integrity and signal production are closely connected. Alpha-linolenic acid is an eighteen-carbon fatty acid with three double bonds. Its unsaturated structure provides a substrate for lipoxygenase-mediated oxygenation. A lipoxygenase does not simply remove the fatty acid from the membrane; it introduces molecular oxygen at a position determined by enzyme selectivity. Plants possess different lipoxygenases and routes for processing oxygenated fatty acids. As a consequence, not all products of fatty acid oxygenation lead to jasmonic acid.

In the canonical jasmonate branch, the hydroperoxide produced by 13-lipoxygenase is converted by allene oxide synthase to an unstable intermediate. Allene oxide cyclase promotes formation of the cyclic OPDA product. Cyclization creates a molecular framework that distinguishes this branch from alternative fates of the hydroperoxide. The hydroperoxide lyase branch, for example, can contribute to green leaf volatile production from oxygenated lipid substrates. This provides a biochemical reason why lipid disruption can lead to both intracellular hormone production and rapidly emitted odors without requiring the volatile to be the final product of JA-Ile synthesis.

The peroxisomal part of the canonical pathway illustrates an application of fatty acid metabolism. In general, beta-oxidation shortens a fatty acyl chain by repeated removal of two-carbon units. In jasmonate biosynthesis the pathway acts on appropriate intermediates derived from OPDA, producing a shorter cyclic molecule with a side chain suitable for JA formation. Tracking the carbon skeleton is useful in examinations because it prevents confusion between the eighteen-carbon starting substrate and the twelve-carbon JA product. The addition of isoleucine during conjugation creates a different molecule; the twelve-carbon label refers to the JA moiety, not the total carbon count of JA-Ile.

The reaction catalyzed by JAR1 connects hormone metabolism with amino acid metabolism. It is a conjugation reaction requiring energy and the availability of the amino acid substrate. The product has physicochemical and recognition properties distinct from free JA. A plant that accumulates JA but cannot efficiently conjugate it may therefore show high total jasmonate content and a relatively weak canonical receptor response. Such a phenotype is not paradoxical once the measured chemical species is specified. Conversely, a reduction in JA-Ile cannot be attributed to a conjugation defect without considering reduced precursor production or increased metabolism of the conjugate.

A chromatographic measurement coupled to mass spectrometry can separate and quantify different jasmonates. Conceptually, the analytical separation reduces the possibility that free JA, OPDA and JA-Ile are all measured as the same quantity. Suitable standards, normalization to tissue mass and attention to recovery during extraction are important for quantitative comparison. Sampling after wounding must also be rapid and consistent because active hormone levels may change substantially within a short interval. A whole-leaf extract averages injured and uninjured cells and may conceal particularly high local concentrations at the wound margin.

Consider a hypothetical time course of free JA and JA-Ile in wild type and a conjugation-defective mutant. In relative units, free JA increases from 1 to 12 in wild type and from 1 to 15 in the mutant after injury, while JA-Ile increases from 0.2 to 5 in wild type but only from 0.2 to 0.5 in the mutant. The pattern is consistent with impaired conversion to the conjugate, but an increase in JA-Ile degradation could produce a similar pattern. Measuring the disappearance of an accessible labeled conjugate or examining the activity and genotype of the conjugating enzyme would help discriminate these explanations.

Hormone metabolism also provides a mechanism for temporal separation between the stimulus and response. The production of JA-Ile after injury changes the receptor-active pool, while subsequent inactivation can return that pool toward its basal level. Transcriptional changes induced by the earlier pulse may persist after hormone concentration has fallen, since the newly produced RNA and proteins have their own lifetimes. As a result, a late sample with low JA-Ile and high protease inhibitor activity does not invalidate a jasmonate-dependent mechanism. The earlier hormone pulse may have initiated the later output.

A hormone concentration expressed per gram of fresh tissue can be altered by changes in tissue water content. A wounded tissue that loses water may show an apparent increase in concentration per fresh mass even if the absolute amount does not increase proportionally. Measuring water content or using an appropriate alternative normalization can help interpret such data. This consideration connects wound physiology to the quantitative principles used in drought studies. It also illustrates why precise chemical analysis must be paired with careful biological sampling.

5. The COI1–JAZ–MYC molecular switch

Gene expression requires a regulatory system capable of preventing expensive responses when they are unnecessary and allowing them when a threat is detected. In the canonical jasmonate pathway, a central feature of this control is regulated removal of transcriptional repression. In the resting state, members of the JASMONATE ZIM-domain family, abbreviated JAZ, interact with transcription factors such as MYC2 and help inhibit their activation of particular target genes. Other proteins can participate in repression complexes. The amount and activity of a JAZ protein are therefore important determinants of pathway output.

COI1, CORONATINE INSENSITIVE1, is an F-box protein associated with an SCF-type E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. The F-box component contributes substrate recognition, while the larger E3 ligase machinery facilitates transfer of ubiquitin to appropriate targets. This is distinct from the proteasome itself. The E3 complex marks proteins for degradation; the 26S proteasome performs regulated proteolysis of many ubiquitinated proteins.

In the presence of the relevant JA-Ile stereoisomer, interaction between COI1 and a susceptible JAZ protein is promoted. The ligand-dependent COI1–JAZ interaction brings the repressor into the substrate-recognition machinery. JAZ proteins become ubiquitinated and undergo proteasome-dependent degradation. The transcription factors previously restrained by these repressors can then act more effectively on appropriate regulatory DNA sequences, depending on the availability of other components and tissue-specific conditions.

The direction of causation is easy to reverse accidentally. The receptor system does not destroy MYC2 to turn on MYC-dependent transcription. It removes the JAZ-mediated restraint. Nor does JA-Ile act as a transcription factor that directly binds defense-gene promoters. Its principal role in this core pathway is to promote a molecular interaction that changes the stability of a regulatory protein. The final increase in transcription is a downstream consequence.

In 2007, studies by Thines and colleagues and by Chini and colleagues established key elements of this model. Evidence linking JA-Ile to COI1–JAZ association and the stability of JAZ repressors offered a mechanistic explanation for earlier genetic observations of jasmonate-insensitive plants. When the ligand-dependent degradation system is disrupted, a repressor may persist even when a stimulus raises hormone concentration. This distinguishes hormonal abundance from hormonal responsiveness.

Consider a mutant that produces normal JA-Ile after wounding but whose JAZ protein lacks a functional degradation signal. If that altered repressor still binds a relevant MYC transcription factor, the expected result is reduced activation of MYC-dependent target genes despite normal ligand accumulation. Increasing external JA-Ile cannot necessarily bypass this stable repressor. In contrast, a mutant deficient in the production of JA-Ile but with normal receptor machinery could respond to an accessible supply of active ligand. The comparison is an example of using perturbation and rescue to locate a defect within a pathway.

MYC2 is a useful representative transcription factor, but it is not the only jasmonate-responsive transcriptional regulator. MYC3 and MYC4, among others, can have partially overlapping functions, and different branches of the signaling network may produce different outputs. Therefore, residual jasmonate responses in a single myc2 mutant do not disprove the model. Redundancy, alternative transcriptional regulators and tissue specificity can explain partial phenotypes.

The pathway also contains negative feedback. Jasmonate-dependent transcription may induce genes encoding additional JAZ repressors, increasing the capacity for repression after the hormone signal diminishes. Active ligand metabolism further limits continued stimulation of the receptor system. These processes permit a rapid defense response without obligating the plant to maintain maximum defense throughout subsequent growth. A decline in JAZ abundance early in a time course and an increase in JAZ transcript later in the same experiment are consequently not contradictory observations.

Genetic evidence and the dynamics of repressor turnover

The behavior of an F-box protein becomes easier to understand by separating substrate recognition from the chemistry of ubiquitin attachment. Eukaryotic ubiquitination employs an E1 activating enzyme, E2 conjugating enzyme and an E3 ligase that helps specify the substrate and facilitate ubiquitin transfer. An SCF complex contains SKP1-related, CULLIN and F-box components, together with associated machinery that supports ligase activity. In jasmonate signaling, the COI1-containing complex recognizes appropriate JAZ substrates in a ligand-dependent manner. A general defect in ubiquitination may affect many cellular processes, whereas a selective defect in COI1 can preferentially affect pathways that depend on that substrate-recognition system.

JAZ proteins include domains that participate in interactions with transcription factors and the hormone-dependent receptor complex. A mutation in a region required for COI1-dependent recognition can stabilize a JAZ protein without necessarily eliminating all of its interactions with other regulatory proteins. The resulting phenotype can be dominant if the altered repressor continues to inhibit transcription factors despite the presence of functional endogenous JAZ proteins that undergo normal degradation. Dominant stability defects are particularly informative because they reveal that a regulated inhibitory component can block a signal even when upstream production and perception components are present.

A simple kinetic model can clarify the relationship between synthesis and degradation. Let P be the abundance of a representative JAZ protein, expressed in arbitrary protein units, s its rate of synthesis in units per minute, and k its effective first-order degradation constant in inverse minutes. Under a simplified constant-rate approximation, dP/dt = s − kP. If synthesis and degradation remain constant long enough, the steady-state abundance is Pss = s/k. The equation assumes a homogeneous protein pool, a constant synthesis rate and a degradation process whose rate is proportional to abundance. Real jasmonate responses violate these assumptions during dynamic hormone pulses, but the model provides a useful starting point for interpreting turnover.

Suppose an unstimulated cell has s = 2 units per minute and k = 0.1 per minute, giving a steady-state abundance of 20 units. If JA-Ile transiently increases the effective degradation constant to 0.4 per minute while synthesis initially remains at 2, the new steady-state value would be 5 units if the conditions were maintained. The protein pool will tend to decline toward that value rather than instantaneously jumping from 20 to 5. Later induction of JAZ transcription could increase synthesis and change the trajectory again. The calculation explains how both increased JAZ degradation and increased JAZ gene expression can contribute to the same time-dependent response.

Protein abundance can be assessed using immunodetection or a tagged reporter, whereas transcription is assessed using RNA measurements or promoter reporters. These techniques answer different questions. A promoter reporter may increase because jasmonate activates transcription of the JAZ gene. A protein fusion may decrease because the encoded protein is being degraded. A fusion that alters protein stability or localization could distort interpretation, so evidence from a functional fusion and appropriate controls is preferable. Measuring both readouts is particularly helpful when the pathway is regulated at more than one level.

To test dependence on the proteasome, an experiment can compare a tagged JAZ protein after hormone application with and without an intervention that reduces proteasome-dependent degradation. Persistence of the reporter under the intervention is consistent with the expected route of turnover. However, broad proteasome inhibition also alters many other proteins and stress responses. A conclusion about specificity requires complementary evidence such as ligand-dependent interaction with COI1, loss of degradation in a suitable receptor mutant and altered stability of a JAZ variant. The classical model gains strength through agreement between biochemical and genetic results rather than relying on one nonspecific inhibitor.

A molecular interaction assay supplies evidence that a ligand promotes association between proteins, but this alone does not establish the full physiological response. In an intact cell, protein expression, localization, degradation machinery and transcriptional targets all contribute to the final output. The original work on JAZ proteins connected several of these levels. Genetic studies showed that altered repressors changed jasmonate responsiveness, while biochemical evidence linked active hormone to association with COI1. This combination provides a model for causal reasoning: molecular interaction explains the regulatory step, and genetic perturbation tests whether that step is functionally important.

The pathway can also be compared with auxin signaling from the plant hormones subunit. In both systems, a small molecule promotes association between an F-box-containing recognition component and a repressor that is subsequently degraded, allowing a transcriptional response. In the auxin model, TIR1/AFB proteins and Aux/IAA repressors are relevant; in the canonical jasmonate model, COI1 and JAZ repressors are involved. The shared principle is regulated de-repression through protein turnover, not interchangeability of receptor, hormone or downstream transcription factor. This comparison is useful for Part B recognition questions and Part C predictions about stabilized negative regulators.

The canonical COI1–JAZ system has biological functions outside insect resistance, including important developmental roles. A mutant defective in this system may alter fertility, growth or morphology independently of an acute feeding treatment. Therefore, when comparing herbivore performance on mutant and wild-type plants, developmental state should be matched as carefully as possible. A complementation experiment helps establish whether the phenotype is associated with the gene, and acute treatment or tissue-specific perturbation may help separate developmental effects from immediate defense mechanisms. The caution does not weaken the genetic model; it identifies the additional evidence needed to assign a particular whole-organism outcome to that model.

6. Hormonal context: salicylic acid and ethylene

The dominant signaling pathway in a particular interaction depends on the type of attack and the plant's physiological context. Jasmonate responses are strongly associated with defense against many chewing herbivores and necrotrophic pathogens. Salicylic acid, abbreviated SA, is important in many responses to biotrophic pathogens and is also implicated in interactions with certain piercing-sucking insects. These associations are useful patterns, not universal rules for assigning attackers to single hormones.

Aphids feed in a manner that can cause relatively limited rupture of cells during stylet penetration compared with a leaf-chewing larva. Their prolonged interaction with the phloem, saliva and other tissues can induce a different set of responses. SA-related markers may increase, JA-associated responses may also occur, and the direction of regulation varies among aphid species, host plants and conditions. Measuring the expression of one marker does not establish that other hormone pathways are inactive or that the marker alone causes resistance.

JA and SA pathways can interact antagonistically under some conditions. Activation of an SA-dependent program may reduce expression of particular JA-regulated genes, and a pathogen or herbivore may exploit this interaction. Yet antagonism is not obligatory at every dose, time or tissue. Some defense responses require overlapping signals or show additive or synergistic effects. A question stating that SA always suppresses every JA response would therefore be false even though antagonistic cross-talk is an important examination concept.

Ethylene is a gaseous plant regulator that modifies the interpretation and output of other signaling pathways. In some contexts, jasmonate and ethylene cooperate in defense transcriptional responses, including an ERF-related branch associated with certain necrotrophic pathogens. The MYC-associated branch is especially relevant to many wound and herbivore defenses. These branches should not be reduced to a fixed binary switch in every tissue. Their relative contribution depends on transcriptional regulators, hormone levels and the biological challenge.

A useful experimental approach is a factorial design in which plants receive neither hormone, JA alone, SA alone or both. A defense output is measured for each treatment and compared with appropriate solvent controls. If JA alone raises the output substantially and the combined treatment produces a lower output, the data support antagonism for that endpoint under those conditions. They do not by themselves identify the molecular step at which the interaction occurs. Genetic perturbation of perception and downstream regulators can help localize it.

Cross-talk matters especially when a plant experiences multiple stressors. Herbivory during drought may occur in a plant already altering stomatal conductance, photosynthesis and carbon allocation through ABA-related processes. The physiological resources available for inducible resistance and tolerance can differ from those in a well-watered plant. Predicting the combined outcome from a single hormone concentration is unreliable. The integrated analysis of systemic and combined stresses belongs to the subsequent chapter, whereas the essential lesson here is that the jasmonate switch operates within a regulatory network.

Distinguishing pathway activity from defense effectiveness

Hormonal cross-talk can be understood at several levels. A signal can change the synthesis or inactivation of another hormone, alter the abundance of its receptor or influence downstream transcriptional regulation. These mechanisms can all reduce the expression of a target gene, yet they make different predictions about hormone concentrations and responses to externally supplied ligand. For example, a reduction in JA-Ile production may be bypassed for some outputs by providing active ligand, whereas repression of the relevant transcriptional response downstream of COI1 may persist. Measuring only one defense transcript cannot discriminate these mechanisms. Measuring ligand abundance, repressor turnover and downstream transcription in the same experimental series is more informative.

Consider a hypothetical four-treatment experiment in which the abundance of a MYC-dependent marker is expressed relative to an untreated control. The control value is 1, JA treatment gives 9, SA treatment gives 2, and the combination gives 4. The effect of JA in the absence of SA is 9 − 1 = 8 relative units; the effect of JA in the presence of SA is 4 − 2 = 2 units. The difference between these treatment effects is 2 − 8 = −6 units. This negative interaction on the chosen additive scale is compatible with suppression of the JA-related output by SA under these conditions. It does not show that SA reduces every jasmonate-related output, nor does it identify whether the interaction occurs before or after JA-Ile perception.

A second marker in the same tissue could behave differently. Suppose a transcript involved in another stress response shows values of 1, 3, 4 and 8 in the four treatments. The effect of JA is then 2 units without SA and 4 units with SA. On this scale the interaction is positive. The two results are not inherently contradictory because distinct target genes can have different regulatory inputs. The chosen scale matters: changes analyzed on an additive scale and changes analyzed as fold ratios are not always described by the same mathematical interaction. At NET level, the key is to use the comparison specified by the experimental question and avoid claiming a pathway-wide conclusion from one marker.

A marker associated with a defense pathway is not necessarily the direct cause of reduced herbivore performance. The marker could be a transcription factor, a signaling component, a protective enzyme or a transcript that merely changes in parallel with the true effector. Functional evidence requires an intervention that changes the relevant output and a biological test showing a corresponding effect on the herbivore or plant. A useful example is the distinction between a JA-responsive gene and an enzyme involved in production of a deterrent. High expression of the former indicates pathway activity, whereas activity and product measurements for the latter can help explain how the response modifies feeding.

Cross-talk can also influence the time at which protection becomes effective. Preformed chemical defenses are available immediately, while new transcription and biosynthesis may require time. A hormone treatment that raises defense gene expression shortly before a feeding trial may not allow sufficient time for accumulation of an effective product. Conversely, a product synthesized after a transient hormone pulse may persist after the corresponding transcript has declined. Sampling hormone, transcript, product and insect performance at the same arbitrary late time can obscure the causal sequence. Selecting time points on the basis of the expected biological process makes an experimental interpretation more defensible.

7. Physical and chemical defenses against insects

A physical defense can reduce the probability that an insect reaches or successfully exploits a plant tissue. Spines and thorns can obstruct feeding by large herbivores, while surface hairs, tissue toughness and cuticular properties are particularly relevant to many insects. In botanical terminology, a thorn is a modified shoot, a spine may be derived from a leaf or part of a leaf, and a prickle is an outgrowth of superficial tissues. These developmental distinctions are separate from the shared defensive function of mechanical obstruction.

Trichomes are epidermal outgrowths with diverse shapes and functions. Non-glandular trichomes can form barriers that impede the movement, attachment or feeding of small insects. Glandular trichomes can store and release substances that repel, immobilize or otherwise impair particular herbivores. Hair density, dimensions, spacing and secretion chemistry matter more than the mere presence of a trichome. An insect adapted to move among the hairs may be less affected than an insect whose body size or behavior makes the same surface difficult to navigate.

The distinction between antixenosis and antibiosis is useful in crop resistance studies. Antixenosis refers to plant traits that reduce insect preference or acceptance, such as reduced settlement or oviposition. Antibiosis refers to traits that reduce insect survival, growth or reproduction after interaction with the plant. A plant compound may contribute to both. For example, a bitter or otherwise aversive compound can reduce feeding preference, and a compound that interferes with metabolism after ingestion can reduce larval growth. Choice and no-choice experiments measure different aspects of these effects.

Protease inhibitors are proteins that can reduce the activity of digestive proteases in the herbivore gut. The insect consumes plant proteins but must hydrolyze them into smaller peptides and amino acids to obtain usable nitrogen. If a plant-derived inhibitor binds an important digestive protease, the rate of protein digestion may decrease. Larvae may respond by eating more tissue, producing more digestive enzymes, expressing inhibitor-insensitive isoforms or altering the composition of their digestive system. Consequently, reduced insect growth and reduced plant tissue loss do not necessarily occur together.

Different classes of protease inhibitors act on different enzyme classes. A serine protease inhibitor that affects a trypsin-like enzyme cannot be assumed to inhibit all cysteine proteases. Inhibition measured against a purified enzyme is also not sufficient to establish efficacy in an intact insect. Gut pH, stability of the inhibitor, concentrations reached in the diet and compensatory responses all influence the outcome. Comparing enzyme activity, food consumption, larval growth and plant damage provides a more complete physiological picture.

Specialized metabolites supply another extensive class of chemical defenses. Glucosinolates in Brassicales are often stored separately from enzymes capable of hydrolyzing them. Tissue damage can bring glucosinolates into contact with myrosinases, producing reactive products whose identity depends on the substrate and reaction context. This arrangement allows a comparatively stable precursor to be converted into a more reactive defense product when tissue is disrupted. Some specialist insects can modify or exploit this chemistry, illustrating that a defense effective against one herbivore may be ineffective against another.

Alkaloids, terpenoids and phenolic compounds can also alter herbivore behavior or physiology. Nicotine is a nitrogen-containing alkaloid associated with tobacco defense and acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in susceptible animals. Phenolic tannins can interact with proteins and change nutritional or digestive conditions, although their effect depends strongly on the herbivore gut environment and chemical structure. Terpenoid defenses can have toxic, repellent or signaling functions. These are examples rather than universal properties of every member of each chemical class.

The production and deployment of defensive molecules connect secondary metabolism to whole-plant physiology. A root can contribute to production of compounds accumulated in leaves, and an induced change in one tissue can alter transport and substrate demands elsewhere. Nitrogen-rich defenses draw on assimilated nitrogen, while many carbon-rich defenses require carbon skeletons and reducing power. The earlier secondary-metabolite chapters explain the main biosynthetic families; here the focus is how their products modify the plant–herbivore interaction.

Wounds also expose the plant to desiccation and opportunistic infection. Local reinforcement and sealing help restrict leakage and protect surviving tissue, although the appropriate response varies among tissues and plants. A wound response that improves physical closure need not produce resistance against the same insect that caused the injury. The endpoint must be specified: containment of injury, prevention of future feeding, reduction of insect performance or maintenance of plant fitness are related but distinct biological outcomes.

Digestive efficiency, sequestration and counteradaptation

When a caterpillar consumes leaf tissue, its growth depends on several successive processes. It must ingest material, release usable nutrients through digestion, absorb them across the gut and allocate absorbed nutrients between maintenance and production of new tissue. A plant defense can interfere with any of these processes. A tough tissue may reduce the rate of ingestion. A digestive inhibitor may reduce the proportion of consumed nutrients released in an absorbable form. A toxic metabolite may increase the energy demand for detoxification or disrupt essential cellular functions. The final insect growth rate integrates all these effects, so it cannot identify the affected step by itself.

A convenient quantitative analysis distinguishes the dry mass ingested I, dry mass of fecal material E and change in insect dry mass G, all measured over a specified interval. Apparent digestibility is calculated as 100(I − E)/I, efficiency of conversion of ingested food as 100G/I, and efficiency of conversion of digested food as 100G/(I − E). These are operational ratios and not direct measurements of every metabolic process. In particular, fecal material may contain substances derived from the insect, and the apparent digested amount does not account separately for all absorption and excretion processes. The ratios are useful for controlled comparisons on a consistent dry-mass basis.

Suppose a control insect consumes 100 mg of dry food, produces 30 mg of dry fecal material and gains 21 mg of dry mass. Apparent digestibility is 70 percent, conversion of ingested food is 21 percent and conversion of apparently digested food is 30 percent. An insect exposed to a defensive compound consumes 100 mg, produces 50 mg of fecal material and gains 15 mg. The three quantities are 50, 15 and 30 percent, respectively. The identical conversion efficiency of apparently digested food, accompanied by lower apparent digestibility, is consistent with a difference arising primarily at the stage represented by apparent digestion in this illustrative dataset. Other physiological evidence would be necessary to assign the difference to a specific digestive enzyme.

In a contrasting dataset, a treated insect consumes 100 mg, excretes 30 mg and gains 7 mg. Apparent digestibility remains 70 percent, but conversion of digested food declines to 10 percent. This pattern would direct attention toward processes after digestion or additional metabolic costs. It could be consistent with energy expenditure on maintenance or detoxification, but the ratios cannot distinguish those processes without other measurements. The important distinction is that similar reductions in body mass can arise through different physiological routes.

Specialist insects may tolerate or exploit defenses that are strongly inhibitory to generalist insects. Their adaptations may include enzymes that modify defensive metabolites, reduced sensitivity of a molecular target, transport mechanisms that alter tissue exposure or behavior that limits contact with the compound. Some insects can sequester plant-derived chemicals in their own tissues and use them in defense against predators. Sequestration is different from detoxification in its simplest sense: rather than merely eliminating an active compound, the insect retains it in a controlled biological context. Neither adaptation implies that the compound is harmless to all insects.

The glucosinolate–myrosinase system illustrates the importance of reaction products and spatial separation. Measuring the amount of intact glucosinolate alone may not predict the effective dose of active breakdown products experienced by an insect. An altered plant that synthesizes normal quantities of glucosinolate but lacks relevant hydrolytic activity may generate a different mixture after tissue damage. Conversely, an insect can modify the consequences of hydrolysis through its own physiology. A convincing mechanistic test therefore relates precursor concentration, enzyme activity, product formation and insect performance rather than assuming that one chemical measurement is sufficient.

Insects can also modify the pattern of mechanical damage before ingestion. Some feeding behaviors reduce contact with defensive secretions by interrupting their delivery to the feeding site. The biological logic is that a plant defense may depend not only on chemical synthesis but on the location and movement of the defensive material. If that delivery system is bypassed, the compound's intrinsic toxicity does not guarantee effective resistance. This illustrates why comparing detached tissue assays with intact-plant feeding can be useful: detachment can alter turgor, transport and secretion, potentially changing the phenotype under observation.

Direct defenses can be combined with management or crop improvement strategies, but an individual resistance trait must be evaluated in its ecological and agronomic context. Increased defensive chemistry could influence non-target interactions, plant growth or quality of harvested tissues. A trait that strongly impairs one insect may exert selection favoring individuals able to tolerate it. Diverse mechanisms and appropriate monitoring can help maintain efficacy, although this chapter does not develop the operational details of pest control. The examination-relevant principle is that plant resistance is a biological interaction, not an intrinsic numerical property of a compound independent of the consumer.

8. Indirect defense and three trophic levels

Plants can influence their attackers through effects on the herbivore's enemies. A predator consumes herbivores; a parasitoid develops in or on a host individual and generally kills that host as development proceeds. When plant traits increase the probability that predators or parasitoids find and attack herbivores, the resulting effect may contribute to indirect defense. This mechanism involves at least three interacting trophic levels: the plant, its herbivore and the natural enemy.

Herbivore-induced plant volatiles, often abbreviated HIPVs, are airborne compounds whose emission changes following herbivore attack. Some arise rapidly from disruption of lipid-containing tissues, while others are synthesized through regulated metabolic pathways after signaling. Green leaf volatiles, terpenoids and other volatile substances can contribute to a blend. Insects can respond to blend composition, relative abundance, temporal pattern and the surrounding background odor. Thus, detecting one volatile in a sample does not establish that it is the active attractant.

In classical experiments, Turlings, Tumlinson and Lewis investigated how parasitoid wasps exploited odors associated with herbivore-damaged plants. The evidence demonstrated that plant-produced odors can provide information that helps a parasitoid locate hosts. An important distinction is that the wasp's orientation behavior establishes attraction or information use under the conditions of the experiment; it does not independently establish how much plant seed production improves in a natural population.

A Y-tube olfactometer is a conceptual method for separating odor preference from contact effects. Clean airflow carries one odor treatment into one arm and another treatment into the opposite arm. The insect's choice is recorded without allowing direct contact with the plant. Randomizing arm positions, controlling airflow and light, and preventing residual odors are important controls. Independent insects and plant odor sources provide replication. If a wasp prefers an attacked plant over an intact one, the result indicates that an attack-associated odor blend is informative to that animal.

To identify an indirect defensive benefit, further experiments can compare plants differing in volatile emission under conditions with and without natural enemies. If a high-emission plant suffers less damage only when the enemy has access, and if other direct defenses are matched, the pattern supports an enemy-mediated contribution. Field conditions add wind, competing odor sources, plant community composition and varying enemy abundance. A compound that attracts an enemy in a laboratory may have a different net effect in the field.

Indirect defense is not restricted to volatiles. Extrafloral nectar can supply food to ants or other natural enemies, and some plants possess structures that provide shelter to protective organisms. These relationships are ecologically contingent. Ants may also protect sap-feeding insects that produce honeydew, potentially worsening plant injury. The presence of a natural enemy or a mutualistic partner must therefore be evaluated in terms of its actual effect on herbivory and plant performance.

Volatiles can also influence responses in other plant tissues or nearby plants. Such effects are not equivalent to animal-style communication with conscious intention. Airborne chemicals can act as cues that modify recipient physiology, and the magnitude of the response depends on dose, sensitivity and context. Distinguish the volatile's effect on a receiving plant from its attraction of predators or parasitoids. These are different experimental questions even if the emitted compound is the same.

Linking volatile emission, behavior and plant outcomes

Volatile measurements begin by distinguishing emission from concentration inside a leaf. A molecule can accumulate in tissue without being released into the surrounding air at the same rate. Emission depends on synthesis, storage, physical properties of the compound and the environmental conditions that influence its movement. A headspace sample reflects compounds that reach the sampled air during a specified interval. Comparison of samples requires attention to airflow, collection time, plant size and the presence of background volatile sources. A larger plant may release more total material while emitting less per unit leaf area.

As an illustrative calculation, a chamber collection over 30 minutes yields 120 nanograms of a compound from a plant with 60 square centimeters of leaf area. If recovery is assumed complete, the estimated emission rate is 120 divided by 30, or 4 nanograms per minute, equivalent to approximately 0.067 nanograms per square centimeter per minute. A second plant releases 180 nanograms over the same time but has 120 square centimeters of leaf area, yielding 6 nanograms per minute or 0.050 nanograms per square centimeter per minute. The second plant has the higher total emission but the lower area-normalized rate. Both descriptions are valid; they answer different questions about the source of odor and the physiological investment per area.

The receiver does not necessarily respond in direct proportion to the total amount of a single compound. A parasitoid may discriminate mixtures through sensory mechanisms that respond to component ratios, concentrations or the presence of particular compounds against a background. Replacing a complete plant odor blend with one abundant molecule can therefore produce a weak response even if that molecule contributes to the natural cue. Conversely, a synthetic mixture that elicits orientation under controlled conditions may omit factors that influence behavior in the field. The ecological significance of a compound is established through the behavior it changes in the relevant context.

A behavioral experiment should distinguish the proportion of insects making a choice from their distribution among the choices. Suppose 60 parasitoids are tested, 18 do not enter either arm within the observation period, and among the remaining 42, 30 enter the arm carrying herbivore-associated odor and 12 enter the control arm. Among responding insects, approximately 71 percent choose the herbivore-associated odor. Among all tested insects, 50 percent choose that arm. Reporting both quantities prevents the nonresponding animals from disappearing from the interpretation. A statistically reliable preference also requires an appropriate analysis and replication; the illustrative counts alone are not evidence of a published experimental result.

Enemy-mediated effects can be quantified in a factorial experiment. Consider two plant genotypes that differ in a volatile trait but have comparable direct resistance in no-enemy conditions. In a hypothetical example, the percentage leaf area lost for low-emission plants is 40 without natural enemies and 30 with natural enemies; for high-emission plants the values are 40 and 15. Enemy access reduces damage by 10 percentage points in the low-emission genotype and 25 in the high-emission genotype. The additional reduction associated with the high-emission genotype in the presence of enemies is 15 percentage points. This pattern is consistent with a stronger enemy-mediated benefit in that genotype, assuming that relevant conditions have been controlled.

Plant fitness is still a separate endpoint. If both genotypes produce the same number of seeds despite different damage levels, the protective difference may not yield a detectable reproductive advantage under the chosen conditions. The plant might have sufficient compensatory capacity, or the emission trait could impose other costs. Conversely, lower herbivore abundance might preserve future growth not captured by a brief experiment. A complete ecological account therefore moves from emission to orientation, from orientation to enemy attack, from enemy attack to plant damage, and from damage to performance. Each step can be investigated independently.

9. Systemic wound defense and experimental localization

A plant is not a collection of fully independent leaves. Vascular tissue connects organs, transports water and assimilates, and supplies routes along which physiological signals can propagate. A wound can therefore produce responses both near the site of damage and in distant, undamaged tissues. A local response is measured in or near injured tissue; a systemic response is measured elsewhere in the same plant. These terms refer to location and do not by themselves identify the transported signal.

A remote leaf may accumulate jasmonate or activate defense genes after another leaf is wounded. One possibility is movement of a hormone or precursor from the damaged leaf. Another is the propagation of rapid electrical, calcium-related, hydraulic or chemical information that stimulates jasmonate synthesis in the remote tissue. More than one mechanism can operate at the same time. Detecting hormone in the remote leaf does not establish that it was synthesized at the wound and transported intact.

Experiments that combine tissue-specific perturbations with time-resolved measurement can distinguish these alternatives. If a remote tissue unable to synthesize a particular hormone fails to accumulate it despite a normal early electrical response, that result supports a requirement for local biosynthetic activity in the receiving tissue under the conditions tested. If the receiving tissue accumulates an isotopically labeled compound initially supplied to the damaged tissue, the label provides evidence of transport or metabolic conversion of a transported precursor. The chemical identity of the labeled material remains important.

A grafting experiment can also help distinguish requirements in the sending and receiving tissues. A signaling-defective shoot and a wild-type partner can be combined in reciprocal arrangements, where the species and tissue organization permit. A failure of the response when the receiving portion lacks a receptor supports a requirement for perception in that portion. A failure when the sending portion lacks a biosynthetic enzyme can indicate that production of a signal or a precursor there is important. Grafting changes tissue continuity and physiology, so ungrafted and self-grafted controls remain necessary.

The term systemin describes a peptide involved in wound defense in tomato and related contexts. Its historical study helped reveal the existence of plant-derived signals capable of eliciting defense responses. However, the presence of systemin in a tomato wound-response model does not justify labeling it the universal mobile wound hormone of all higher plants. Arabidopsis and other species can employ distinct peptide and signaling systems. In an exam question, plant species and the specific experimental evidence should guide the interpretation.

Systemic wound defense is also distinct from the canonical systemic acquired resistance associated with certain pathogen responses. Both involve communication across plant organs, and their pathways may intersect, but a remote jasmonate-associated antiherbivore response is not automatically an SA-dependent SAR response. The triggering organism, signaling requirement and resistance endpoint are relevant to classification. Broader SAR and induced systemic resistance are treated in the following node.

Spatial patterns offer important information. Leaves that are strongly connected through vascular architecture can display different response timing from equally distant leaves that lack the same connection. Physical distance alone may therefore be a poor predictor of the order in which systemic responses appear. A map of the whole plant, combined with imaging or sequential sampling, is more informative than pooling all undamaged leaves into one sample and treating the average as a universal response.

Experimental separation of signaling and transport

A signal that moves between plant organs need not have the same chemical identity at the point of reception as the material released at the wound. A local electrical change can initiate a sequence of alterations in membrane behavior along connected tissues. A change in water relations may modify mechanical conditions in other cells. A small molecule or precursor may move through a transport pathway, and receiving cells may synthesize another signal in response. The important conceptual separation is between the physical carrier of information and the biochemical mechanism that produces a defensive output at the destination.

The classical study of tomato systemin illustrates the transition from observation to identification of a candidate wound signal. Wounded tomato leaves were known to induce proteinase inhibitors in other parts of a plant. Identification of a small peptide capable of stimulating defense-gene expression provided a chemical candidate linking wound-associated events to downstream defenses. The identification of mutants affecting the wound and systemin response then allowed investigators to ask which steps were shared with other treatments. Such studies helped develop the relationship between peptide signaling and lipid-derived jasmonate responses. They did not establish one universal systemic pathway across all plant taxa.

Consider a conceptual graft experiment in which A is a functional plant tissue, B lacks an enzyme needed for a wound-induced ligand, and C lacks a receptor required for a particular defense output. If a wounded A donor elicits the output in a B recipient, but not in C, the result supports a requirement for recipient perception and suggests that the missing biosynthetic step in B can be bypassed in this configuration. If a wounded B donor fails to elicit the output in A, the missing biosynthetic step may be important for the donor's generation of a transferable stimulus. Several interpretations remain possible: the actual transported material may be the ligand, a precursor or another signal generated downstream of the missing step.

Controls make the experiment interpretable. A self-grafted A–A combination tests whether the procedure permits the systemic response. Direct application of the ligand to recipient B can assess whether its downstream signaling machinery is competent, while direct application to C tests the expected receptor dependence. The wounds should be comparable and the receiving tissue should be sampled before secondary damage occurs. Where grafts contain heterogeneous tissue, the genotype of the measured tissue must be clear. These controls prevent a negative result from being attributed to signal failure when the real cause is poor vascular reconnection or general tissue injury.

The comparison of local and systemic responses also has a timing dimension. A locally applied candidate substance might be followed by a very rapid electrical change in distal tissue, a later increase in jasmonate and still later accumulation of a defensive protein. These events could form a causal sequence, but their order alone is insufficient evidence. Selective perturbation of the early systemic signal and measurement of the later outputs can test dependency. Conversely, if distal JA-Ile production is disrupted without eliminating the electrical event, the result separates receipt of an early signal from downstream synthesis or perception. A multi-readout experiment is more informative than a single final measurement of defense gene expression.

Systemic responses are particularly useful when a plant's future risk is spatially correlated with present attack. A mobile larva may move among connected leaves, and a second insect may arrive at tissues not yet injured. Nevertheless, induction in every distant organ is not necessarily optimal. A reproductive organ, young leaf or mature source leaf differs in future value and in metabolic capacity. Vascular architecture and local competence influence which tissues respond. The presence of systemic signaling should therefore not be equated with a perfectly uniform plant-wide concentration of every defense compound.

10. Resistance, tolerance and resource allocation

A plant that restricts caterpillar growth can still suffer substantial damage, while a plant that allows caterpillars to grow may replace much of its lost tissue. Resistance and tolerance are therefore complementary concepts. Resistance alters the probability or extent of attack or the success of the herbivore. Tolerance describes the plant's ability to maintain growth, survival or reproduction across a given range of injury. In evolutionary studies, reproductive fitness is especially important because short-term biomass recovery need not predict seed production.

Tolerance can arise through changes in photosynthesis of remaining leaves, mobilization of stored carbohydrates, activation of dormant buds, changes in resource allocation, delayed senescence or production of new tissue. Compensation requires viable meristems, energy and substrates. Damage to an apical meristem may stimulate lateral branching if axillary buds remain functional, whereas destruction of all available meristems can prevent this response. Damage timing and the amount of remaining growing season can change the outcome.

Undercompensation means that the damaged plant performs worse than the corresponding undamaged control. Exact compensation means similar performance, within measurement uncertainty, while overcompensation describes higher performance following injury in some contexts. Overcompensation is not a general rule and must be established against properly matched undamaged plants. An increase in the number of branches is not sufficient if seed production or survival decreases. Choosing the correct response variable is fundamental to interpretation.

Tolerance is commonly analyzed as a relationship between a measure of plant performance and the intensity of injury. Suppose seed production is represented by F, measured as seeds per plant, and injury D is measured as a fraction of leaf area removed. A simple local approximation is F = F0 + bD, where F0 is the expected seed production without damage and b is the slope in seeds per plant per unit fraction of damage. A less negative slope suggests greater tolerance over the measured range, when comparisons use comparable conditions and an appropriate fitness measure.

For example, consider an illustrative dataset in which plant genotype A produces 100 seeds without injury and 70 seeds after removal of half its leaf area. Its slope across the two points is (70 − 100)/0.5 = −60 seeds per plant per unit injury fraction. Genotype B produces 80 and 72 seeds at the same injury levels, giving (72 − 80)/0.5 = −16. B has the less negative slope despite its lower baseline performance. This example separates tolerance from the absolute productivity of undamaged plants.

The linear equation is a convenient teaching approximation, not a universal biological law. At mild damage, compensatory responses may maintain performance, whereas severe damage can exceed the plant's capacity for recovery. A curve may therefore be more suitable than a straight line across the full range. Injury type also matters: removal of half the mature leaves is not necessarily equivalent to loss of half the reproductive buds. Comparing tolerance requires standardizing injury or measuring it carefully and including the relevant injury pattern in the interpretation.

Allocation to defense can reduce the resources immediately available for growth, but tradeoffs are not inevitable in every environment. A defense trait can save future resources by preventing more extensive attack; the net cost depends on herbivore pressure and resource conditions. Constitutive and induced traits differ in when the costs occur. An insect-resistant genotype that has lower growth under herbivore-free conditions may have higher reproductive output in an environment with persistent feeding.

A factorial experiment with resistant and susceptible genotypes, with and without herbivores, helps separate these effects. Comparing genotypes only in the presence of herbivores confounds baseline growth differences with benefits of resistance. Comparing only undamaged plants cannot measure protection. To examine tolerance specifically, researchers should impose or quantify comparable injury levels rather than assume that equal numbers of insects cause equal damage across genotypes. The interaction between genotype and measured injury is informative about differences in tolerance.

The shape of compensation and the cost of defense

Resource allocation provides a mechanistic explanation for different compensation curves. A plant has a finite rate of assimilate production under a given set of light, water and nutrient conditions. Some of that assimilate supports maintenance respiration, some is incorporated into new structure, and some may be stored or used for defense. Following defoliation, the fraction available for each process can change. Remaining tissues may increase their activity, stored reserves may be mobilized, and new sinks may compete with existing reproductive organs. These adjustments explain why the relationship between damage and performance is not determined by lost leaf area alone.

A plant with substantial stored carbohydrate may replace leaves during a period when current assimilation is insufficient to support rapid growth. However, mobilization of reserves can reduce the resources available for later flowering, survival during adverse conditions or recovery from a second disturbance. An experiment ending immediately after leaf replacement may therefore overestimate long-term compensation. The appropriate observation period depends on the biological outcome under investigation. For an annual crop, final harvested yield may be an important endpoint; for a perennial, survival and future reproductive performance can also matter.

A source–sink perspective helps explain the effects of damage to distinct organs. Mature photosynthetic leaves often function as sources, supplying assimilates to developing leaves, roots and reproductive structures. Removing a major source can reduce supply to several sinks. Removing an actively growing sink can reduce demand and may redirect resources to other tissues. Thus, loss of equal tissue mass from a mature source and a developing sink can have different consequences for the balance of the plant. After injury, the physiological identity of an organ may change as new growth becomes active or previously growing tissue matures.

Tolerance can be expressed as an absolute change or a relative change. For the example of genotype A with 100 undamaged seeds and 70 damaged seeds, relative performance is 70 percent. For genotype B with 80 and 72 seeds, relative performance is 90 percent. On both the absolute slope and relative performance measures B shows greater tolerance over the specified interval. However, rankings can differ in other datasets. A high-yield genotype may lose more seeds in absolute terms but a smaller proportion of its baseline output. The study should state the measure chosen and justify its biological relevance rather than assuming that absolute and relative tolerance are always equivalent.

Consider a hypothetical fitness curve measured at three injury levels. A plant produces 100 seeds without injury, 105 following mild injury and 40 following severe injury. The mild injury result is compatible with overcompensation if the difference is supported by replication and sampling uncertainty, but the severe injury result demonstrates that the plant's compensatory capacity is limited. A straight line fitted across the full range would conceal the increase at mild injury. This matters when comparing genotypes because one may tolerate mild herbivory particularly well but decline sharply beyond a threshold, while another shows a gradual decline across the entire range.

The cost of resistance can also be estimated through a controlled comparison in the absence of attack. Suppose a resistant genotype produces 90 seeds without insects and 75 with insects, while a susceptible genotype produces 110 without insects and 45 with insects. Without insects the resistant genotype produces 20 fewer seeds, whereas under the specified insect treatment it produces 30 more seeds. This pattern supports a context-dependent advantage of resistance but does not alone prove that the baseline difference is caused by resource expenditure on a particular defense. The genotypes may differ in other traits. A near-isogenic comparison, complementation or a controlled manipulation of the defense trait can help identify its contribution.

Environmental conditions influence these tradeoffs. Low light can constrain carbon acquisition, nitrogen limitation can restrict synthesis of proteins and nitrogenous metabolites, and drought can change photosynthesis, turgor and growth. A genotype that compensates successfully under favorable conditions may fail under resource limitation. The reverse pattern may occur for some responses if damage changes resource demand in a way that relieves a limitation. Consequently, tolerance and resistance should be described for the environmental conditions in which they were measured. The integrated response to combined stresses is the focus of the next node.

11. Integrating molecular and ecological evidence

A mechanistic model of herbivore defense is strongest when different measurements connect consecutive steps of a causal sequence. An increase in JA-Ile after wounding identifies a biochemical response, while degradation of a tagged JAZ protein provides a readout of receptor-dependent protein turnover. A change in MYC target-gene expression addresses transcriptional consequences. A reduction in insect growth or survival measures the biological outcome. No single readout is a substitute for the whole chain.

Consider an illustrative four-genotype experiment. Wild-type plants show increased JA-Ile, reduced JAZ abundance and strong protease inhibitor expression after a standardized wound. A biosynthetic mutant shows little JA-Ile, retains JAZ and induces little inhibitor. A coi1 mutant accumulates JA-Ile but retains JAZ and induces little inhibitor. A line carrying a stable JAZ repressor likewise retains the repressor and fails to strongly induce the MYC-dependent output. The shared inhibitor phenotype hides different defects. Supplying active ligand to the biosynthetic mutant should improve the response if the downstream machinery is functional, whereas the receptor and stable-repressor defects are not simply corrected by raising ligand concentration.

The experiment illustrates epistasis logic without requiring that every phenotype be absolute. Biological pathways have partially redundant components and parallel branches. A mutant may retain some induction because other transcription factors or pathways contribute. The crucial evidence is the pattern of specific readouts and rescue under controlled conditions. A developmental defect in a coi1 mutant also requires caution: differences in leaf age, morphology or nutritional composition can alter insect performance independently of inducible signaling.

Measurement of herbivore growth should include starting mass, elapsed time and, when relevant, consumption. A caterpillar starting at 20 mg and reaching 35 mg has gained 15 mg; one starting at 25 mg and reaching 38 mg has gained 13 mg, despite its larger final mass. The initial difference makes final mass alone misleading. If an experiment also records dry food consumed, approximate conversion efficiency can be computed as dry mass gain divided by dry food consumed, expressed as a percentage. The numerator and denominator must use comparable mass bases. Differences in water content would otherwise distort interpretation.

A no-choice feeding trial exposes an insect to a designated plant genotype or diet, helping measure consumption and performance when alternatives are not available. A choice trial allows selection among treatments and is appropriate for testing preference. If larvae avoid one plant but grow equally well when forced to feed on it, reduced preference is a better-supported mechanism than reduced physiological performance. If they choose both plants equally but grow more slowly on one, antibiosis is supported. A complete study can measure both.

The biological sample size is the number of independent experimental units, not necessarily the number of individual observations. Ten larvae on a single leaf do not always represent ten independent plant replicates because all experience the same plant condition. Similarly, repeated fluorescence measurements of the same leaf form a time course, not independent biological replicates at each moment. Correct analysis depends on recognizing which unit was independently assigned to a treatment.

A good Part C answer distinguishes observation from inference. A graph showing a lower larval mass on plants with a higher concentration of a compound establishes an association. Adding the compound to otherwise matched diets, or comparing genetically altered plants with appropriate complementation, can test whether it contributes causally. Demonstrating that a purified enzyme is inhibited provides mechanistic support. Measuring plant fitness under realistic attack conditions addresses ecological relevance. Each experiment supports a different link, and the strongest conclusion remains limited to the links actually tested.

Evaluating competing causal models

One common error in an examination question is to treat genetic rescue as a proof of the precise identity of a mutated enzyme. Suppose a wounded mutant does not accumulate a defense product, while application of JA-Ile restores that product. The result supports the existence of an accessible route from active ligand to the measured output. It also suggests that the endogenous defect lies upstream of that route or can be bypassed by the treatment. It does not prove that the mutated gene directly encodes a jasmonate biosynthetic enzyme. The defect could affect wound perception, signaling toward lipid metabolism, precursor availability or ligand turnover. Additional chemical or molecular evidence is needed to identify the actual step.

A second error is to infer a shared signaling pathway solely because two treatments produce the same outcome. Mechanical wounding and an insect-derived elicitor could each induce the same defensive protein through partially overlapping routes. Testing mutants that selectively affect one treatment can reveal differences in recognition. Testing a mutant in a shared component can show whether both routes converge on that component. The presence of a common endpoint is compatible with convergence, parallel pathways or indirect physiological effects. A well-constructed experiment measures intermediate responses that distinguish these possibilities.

A useful comparative dataset may include a rapid calcium response, active jasmonate abundance, JAZ abundance, defense transcript and insect growth. A reduction in the earliest readout accompanied by reductions in subsequent outputs suggests that the defect may occur relatively early, but the earliest measured readout is not always the first altered event. Normal calcium signaling with low JA-Ile suggests a defect downstream of or parallel to calcium signaling. Normal JA-Ile with stable JAZ suggests impaired perception or turnover. Normal transcription with low defensive product points toward translation, enzyme activity, precursor availability or product stability. The chain supplies a reasoning framework rather than a guarantee that every response follows one linear pathway.

The distinction between necessary and sufficient conditions can organize such analysis. A component is necessary for a specific outcome under defined conditions if loss of its function prevents that outcome. A component or treatment is sufficient in a defined context if its presence produces the outcome without requiring the other proposed initiating conditions. Neither term is absolute outside the tested context. A redundant pathway may mask necessity when only one gene is removed, and a chemical treatment at high concentration may bypass mechanisms used at physiological concentrations. Appropriate wording avoids converting an experimental observation into an unjustified universal claim.

Finally, resistance and tolerance measurements should be interpreted together when the aim is to explain plant success. A mutant with a defective jasmonate response may sustain greater feeding damage, but its final seed output may depend on compensatory growth. A mutant that maintains seed production despite high damage may be tolerant without possessing strong direct defenses. Conversely, a plant with high defense-related gene expression may have poor fitness if the response is costly or ineffective against the particular insect. The biological question determines which measurements are decisive. The exam-ready answer identifies the observed endpoint, states the causal explanation consistent with the evidence and names the additional experiment that would distinguish remaining alternatives.

Misconceptions and examination traps

All herbivores remove leaf tissue visibly

Correction: Sap feeders can divert solutes with little visible area loss. Reasoning: Leaf-area assays suit chewing injury but not every feeding mode. Why this is tempting: Chewing damage is easier to photograph than sap withdrawal. Exam trap: A sap feeder causes yield loss despite unchanged leaf area.

Wounding equals herbivory

Correction: Mechanical injury lacks some insect-associated cues. Reasoning: Match injury geometry and add insect secretion in a controlled experiment. Why this is tempting: Both treatments damage cells and share many early markers. Exam trap: Matched punctures and punctures plus secretion induce different responses.

DAMP means an insect molecule

Correction: DAMPs originate in damaged plant tissue; HAMPs are herbivore-associated. Reasoning: Classify by origin rather than downstream signal. Why this is tempting: Both types of cues may activate overlapping signaling pathways. Exam trap: A question gives extracellular ATP and insect-derived peptide in one experiment.

All oral secretions stimulate defense

Correction: Some components suppress or alter signaling. Reasoning: Compare defined candidates and appropriate controls. Why this is tempting: Elicitors are often emphasized in simplified accounts of feeding. Exam trap: A secretion treatment reduces a wound-induced marker.

Depolarization is identical to calcium influx

Correction: Potential changes reflect net charge movements. Reasoning: Distinguish ionic flux from the electrical readout. Why this is tempting: Both signals frequently appear in the same wound time course. Exam trap: Voltage and Ca²⁺ traces have different kinetics.

Plants possess neuronal synapses

Correction: Plant GLR channels mediate plant-specific signaling. Reasoning: Do not infer neurons from receptor nomenclature. Why this is tempting: GLR nomenclature is borrowed from related animal receptor families. Exam trap: A GLR mutant lacks a distal response but has no neural tissue.

JA and JA-Ile are interchangeable

Correction: JA-Ile promotes canonical COI1–JAZ interaction. Reasoning: Distinguish biosynthesis from conjugation and perception. Why this is tempting: JA is often used as shorthand for multiple jasmonates. Exam trap: JA application and JA-Ile application yield different rescue results.

COI1 is a protease

Correction: COI1 is an F-box component of an E3 ligase. Reasoning: Ubiquitination marks substrate; the proteasome degrades it. Why this is tempting: The F-box protein is named in accounts of repressor degradation. Exam trap: Proteasome inhibition and COI1 mutation both stabilize JAZ.

MYC is destroyed to induce defense

Correction: Removal of JAZ releases MYC repression. Reasoning: Follow the negative regulator, not the activated factor. Why this is tempting: Pathway arrows are often mistaken for activation by destruction of the transcription factor. Exam trap: A stable JAZ variant blocks induction despite high JA-Ile.

SA always suppresses JA

Correction: Cross-talk depends on context. Reasoning: Use factorial hormone data rather than categorical rules. Why this is tempting: Simple textbook diagrams emphasize antagonism rather than conditional interactions. Exam trap: A factorial JA × SA treatment has an interaction term of either sign.

All trichomes secrete toxins

Correction: Non-glandular hairs can act mechanically. Reasoning: Separate morphology from chemistry. Why this is tempting: Glandular and nonglandular hairs are easily conflated. Exam trap: Microscopy shows a stiff nonsecretory surface hair.

Protease inhibitors guarantee lower consumption

Correction: Insects may compensate by eating more. Reasoning: Measure ingestion, enzyme activity and growth independently. Why this is tempting: Reduced enzyme activity is often assumed to predict total food intake. Exam trap: Insects compensate for reduced digestion by eating more.

A volatile attracts only beneficial enemies

Correction: Different organisms may use the odor. Reasoning: Measure net ecological effects. Why this is tempting: Attraction of a parasitoid is more often measured than the whole community response. Exam trap: An odor increases enemy visits but seed production does not improve.

Distal JA must have traveled intact

Correction: Distal tissues can synthesize jasmonate. Reasoning: Use tissue-specific perturbations or tracer evidence. Why this is tempting: A distal JA increase is frequently confused with proof of its transport. Exam trap: Distal hormone accumulation occurs despite impaired local synthesis.

Tolerance means low damage

Correction: Tolerance is performance at a specified damage level. Reasoning: Compare fitness–damage slopes, not damage alone. Why this is tempting: Plants with less damage are easily mistaken for plants that better withstand a fixed amount of damage. Exam trap: Two plants sustain identical damage but yield different relative fitness.

Original examination practice

These are original practice questions, not previous-year examination questions. Choose the single best option in each item; Part C questions require interpretation of the stipulated evidence or illustrative data.

1. Part B — Which type of insect feeding can substantially alter assimilate distribution without extensive removal of leaf area?

A. Mesophyll chewing; B. Leaf-margin skeletonization; C. Phloem feeding; D. Consumption of entire leaves

Answer and explanation

C. Phloem feeding. Stylets can reach sieve elements and withdraw translocated assimilates.

2. Part B — What distinguishes a DAMP from a HAMP?

A. Biological origin of the cue; B. Whether calcium rises after recognition; C. Whether the response occurs in the nucleus; D. Whether the cue causes a change in gene expression

Answer and explanation

A. Its biological origin. DAMPs are endogenous damage-associated cues, whereas HAMPs originate from or are associated with the herbivore.

3. Part B — Which receptor-related plant proteins contribute to glutamate-dependent systemic wound signaling in Arabidopsis?

A. GLRs including GLR3.3 and GLR3.6; B. COI1 and JAR1; C. MYC2 and JAZ1; D. Myrosinase and allene oxide cyclase

Answer and explanation

A. GLRs including GLR3.3 and GLR3.6. These channels contribute to calcium-related propagation, not neuronal transmission.

4. Part B — Which fatty acid is a canonical precursor of jasmonate?

A. Palmitic acid; B. Alpha-linolenic acid; C. Oleic acid exclusively; D. Arachidonic acid exclusively

Answer and explanation

B. Alpha-linolenic acid. The 18-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acid is converted through oxylipin reactions.

5. Part B — What is the cyclic chloroplast product preceding peroxisomal jasmonate processing?

A. JA-Ile; B. OPDA; C. Methyl salicylate; D. Abscisic acid

Answer and explanation

B. OPDA. 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid is produced through the LOX–AOS–AOC pathway.

6. Part B — Which jasmonate conjugate promotes canonical COI1–JAZ association?

A. Free JA in the canonical binding assay; B. Salicylic acid; C. ACC; D. JA-Ile

Answer and explanation

D. JA-Ile. The isoleucine conjugate is the central ligand in this perception mechanism.

7. Part B — What type of protein is COI1?

A. Basic helix–loop–helix transcription factor; B. Cysteine protease; C. Lipoxygenase; D. F-box protein

Answer and explanation

D. F-box protein. It participates in SCF ubiquitin ligase substrate recognition.

8. Part B — Which cellular machinery degrades ubiquitinated JAZ proteins?

A. Ribosome; B. 26S proteasome; C. Allene oxide cyclase; D. Cell-wall peroxidase

Answer and explanation

B. 26S proteasome. The ubiquitin ligase promotes substrate tagging, while the proteasome catalyzes proteolysis.

9. Part B — Which regulatory protein is relieved from JAZ-mediated inhibition in the standard model?

A. COI1; B. MYC2; C. OPR3; D. JAR1

Answer and explanation

B. MYC2. MYC transcription factors are activated through release of repression, rather than degradation.

10. Part B — What is the role of newly synthesized JAZ repressors during recovery?

A. Permanent commitment to constitutive defense; B. Irreversible activation of COI1 synthesis; C. Replacement of the proteasome; D. Negative feedback

Answer and explanation

D. Negative feedback. Restoring repression helps terminate or limit the transcriptional response.

11. Part B — Which plant surface structures may be glandular or non-glandular?

A. Trichomes; B. Sieve plates; C. Casparian strips; D. Thylakoid grana

Answer and explanation

A. Trichomes. Both can contribute to protection, but glandular forms can secrete specialized chemicals.

12. Part B — What is antibiosis?

A. Reduced preference only; B. Maintenance of plant yield at matched damage; C. Reduced herbivore performance after interaction; D. Predator recruitment only

Answer and explanation

C. Reduced herbivore performance after interaction. It differs from antixenosis, which concerns acceptance or preference.

13. Part B — What is a typical direct function of plant protease inhibitors?

A. Direct synthesis of JA-Ile; B. Interference with herbivore digestive enzymes; C. Opening of plant stomata; D. Phloem loading of sucrose

Answer and explanation

B. Interference with herbivore digestive enzymes. Their effectiveness depends on enzyme class, inhibitor stability and insect counteradaptation.

14. Part B — Which enzyme hydrolyzes glucosinolates in the classical damage-activated system?

A. Rubisco; B. JAR1; C. Catalase; D. Myrosinase

Answer and explanation

D. Myrosinase. Disruption can permit enzyme and substrate to interact and produce defensive products.

15. Part B — Which type of defense recruits herbivore enemies?

A. Antibiosis by ingested inhibitor; B. Tolerance; C. Osmotic adjustment; D. Indirect defense

Answer and explanation

D. Indirect defense. The plant influences herbivory through another trophic level.

16. Part B — What is a parasitoid?

A. An organism developing in or on a host and usually killing it; B. A grazer that never kills a host individual; C. A producer converting light energy to biomass; D. A pathogen restricted to plant xylem

Answer and explanation

A. An organism that develops in or on a host and usually kills it. Unlike a predator that consumes multiple prey, parasitoid development is typically associated with an individual host.

17. Part B — What does systemic wound defense mean?

A. Movement of intact JA in every species; B. Defense responses in distant, initially undamaged tissues; C. Response restricted to ruptured cells; D. Inherited genetic resistance only

Answer and explanation

B. A response in undamaged tissues distant from the wound. The term specifies response location rather than a single mobile signal.

18. Part B — Which measure is central to tolerance?

A. Number of herbivores landing on plants; B. Maximum constitutive JA level; C. Area lost without measuring performance; D. Fitness or performance at comparable damage levels

Answer and explanation

D. Plant fitness or performance at comparable damage levels. Low damage alone indicates resistance, not necessarily tolerance.

19. Part B — When is overcompensation established?

A. Defensive gene expression exceeds baseline; B. Branch number rises even as reproduction decreases; C. The insect grows more slowly; D. Performance exceeds that of matched undamaged controls

Answer and explanation

D. When a damaged plant performs better than matched undamaged controls. The selected fitness or performance endpoint and conditions must be explicit.

20. Part B — Which hormone often interacts with JA during plant defense without universal antagonism?

A. Gibberellin as the sole universal JA antagonist; B. Starch; C. Salicylic acid; D. Cellulose

Answer and explanation

C. Salicylic acid. SA–JA interaction depends on organism, tissue and timing.

21. Part C — Wounding gives JA-Ile values 8 in WT and 1 in mutant X; both show strong induction when supplied with JA-Ile. Where is X most likely defective?

A. A nonfunctional COI1 receptor; B. Complete proteasome failure; C. A defect affecting active-ligand availability upstream of perception; D. Loss of all downstream transcriptional machinery

Answer and explanation

C. Ligand production upstream of perception. Rescue with active ligand supports functional downstream machinery, though uptake and alternate pathways should be checked.

22. Part C — A plant accumulates normal JA-Ile after injury, but its JAZ persists and MYC targets remain low. What two classes of defect fit?

A. Defective COI1 or degradation-resistant JAZ; B. Greater JA biosynthesis alone; C. Loss of all JAZ repressors; D. Enhanced proteasomal destruction of JAZ

Answer and explanation

A. COI1 function or degradation-sensitive JAZ recognition. Both can prevent repressor turnover despite adequate ligand.

23. Part C — A mutant shows a rapid Ca²⁺ rise locally but not in connected remote leaves; direct wounding of remote leaves still induces defense. What is the simplest inference?

A. Complete absence of distal defense machinery; B. Irreversible loss of viability of remote leaves; C. Defective inter-organ signaling; D. Universal inability to produce calcium signals

Answer and explanation

C. Defective inter-organ signal propagation. Local competence is intact in the receiving leaf when it is directly stimulated.

24. Part C — Treatment A removes 20% leaf area in one cut; treatment B removes 20% by repeated bites and has higher JA. Does this isolate a HAMP effect?

A. Yes; identical total area guarantees identical stimuli; B. Yes; any JA increase proves receptor-specific recognition; C. No; insects cannot supply elicitors; D. No; wound geometry, timing and insect cues differ

Answer and explanation

D. No. The timing and geometry of damage are confounded with insect-derived chemical cues.

25. Part C — WT plus oral secretion produces 10 relative units of defense transcript; wound plus buffer produces 4; intact plus secretion produces 1. What conclusion is justified?

A. A specific receptor has been identified; B. Secretion enhances the response in wounded tissue; C. Intact plants cannot ever perceive oral secretion; D. Wounding does not contribute to induction

Answer and explanation

B. Secretion enhances the response in the wounded context. Intact treatment may lack access; the data do not identify a specific receptor.

26. Part C — Larvae avoid genotype A in a choice trial but show equal growth on A and B in no-choice trials. Which resistance component is supported?

A. Antibiosis demonstrated by lower growth; B. Tolerance; C. Antixenosis; D. Enemy-mediated indirect defense

Answer and explanation

C. Antixenosis. Avoidance is demonstrated, but reduced performance on forced exposure is not.

27. Part C — Two caterpillars gain 12 and 9 mg dry mass after consuming 40 and 20 mg dry food. Which has higher conversion efficiency?

A. First larva, because it gains more mass; B. Both 30%; C. Second larva, 20% versus 40%; D. Second larva, 45% versus 30%

Answer and explanation

D. Second larva: 45% versus 30%. Compute gain divided by consumption; lower absolute gain can coincide with higher efficiency.

28. Part C — A protease inhibitor reduces trypsin activity, but larvae consume more leaf tissue and maintain mass gain. Does the inhibitor necessarily reduce crop injury?

A. No; compensatory feeding can offset reduced digestion; B. Yes; reduced enzyme activity guarantees reduced tissue loss; C. Yes; mass gain proves feeding ceased; D. No; inhibitors never affect insects

Answer and explanation

A. No. Compensatory feeding can preserve growth and increase damage despite an enzyme-level effect.

29. Part C — A trichome mutant is more attractive to insects and has altered volatile emission. What prevents attributing preference solely to physical hairs?

A. Trichome density alone explains all choice behavior; B. Insects cannot detect volatiles; C. The mutation proves greater tolerance; D. Altered volatiles are a confounder

Answer and explanation

D. The chemical phenotype is a confounder. A rescue or comparison that separates morphology from volatile changes is needed.

30. Part C — A volatile attracts parasitoids in an olfactometer. What additional evidence supports indirect plant defense?

A. Higher emission alone; B. Enemy-dependent reduction of damage or increase in plant performance; C. Attraction of an unrelated insect species alone; D. Increased JA transcript alone

Answer and explanation

B. Reduced herbivory or improved plant performance dependent on enemy access. Odor orientation alone does not quantify a plant benefit.

31. Part C — In the illustrated tolerance graph (Figure 11), genotype A makes 100 seeds undamaged and 70 after 50% injury; B makes 80 and 72. Which is more tolerant in this range?

A. A, because its undamaged yield is larger; B. B, because its decline per damage fraction is smaller; C. A, because it loses more seeds; D. Equal, because both sustain 50% injury

Answer and explanation

B. B. The slopes are −60 and −16 seeds per plant per injury fraction, respectively.

32. Part C — A and B receive five larvae each; A loses 10% leaf area and B loses 40%. Can their final seed counts directly rank tolerance?

A. Yes; equal larval number ensures equal damage; B. No; damage must be matched or modeled; C. Yes; tolerance equals number of larvae offered; D. No; reproductive performance cannot measure tolerance

Answer and explanation

B. No. Unequal damage confounds resistance and tolerance; compare performance at matched damage.

33. Part C — JA alone increases a marker to 10, SA alone to 2, combined to 4 and control to 1. What conclusion is supported?

A. SA attenuates the JA-associated marker in this treatment context; B. SA abolishes every JA response in all plants; C. SA alone reduces this marker below control; D. There is no interaction in the observed means

Answer and explanation

A. SA antagonizes the JA-associated output in this treatment context. The interaction is endpoint-specific and does not establish universal antagonism.

34. Part C — After wounding, JAZ protein falls at 10 minutes but JAZ mRNA rises at 60 minutes. Are these observations inconsistent?

A. No; protein turnover and transcription operate on different timescales; B. Yes; transcript and protein must always change together; C. Yes; JAZ cannot be transcribed after wounding; D. No; proteasomal degradation is unnecessary for any JA response

Answer and explanation

A. No. Early protein degradation and later transcriptional negative feedback can coexist.

35. Part C — A distal leaf accumulates JA after a remote wound. Does that prove JA movement from the wound?

A. Yes; any distal increase proves intact JA transport; B. Yes; electrical signals cannot induce local biosynthesis; C. No; distal synthesis could explain the accumulation; D. No; distal tissues never synthesize JA

Answer and explanation

C. No. Distal biosynthesis could be induced by other mobile signals; tracer or tissue-specific experiments are required.

36. Part C — An Arabidopsis line lacking MYC2 retains some induction of a JA-responsive transcript. What is a plausible explanation?

A. COI1 must be entirely absent; B. JAZ degradation is impossible in this line; C. MYC2 is the only transcriptional regulator in all species; D. Redundant or alternative transcriptional regulators

Answer and explanation

D. Redundant or alternative transcriptional regulators. The pathway includes more than one transcription factor and tissue-dependent outputs.

37. Part C — A chemical inhibitor blocks RBOH activity and reduces a ROS reporter, but the plant still shows a calcium rise. What is the limited conclusion?

A. Some calcium response remains despite reduced measured ROS output; B. ROS never influences calcium; C. All RBOH-dependent processes are proven absent; D. Calcium must be produced by the proteasome

Answer and explanation

A. Some calcium responses can remain without the measured RBOH-dependent ROS output. The result does not rule out feedback or ROS effects at later times.

38. Part C — Ten larvae are fed on one leaf of genotype A and ten on one leaf of genotype B. What limits statistical inference?

A. One independent treatment unit per genotype; B. Ten independent genotype replicates per group; C. Twenty independent plant replicates; D. No issue, because all insects are separate organisms

Answer and explanation

A. Only one independent plant or leaf treatment unit per genotype. Larvae sharing a plant are not automatically independent biological replicates.

39. Part C — A wounded plant produces more branches but fewer seeds than its undamaged control. Does branching establish fitness overcompensation?

A. Yes; all branching indicates reproductive overcompensation; B. No; reproductive performance decreased; C. Yes; seed output is unrelated to fitness; D. No; compensatory growth is biologically impossible

Answer and explanation

B. No. Reproductive output is lower; vegetative morphology and fitness are different endpoints.

40. Part C — A high-volatile plant has less injury only when parasitoids are present, with direct defenses matched. Which mechanism is supported?

A. Enemy-mediated indirect resistance; B. Direct antibiosis independent of enemies; C. Tolerance established at matched injury; D. Universal inability of larvae to feed

Answer and explanation

A. Enemy-mediated indirect resistance. The genotype-by-enemy-access interaction connects emission with a protective ecological effect.

41. Part B — Which pair is correctly matched?

A. COI1 — protease; B. MYC2 — lipid precursor; C. JAZ — transcriptional repressor; D. JAR1 — ion channel

Answer and explanation

C. JAZ restrains transcription factors. COI1 is an F-box protein, MYC2 is a transcription factor and JAR1 catalyzes jasmonate conjugation.

42. Part B — Which response is most directly classified as indirect defense?

A. Leaf toughness; B. Attraction of parasitoids by plant odor; C. Trypsin inhibitor; D. Alkaloid toxicity

Answer and explanation

B. The plant acts through a third trophic level. The other choices act on feeding access or physiology.

43. Part B — Consider the statements: I. OPDA is a plastid-derived intermediate in canonical jasmonate synthesis. II. JAR1 contributes to JA-Ile formation. III. JAZ directly hydrolyzes JA-Ile. IV. COI1 is an F-box protein. Which are correct?

A. I and III only; B. II and III only; C. I, II and IV only; D. III and IV only

Answer and explanation

C. I, II and IV follow the biosynthetic and perception mechanism. III is false: JAZ represses transcription factors rather than acting as a JA-Ile hydrolase. All other combinations include III or omit correct statements.

44. Part B — Assertion: Stable JAZ can reduce JA responses even at high JA-Ile. Reason: JAZ represses relevant transcription factors.

A. Both true, reason unrelated; B. Assertion true, reason false; C. Both true, reason explains assertion; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

C. If the repressor persists and remains functional, abundant hormone cannot fully release the relevant transcriptional output.

45. Part C — A plant with 0.02 m² functional leaf area assimilates 12 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Injury leaves 0.015 m² at 14 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. What fraction of original whole-leaf assimilation remains?

A. 87.5%; B. 75%; C. 116.7%; D. 100%

Answer and explanation

A. Initial total is 0.24 µmol/s; remaining total is 0.21 µmol/s. The ratio is 0.875. Area-normalized rate alone would incorrectly suggest an increase.

46. Part C — A larva consumes 100 mg dry food, excretes 50 mg and gains 15 mg dry mass. What are apparent digestibility and conversion of apparently digested food?

A. 50%, 30%; B. 50%, 15%; C. 70%, 30%; D. 15%, 50%

Answer and explanation

A. Apparent digested mass is 50 mg, so digestibility is 50%; gain divided by that amount gives 30%.

47. Part C — JAZ synthesis is 2 units/min and effective first-order degradation changes from 0.1 to 0.4 min⁻¹. Under constant-rate assumptions, what happens to steady-state abundance?

A. 20 to 80; B. 20 to 5; C. 5 to 20; D. 2 to 0.4

Answer and explanation

B. Pss = s/k. Degradation increases fourfold and the calculated steady-state abundance falls fourfold; the actual transition takes time.

48. Part C — A candidate elicitor causes a response in wounded WT but not mutant M. Both respond normally to a distinct DAMP. What inference is best?

A. M certainly encodes the ligand; B. All calcium signaling is absent; C. M may affect candidate-specific recognition or signaling; D. M proves no HAMP exists

Answer and explanation

C. Selective loss narrows the candidate step, but does not prove direct binding or identify the gene's biochemical activity.

49. Part C — Low-emission plants lose 40% tissue without enemies and 30% with enemies; high-emission plants lose 40% and 15%. What is the additional damage reduction associated with high emission under enemy access?

A. 10 percentage points; B. 25 percentage points; C. 40 percentage points; D. 15 percentage points

Answer and explanation

D. Enemy-associated reductions are 10 and 25 points, respectively. Their difference is 15 points, supporting an interaction under the controlled assumptions.

50. Part C — A receptor-defective recipient fails to respond to wounding of a WT graft partner but a biosynthesis-defective recipient responds. What is the safest conclusion?

A. The transported molecule must be JA-Ile; B. No signal is transmitted; C. Every wound response uses the same mobile compound; D. Recipient perception is required for the measured response, and the recipient biosynthetic defect can be bypassed

Answer and explanation

D. The result supports functional localization but cannot determine the transported chemical identity; other signals or precursors can explain the rescue.

51. State SET — Which distinction is essential when comparing crop resistance to herbivory?

A. Both mean increased seed production; B. Antixenosis concerns preference; antibiosis concerns performance; C. Tolerance always prevents feeding; D. Resistance and tolerance are identical

Answer and explanation

B. Preference, insect performance and plant fitness at matched damage are distinct outcomes.

52. State SET — Which observation alone demonstrates overcompensation in reproductive performance?

A. More branches after injury; B. More JA; C. Higher seed output in injured plants than matched uninjured controls with adequate supporting evidence; D. Less larval growth

Answer and explanation

C. Only the matched reproductive endpoint addresses the stated compensation claim; structural and defense markers cannot substitute for fitness data.

53. State SET — An olfactometer experiment finds 30 choices for attacked-plant odor, 12 for control, and 18 nonresponders. What proportion of responding insects chose the attacked-plant odor?

A. 50%; B. 30%; C. 71.4%; D. 83.3%

Answer and explanation

C. The denominator is 42 responders; 30/42 ≈ 71.4%. Report nonresponders separately.

54. State SET — Which statement about plant GLRs is correct?

A. They can participate in wound-related ion signaling without implying neuronal synapses; B. They demonstrate animal-like neurons in plants; C. They are all transcription factors; D. They synthesize JA-Ile

Answer and explanation

A. GLRs contribute to plant-specific membrane and calcium signaling; the nomenclature is not evidence of neurons.

Revision and mastery

Herbivory combines physical disturbance and biological information. The canonical jasmonate pathway connects lipid-derived JA-Ile to regulated JAZ degradation and release of MYC-mediated repression. Direct defenses act on herbivore access or performance; indirect defenses act through natural enemies. Systemic wound defense prepares distal tissues through interacting signals. Tolerance is measured at comparable injury levels.

Terminology and high-yield distinctions

DAMP
A damage-associated molecular pattern originating from damaged host tissue.
HAMP
A herbivore-associated cue, including host-derived molecules processed by the insect.
Antixenosis
Reduced acceptance, landing, oviposition or feeding preference.
Antibiosis
Reduced herbivore survival, growth or reproduction after contact or consumption.
Tolerance
Maintenance of performance at a specified damage level.
HIPV
Herbivore-induced plant volatile; may inform natural enemies, other herbivores or neighboring tissues.

Core mechanism

Membrane lipid → alpha-linolenic acid → LOX/AOS/AOC → OPDA (plastid) → OPR3 and β-oxidation (peroxisome, canonical route) → JA → JAR1-dependent JA-Ile formation → COI1–JAZ association → SCF-dependent ubiquitination of JAZ → proteasomal degradation → release of MYC repression. Other routes and feedback modify this canonical scheme.

DefenseDiagnostic readoutKey control
AntixenosisPreference or settlementRandomized choice and no-choice comparison
AntibiosisHerbivore growth or survivalMatched intake and initial condition
Indirect defenseEnemy-mediated reduction in damageEnemy access versus exclusion
TolerancePerformance–damage relationshipMatched damage and undamaged baseline

Quantitative quick reference

  • Apparent digestibility = 100 × (ingested dry mass − fecal dry mass)/ingested dry mass. ECI = 100 × growth/ingested dry mass; ECD = 100 × growth/(ingested − fecal dry mass).
  • Relative signal change ΔF/F₀ = (F − F₀)/F₀; apparent propagation speed = distance/time.
  • Whole-leaf assimilation = net assimilation per area × intact photosynthetic area, under uniform-rate assumptions.
  • Tolerance slope = Δperformance/Δdamage. A less negative slope indicates higher tolerance over the measured range if performance declines with damage.
