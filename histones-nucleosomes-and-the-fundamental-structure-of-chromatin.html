1. The packaging problem: storing DNA without hiding its information

The DNA inside a nucleus is not a short, rigid object that can simply be placed in a container. It consists of extraordinarily long double-stranded molecules, each carrying a particular linear sequence of bases. A typical human diploid cell before DNA replication contains roughly six billion base pairs of nuclear DNA. At the dimensions of B-form DNA, the sum of their contour lengths is about two metres. A nucleus only a few micrometres across must accommodate this material together with the machinery that reads, copies and repairs it.

Folding the DNA indiscriminately would not solve the entire problem. A dense, unorganized bundle could create tangles and obstruct access to selected sequences. On the other hand, leaving DNA fully extended would be spatially impractical. The cell requires packaging that is compact, reproducible and reversible at local sites. A gene must retain its sequence when the surrounding material changes from a more accessible to a more compact arrangement. The molecular solution in most eukaryotic nuclear chromosomes begins with the association of DNA with histones.

Recall the distinction between a chromosome, a gene and a genome. A gene is a functional region of a genome; a chromosome is a physical genomic unit containing DNA and associated material; the genome is the complete genetic complement under consideration. In the previous chapters, sequences were classified according to their arrangement, number and relatedness. Here, the question changes from what sequences are present to how a DNA molecule containing those sequences is organized in three-dimensional space. One histone particle need not coincide with a gene boundary. Its DNA can encompass part of a promoter, an exon, an intron, a repeat or an intergenic segment.

The term chromatin refers to the DNA–protein material of eukaryotic chromosomes, not simply to DNA at a particular stage of the cell cycle. It includes histones and numerous nonhistone proteins; associated RNAs can also contribute in particular contexts. When a chromosome condenses during cell division, its chromatin changes its spatial organization rather than becoming a different chemical substance. When chromatin is relatively open in interphase, it is still associated with proteins. Removing those proteins in the laboratory produces naked or deproteinized DNA, an experimental comparison rather than the normal physical state of most nuclear DNA.

The problem of packaging therefore has two linked dimensions. The first is local: DNA must interact with a protein surface to form a stable but adjustable building block. The second is collective: many local building blocks must organize into an extended chromosome. This chapter concentrates on the first dimension, establishing the molecular unit whose larger arrangements will be examined in the next chapter.

Before proceeding, recall three ideas from nucleic acid chemistry: base pairing preserves information in a DNA duplex, its backbone carries charged phosphates, and a protein's amino acid sequence determines its interaction surfaces. The chapter on nuclear organization explains the surrounding nuclear environment. These prerequisites are sufficient to distinguish a change in DNA sequence from a change in its physical packaging. The latter can be reversible without altering genetic information.

2. Why histones and DNA associate

A DNA double helix has two sugar–phosphate backbones on its exterior. Under ordinary cellular conditions the phosphate groups carry negative charges. The complementary bases are largely inside the helix, although their edges can be approached through the major and minor grooves. A protein that associates with DNA therefore encounters a negatively charged surface as well as grooves with a characteristic shape and chemical pattern.

Histones are relatively small proteins rich in basic amino acids, especially lysine and arginine. Their side chains are generally positively charged at physiological pH. Positive and negative charges attract one another, making association between the histone surface and the DNA backbone favorable. This explains why histones can associate with DNA throughout a genome instead of requiring a unique base sequence at every binding site. It does not mean that histones bind all DNA sequences with identical affinity.

The double helix is also a mechanically resistant polymer. To wrap it around a small particle requires bending and local deformation; that costs free energy. The histone surface supplies numerous favorable contacts that compensate for much of this cost. Many individually reversible interactions cooperate across a substantial length of DNA, so the overall particle can be stable while individual contacts occasionally break and reform. In addition to electrostatic attractions, hydrogen bonding, close packing and the shape of the protein–DNA interface contribute to the organization. DNA remains a double helix through the wrapped region, though its geometry varies locally.

Ionic conditions matter. Dissolved ions screen electrostatic interactions, reducing the effective attraction of opposite charges and the repulsion of like charges at a distance. As salt conditions change, histone–DNA affinity and interactions between nearby DNA segments can both change. High salt can help dissociate histones from DNA in experimental preparations; controlled return to suitable conditions can support reassembly. However, one cannot predict the degree of chromatin compaction from a single charge argument because several interactions respond to ionic strength simultaneously.

Binding is not a covalent joining of histone and DNA. No base pair needs to be removed and no phosphodiester bond needs to be cut to form a nucleosome. This point is important when considering access to the genome: the cell can rearrange the proteins around a DNA segment without changing the sequence of bases. A mutation in a histone surface can alter the stability of such packaging without itself changing a DNA base in the packaged region.

A helpful way to test the importance of the protein surface is to compare the same DNA sequence assembled with normal histones and with histones carrying charge-altering substitutions. The sequence has not changed, so any difference in protection or affinity must arise from some difference in the protein preparation or the assembly conditions. If a basic residue that normally contacts DNA is replaced by an uncharged residue, the direct electrostatic contribution may be weakened. However, an amino acid substitution can also change protein shape, so a control that establishes proper folding is needed before attributing the result entirely to charge. Similarly, the influence of salt on the whole array cannot be interpreted without distinguishing DNA–histone binding from particle–particle interactions.

The sum of all favorable interactions does not mean DNA is immobilized indefinitely. The thermodynamic preference for the associated state and the rates of association or dissociation are different properties. A strong equilibrium preference can coexist with a measurable rate of local unwrapping. Conversely, observing a slowly dissociating particle does not by itself establish that every contact is especially strong. This distinction explains how a stable nucleosome can nevertheless allow repeated episodes of accessibility.

3. How experiments revealed a repeating unit

Early studies of chromosome material established that histones were abundant partners of nuclear DNA, but this alone did not reveal how the proteins and DNA were arranged. One could imagine a continuous protein coating around a DNA thread, a disorganized collection of binding proteins or a regular sequence of particles. To distinguish these possibilities required measurements of physical spacing and of the accessibility of DNA.

In the early 1970s, nuclease experiments revealed that chromatin DNA was not equally susceptible to enzymatic cleavage at every position. When chromatin was partially digested, investigators recovered DNA fragments concentrated around regularly spaced lengths rather than only a broad range of random sizes. A repeating protection pattern suggested that the DNA was organized into units separated by preferentially cut segments. A nuclease is an enzyme capable of breaking nucleic acid phosphodiester bonds. The interpretation of a nuclease experiment depends on which parts of a substrate are physically exposed to that enzyme.

Electron microscopy provided a complementary view. Under suitable spreading conditions, chromatin appeared as a thread bearing repeated, roughly globular particles. The particle-like structures were seen directly, whereas nuclease digestion supplied a measurement in base pairs. Neither observation in isolation determined the exact protein composition of the particle. A regular digestion ladder could arise from any repeating protective complex, and a micrograph could be influenced by sample treatment. Agreement between the independent observations made the repeating-unit hypothesis much stronger.

In 1974 Roger Kornberg proposed a model in which the repeating structure involved a complex containing two molecules of each of four histones associated with roughly 200 base pairs of DNA per repeat. Subsequent isolation of protected core particles showed that the most tightly bound DNA region was shorter than this average repeat. This difference established that DNA in one repeat was divided into two structurally distinct portions: DNA closely associated with the particle and DNA connecting it to the next particle.

Later structural studies resolved the path of the DNA and the arrangement of histones. In 1997 Luger and colleagues reported a high-resolution nucleosome core particle structure using X-ray crystallography. Their particular crystallized particle contained 146 base pairs; the conventional canonical core length is now usually stated as approximately 147 base pairs. These numbers are compatible descriptions of closely related particle preparations. This evidence transformed a useful model of repetitive chromatin into a molecular structure with identifiable protein subunits and a traceable DNA path.

The historical lesson is also an examination lesson. Microscopy can establish the existence and rough size of particles; digestion can reveal recurring DNA protection and spacing; protein composition measurements can identify the associated molecules; structural determination can establish their spatial arrangement. Assigning all these conclusions to one experiment exaggerates its power.

Protein analysis was important because the repeating DNA length does not identify the mass or molecular identity of the protecting material. If a purified fraction contains the four core histones in approximately equal molar proportions, that supports the model of equivalent copy numbers but does not by itself establish how many molecules occur per particle. An independent particle mass measurement or detailed structural observation is required to distinguish one copy of each from two copies of each. Similarly, equal mass proportions would not imply equal molecular counts, because the different histone proteins have different molecular masses. The combined evidence therefore had to establish both DNA length and protein stoichiometry.

4. The histone octamer: four protein types, eight molecules

The canonical core particle contains four kinds of core histone: H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. Each is represented twice. Thus the core is an octamer, a molecular assembly of eight histone proteins. It is not a complex of eight different histone types. Histone H1 belongs to a different structural category and is not counted as part of this octamer.

Core histones share a structural motif called the histone fold. Its alpha helices and connecting loops provide surfaces through which histones pair with one another. H3 associates with H4 to form a heterodimer, meaning a two-protein complex containing different polypeptides. Two H3–H4 dimers associate to form a central (H3–H4)₂ tetramer. H2A and H2B form a second type of heterodimer. One H2A–H2B dimer associates on each side of the central tetramer. The resulting stoichiometry is H3₂ H4₂ H2A₂ H2B₂.

The octamer has a disk-like overall shape. Its protein surfaces are arranged so that a continuous double helix can wrap around the outside. The arrangement is approximately twofold symmetric around a central region referred to as the dyad. Twofold symmetry here means that a rotation of about 180 degrees relates much of one half of the core arrangement to the other. It does not require the DNA sequence on the two halves to be identical. A particular genome sequence can be asymmetric even when the protein scaffold is approximately symmetric.

The assembly logic also explains why removing different components produces different consequences. If H2A–H2B dimers become destabilized, a histone complex may retain H3–H4 association but lose the complete set of surfaces normally supporting the wrapped DNA. A particle with one H2A–H2B dimer missing is often called a hexasome; a DNA-associated H3–H4 tetramer is called a tetrasome. Neither is an ordinary intact canonical octamer. Such intermediate states can occur during chromatin dynamics. They help explain why nucleosomes can undergo partial disassembly without requiring all eight proteins to separate at once.

The tetramer-plus-two-dimers representation describes the composition and a useful assembly route; it should not be interpreted as requiring a long-lived free octamer to float around the nucleus before DNA binds. In cells, proteins that assist histone delivery can transfer components to DNA in a coordinated way. The structural relationship between the subunits is what makes this model valuable.

5. How DNA wraps around the histone core

The nucleosome core particle consists of the histone octamer and approximately 147 base pairs of double-stranded DNA. The DNA winds about 1.65–1.7 times around the outside of the octamer in a left-handed superhelical path. The term superhelix is used because the axis of the double helix itself follows a curved, helical path around the protein core. It is essential to distinguish this from the ordinary helical twist of the two DNA strands around one another.

B-DNA is right-handed at the level of the double helix. It can nevertheless follow a left-handed superhelical path around the histone assembly. Imagine a flexible double-stranded rope that is already twisted internally; bending its whole axis around a cylinder in the opposite sense does not reverse the original internal twist. In the nucleosome both levels of winding coexist. The distinction becomes especially important in DNA topology problems.

The core particle is approximately 11 nm across and about 5–6 nm high. These dimensions describe a single particle rather than the diameter of all chromatin in a nucleus. At several positions along the superhelical path, the inward-facing DNA minor groove and phosphate backbone approach the histone surface. Repeated favorable interactions help hold the double helix in a strongly curved arrangement. The DNA is not threaded through a closed protein ring; it lies outside the octamer. This arrangement makes it possible for DNA at the particle edges to unwrap without passing an entire chromosome through an opening.

At the dyad, the central base-pair region lies near the approximate symmetry axis of the core. Moving away along either direction reaches DNA closer to the entry and exit points. At these outer positions there are fewer remaining contacts to restrain further unwrapping. If a short segment near an end temporarily releases, the underlying sequence may become accessible while much of the particle remains intact. Access to a site nearer the dyad generally requires more extensive disruption of the wrapped structure, although the exact behavior depends on sequence and molecular context.

The number 147 describes the length of DNA associated with a canonical isolated core, not the number of helical turns of double-stranded DNA and not the spacing between every pair of nucleosomes. At roughly 10.5 bp per turn in relaxed B-DNA, 147 bp corresponds to about 14 ordinary double-helical turns. Those turns are carried along a path making only about 1.65 superhelical turns around the octamer. In the wrapped state the local twist is not perfectly uniform, so the calculation of ordinary turns is an approximation used to clarify the two different geometrical scales.

A restriction enzyme recognizing a particular base sequence offers a simple illustration of rotational exposure. Two otherwise comparable sites may occupy different orientations on the wrapped helix: the groove carrying the recognition information can face away from or towards the histone surface. The exposed site may be easier for a binding protein to approach even if its distance from the particle edge is similar. This differs from translational positioning, which describes where the particle center lies along the linear genomic sequence. Moving the particle by several base pairs can change both the distance of a site from the edge and the orientation of that site relative to the core. These are reasons why counting nucleosomes alone cannot predict access at single-base resolution.

6. Histone tails: flexible extensions of the structural core

A histone is not shaped entirely like the compact folded surface drawn in the octamer diagram. Several regions, especially the amino-terminal tails, extend away from the folded core. The amino terminus is the end of a protein chain carrying its terminal amino group. These tails are relatively flexible, and their positions fluctuate with surrounding interactions. In many nucleosome drawings they project from the particle as short lines rather than as rigid structural rods.

The tails contain lysine and arginine residues that contribute to interactions with DNA, neighboring nucleosomes and other chromatin-associated proteins. Some contacts occur within a particle, and others occur between particles. For example, a region of the H4 tail can interact with a negatively charged surface on a neighboring nucleosome. Such interactions help explain why an array of particles can exhibit collective properties that are not obvious from an isolated core. The details of large-scale chromatin folding will be examined separately.

Histone tails also provide sites for chemical modification. Acetylation of a lysine side chain neutralizes its positive charge; methylation of lysine does not produce the same charge-neutralizing effect. A modification can alter a direct physical interaction or create a recognizable binding surface for a protein that detects that modification. These are distinct mechanisms. A change in charge alone cannot explain the action of every modification. Nor does the presence of a single modified residue prove that an entire chromosome region must be transcriptionally active or inactive.

The structural consequence of a tail perturbation depends on where the altered residue normally acts. Removing a tail need not remove the histone fold or break the entire octamer. It may instead affect how strongly neighboring particles associate, how readily an external protein binds or how readily wrapped DNA is exposed. If electron microscopy shows intact particles but the array becomes more extended after tail removal, a plausible interpretation is that interparticle contacts were weakened. Protein composition and DNA protection measurements would help determine whether the local particles remained intact.

For this chapter, the essential distinction is between the compact histone-fold scaffold and flexible extensions that influence chromatin behavior. Specific modification enzymes, reader proteins and inheritance of chromatin states belong to the later gene-regulation chapters.

7. Linker DNA, H1 and the chromatosome

A chromosome contains a continuous DNA molecule. If consecutive segments of it wrap around distinct octamers, some DNA must connect the particles. This connecting portion is linker DNA. It is generally more exposed to the surrounding solution than the core-associated DNA because it does not make the same continuous sequence of close contacts with the octamer. Its length varies between organisms, tissues and genomic regions.

Histone H1 is known as a linker histone because it associates with DNA near the entry and exit of the core particle and with nearby linker DNA. It is structurally different from the four core histone types and is typically present outside the octamer. H1 includes a globular DNA-binding domain and flexible terminal regions; the long basic carboxy-terminal region contributes substantially to linker DNA interactions. A carboxy terminus is the end of a protein chain bearing its terminal carboxyl group.

By associating near the entry and exit region, H1 can constrain the relative arrangement of the emerging DNA arms. The two arms need not meet at an identical angle in every particle, and different H1 proteins can bind in somewhat different geometries. Nevertheless, the general consequence is stabilization of local DNA organization and an influence on how neighboring particles approach one another. This is why the addition or removal of H1 can alter chromatin array behavior even though the octamer itself contains no H1.

A chromatosome is commonly described as a core particle plus linker histone H1 and a short additional segment of protected DNA. In classical nuclease protection experiments, a length of approximately 160–168 bp is associated with this form, compared with approximately 147 bp for a core particle. The difference of about 20 bp is not necessarily the entire linker between successive nucleosomes. It represents extra DNA protection near the entry and exit region. Some other particles or digestion conditions can yield similar fragment sizes, so a measured length alone does not conclusively establish H1 occupancy.

The simplified textbook model assigns one H1 molecule to each nucleosome that carries linker histone. Actual occupancy is variable. Cells can contain regions with less than one H1 molecule per core on average, and histone H1 isoforms differ in abundance. Consequently, an examination question asking for total H1 molecules must either state full occupancy or provide an occupancy fraction. The eight core histones are counted independently of this additional component.

These distinctions resolve the apparent conflict among three commonly encountered numbers. Around 147 bp refers to the protected core; around 166 bp may describe the chromatosome footprint; and roughly 180–200 bp is a common example of the repeat length in an array. They refer to different physical measurements. A long linker increases the repeat length without requiring an increase in core DNA length.

It is useful to visualize H1 as a component at the DNA entrance and exit rather than as a cap sealing the whole particle. The linker arms contain DNA outside the immediate core contact surface, and H1 can interact with them while also contacting the nucleosomal DNA near the dyad region, depending on its binding geometry. Consequently, loss of H1 can make the entering and exiting DNA arms less constrained while leaving most core-associated contacts intact. This is a prediction about molecular flexibility, not a guarantee that every H1-depleted nucleus will have the same chromosome appearance. Other proteins, salt conditions and local spacing still contribute.

8. Beads on a string and the nucleosome repeat length

When a chromatin segment contains many nucleosomes, its DNA follows an alternating path through wrapped core regions and intervening linkers. Under conditions that allow the array to spread, this organization resembles beads connected by a string. The approximately 10–11 nm fiber is a useful representation of the first level of chromatin packaging. In a living nucleus, the beads need not lie along a perfectly straight line or be equally spaced, and neighboring particles can interact.

The nucleosome repeat length, commonly abbreviated NRL, is the average genomic distance occupied by one nucleosome together with its associated share of linker DNA. Equivalently, in a regularly spaced array, it is the distance along the DNA sequence from the center of one nucleosome to the center of its neighbor. It is measured in base pairs rather than nanometres. If the core-associated length is C and the mean linker length is L, the repeat length R is C + L. A repeat length of 200 bp and a core of 147 bp imply an average linker length of 53 bp.

In a finite linear array the accounting of the ends matters. An array containing n core particles connected only by internal linkers has n − 1 internal linker segments. If every linker has length L and there are no free terminal extensions, its DNA length is nC + (n − 1)L. By contrast, the usual estimate nR assigns one complete repeat to each core, implicitly including terminal DNA amounting to one additional linker. The two expressions differ by exactly L. On a circular, evenly spaced array with no gap, n cores can indeed be connected by n linkers.

The repeat length is not universally 200 bp. Cells can differ in linker length, and a chromatin domain can contain nonuniform spacing, gaps and regions with few nucleosomes. A repeat length inferred from a digestion ladder is usually a statistical average over the population represented by the sample. It should not be used to claim that a particular linker has a known exact length. A change in mean repeat length may reflect changes in spacing or in the populations of particles contributing to the measurement.

Regular spacing makes it easier to describe an array quantitatively, but the arrangement of particles is not dictated by the coding pattern of genes. A promoter may have a relatively exposed region bordered by particles, whereas a neighboring coding region may show an array of occupied positions. The implications of these arrangements depend on the ability of other proteins to recognize and alter the underlying chromatin.

9. Micrococcal nuclease: the logic of the protection experiment

Micrococcal nuclease, often abbreviated MNase, is an enzyme that cleaves nucleic acids and is used to probe chromatin accessibility. When the enzyme encounters a chromatin sample, exposed linker DNA is generally cleaved more readily than DNA closely associated with a histone octamer. The difference in accessibility creates a characteristic distribution of surviving DNA lengths. The aim of the classical experiment is to infer the organization of chromatin from these lengths, not to sequence the genome.

Imagine starting with three neighboring nucleosomes on one DNA molecule. If the enzyme cuts the linker on either side of all three but leaves their internal connecting linkers intact, the released fragment retains three core-associated regions and two intervening linkers, possibly with terminal linker DNA. Other cleavage events separate pairs of neighboring nucleosomes, and still others release individual particles. At a suitable intermediate digestion stage, the sample therefore contains DNA associated with one, two, three or more nucleosomes. The occurrence of successive fragment classes at a characteristic interval gives the familiar nucleosomal ladder.

To measure DNA lengths, proteins are removed after digestion, and DNA fragments are separated by gel electrophoresis. DNA molecules of different lengths move differently through the gel matrix; shorter fragments generally travel farther under the same conditions. A size marker containing DNA of known lengths calibrates the positions. The bands in the chromatin lane consist of DNA molecules, not intact histone octamers visible as protein bands. The original chromatin organization is inferred from the length distribution of the released DNA.

If digestion is allowed to proceed further, the exposed ends of a fragment are trimmed, leaving DNA more closely protected by the histone particle. Under suitable conditions a prominent class near 147 bp can be recovered. Samples containing H1 may additionally show protection around 160–168 bp under appropriate digestion conditions. Extensive exposure can eventually damage even core-associated DNA. The experiment is a kinetic comparison: relative resistance to cleavage is not absolute immunity.

A well-designed experiment includes protein-free DNA treated under matched conditions, a DNA size marker and several digestion time points. A no-enzyme control establishes that sample handling did not already produce the fragments of interest. The naked DNA control tests whether a regular ladder requires association with chromatin proteins rather than simply the sequence composition of the DNA. Comparing identical starting DNA amounts and similar digestion conditions is essential when interpreting band intensities.

The nuclease has its own sequence preferences and may trim different sites at different rates. Therefore neither a cleavage-resistant fragment nor a change in digestion speed automatically proves a change in histone abundance. Local sequence, protein binding other than histones, nucleosome movement and digestion severity can influence the observed result. Protein composition, microscopy or another independent accessibility measurement can strengthen an inference. The experimental distinction between evidence and conclusion is exactly what a Part C question often tests.

10. Reading a digestion ladder without confusing footprint and spacing

Suppose partial digestion produces major DNA fragment classes with centers at 190, 380, 570 and 760 bp. The spacing between adjacent classes is 190 bp. A plot of fragment length against the number of nucleosomes represented would be approximately linear, with a slope of 190 bp per particle. This is the repeat length of the sampled array, under the simplifying assumption that the measured classes reflect its repeating organization. If a separate stronger digestion recovers a 147 bp core, the inferred mean linker is 43 bp.

Now consider a different sample showing major peaks at 172, 362 and 552 bp. The separation is again 190 bp even though the first peak is at 172 bp. Terminal trimming can shift the first peak without changing the spacing between neighboring cores. A useful idealization is Sₙ = nR + b, where Sₙ is the length of the fragment containing n particles, R is the repeat length and b is a common end correction in base pairs. Taking a difference removes b: Sₙ₊₁ − Sₙ = R. This is why comparing multiple peaks is more robust than treating the first band as the repeat length.

The relative intensity of a band gives different information from its position. Position estimates the DNA size class; intensity, under appropriate staining conditions, reflects the quantity of DNA in that class. If stronger digestion increases the intensity of the mononucleosomal band while decreasing longer fragments, this is compatible with separation of previously connected particles. It does not mean that the sample has synthesized new histone proteins during the experiment. Longer fragments contain more DNA per molecule, so raw staining intensity is also not automatically equal to the number of particles.

A broad ladder can result from nonuniform linker lengths or mixed chromatin populations. For example, one population with 175 bp spacing and another with 200 bp spacing could yield overlapping fragment classes whose separation becomes harder to assign. A smear may arise from extensive nuclease digestion or from loss of regular protection, but these interpretations are not interchangeable. A digestion time series helps distinguish early loss of a periodic pattern from late degradation of an initially regular array.

When comparing H1-depleted and control chromatin, consider at least two observables. The partial-digestion spacing reports the organization of the array, while the core-sized protected fragment reports local protection. If the latter remains near 147 bp but an intermediate class near 166 bp is reduced, one reasonable hypothesis is that loss of H1-associated protection has occurred without widespread octamer destruction. Confirmation requires independent evidence of H1 depletion. It is not necessary to invoke DNA deletion or loss of all nucleosomes.

As a further worked example, consider a hypothetical partial digestion of two cell types with dimer and tetramer peaks at 354 and 714 bp for sample X, and 394 and 794 bp for sample Y. Each size interval spans two additional particles. The slopes are therefore (714 − 354)/2 = 180 bp and (794 − 394)/2 = 200 bp. With identical 147 bp cores, their average linkers are 33 and 53 bp. Notice that the absence of a resolved monomer or trimer peak does not prevent the calculation if the particle numbers corresponding to the remaining peaks are established. It would be incorrect to call 360 or 400 bp a repeat because each difference represents two repeat units.

Suppose the dimer band contains 720 arbitrary units of DNA signal and the monomer band contains 480 under linear staining conditions. For a 180 bp monomer and 360 bp dimer, relative molecule counts are proportional to signal divided by fragment length: 480/180 = 2.67 and 720/360 = 2.00. Although the dimer band contains 1.5 times as much DNA mass, it contains fewer DNA molecules. The numerical ratio of dimer molecules to monomer molecules is 0.75. This calculation does not quantify actual nucleosome occupancy in the original sample, since digestion and recovery may favor some fragment classes.

11. Quantitative accounting: length, occupancy and histone number

B-form DNA has an average axial rise of approximately 0.34 nm per base pair. If N is the number of base pairs, the relaxed contour length is approximately 0.34N nm. A contour length measures distance along the DNA axis, not the distance between its ends when folded. For 6 × 10⁹ bp, the calculation gives 2.04 × 10⁹ nm. Because one metre equals 10⁹ nm, this is 2.04 m. The calculation assumes the DNA can be represented as relaxed B-form duplex and does not include the additional length of other genomic compartments.

For a large uniformly occupied region of length G bp and average repeat length R bp, the approximate particle count is G/R. This follows directly by dividing the total number of base pairs into groups of R base pairs. It is an estimate: gaps, nonuniform spacing, terminal DNA and partially occupied regions can change the actual count. Each complete canonical octamer contains eight histone molecules, two of each kind. Thus for n complete canonical particles there are 2n copies of each core histone and 8n core histone molecules in total.

Example 1: A 36,000 bp DNA segment is modeled as 180 complete repeat units, each containing 147 core bp. Its repeat length is 36,000/180 = 200 bp. Its mean linker is 200 − 147 = 53 bp. The total core-associated DNA is 180 × 147 = 26,460 bp and the remaining DNA is 9,540 bp. There are 360 molecules of each core histone and 1,440 core histone proteins in all. If H1 occupancy averages 0.75 per core, the expected number of H1 molecules is 135, not 180. With the stated average occupancy, the total histone count is 1,575.

Example 2: Suppose ten complete cores occur on a linear DNA fragment with no terminal extensions and all nine internal linkers are 35 bp. Total DNA length is 10 × 147 + 9 × 35 = 1,785 bp. Using ten complete repeats of 182 bp would give 1,820 bp, overestimating by 35 bp because that model includes an extra linker's worth of DNA. If the same ten particles form an ideal circular array with ten internal linkers, the 1,820 bp result would instead be appropriate.

Example 3: Two equally long chromatin domains of 18,000 bp are assumed fully organized into complete repeat units. Domain A has R = 180 bp, whereas domain B has R = 200 bp. The approximate counts are 100 and 90 particles. Core DNA occupies 147/180 = 81.7% of domain A and 147/200 = 73.5% of domain B. The difference is 1,470 bp of core-associated DNA. Although A has more histone octamers per unit sequence length, these data do not establish which domain has the greater three-dimensional compaction. Linker geometry, H1 and neighboring interactions also matter.

Example 4: A cell contains 6 × 10⁹ bp of nuclear DNA. For a deliberately idealized 200 bp repeat and complete occupancy, the estimate is 3 × 10⁷ nucleosomes and 2.4 × 10⁸ core histone molecules. H3 accounts for 6 × 10⁷ of these molecules. This is a useful order-of-magnitude calculation, not a measured histone inventory. When the DNA content doubles during replication, maintaining similar nucleosome density requires a corresponding increase in the amount of histone material in the replicated chromosomes.

Notice that none of these calculations requires a special kinetic law. They use conservation of DNA length and the stoichiometry of the octamer. The important examination skill is selecting the right denominator: core length for a footprint calculation, repeat length for array density, and the number of complete octamers for the histone count.

12. What wrapping means for DNA topology and compaction

The shape of DNA must be distinguished from its topology. A linear unconstrained DNA molecule can rotate its ends and relieve torsional stress; a covalently closed circular duplex or a rotationally constrained DNA domain cannot freely do so. The linking number, Lk, counts the linking of the two strands in a closed duplex. It can be described as the sum of twist, Tw, which measures winding of the strands about the local helix axis, and writhe, Wr, which describes the coiling of that axis in space. Thus Lk = Tw + Wr for the closed or appropriately constrained system.

Wrapping around a histone octamer introduces a left-handed path of the duplex axis, but the geometry of the wrapped region alone does not supply the complete linking number change for an entire circular molecule. The entry and exit paths and changes in local twist also contribute. In the standard nucleosome topological experiment, an assembled canonical nucleosome typically constrains about one negative linking-number unit, rather than exactly 1.65 units. The distinction between the geometric number of superhelical turns and a measured change in linking number resolves the apparent contradiction.

A useful experiment starts with relaxed closed circular DNA and assembles histones onto it. If no strand passage or break occurs, total linking number remains unchanged, and torsional changes in the wrapped region must be compensated elsewhere. If a topoisomerase relaxes accessible compensatory torsion while nucleosomes remain assembled, the linking number can change. Removing the histones afterward reveals negatively supercoiled DNA relative to the initially relaxed reference. This provides evidence that the particles constrain negative supercoiling. The exact extent depends on conditions and particle organization.

For an ideal example, take a closed circular plasmid whose relaxed linking number is 400. If six canonical nucleosomes are assembled, topoisomerase relaxes the accessible DNA while the particles remain intact, and each particle constrains approximately −1 linking-number unit, the deproteinized DNA is expected near Lk = 394. Without topoisomerase or strand breakage, Lk would remain 400. The difference is a topological outcome, not a deletion of six bases or a change in the number of DNA strands.

Local compaction is another distinct concept. A 147 bp stretch of relaxed B-DNA has a contour length close to 50 nm; wrapping it around an approximately 11 nm particle places distant portions of the contour close together. Dividing 50 by 11 gives roughly 4.5, a geometric comparison between contour length and particle diameter. It does not measure the actual longitudinal packing ratio of an entire chromatin fiber. That ratio also depends on linkers and how particles are oriented. The path length of the DNA is not reduced to 11 nm merely because the particle is that wide.

At the larger scale, comparing two metres of DNA to a nucleus of about 10 micrometres gives a length-scale ratio near 200,000. This is not a direct volume-compaction factor or a calculation of nucleosome density. It illustrates why packaging must continue beyond individual particles. The next chapter will develop chromosome-scale organization without assuming that one fixed fiber diameter exists everywhere.

13. Access to wrapped DNA: a dynamic rather than sealed particle

If wrapping were irreversible, it would make the genome unusable. The physical structure of the particle permits a different outcome. DNA can transiently detach from the outer contacts near its entry and exit regions, exposing a short stretch of sequence. It may then rebind without any permanent change in particle position. This reversible behavior is sometimes termed nucleosome breathing. Thermal motion helps individual contacts break and reform; the spontaneous exposure of a site is not itself proof that ATP has been consumed.

A DNA-binding protein can capture a temporarily exposed site by associating with it before the DNA rewraps. The observed accessibility depends on the frequency and duration of exposure and the concentration and binding properties of the external protein. The same sequence can therefore be more accessible when located near a nucleosome edge than when located deeper in a strongly wrapped region. Sequence-specific proteins differ in whether they can recognize their sites on nucleosomal DNA; some can bind without requiring complete particle eviction.

The particle can also change its position relative to the DNA. ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes are proteins that use chemical energy to alter the relationships between histones and DNA. In a simplified sliding mechanism, a remodeler grips nucleosomal DNA and uses ATP-driven movement to disturb local histone–DNA contacts. Rearrangement propagates through the particle and the DNA settles into a new register on the histone surface. The result is a shift of the footprint along the sequence, potentially exposing one site while covering another. DNA sequence length and order are unchanged. Other remodeling outcomes include partial disassembly or exchange of histone components, depending on the complex.

Positioning means the genomic coordinates of a particle. Occupancy means how often a particular DNA region is associated with a particle in the population or time interval being considered. Spacing means the distance between neighboring particles. These quantities are related but not interchangeable. For instance, every DNA molecule in a sample could contain a nucleosome in approximately the same region, but slight shifts in its precise center would produce broad positioning data while occupancy remained high. A region can also have a well-defined preferred position occupied in only a subset of cells.

The intrinsic DNA sequence contributes to positioning because different sequences vary in their bending and twisting properties. A sequence that more readily adopts the required geometry may associate more favorably with a core. But in living cells, competing DNA-binding proteins, nearby nucleosomes and ATP-dependent rearrangements can override intrinsic preferences. An observed positioned particle is not necessarily a particle whose location is encoded by DNA alone.

Distinguishing these processes prepares us for later study of gene expression. If an experiment shows increased access to a promoter after ATP-dependent remodeling, it does not necessarily establish that transcription has increased; transcription also depends on appropriate machinery and regulatory signals. It does establish a plausible structural step that can make access possible.

14. How the same building block is maintained in living cells

Histones must be supplied wherever DNA is newly packaged, but free basic histones can associate with nucleic acids and other cellular components inappropriately. Histone chaperones are proteins that bind histones, assist their transport or deposition, and limit nonproductive interactions. The word chaperone refers to assistance with proper molecular handling rather than to a permanent structural component of every nucleosome.

During DNA replication, the parental DNA duplex is opened so each strand can serve as a template. Nucleosomes in the path of the replication machinery cannot simply remain as intact obstacles. Histone components are displaced or transferred locally as the fork advances; parental histones are recycled onto daughter DNA, and newly synthesized histones supplement the supply. Assembly can proceed through association of H3–H4 with DNA followed by H2A–H2B dimers. The outcome is restoration of arrays on both new duplexes rather than leaving half the daughter DNA permanently naked.

Histone synthesis and deposition are coordinated with DNA replication, but replacement of components can also occur outside S phase. A chromatin particle may undergo repeated changes as proteins access DNA. A variant is an alternative form of a histone that can replace a standard histone at particular sites or under particular conditions. For example, H3.3 is a replacement H3 variant deposited in many contexts independently of replication; H2A.Z is a variant of H2A associated with distinctive local particle properties. Such variants retain broadly related structural roles without requiring identical behavior.

A particularly useful structural example is CENP-A, an H3-related histone found in centromeric chromatin in many eukaryotes. Its incorporation helps establish a specialized particle that contributes to centromere identity. The complete structure and function of the centromere will be developed later. The relevant lesson here is that different regions of the same chromosome can use related but nonidentical protein building blocks.

Canonical particles dominate the standard description of eukaryotic nuclear chromatin, but the pattern is not universal across all cellular DNA. Most bacteria organize their chromosomes with nucleoid-associated proteins rather than the canonical eukaryotic H2A–H2B–H3–H4 octamer. Many archaea have histone-related proteins and histone-based DNA packaging, but their assemblies need not match the eukaryotic octamer. Mitochondrial DNA in mammals is organized in nucleoids containing proteins such as TFAM, not conventional nuclear nucleosomes. Mature sperm of many animals also replace much of their histone-based packaging with protamines, although the extent of replacement is species dependent.

These exceptions do not invalidate the nucleosome model. They show that the model is a statement about a particular widespread mechanism of DNA organization, not a universal law for all genomes. The identity of the biological sample must therefore be established before applying the textbook histone-count equations.

Replication-coupled assembly illustrates a conservation principle. Before replication, a segment of 20,000 bp with a mean 200 bp repeat contains about 100 particles under the idealized full-occupancy model. After replication, the two daughter copies together contain 40,000 bp and require about 200 particles to recover the same average density. If all 100 parental octamers' worth of histone protein were recycled, an additional 100 octamers' worth of material would still be necessary. This is a mass-balance estimate; it does not say that every parental octamer remains intact or that exactly 50 old particles go to each copy. Tracking a labeled parental histone and counting all histones are different experimental tasks.

The structural distinction between histone variants and chemical modifications is equally important. A variant is a different protein sequence, often encoded by a distinct gene. A post-translational modification changes a chemical group on an existing histone protein after synthesis. Both may affect interactions, but a variant can differ in several surface properties while a modification may alter one site. If a gel or antibody detects a modified H3 molecule, it does not establish that this molecule is H3.3. Conversely, detecting H3.3 does not specify its modification state.

15. Integrating structural evidence and avoiding common misconceptions

Consider an investigator comparing two preparations from the same cell type. Both yield similar amounts of DNA after purification. The first shows a regular series of MNase-resistant fragments, whereas the second loses this pattern after proteins are removed before digestion. The appropriate conclusion is that protein association contributes to the recurring protection pattern. To determine whether those proteins are canonical histones, the investigator must establish their composition. To determine whether H1 is present, a specific protein measurement or suitable corroborating evidence is needed. The DNA length distribution alone cannot supply every structural detail.

Suppose another experiment removes H1 from an array without removing H2A, H2B, H3 or H4. Under matched conditions the particles retain core-sized protected DNA, but microscopy shows a less compact arrangement. This supports a distinction between local core formation and stabilization of linker organization. It would be incorrect to count seven remaining histone molecules in each core, because H1 was never one of the eight. The core may be retained while its surrounding organization changes.

A third experiment compares arrays with equal repeat lengths but different accessibility to an enzyme. The result need not be contradictory. Equal spacing says nothing definitive about the momentary exposure of an individual site, the positions of specific protein contacts or the geometric arrangement of the arrays. Conversely, a shift in repeat length does not demonstrate a change in the number of core histone types. A single measurement should be interpreted at the physical level it actually probes.

The following structural distinctions are useful for revision. A core particle is not the same object as the full repeat; a chromatosome is not an octamer containing a fifth core histone; the left-handed path of the duplex axis is not left-handed B-DNA; and a regular DNA ladder is not a direct image of histone composition. Histones are generally basic proteins but their positive charges do not make the entire chromosome electrically neutral in any simple fixed ratio. Chromatin remains an environment of interacting ions, proteins and nucleic acids.

The basic particle provides a compact, modular and reversible means of associating proteins with DNA. Its repeated arrangement also creates a new problem: how thousands or millions of particles cooperate to organize long chromosomal regions without requiring the entire array to be rigid. The next chapter considers that collective behavior, including the limits of idealized fiber models and the structural roles of chromosome-associated proteins.

A practical examination strategy is to identify the level of measurement before selecting an answer. A nuclease-protected footprint concerns DNA accessibility; a repeat-length measurement concerns average spacing; a protein composition measurement concerns molecular identity and stoichiometry; microscopy describes spatial organization under the conditions of observation. A perturbation may alter one of these levels without visibly altering another. The strongest reasoning uses two independent observables and explicitly checks whether an alternative explanation can produce the same result. This approach is more reliable than memorizing one fixed chromosome packing diagram.

16. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Question 1

Which composition describes the canonical octamer?

A. One each of H1, H2A, H2B, H3 and H4; B. Two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4; C. Four H1 and four H3; D. Eight H4

Answer and explanation

B. Two copies of four core histones give eight proteins. A confuses histone types with molecules; H1 is external. C and D omit essential core components.

Question 2

What does the 147 bp value ordinarily represent?

A. Full repeat length in all cells; B. Linker length; C. DNA associated with the canonical core particle; D. The chromatosome footprint including H1

Answer and explanation

C. Approximately 147 bp is wrapped on the core. A is variable, B is the intervening segment and D usually includes further protected DNA.

Question 3

Which statement about DNA winding is accurate?

A. B-DNA becomes uniformly Z-DNA; B. DNA strands must separate; C. The duplex axis winds left-handed while the DNA remains predominantly right-handed double helix; D. DNA passes through a sealed octamer ring

Answer and explanation

C. Two levels of winding differ. A confuses superhelical path with local helix form; B and D are not required for wrapping.

Question 4

H1 is removed selectively. Which is the most direct prediction?

A. Every H3 is removed; B. Linker-associated protection may decline; C. DNA loses its phosphates; D. Every core becomes a tetramer

Answer and explanation

B. H1 associates near the DNA entry/exit and linker. A and D confuse external H1 with core constituents; C would involve a chemical alteration of DNA.

Question 5

Why do DNA and histones associate favorably?

A. Negative histone lysines repel DNA; B. Covalent DNA–histone bonds are obligatory; C. Histones destroy base pairing; D. Basic histone side chains interact with negatively charged DNA and other complementary surfaces

Answer and explanation

D. Noncovalent contacts compensate for the cost of DNA bending. A reverses charges, B invents a requirement and C incorrectly implies unwinding the duplex.

Question 6

Which method most directly established the detailed spatial arrangement of core histones?

A. X-ray crystallography of the core particle; B. Counting chromosomes in metaphase; C. DNA gel alone; D. Measuring genome size

Answer and explanation

A. Structural diffraction analysis resolves molecular arrangement. B and D concern larger-scale information, and C reports DNA lengths rather than atomic arrangement.

Question 7

Assertion: Histone H1 is not part of the canonical octamer. Reason: H1 associates near the DNA entry and exit rather than forming one of the four paired core histone types.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. The linker-associated structural role is precisely why H1 is distinct from the core. B misses the connection; C and D deny true statements.

Question 8

Which property can differ even when nucleosome repeat lengths are identical?

A. The positions of every base pair in a defined DNA sequence must change; B. Nucleosomal accessibility; C. The definition of base pair; D. The number of DNA strands in a duplex

Answer and explanation

B. Equal spacing does not imply identical exposure or arrangement. A is not necessary; C and D are unrelated.

Question 9

Which structure contains a central (H3–H4)₂ tetramer and two H2A–H2B dimers?

A. A canonical octamer; B. A hexasome lacking one dimer; C. A tetrasome; D. Histone H1

Answer and explanation

A. The tetramer and two dimers sum to eight. B has six core molecules, C four and D is a distinct histone.

Question 10

Which statement about histone tails is most accurate?

A. They are all rigid and buried; B. They are the DNA backbone; C. They are flexible extensions that can contact DNA and other particles; D. All modifications neutralize lysine charge

Answer and explanation

C. Tail contacts and modifications affect local structure and recognition. A and B misidentify tails; D confuses acetylation with other modifications such as methylation.

17. Part C — Experimental and quantitative reasoning

Question 1

Partial MNase digestion produces bands at 185, 385, 585 and 785 bp; strong digestion yields 147 bp. Find the mean linker length.

A. 38 bp; B. 53 bp; C. 185 bp; D. 200 bp

Answer and explanation

B. Adjacent bands differ by 200 bp, so R = 200. L = 200 − 147 = 53. A uses the first trimmed fragment instead of the slope. C and D are fragment/repeat lengths, not linker length.

Question 2

A 24,000 bp array contains 120 complete repeats. How many H4 molecules and core-associated base pairs are expected?

A. 120 and 24,000; B. 960 and 17,640; C. 240 and 17,640; D. 240 and 24,000

Answer and explanation

C. Two H4 per core gives 240; 120 × 147 = 17,640 bp. A undercounts H4, B counts all core histones and D includes linker DNA.

Question 3

A linear 12-core array has 40 bp internal linkers and no terminal DNA. Calculate its length.

A. 2,204 bp; B. 2,244 bp; C. 1,764 bp; D. 480 bp

Answer and explanation

A. 12 × 147 = 1,764; 11 × 40 = 440; sum 2,204. B incorrectly uses 12 linkers. C and D each omit one DNA category.

Question 4

For 250 cores with mean H1 occupancy 0.6, how many histone proteins of all five types are expected?

A. 1,150; B. 2,250; C. 2,150; D. 150

Answer and explanation

C. 250 × 8 = 2,000 core histones and 250 × 0.6 = 150 H1, total 2,150. A undercounts the core, B assumes full H1 occupancy and D counts only H1.

Question 5

Two arrays of 36 kb have repeats of 180 and 240 bp, respectively. Assuming complete repeats, how many additional octamers occur in the shorter-repeat array?

A. 50; B. 150; C. 200; D. 400

Answer and explanation

A. 36,000/180 = 200 and 36,000/240 = 150, so difference 50. B and C are individual counts; D confuses the octamer difference with a difference in total core histone molecules.

Question 6

A researcher sees a 166 bp protection class in untreated chromatin but not after H1 depletion. The 147 bp class persists. Which interpretation fits best?

A. Loss of all histone cores; B. Loss of some linker-associated protection with core preservation; C. Loss of 19 bp from every genomic repeat; D. Conversion to single-stranded DNA

Answer and explanation

B. Selective loss of the longer footprint fits reduced H1 protection. A conflicts with persistence of core-sized DNA; C and D confuse digestion products with original DNA structure.

Question 7

The same DNA is digested before and after removing proteins. Only the chromatin preparation yields periodic bands. What can be concluded?

A. Protein organization contributed to periodic protection; B. H1 is the sole protecting protein; C. All DNA is arranged identically in vivo; D. DNA fragments are histone molecules

Answer and explanation

A. The control supports a contribution of associated proteins. B and C exceed the evidence, and D confuses DNA electrophoresis with protein analysis.

Question 8

A closed plasmid has Lk = 300 before assembly. Five nucleosomes form without strand passage. What is Lk before any topoisomerase action?

A. 295; B. 292; C. 300; D. 305

Answer and explanation

C. Linking number is invariant without strand passage/breakage. A would be a simplified expectation after topoisomerase relaxation and removal of particles. B confuses superhelical turns with ΔLk; D is unsupported.

Question 9

A chromatin remodeler moves a nucleosome center by 30 bp while protein occupancy stays unchanged. Which conclusion is warranted?

A. DNA was deleted; B. The octamer was converted into H1; C. Footprint position changed without necessary loss of the particle; D. The cell completed replication

Answer and explanation

C. Position and occupancy are different. Sliding changes the DNA segment wrapped without requiring sequence loss. A, B and D are not implied.

Question 10

An H4-tail truncation leaves the 147 bp footprint and octamer protein composition intact but reduces array compaction. Which explanation is most consistent?

A. Interparticle interactions weakened; B. All DNA became naked; C. H1 became a core histone; D. The DNA double helix changed its sequence

Answer and explanation

A. Tail loss can weaken contacts among particles while preserving core assembly. B contradicts the protection data, C changes the definition of the core and D has no evidence.

Question 11

Equal-length domains have the same mean repeat length, but a binding protein reaches an edge site more easily than a dyad site. Which mechanism explains the difference?

A. All nucleosomes must dissociate; B. Short transient unwrapping exposes edge DNA; C. The core DNA always has no bases near its edge; D. The dyad is outside the chromosome

Answer and explanation

B. Local unwrapping can reveal edge positions without complete removal. A is unnecessary and C and D are structurally false.

Question 12

A 200 bp repeat has 147 bp core DNA. A mutation increases the mean repeat to 210 bp without changing core length. Which statement follows under the ideal model?

A. The core gained ten bp; B. Mean linker increased from 53 to 63 bp; C. H1 is necessarily absent; D. Every histone has been lost

Answer and explanation

B. Subtract core length from each repeat length. A contradicts the premise; C and D cannot be inferred from spacing alone.
