1. Why cells exchange DNA rather than merely copy it

DNA replication preserves information by making another copy of an existing sequence. Homologous recombination uses the same basic language of complementary bases, but it changes the relationship between DNA molecules. A strand from one molecule can pair with a complementary strand in another molecule, displacing its previous partner. Once such a pairing has been established, the second molecule can serve as a template for synthesis, or the connected molecules can undergo a rearrangement of their strands. The result can be restoration of missing sequence, a new combination of inherited markers, or both.

Consider a chromosome broken through both strands at the same position. A single-strand lesion can often be repaired by reading the other strand at that position. A double-strand break is different: at the gap between the broken ends there may no longer be a continuous template on either strand. If an intact sister chromatid contains the same sequence, the broken chromosome can use information stored in that physically separate molecule. The first challenge is to find it. The second is to establish pairing without indiscriminately melting all the available DNA. The third is to copy or exchange information and then separate the two molecules safely. Homologous recombination addresses each of these challenges in a coordinated series of molecular reactions.

The word homologous in this context means that the DNA regions share corresponding sequence sufficiently similar to support the reaction. It does not require that two entire chromosomes be identical. Maternal and paternal copies of a chromosome share the same general sequence organization, but often differ at particular bases. Sister chromatids usually carry nearly identical sequences because they originate from the same replication event. A donor plasmid can also carry a region that corresponds to part of a chromosome. These situations provide different biological substrates for the same fundamental type of DNA recognition.

The repair outcome should not be confused with a genetic crossover. A break repaired using a sister chromatid may yield precisely the original sequence, so an ordinary genetic analysis might reveal no novel allele combination. Nevertheless, strand exchange and DNA synthesis have occurred. Conversely, a crossover between two nonsister chromatids can alter the linkage of markers without changing the number of copies of either marker in the complete collection of meiotic products. A molecular reaction must therefore be distinguished from the genetic signature by which it is detected.

Another distinction is between a reciprocal exchange of chromosome arms and a local replacement of sequence information. In a reciprocal crossover the distal DNA regions of two chromatids become associated with different proximal regions. In gene conversion a recipient acquires sequence corresponding to a donor without a necessarily detectable reciprocal replacement at that donor. One can observe gene conversion within a crossover product or within a noncrossover product. These are two different descriptions of genetic outcomes, not mutually exclusive labels.

Recombination has to be controlled because the same process that can rescue a broken chromosome can connect repeated sequences at incorrect genomic positions. Accurate pairing with a sister chromatid tends to preserve the organization of the genome. Pairing with a dispersed repeat can instead produce a deletion, inversion or exchange between chromosomes. The important biological problem is not simply to maximize strand exchange, but to accomplish an appropriate exchange and complete it in a way that preserves functional chromosomes. This balance between flexibility and stability will explain many of the phenotypes considered later.

2. DNA substrates, homology and strand identity

The double helix is made of two antiparallel polynucleotide chains. At each ordinary base-paired position, one strand contains a base complementary to the base on the other. Adenine ordinarily pairs with thymine and guanine with cytosine. The two backbone directions are opposite: if one chain is followed from its five-prime end toward its three-prime end, its partner runs three-prime to five-prime in the same spatial direction. Keeping track of this geometry is indispensable when predicting recombination products.

Suppose that the top strand of a donor duplex reads 5′-AGTCCGTA-3′ from left to right. The lower strand reads 3′-TCAGGCAT-5′ in that same drawing. A free strand identical to the top strand cannot form an ordinary Watson–Crick duplex with the donor top strand, but can pair with the donor lower strand. If the free strand enters the double helix, the original top strand becomes locally displaced. The invading strand and displaced strand have corresponding sequences and polarity, while the invading strand and its new pairing partner have complementary sequences and opposite polarity. A student who understands these distinctions can identify which of the strands a polymerase will use as template later.

A short stretch of identity between DNA molecules is not equivalent to a complete biological recognition signal. In homologous recombination the productive pairing generally extends across a region of sequence correspondence. Stable strand exchange is influenced by the length of compatible sequence, the number and arrangement of differences, the structure and accessibility of the substrates, and the proteins present. There is no single universally applicable minimum length that describes every RecA-family reaction and every organism. A simplified exam problem may specify a threshold; such a threshold should be used as a condition of that problem rather than memorized as a universal law.

Distinguish the physical strands from the sequence information they carry. During the reaction a parental strand can remain covalently intact while it changes its base-pairing partner. The new duplex can contain one strand originating from each parent. If the two parents differ at a particular nucleotide, the new duplex can contain a mismatch at that position. This mixed-origin base-paired region is called heteroduplex DNA. Even where the sequences match perfectly, a heteroduplex can exist in the physical sense: the two strands have different molecular origins. A visible mismatch is a possible consequence, not a requirement for formation of a heteroduplex.

A nick is a discontinuity in the phosphodiester backbone of one strand, without necessarily losing a nucleotide or breaking the opposing strand. A single-stranded gap is a region in which nucleotides are missing from one strand, leaving its complement exposed. A double-strand break interrupts both backbones. These are chemically different structures, and they are not interchangeable labels in diagrams. The original Holliday model begins with a pair of nicks at corresponding positions in homologous duplexes. A commonly used contemporary framework begins instead with a double-strand break in one molecule, processed to expose single-stranded tails. Both models explain strand exchange but do not prescribe the same initial lesion.

A free three-prime hydroxyl group is useful for a separate chemical reason. DNA polymerase adds an incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphate to this group. When the three-prime end of an invading strand is appropriately paired to a donor template, the end can prime DNA synthesis. A five-prime single-stranded tail may have sequence similarity and may participate in some DNA interactions, but it cannot simply substitute for a three-prime primer terminus in the standard extension reaction. The distinction is about direction of covalent synthesis, not just which end is drawn on the right.

The words homologous chromosome and homologous DNA also need to be separated. In a diploid organism homologous chromosomes are the corresponding members of a chromosome pair. Homologous DNA regions may occur on sister chromatids, on homologous chromosomes, or at duplicated positions that are not allelic. A repeated segment on a different chromosome can be homologous as a DNA sequence without making those chromosomes a normal homologous pair. This fact underlies the risk of nonallelic homologous recombination. A question about a crossover must specify whether the substrates are sisters, homologs or repeated regions, because their exchange can have different genetic consequences.

3. The genetic observations that demanded a molecular explanation

The classical picture of recombination was developed before the enzymes responsible for strand exchange had been identified. Genetic crosses demonstrated that markers located on the same chromosome could be inherited in new combinations. A reciprocal exchange between homologous chromatids accounted for many such observations. However, studies of fungi revealed segregation patterns that could not be accounted for merely by exchanging the chromosome segments on either side of a locus.

Fungal meiosis is particularly useful for this question because the products of a single meiotic event can often be recovered together. Instead of averaging alleles across thousands of unrelated offspring, the investigator can ask whether the products of an individual meiosis retain the expected allele balance. For a diploid heterozygous for A and a, ordinary replication followed by meiosis produces four chromatids in which two carry A and two carry a, provided that no conversion or mutation changes the allele state. A reciprocal crossover changes associations between neighboring markers but does not by itself change this two-to-two inventory at the locus.

Occasionally the products contain three copies of one allele and one of the other. This observation is called non-Mendelian segregation in the context of a single meiotic tetrad. It does not require violation of the chromosomal separation mechanism. If one chromatid has acquired the alternative sequence during recombination, the chromatids can separate normally yet carry a changed inventory of alleles. The scientific challenge was to connect a local, nonreciprocal genetic event to a molecular process that could also explain reciprocal exchange.

In 1964 Robin Holliday proposed a mechanism for gene conversion in fungi. The proposal connected the genetic data to the double-helical structure of DNA. A strand from each homolog could exchange its pairing relationship with the other duplex, generating a joint intermediate. As the exchanged region extended, strands from different parental DNA molecules could pair across an allelic difference. Correction of a mismatch in such a region could restore the previous allele or convert the allele state. A second choice, involving separation of the joint DNA structure, could determine whether distal markers were exchanged. Thus one model connected the local sequence event to the larger chromosome-level event.

The model was valuable because it offered a structural explanation of gene conversion without requiring an entire new round of chromosomal replication. It was also experimentally useful: a DNA junction with four duplex arms was a prediction that could be investigated using model substrates, structural analysis and proteins acting on branched DNA. The later discovery that enzymes can recognize, migrate and cleave these junctions provided a biochemical basis for the kind of intermediate that the genetic model required. The history illustrates how a genetic observation can motivate a molecular structure, which can in turn be tested.

The original model is not the only path to a homologous recombinant. In 1983 Szostak, Orr-Weaver, Rothstein and Stahl proposed a double-strand-break repair model of recombination. In that framework a break in one chromatid is followed by processing of its ends and invasion of an intact homologous duplex. The intact donor provides information for repair synthesis. The resulting intermediates can include two Holliday junctions rather than the single junction of the classical model. This framework explains why missing DNA can be restored through recombination and also connects recombination more directly to DNA damage repair.

Later genetic and molecular observations showed that many noncrossovers are generated without resolution of a stable double Holliday junction. A newly extended invading strand can be displaced and anneal to the other broken end. This pathway is known as synthesis-dependent strand annealing. The lesson is not that the 1964 model has become irrelevant. Rather, the four-way junction and heteroduplex concepts remain foundational, while the initiating structures and routes to final products must be distinguished. The appropriate model is selected according to the experimental evidence and the biological context.

The two models make separable predictions. If a system requires resection of a double-strand break, a defect in end processing may reduce repair even if a junction-cleaving enzyme remains functional. If a single junction is supplied directly as a synthetic substrate, end processing is unnecessary for testing whether that particular junction can be resolved. A well-designed experiment separates the step being tested from upstream events. This is why the existence of a Holliday junction in a biochemical reaction does not establish that every recombination reaction starts with symmetrical nicks.

4. Creating a recombination-competent DNA end

A fresh double-strand break can have many different end structures. Two strands may end opposite one another, or one strand may protrude. Some broken ends carry chemical modifications that must be removed before they can participate in ordinary DNA synthesis or ligation. The recombination pathway commonly converts these heterogeneous starting structures into a simpler functional substrate: single-stranded DNA with an available three-prime end. This preparation is known as end resection.

In a standard double-strand-break repair model, the strands that terminate in five-prime ends at the break are preferentially removed. The corresponding three-prime-ended strands become exposed. Consider a horizontal DNA duplex with the upper strand running five-prime to three-prime from left to right. At the break, the left duplex has an upper three-prime end and a lower five-prime end. The right duplex has an upper five-prime end and a lower three-prime end. Removal of the lower strand from the left end and the upper strand from the right end therefore exposes two tails of opposite spatial orientation, but both tails terminate in three-prime ends at the original break region. The two ends are chemically equivalent in the capacity to prime synthesis.

Resection is often described as net five-prime to three-prime degradation of the DNA strand that must be removed. This is a description of the net result and not a claim that every individual enzyme involved has the same catalytic polarity. For example, in eukaryotic cells several nuclease and helicase activities cooperate to produce the final three-prime single-stranded substrate. An endonucleolytic incision away from a blocked end can be followed by processing in different directions. At the examination level the important result is which strand remains available and what information that strand can recover.

In Escherichia coli the RecBCD complex is a major route for processing double-stranded DNA ends. It combines DNA unwinding and nuclease functions, and its activity is regulated by encounter with an appropriately oriented Chi sequence. The complex alters its processing behavior and promotes RecA loading onto the recombination-competent single-stranded product. The Chi response is important because it connects a bacterial DNA-end processing enzyme to the downstream homology-search reaction. Chi is not a universally conserved origin of homologous recombination or a substitute for the homologous region in the donor. Other bacteria can use other end-processing systems.

Single-stranded gaps can also be recombination substrates. In E. coli, RecFOR pathway proteins promote productive RecA assembly at appropriate single-stranded gaps, in the presence of single-strand binding protein. A gap does not necessarily need to be converted to a two-ended break before homology-dependent repair can occur. A useful exam comparison is that RecBCD is associated with double-strand end processing, whereas RecFOR is associated especially with handling gaps and facilitating RecA loading. Both routes converge on a recombinase filament, rather than each catalyzing an entirely unrelated form of strand exchange.

In eukaryotes, the MRN complex in mammals, corresponding to MRX in budding yeast, contributes to initiation of resection with CtIP or its yeast counterpart Sae2. Further processing involves nucleases and helicase-associated activities, including EXO1 and DNA2-containing pathways in suitable systems. These names are less useful than understanding their order: end recognition and processing first, then protection of the resulting single-stranded DNA, then assembly of a strand-exchange filament. The recombinase does not by itself perform all of the preparatory processing.

An exposed single-stranded region is vulnerable to nucleases and inappropriate base pairing with itself. Bacterial SSB and eukaryotic RPA bind single-stranded DNA, reduce secondary structure and protect the substrate. However, because these proteins occupy the DNA, the recombinase has to be loaded in a regulated fashion. In vertebrates BRCA2 facilitates assembly of RAD51 on suitable single-stranded DNA. Without the appropriate mediator, abundant RAD51 protein may not be sufficient to restore an efficient strand-exchange reaction. The quantity of an enzyme and its productive localization are separate experimental variables.

End processing also influences competition between repair pathways. Conventional end joining works with broken ends that can be brought together and processed for ligation. Extensive resection changes the substrate into one better suited to homology-dependent reactions. In many eukaryotic somatic cells, cell-cycle-dependent regulation makes extensive resection more favorable after replication has begun, when a sister chromatid becomes available. This does not mean that every break in S phase undergoes homologous recombination or that every G1 break must use the same alternative pathway. The timing of damage, end structure, local context and availability of a donor all influence the result.

Predict the phenotype of an end-processing mutant by asking which substrate it fails to produce. A cell may detect a break but accumulate poorly resected ends. If the recombinase is functional, supplying it with an already prepared single-stranded substrate in a biochemical assay could reveal normal strand pairing. By contrast, an inactive recombinase would fail to pair even a properly processed substrate. This distinction prevents the common inference that every recombination-deficient mutation directly destroys strand-exchange catalysis.

5. RecA and RAD51: building a filament for a homology search

A DNA molecule in a cell is surrounded by vast amounts of unrelated DNA. A free single strand cannot efficiently inspect this material by simply waiting for every potential donor helix to melt. RecA in bacteria and RAD51 in eukaryotes solve this problem by organizing the invading DNA into a nucleoprotein filament. The DNA is associated with many recombinase subunits in an extended arrangement that supports the recognition of compatible sequence in a duplex. The protein–DNA complex is the active search unit.

ATP binding favors the functional DNA-binding state of RecA-family recombinases. RecA and RAD51 are ATPases, meaning that they can catalyze hydrolysis of ATP. Their nucleotide states influence filament stability, assembly, turnover and the dynamics of the reaction. It is misleading to assert that hydrolysis supplies a fixed amount of energy for each complementary base pair formed. The contribution of nucleotide binding and hydrolysis varies among particular recombinases and reactions. In simplified biochemical systems, early pairing or limited exchange can occur with conditions that restrict hydrolysis, while turnover and extended processing depend strongly on nucleotide cycling.

The first major stage is called presynapsis. It includes production of single-stranded DNA, its binding by protective proteins, and assembly of the recombinase filament. Synapsis is the productive encounter of the filament with a donor duplex and establishment of matching base-pairing relationships. Postsynapsis includes the reactions after that pairing, such as repair synthesis, exchange extension, processing of joint molecules and removal of the recombinase. These stages are functional categories. Their boundaries may overlap in the actual molecular system, but they help assign the defect of a mutant to a specific part of the pathway.

During the homology search, the nucleoprotein filament makes transient encounters with duplex DNA. A nonmatching encounter is unlikely to progress to a stable extended pairing. A sufficiently compatible region allows the invading strand to form new base pairs with the complementary donor strand. As the interaction extends, the original donor partner is displaced from that region. The process differs from recognition of a short enzyme binding motif because the identity of the target depends on the sequence of the invading DNA itself. If the invading sequence changes, the preferred homologous target may change even though the recombinase protein is unchanged.

An ATP-binding-defective mutant can fail because it does not establish the active filament architecture. A mutant that binds nucleotide and DNA but hydrolyzes ATP inefficiently can instead form unusually persistent complexes. Such a mutant need not exhibit precisely the same phenotype as an ATP-binding mutant. A static observation of DNA-associated protein is therefore insufficient to establish efficient exchange. One must also examine the progression from binding to pairing and the production of completed recombinant molecules.

The eukaryotic recombinase DMC1 is a related protein used in meiosis in many organisms, where it helps promote interactions between homologous chromosomes. RAD51 has central roles in mitotic repair and also participates in meiotic recombination, with the relative contributions depending on organism and meiotic context. Do not equate RecA with DMC1 in every biological setting, or infer that RAD51 exists only in mitosis. What is conserved is the general principle of recombinase-mediated DNA pairing and exchange, while the control of donor choice and participation of associated proteins differs.

Classic purified-protein experiments helped demonstrate that RecA directly participates in homologous pairing. Shibata and colleagues reported pairing of single-stranded DNA with homologous superhelical duplex DNA using purified E. coli RecA in 1979. Reconstitution was particularly informative because it reduced the number of cellular components needed for the measured activity. The observation supported a direct biochemical role rather than a merely indirect regulatory association. It did not imply that purified RecA alone completes all chromosome repair in a living cell.

When designing an interpretation of such an experiment, compare homologous and unrelated donor sequences under otherwise matching conditions, include a reaction without recombinase and distinguish DNA association from true base-paired product formation. The topology of donor DNA can influence the ease of invasion, so equivalent substrates should be used when possible. Measuring a stable joint molecule rather than total protein-bound DNA makes the readout more informative. A mutation that eliminates protein–DNA binding would affect both measurements, whereas one that binds DNA but fails to recognize homology could preserve the first and eliminate the second.

6. Energetics of strand exchange and DNA processing

A central feature of homologous pairing is that it conserves the distinction between thermodynamic possibility and kinetic accessibility. Two complementary DNA strands can form a stable duplex, but this does not imply that a complementary sequence buried inside a double helix is immediately accessible. In a donor duplex, the bases that could pair with the incoming strand are already paired. Productive invasion requires an intermediate in which new pairing interactions can be tested before the system commits to displacement of the previous partner. The recombinase filament facilitates this exchange while avoiding the requirement that the entire donor molecule become single-stranded. This helps explain how sequence recognition can occur within a long molecule whose overall double-helical organization remains intact.

The free-energy change of the complete reaction must not be inferred merely by counting hydrogen bonds. Base stacking, electrostatic effects, DNA deformation, hydration, protein binding and nucleotide-dependent transitions all influence the energetic landscape. In a simplified exchange between perfectly matching strands, the number of ordinary base pairs may be nearly the same before and after exchange. Yet the transition between the two arrangements can face a kinetic barrier, because the original pairing must be disrupted and the incoming strand must be appropriately aligned. A recombinase can facilitate productive transitions without creating a new chemical rule for base recognition. ATP-dependent changes in the filament can regulate the lifetime of intermediates and the ability of the system to reset for further rounds of activity.

A second energetic distinction concerns the separate activities of the downstream enzymes. DNA polymerase uses incoming deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates to extend the primer, releasing pyrophosphate. ATP-dependent motor proteins such as RuvB use nucleotide hydrolysis to promote DNA translocation. Ligases use an activated chemical intermediate to seal a phosphodiester bond, with ATP or NAD-dependent systems occurring in different organisms. These are different energy-coupling reactions. If a biochemical assay contains ATP but lacks the deoxyribonucleotide substrates needed for DNA synthesis, the ATP cannot simply replace all four DNA precursors. Similarly, the ability to synthesize DNA does not replace the need for an appropriate junction-processing activity.

The distinction is experimentally useful when the stages of the pathway are isolated. In a pairing assay designed to measure formation of a D-loop, the absence of deoxyribonucleotide incorporation does not necessarily indicate failure, because synthesis may not be part of the reaction being measured. In a complete repair reaction, by contrast, missing sequence cannot be restored if the nucleotide precursors and required polymerase activity are unavailable. A successful early-stage assay is therefore not evidence that all later energy-dependent reactions have occurred. The appropriate interpretation always depends on the defined starting substrate and the product being measured.

This perspective also clarifies why no universal ATP-per-base-pair stoichiometry should be assigned to homologous recombination. The number of ATP molecules hydrolyzed by a filament depends on its assembly, turnover, duration and the circumstances of the reaction; the amount of motor activity required to move a junction depends on its substrate and pathway. A numerical examination problem may specify a motor's hydrolysis rate and an ideal step size, permitting a conditional calculation of displacement. Such a calculation describes that hypothetical motor model, not a general energetic law of chromosome repair.

7. Strand invasion and the displacement loop

The term strand invasion describes a change in the identity of the strands paired within a donor DNA molecule. Consider a single-stranded tail protruding from a broken recipient duplex. The tail carries sequence that also occurs in the intact donor. Its recombinase filament interacts with the donor duplex, and the complementary strand of the donor pairs locally with the recipient tail. The other donor strand must become unpaired from its original partner over the invaded interval. That displaced strand is not necessarily cut. It can remain covalently continuous with the rest of the donor chromosome.

The resulting three-stranded structure is often called a displacement loop, or D-loop. It is important to interpret three-stranded correctly: the invading strand is not forming three conventional Watson–Crick bonds at each position. In the established heteroduplex portion two strands are paired, and the third is displaced. There can be additional transient contacts during recombinase-mediated recognition, but the stable pairing product is understood in terms of an ordinary complementary duplex and a locally unpaired strand. The invading strand often extends from a recipient double-stranded region, and the remainder of the donor molecule is still double-stranded on both sides.

The geometry can be reconstructed using a short sequence. Suppose a recipient tail ends in 5′-GACTTA-3′, with its three-prime end available for extension. A donor contains the corresponding top-strand sequence 5′-GACTTAGC-3′ and its bottom-strand complement 3′-CTGAATCG-5′. If the recipient invades the donor, its last adenine pairs with the thymine on the donor bottom strand. The next template base in the direction of synthesis is cytosine, so the first newly incorporated nucleotide will be guanine. The following template base is guanine, so the next incorporated nucleotide will be cytosine. The recipient strand has gained the sequence GC by copying the donor, without requiring transfer of a donor strand segment into the final recipient DNA.

This distinction between copying information and transferring a covalent segment is essential. A diagram of newly synthesized DNA should use a separate symbol for new nucleotides instead of implying that every donor-colored tract consists of original donor molecules. Newly synthesized DNA has the sequence dictated by the donor template, but its nucleotides come from the cellular nucleotide pool. A genetic assay does not generally distinguish those molecular origins. A label attached to the original donor strands, by contrast, could in principle provide additional information about physical strand inheritance.

The topological state of the donor can affect strand invasion. Negative supercoiling tends to favor local unwinding in many duplex contexts, because a portion of the torsional strain can be relieved by opening the DNA. A relaxed or positively supercoiled molecule may respond differently to the same reaction. This is one reason classical in vitro pairing experiments often use a defined circular donor. However, homologous recombination in cells is not restricted to negatively supercoiled naked DNA circles. Chromatin organization, associated proteins and topological enzymes influence access to actual cellular substrates.

A D-loop is a joint molecule, but it is not automatically a classical four-way Holliday junction. A three-stranded invasion intermediate can be extended by polymerase, can be reversed, or can participate in additional strand interactions that eventually produce a four-arm junction. The distinction matters in a question that supplies an electron micrograph or a drawing of connected DNA molecules. The number of lines drawn across one another on the page is not sufficient to determine whether a genuine covalent junction is present. One needs to know how strands continue through the central region and which are paired.

Following invasion, repair synthesis requires an extendable three-prime end and a functional polymerase. If the invading end carries a terminal mismatch, extension may be impaired even though an upstream section of the tail is successfully paired. If it lacks the necessary hydroxyl group, the usual polymerase reaction cannot proceed until the end is repaired. Thus a positive strand-pairing measurement need not predict successful DNA restoration. A mechanistic assay can detect the D-loop independently of nucleotide incorporation and thereby distinguish the defect.

After extension, the new strand may remain associated with the donor long enough to capture the other broken end, or it may dissociate and pair with a complementary strand from the recipient. Different choices can lead to different intermediate structures even when the same donor and the same initial break are used. Strand invasion is therefore a shared starting operation for several repair outcomes, not a guarantee that a crossover will occur.

8. Reconstructing the original single Holliday junction model

The original Holliday model is best learned by tracing individual backbones rather than memorizing a drawing. Begin with two homologous double helices aligned in the same sequence orientation. Label their top strands P and Q, and their complementary bottom strands p and q. Both upper strands run in the same spatial direction from five-prime to three-prime, while both lower strands run in the opposite direction. The labels record molecular origin; they do not mean that the four strands carry entirely different sequences.

The historical scheme begins when equivalent strands of both duplexes acquire nicks at corresponding positions. For example, each upper strand can be nicked at the same aligned coordinate. Each nick provides a discontinuity between a three-prime hydroxyl and a five-prime end, under the simple chemical assumptions of the model. The uncut lower strands remain continuous. The corresponding upper strand segments can exchange pairing partners, such that a segment originating in one parent is now paired with the complementary strand of the other parent. At the crossover position the relevant ends can be covalently joined.

DNA ligation closes the nick after the exchange of partners. The two molecules are then connected through a structure in which four duplex arms converge at a central strand crossover. The unbroken strands still record their original continuity, while the newly rejoined strands traverse the junction from one parental region to another. Because of these connections, the molecules cannot be interpreted as simply two independent duplexes touching one another. The junction is a real DNA intermediate.

This model does not imply that two DNA helices can freely pass through one another without changing their chemical connections. The strand exchange is made possible by cleavage and joining in the proposed mechanism. A line that crosses another in a textbook illustration could otherwise represent one strand passing over another in three-dimensional space without any covalent connection. To avoid the ambiguity, diagrams should label strands and use explicit joins or gaps where bonds are formed or cut.

The classical model accounts for heteroduplex formation because exchanged strands can form new duplexes over an interval. If corresponding alleles are identical across that interval, the new base pairs are normal even though their strands have different origins. If an A:T pair in one parental duplex corresponds to a G:C pair in the other, mixed pairing can yield A:C and G:T mismatches depending on which strands have exchanged. Such mismatches can subsequently be corrected. In this way the single structural intermediate offers an explanation for nonreciprocal sequence changes.

Branch migration is a movement of the junction along homologous DNA. As the crossover position moves, some base pairs in the original duplex arrangements are replaced by base pairs between strands of different parental origins. The length and location of the heteroduplex region can therefore change. This migration does not require cutting the phosphodiester backbone at every nucleotide position. Instead, it primarily involves breaking and reforming base-pairing interactions as the junction moves, with the strands maintaining the continuity established by the earlier exchange.

A junction can be represented in an open square arrangement, with four duplex arms extending from its center. Under some conditions, DNA arms stack to produce a more compact crossed arrangement. Different drawings can correspond to the same covalent structure in different conformations. Rotating one part of a model may change which lines look horizontal or vertical without changing the molecular outcome of a particular cleavage. This is why an instruction such as cut horizontally to make a crossover is unsafe unless it is tied to a particular labelled diagram.

Two distinct pairs of strands can be cleaved in the simple single-junction model. The cleavage positions and the manner in which the resulting nicks are joined determine which parental left and right regions are connected in the final duplexes. One arrangement restores the original flanking-marker association, producing a noncrossover. The other exchanges the association of the flanks, producing a crossover. Both products can retain a local heteroduplex region. The two cleavage choices are geometric possibilities, not evidence that a real enzyme chooses each with equal probability.

The symmetric-nick model is historically important because it unifies crossover and conversion in a simple structural scheme. It is not a universal description of how recombination begins in cells. A modern diagram of repair following a double-strand break can produce a single joint structure or a double Holliday junction by a different sequence of operations. Answering an exam question correctly requires recognizing which model the question has specified. If the question states that the DNA begins with paired single-strand nicks, use the classical scheme; if it begins with a break and resection, follow the donor-template repair model.

9. Branch migration: movement of a junction without movement of a gene

Branch migration changes the location at which strands pass between the two duplexes. Imagine that two homologous duplexes have a junction at coordinate zero, and that they share matching sequence for fifty bases to the right. If the junction moves ten bases to the right, the region over which the parental strands are paired with new partners changes accordingly. No gene has physically traveled ten base pairs along its covalent backbone. The backbone sequence remains ordered; the boundaries between different pairing relationships have moved.

The distinction is similar to changing the contact surface between two complementary strips rather than moving the printed characters on either strip. This analogy is useful only for the base-pairing aspect. A real DNA junction has covalent connections, helical twist and constraints on its three-dimensional motion that a flat strip does not reproduce. The actual branch point can move in either direction depending on the substrate and the forces acting on it.

A perfectly homologous tract can permit spontaneous branch migration through thermal fluctuations. At a particular step, existing base pairs open and new ones form. When both alternative pairing states have comparable stability, there may be little free-energy bias for movement in one direction. The position can therefore fluctuate rather than travel steadily. This explains why a motor protein can be biologically valuable even though the underlying base-pair rearrangement is chemically possible without a continuous energy input.

Heterology, such as a nonmatching nucleotide or a larger insertion, can impede migration by making an alternative paired state less favorable or geometrically difficult. A mismatch is not always an absolute barrier. The size and nature of the difference, surrounding sequence, DNA structure and motor activity determine whether migration can proceed. At the level of a simplified model a block may be treated as an impenetrable barrier if explicitly stated. In a biological interpretation it is preferable to predict a reduced probability or altered distribution of migration rather than a universal stop at every mismatch.

In E. coli RuvA and RuvB provide a well-characterized system for active branch migration. RuvA recognizes the four-way junction and helps organize the DNA arms into a suitable configuration. RuvB is an ATP-dependent motor that associates with the DNA and drives translocation through the complex. As DNA passes through the organized junction, old pairing partners are replaced by new ones. RuvB functions in oligomeric rings in the characterized bacterial system. The directionality and rate depend on the way the motor complex is assembled and on the substrate.

RuvA is often described as a junction-recognition protein and RuvB as a branch migration motor. These assignments are more informative than treating both as generic helicases. RuvA does not serve as the principal ATP-driven translocase in this system, and RuvB does not provide the canonical resolving endonuclease activity. A defect in RuvA can prevent efficient targeting or organization of the motor even if RuvB retains ATPase activity in isolation. A defect in RuvB can preserve recognition of a junction while preventing efficient ATP-driven movement.

The RecG protein in E. coli is another DNA translocase involved in processing branched DNA structures. Its existence illustrates that RuvAB is not the only route by which branched intermediates can be remodeled. The functions of these systems overlap in some contexts but are not identical for every substrate. For a standard examination comparison, RuvAB is the model junction-migration system, RuvC is the model junction resolvase, and RecA is the principal strand-exchange recombinase. Other organisms need not use the same full set of bacterial proteins.

Consider two assays using junction substrates. In one assay a branch point is initially positioned at an engineered location and a readout reports whether migration reaches a downstream position. In another assay the branch point is fixed by sequence differences and cleavage products are measured. A protein deficient in ATP-dependent migration could show reduced activity in the first assay yet permit normal cleavage in the second, provided the resolvase is functional and the substrate is suitable. A cleavage-defective resolvase would have a different pattern. Such orthogonal assays reveal the order of molecular steps more clearly than survival alone.

The biological function of branch migration is not limited to extending the heteroduplex to its maximum possible length. Movement can reposition the junction relative to available cleavage sites, remodel intermediate structures or help particular routes of intermediate removal. Excessive or misdirected processing could encounter sequence differences or influence which markers lie in the final heteroduplex tract. A crossover position measured genetically therefore does not necessarily reveal the initial position of strand invasion or the exact original break site.

A quantitative analogy makes the difference between thermal and motor-driven movement clearer. In an idealized one-dimensional random walk, each step moves a junction one base pair left or right with equal probability and the steps are independent. After n steps, the mean signed displacement is zero, but the root mean squared displacement is the square root of n base pairs. After one hundred steps that characteristic displacement is ten base pairs, not one hundred. A hypothetical motor moving consistently in one direction at a defined step rate would show a much larger net displacement in the same number of steps. Real branch migration has sequence-dependent energies, boundaries and protein interactions, so the random-walk relationship is an educational model rather than a measured universal law.

10. Resolving a four-way junction and predicting the products

Formation of a joint molecule is not sufficient to restore two separate chromosomes. The covalent connectivity of the intermediate has to be changed or removed. In the classical single-junction scheme this is accomplished by resolution: a structure-specific nuclease introduces a pair of appropriately positioned cuts, and the resulting nicked duplex products can be sealed. A conventional resolvase does not merely dissociate proteins from DNA. It changes the continuity of selected DNA strands.

The bacterial RuvC protein is a model Holliday junction resolvase. It recognizes the junction structure and introduces related incisions in two strands. The geometry of the cuts allows separation into two duplex molecules containing ligatable nicks, assuming the usual end chemistry. DNA ligase subsequently restores backbone continuity. RuvC can exhibit preferences for sequences at which cleavage occurs, which illustrates that recognition of the DNA structure and selection of a particular cleavage position are distinct features. RuvA and RuvB can cooperate with RuvC in processing the intermediate.

An important experimental advance was the use of synthetic four-way junctions as substrates. Because the four strands and their sequences could be specified in advance, investigators could distinguish a nuclease that recognized the branch structure from one that simply degraded duplex DNA. Appropriate comparisons include an ordinary duplex, a junction substrate, a reaction without the candidate enzyme and products analyzed under conditions that reveal the lengths of cut strands. A preference for branched substrates together with paired cuts at related positions supports the interpretation of a junction-resolving activity. It does not by itself establish how frequently that route is used in living cells.

To predict products without depending on the orientation of a drawing, assign the left flanks of the two parental duplexes the labels L1 and L2, and their right flanks R1 and R2. The initial duplexes are L1 joined to R1 and L2 joined to R2. A noncrossover product pair retains those left-right associations. A reciprocal crossover product pair consists instead of L1 joined to R2 and L2 joined to R1. A heteroduplex near the original pairing region can occur in either case. The flanking labels should be far enough from the locally exchanged tract that mismatch correction at those markers is excluded in the question.

For a concrete genetic example, let one parental chromatid carry markers A and B, and the other a and b, with a recombination region between the markers. Before exchange the two combinations are AB and ab. A noncrossover preserves AB and ab, apart from any changes within the intervening tract. A reciprocal crossover produces Ab and aB. In a normal meiosis involving four chromatids, a single crossover between two nonsister chromatids affects only the participating two; the other two can retain the parental combinations. Counting the products requires specifying whether the denominator is all four chromatids or just the two participating ones.

A local conversion is different. If an internal marker C becomes c through repair of a mismatch, the flanking labels could still remain A and B in the converted chromatid. Alternatively, the same local conversion could accompany a crossover that places A next to b. The occurrence of a local change cannot establish the linkage of distal markers. In experimental genetics, placing distinguishable markers on both sides of the recombination region enables the two outcomes to be scored separately.

Consider a hypothetical culture in which one hundred independent two-ended repair events are scored with informative flanking markers. If seventy are noncrossovers and thirty are crossovers, the crossover fraction among the scored events is 0.30. It does not follow that thirty percent of all chromatids in a population are recombinant, because not all chromatids need have undergone a scored event. Nor does it imply that a particular resolvase always cuts in one orientation thirty percent of the time: some events may have followed a route that never created a junction. The observation is a distribution of final outcomes, not a direct measurement of one enzyme's microscopic cleavage preference.

Failure to resolve a junction can impair chromosome segregation even if much of the original damage has been repaired. Two physically connected DNA molecules cannot always separate freely during cell division. Unresolved joint molecules may therefore accumulate in cells defective in late-stage processing. Such accumulation is conceptually different from a failure of strand invasion, which may cause broken or resected DNA to persist without forming the same junction population. Time-resolved measurements of DNA structures can discriminate these phenotypes.

Eukaryotes possess several pathways for processing junction-like intermediates. Structure-selective nucleases can generate crossover or noncrossover products depending on the intermediate and cleavage geometry. In contrast, the dissolution of a double Holliday junction by coordinated migration and topological unlinking gives a noncrossover. These processes should not be collapsed into a generic final step simply called ligation. The important distinction is whether strands are cut and rejoined to separate the molecules or whether the joint topology is eliminated without classical paired junction cleavage.

A common diagrammatic trap arises when the same four-way junction is redrawn after rotation. A pair of strands that appeared vertical can now appear diagonal or horizontal. The resulting crossover classification must not change because a drawing was rotated. Trace the covalent path from L1 through the rejoined central region and identify whether it reaches R1 or R2. Then trace L2 independently. If one final duplex connects L1 to R2, the reciprocal partner in a simple balanced two-product model connects L2 to R1. This method is more reliable than memorizing the direction in which a pair of scissors is drawn.

11. Double-strand-break repair: from one damaged chromatid to two junctions

The original Holliday scheme begins by modifying both participating duplexes at corresponding positions. A double-strand-break repair scheme begins with an asymmetric problem. One chromatid has lost continuity while the other remains intact. The intact chromatid contains information the broken molecule needs. In the standard two-ended repair model, the first task is to create single-stranded three-prime tails at the break; the second is to establish a donor interaction that permits copying; the third is to reconnect the two broken ends in the correct order.

Label the damaged molecule X and the intact homologous donor Y. After resection, one tail of X can invade Y and pair with the complementary strand. The remaining tail of X is not yet paired to Y. Polymerase extends the invading three-prime end using Y as a template. As the extension grows, a segment of the displaced Y strand becomes available to pair with the complementary sequence on the second tail of X. This interaction is described as second-end capture. It helps connect both sides of the original break to the same donor-associated structure.

The polarity of the two broken ends explains why second-end capture is possible. Each original end carries a three-prime terminus, but the tails belong to opposite strands of the broken chromosome. If the first invading strand copies a region of the donor, the newly synthesized tract includes sequence complementary to the corresponding strand on the other recipient end. The displacement and extension processes provide an opportunity to establish the second pairing. The result depends on compatible sequence and correct orientation; merely bringing both broken ends near a donor does not ensure the original order can be recovered.

Additional synthesis and ligation can establish a double Holliday junction intermediate in the classical double-strand-break repair model. The two junctions define a connected region between X and Y. The two molecules are joined at two distinct branch points rather than just one. Their shared structure can include heteroduplex DNA and stretches of newly synthesized DNA. The exact arrangement of mixed-origin base pairs depends on the path by which the intermediate developed and how the junctions moved.

A useful mental distinction is between repair of the gap and removal of the joint molecule. A polymerase can fill in the missing sequence while the two duplexes remain connected. Completion of synthesis therefore does not prove that the final chromosomes are independent. Conversely, removing a joint molecule before sufficient synthesis has occurred may leave a partially restored recipient that still requires processing. An experimental readout that detects recovery of an internal marker is different from a readout that detects physical separation of full-length products.

The 1983 double-strand-break model proposed that broken DNA could be enlarged into a double-stranded gap and that repair of such a gap using homologous information could account for gene conversion. A gap physically lacks some recipient sequence. If the corresponding donor carries an alternative allele, synthesis of new DNA across the missing region may directly reproduce the donor allele. This is a route to conversion that does not depend exclusively on correction of a mismatch. Heteroduplex formation around the gap can provide additional opportunities for mismatch correction.

The standard textbook image of two junctions is a model of one possible intermediate, not a promise that each break follows this route. A cell can use multiple pathways after the initial invasion. If a question states that a stable double Holliday junction was isolated, it is appropriate to analyze resolution or dissolution of that junction. If it only states that a damaged gene was repaired using a homologous donor, the presence of such an intermediate cannot be assumed. Likewise, detection of a D-loop does not establish that the second end has been captured.

The contrast between the original model and the double-strand-break model can be tested by labeling the recipient and donor separately. Suppose the recipient contains a missing segment and the donor is intact. A system that merely exchanges preexisting strand partners without any synthesis cannot restore the total number of nucleotides in the recipient unless it transfers sequence material or uses another source. A polymerase-dependent restoration of the missing region is therefore evidence for a copying step. To distinguish copying from redistribution of donor DNA, one can track the integrity of the donor as well as incorporation of new nucleotides. A conclusion about the initial lesion must also rely on a direct observation or defined starting substrate.

The biological importance of the two-ended repair model is that it explains how a chromosome can recover information from a physically separate molecule while retaining its original flanking organization. It also explains why repair can sometimes produce a crossover. The next issue is to understand which operations determine whether the two molecules emerge with their original distal associations or exchanged associations.

12. Alternative routes after invasion: SDSA, resolution and dissolution

One possible outcome after repair synthesis is that the extended recipient strand leaves the donor duplex. The donor's two original strands can pair with one another again. The newly extended recipient strand then pairs with complementary sequence on the other resected end of the broken chromosome. Further synthesis where needed and ligation complete restoration of the damaged molecule. This route is known as synthesis-dependent strand annealing, abbreviated SDSA.

The logic of SDSA can be understood by considering what is carried away from the donor. The extended strand contains sequence copied during its temporary association with the donor. It need not carry a distal donor chromosome arm. When it anneals to the other broken end of the recipient, the left and right recipient flanks are brought together through the recovered sequence. Consequently, the standard SDSA route yields a noncrossover with respect to distal markers. Local gene conversion remains possible because the copied interval may contain donor-specific alleles.

A noncrossover should not be mistaken for a reaction with no strand invasion. SDSA requires productive homologous pairing and synthesis before the new strand is returned to the recipient. It lacks the stable double-junction stage of the classical alternative, not the earlier strand-exchange operation. A mutation that impairs RAD51-mediated invasion can reduce SDSA just as it can reduce double-junction repair. Conversely, a factor required for a specific junction-processing pathway may leave some SDSA events relatively unaffected.

The name annealing in SDSA refers to pairing of complementary single-stranded sequences during reunion of the two sides of the broken molecule. This differs from initial strand invasion, which requires accessing a complementary strand already paired within a donor duplex. Simple annealing of two exposed single strands does not face precisely the same structural challenge as invasion of an intact double helix. The two operations can therefore use different proteins or be affected differently by experimental conditions.

Single-strand annealing, abbreviated SSA, is a different homology-dependent repair route. If a double-strand break lies between two direct repeats, resection can expose complementary repeat sequences on opposite sides of the break. Annealing of those repeats, removal of unpaired tails and restoration of continuity can eliminate the intervening segment and one repeat copy. This mechanism need not involve RAD51-mediated invasion of an intact donor duplex. Although both SSA and SDSA contain the word annealing, their substrates, dependence on donor-directed synthesis and typical genetic consequences differ. SSA is introduced here only to prevent confusion with the strand-exchange mechanisms of the node.

In the double-junction route, the two junctions can be processed by nucleases. The pattern of resolution across both junctions determines how the distal regions of the two DNA molecules are connected. A simple labelled model may represent each junction as one of two possible switching operations. If the two operations together restore original distal associations, the final molecules are noncrossovers. If they leave an odd number of net switches, the final molecules are crossovers. This is a bookkeeping model for a specified strand connectivity, not a claim that the molecular cuts are always independent or equally likely.

A second route for eliminating a double Holliday junction is dissolution. In a well-studied eukaryotic model, a RecQ-family helicase such as BLM in mammals promotes convergent migration of the two junctions, while topoisomerase III alpha and associated proteins help remove the resulting DNA interlinks. The key principle is topological unlinking without the classical paired endonucleolytic cuts at both junctions. Dissolution yields noncrossover products. This provides an important way for somatic cells to repair DNA while avoiding potentially harmful exchange of distal chromosome arms.

It is useful to understand the role of the topoisomerase by recalling that two DNA molecules can become linked even if their complementary base-pairing relationships have been rearranged. A helicase can alter the structure of a joint molecule without necessarily removing every topological interlink. A topoisomerase transiently changes covalent strand continuity in a controlled manner and allows strand passage followed by restoration of continuity. In the dissolution reaction the helicase and topoisomerase activities address different aspects of the same physical problem. Calling dissolution a simple melting reaction would miss the requirement to remove the remaining linkage.

Resolution and dissolution should therefore be distinguished by mechanism and outcome. Resolution involves nuclease-mediated processing of junctions and can yield different flanking-marker associations. Dissolution removes double-junction topology in a way that retains noncrossover distal associations. SDSA avoids formation of a stable double-junction substrate by removing the extended invading strand and pairing it with the other recipient end. All three routes can restore a damaged sequence using homologous information, but their late intermediates and outcome tendencies are different.

Repair of a one-ended break is another context in which the two-ended scheme cannot be transferred without modification. A collapsed replication fork can leave only one accessible broken DNA end in a particular local structure. If that end invades homologous DNA, synthesis may have to extend much farther to restore a functional chromosome. Break-induced replication is a standard conceptual example of such a route. The reaction does not rely on immediate capture of a nearby second end in the same way as ordinary two-ended repair. A detailed molecular treatment of this pathway would go beyond the scope of this chapter, but the distinction explains why the number and arrangement of DNA ends matter.

A question about final products may provide an observed increase in crossovers after loss of a dissolution-promoting protein. It is reasonable to propose that intermediates that would otherwise dissolve can now be processed through crossover-producing routes. It is not reasonable to conclude solely from that observation that the mutation increases the initial rate of strand invasion. An increase in one final outcome can reflect altered partitioning between late pathways rather than increased initiation. This distinction between flux into the repair network and distribution among branches is a recurring theme in molecular reasoning.

13. Gene conversion: why local inheritance can be nonreciprocal

Gene conversion is recognized genetically when one DNA sequence becomes more like another related sequence in a manner that does not require a reciprocal replacement at the donor. The name does not imply conversion of an entire gene into an unrelated gene or a change in the fundamental properties of a chromosome. The affected tract may be smaller than a gene, encompass a whole gene, or extend beyond a gene depending on the recombination event. At the examination level the most useful unit is an informative marker located within a region affected by pairing or repair synthesis.

Consider two homologous duplexes containing different alleles at one aligned base pair. One duplex contains A paired with T and the other contains G paired with C. If a strand from the first duplex pairs with a complementary-origin strand from the second, a mismatch can appear because the bases at that position no longer represent the original matched pair. For example, the A from one parent may become paired opposite C from the other. Recognition and repair of that mismatch can replace one base according to the information on the opposing strand.

If the A-containing strand is corrected using the C-containing strand as template, the appropriate replacement is G. If the C-containing strand is corrected using the A-containing strand as template, the appropriate replacement is T. The chemical restoration of ordinary base pairing is successful in both cases, but the genetic outcomes differ. One product now carries G:C, the other A:T. The direction of mismatch correction determines whether a local marker has been restored to its previous state or converted to the alternative state, relative to the recipient being followed.

Mismatch repair in recombination should not be reduced to the familiar rule that replication errors are corrected by removing the newly synthesized strand. The substrate here may be a heteroduplex made from two preexisting parental strands or a mixture containing newly synthesized DNA. Strand-discrimination signals and repair biases depend on the biological system and the particular intermediate. In an exam problem one may be told which strand was excised, or which strand provides the template. That information should be used explicitly rather than assuming that the donor allele always wins.

A gap-repair event can produce the same observed allele conversion without a mismatched base being the immediate cause. Suppose a damaged chromatid originally carried the allele a but a double-strand gap removes the entire distinguishing sequence. If repair synthesis uses a donor carrying A, the newly synthesized recipient tract may carry A. The original a information has been lost at the gap. In this case the sequence transfer follows directly from copying the donor. The final genetic observation of conversion alone does not prove which of these molecular routes was used.

The relationship to ordinary segregation becomes clear in a tetrad. Begin with two A-bearing chromatids and two a-bearing chromatids following replication of a heterozygote. Suppose one of the a chromatids undergoes repair and becomes A, while the other three chromatids retain their marker states. The tetrad now contains three A chromatids and one a chromatid. The donor can remain A; no new a allele has to be created at that donor. The reciprocal balance characteristic of ordinary crossover is therefore absent at the converted locus.

In some fungi the products of meiosis undergo an additional mitotic division before spore formation. If all four meiotic products contain fully paired DNA at the marker, their allele states are duplicated during this division. A three-to-one tetrad then yields a six-to-two octad, under the simplifying assumption that all products are recovered and no additional change occurs. A two-to-two tetrad ordinarily yields four-to-four. If a heteroduplex mismatch persists through meiosis and is segregated during the subsequent DNA replication and mitosis, the two descendants of a single meiotic product can carry different alleles. This phenomenon is called postmeiotic segregation.

For example, three meiotic products with fully resolved marker states could contribute four A spores and two a spores after the additional division, while the fourth product contains an unrepaired A/a heteroduplex and contributes one A and one a descendant. The octad then contains five A and three a. An octad of five-to-three therefore provides a particularly useful conceptual example of persistent heteroduplex information being separated in a later division. The precise ordering of spores can provide more information, but ordered-ascus linkage analysis is reserved for the inheritance unit.

Notice that conversion is defined relative to the parental alleles and the segregation pattern, not by whether the converted allele is dominant. If A and a encode two different molecular sequences, either can in principle be transferred in a suitable context. Dominance is a relationship between genotype and phenotype, whereas conversion is a DNA sequence event. A dominant phenotype can mask a particular marker in some assays, so a genotype-sensitive readout is preferable when the purpose is to measure conversion itself.

Gene conversion can also occur between related sequences within a genome, such as duplicated genes. Over evolutionary time, repeated transfer of sequence between copies can contribute to similarity within a family. This helps explain why sequence comparisons between paralogous genes need not reflect only independent accumulation of mutations after duplication. The outcome depends on donor choice, conversion direction and selection. It does not follow that all duplicated genes are constantly homogenized or that every region of similarity was produced by conversion.

An important distinction is between allele balance at one site and linkage of markers on either side. A tetrad can show a three-to-one ratio at an internal locus while the external markers retain the parental combinations. That is evidence for a conversion associated with a noncrossover at the scored flanks. A different tetrad can show the same internal three-to-one ratio and recombinant external markers. That is conversion associated with a crossover. Conversely, a reciprocal crossover between markers can occur while the internal locus retains an ordinary two-to-two ratio. The classification must specify which observation is being described.

The absence of conversion at a marker does not prove that a heteroduplex never formed there. The original allele could have been restored, both parents might have carried the same base pair, or a mismatch could have been handled in a way that leaves no detectable departure in the particular assay. A tract may also lie outside the few marker positions chosen by the investigator. Genetic reporters measure informative differences, not every molecular exchange. A conversion tract inferred from marker positions is therefore a region bounded by observations, not necessarily the exact physical boundary of the heteroduplex.

14. Template choice in mitotic repair, meiosis and bacterial recombination

The same elementary pairing mechanism serves different biological objectives. In many mitotically dividing eukaryotic cells, homologous repair is most beneficial when it restores the original chromosome without creating new associations of distant regions. A sister chromatid is usually an attractive donor because its sequence is nearly identical to that of the damaged molecule and it is held near the original chromosome after replication. Repair can then recover sequence with minimal genetic change. Mechanisms favoring noncrossover outcomes are particularly compatible with this objective.

A homologous chromosome can also serve as a donor when suitable sequence is available. However, it may contain different alleles. Local copying can alter the genotype of one chromatid. A crossover between homologous nonsister chromatids in a mitotic cell can, depending on subsequent chromatid segregation, produce daughter cells that have lost heterozygosity over distal regions. Loss of heterozygosity means that a locus previously represented by two distinct alleles becomes represented by the same allele state in both chromosomal copies of a descendant cell. It need not be accompanied by a reduction in chromosome copy number.

For a simplified example, assume a diploid cell carries homologs with distal markers B and b. After replication there are two B chromatids and two b chromatids. A reciprocal exchange between one B chromatid and one b chromatid can alter which distal segment is connected to a particular centromere. If the daughter cell inherits chromatids that now both carry B distally, it can become B/B at that distal marker even though it retains two chromosomes. Another segregation arrangement can retain B/b heterozygosity. The crossover creates the possibility of loss of heterozygosity; it does not establish that every daughter will exhibit it.

Meiotic recombination places different demands on the same general molecular process. Programmed double-strand breaks initiate recombination in many meiotic systems, with Spo11 providing the canonical initiating activity. Donor-choice mechanisms favor interactions between homologous chromosomes in many organisms, and selected recombination events mature into crossovers. These crossovers contribute to the physical associations between homologs that support their orderly segregation during the first meiotic division. Other repair events become noncrossovers. The chromosome movements, meiotic stage descriptions and genetic mapping relationships belong to the earlier cell-division and later inheritance chapters.

The distinction between intersister and interhomolog exchange is particularly important when interpreting a meiotic phenotype. A recombinase may be active yet fail to support the normal pattern of interhomolog interactions if donor choice is altered. Counting total repair products is then insufficient to demonstrate normal meiotic function. An assay that distinguishes sister and homolog templates provides more specific information. Similarly, counting only crossovers cannot determine the total number of initiated breaks, because many breaks are repaired without distal exchange.

In bacteria there is no ordinary eukaryotic meiotic division, but homologous recombination is crucial for chromosome repair and can also help integrate incoming genetic material. If a DNA fragment acquired by transformation or another transfer process contains a region corresponding to the bacterial chromosome, it can participate in RecA-mediated homologous pairing. The process of DNA entry and the process of establishing it within a chromosome are distinct. DNA transfer can occur without stable chromosomal integration, and a recombination defect can reduce integration even if entry remains intact. A plasmid with a functional replication origin may be maintained independently rather than recombining into the chromosome.

Bacterial cells can also contain replicated chromosome regions that serve as repair templates. Their donor availability depends on chromosome copy number, replication status and species-specific physiology rather than the eukaryotic G1, S and G2 framework. It would therefore be wrong to conclude that bacteria cannot use template-guided repair because they lack eukaryotic sister-chromatid terminology or a conventional mitotic cell cycle. The underlying requirement is access to a suitable DNA template, not possession of a particular cell-cycle naming scheme.

In archaea, RadA is a related strand-exchange recombinase. Its existence, together with bacterial RecA and eukaryotic RAD51 and DMC1, illustrates an evolutionarily conserved solution to the challenge of homologous pairing. The conservation of a recombinase family does not imply conservation of every accessory factor or identical reaction regulation. For example, one should not transfer the E. coli RuvABC pathway unchanged into a diagram of mammalian chromosome repair. Comparative questions often test precisely this distinction between conserved principles and organism-specific molecular machinery.

15. Fidelity, inappropriate template selection and biological consequences

The ability to find a homologous region is central to accurate repair, but similarity is not sufficient to guarantee that the donor is the appropriate locus. A cell with a duplicated gene or a repeated sequence may contain several regions capable of pairing with the same invading strand. If the repair machinery chooses a copy in the wrong location, it can recover a chemically valid stretch of DNA while producing an abnormal chromosome arrangement. This is an important example of the difference between nucleotide-level fidelity and chromosome-level fidelity.

Consider a chromosome containing two direct repeats, both written in the same orientation. Represent the arrangement as L–X–M–X–R, where X denotes the repeat and M is a unique intervening region. If the two repeat copies align and undergo an appropriate intramolecular reciprocal exchange, the main chromosome can retain L–X–R and lose the intervening segment in an excised molecule. The exact physical fate of the excised product depends on the substrate topology and whether it is maintained. The reduction in copy number on the main molecule is a consequence of alignment between nonallelic repeats, not a failure to recognize their similarity.

If the repeats lie on different chromatids, an unequal reciprocal crossover can generate one product with a duplication and another with a corresponding deletion. To reason about such a case, draw both parental molecules with their repeats and intervening intervals. Offset the alignment so that the first repeat of one chromatid pairs with the second repeat of the other. Then exchange distal arms at the paired repeats. The product that retains both original intervening intervals plus an additional interval is the duplication product; its reciprocal partner loses an interval. Counting across the two products can conserve the total amount of DNA even though each individual product has abnormal copy number.

Inverted repeats provide another geometric possibility. Within a single chromosome, recombination between oppositely oriented homologous regions can invert the intervening DNA. Unlike deletion, inversion need not reduce the amount of sequence on the chromosome. It changes the orientation of the segment and hence potentially its relationship with promoters or neighboring sequences. In a different substrate configuration, particularly involving separate chromatids or chromosomes, inverted-repeat recombination can have more complicated outcomes. Do not apply an intramolecular inversion rule to a different arrangement without tracing strands.

The same topological principles are encountered in site-specific recombination, but the initiating recognition mechanism is different. A site-specific recombinase recognizes its characteristic DNA sites, whereas homologous recombination depends on correspondence between the interacting DNA sequences and uses a RecA-family strand-exchange mechanism in the canonical pathways discussed here. The presence of an inversion or deletion as a final product cannot identify which reaction made it. An investigator must examine the substrate requirements, the proteins involved and the sequence features at the exchange sites. Node 3.A.12 develops the site-specific alternative in detail.

Cells can reduce inappropriate exchanges through multiple layers of control. Donor proximity and chromosome organization favor certain interactions. Sequence differences can reduce successful pairing or extension, while mismatch-recognition systems can reject some interactions between imperfectly matched DNA. Regulation of end processing and recombinase assembly restricts the contexts in which potentially dangerous joint molecules form. Late processing mechanisms can favor noncrossovers where distal exchanges would be harmful. These controls are complementary rather than redundant; disrupting one does not necessarily eliminate all others.

A mutation in a mismatch-recognition protein can therefore have more than one consequence. It may reduce correction of mismatches after a heteroduplex is formed, altering gene conversion or postmeiotic segregation patterns. It can also relax barriers against exchange between divergent sequences in some systems. In interpreting an experiment, it is essential to distinguish a change in the formation of a joint molecule from a change in the way an existing mismatch is repaired. A reporter requiring a specific restored allele can confound these two effects if no independent readout of strand exchange is available.

Genome stability depends on the successful completion of recombination as well as the appropriate selection of donor. A cell with impaired RAD51 function may fail to repair damage using homologous templates and accumulate chromosome abnormalities. A cell with defective late junction processing may accumulate joint molecules instead. A cell that retains efficient invasion but uses the wrong donor may survive while carrying rearrangements. These three possibilities all connect recombination with genome instability but represent different causal mechanisms. A careful exam solution identifies the stage at which the defect acts rather than naming a disease as a substitute for mechanism.

16. How experiments distinguish DNA pairing, synthesis and completion

Biochemical evidence can establish what a purified protein is capable of doing, whereas cellular genetics can establish what happens when that activity is absent or modified in a biological system. Neither approach alone answers every mechanistic question. A purified-protein reaction may demonstrate homologous pairing in the absence of many cellular regulators, but it does not establish that the same substrate preference dominates in a nucleus. A mutant with reduced survival after DNA damage indicates that the missing activity contributes to survival, but does not directly reveal its precise biochemical substrate.

A simple strand-invasion assay can provide a homologous single-stranded substrate and a duplex donor. A readout that separates free substrate from a stable joint molecule tests whether productive association has occurred. Useful controls include a nonhomologous donor of comparable length and topology, a reaction without recombinase and a condition that lacks the necessary nucleotide cofactor. A product observed only with appropriate sequence correspondence and functional recombinase supports sequence-dependent joint formation. If a DNA-binding protein binds both homologous and nonhomologous substrates equally but a true joint product forms only in the homologous reaction, binding and pairing have been separated experimentally.

One limitation of such an assay is that an apparently slowly migrating DNA complex might contain protein-bound material rather than a specific DNA joint. Removing or accounting for protein before analyzing the DNA structure, comparing known standards and using a readout sensitive to the particular paired intermediate can help address this ambiguity. The goal is to infer structure from evidence, not from migration speed alone. A gel band that has shifted relative to free DNA is an observation; the statement that the band represents a D-loop is an interpretation requiring suitable support.

A repair-synthesis assay can be designed to detect incorporation of new nucleotides into the recipient after its three-prime end pairs with a donor. It is possible to compare productive pairing under conditions lacking polymerase activity with extension under conditions that support synthesis. If the amount of D-loop formation is normal but newly synthesized recipient DNA is strongly reduced, the defect likely lies at or after the transition to repair synthesis. Alternative explanations include instability of the nascent joint or a nonextendable primer terminus, so the design should assess those features where relevant.

A junction-migration assay can start with a defined four-way junction rather than requiring a preceding strand-invasion reaction. A reporter can distinguish the initial branch point from a branch point that has moved across a specified tract. Introducing the RuvAB system and ATP can test motor-driven movement in the bacterial model. A parallel control with no ATP can reveal the contribution of nucleotide-dependent activity, but some spontaneous movement may still occur if the junction has homologous arms. A substrate with sequence heterology that limits movement may help interpret the position-specific readout.

The resolving activity can be investigated using a junction whose structure is known. The labelled substrates should make it possible to distinguish cleavage at particular positions from nonspecific degradation. A conventional double incision can be inferred by mapping products from both affected strands and demonstrating that the products have the expected duplex organization. Adding a DNA ligase in a separate controlled assay can test whether the resulting breaks are compatible with sealing. A reduction in junction cleavage in a mutant reaction does not necessarily demonstrate that the enzyme has lost all DNA-binding activity; binding and catalysis are separate functions.

In a cellular experiment, a defined DNA break can be placed in a recipient containing distinguishable marker sequences. The investigator can provide a homologous donor with a sequence difference that can be copied into the repaired recipient. Recovery of the donor marker is evidence that information has been transferred, provided alternative sources of the marker have been excluded. Distal markers allow the investigator to distinguish repair with and without a crossover. A simple phenotype such as restoration of a functional gene can be useful for selection, but direct marker analysis can distinguish recombination from spontaneous reversion or other mechanisms that also restore the phenotype.

The denominator matters when interpreting a reporter. If fifty colonies carry a recombinant marker out of one hundred surviving colonies, the proportion among survivors is one-half. If only one hundred out of one thousand treated cells survived, the recombinant colonies represent five percent of the starting cells. Both statistics can be valid but answer different questions. A mutant that sharply reduces survival could appear to increase the recombinant fraction among survivors even while reducing the absolute yield of recombinant products. To compare pathway activity fairly, the experiment must report how the population was selected and what denominator was used.

A time course can reveal an order of events that a single endpoint obscures. Imagine that resected ends appear first, joint molecules rise later and fully repaired products rise after that. Such a sequence is compatible with a pathway in which end processing precedes invasion and joint processing precedes completion. It does not prove that every detected resected end becomes one of the measured joint molecules; some may enter other pathways. To strengthen the inference, combine time-course information with perturbations that block specific activities and see whether the expected upstream structures accumulate while downstream products decrease.

For example, a recombinase-defective cell might show resection but little stable joint formation, whereas a junction-resolution mutant could accumulate joint structures and show delayed appearance of separate products. An end-resection mutant might show less exposed single-stranded DNA and reduced joint formation. These expected patterns are not absolute experimental diagnoses, because proteins can have multiple functions and checkpoint feedback can alter the kinetics of several steps. The strongest conclusion combines multiple measurements and rescue with the functional gene. A successful rescue makes it more plausible that the phenotype is linked to the altered protein rather than an unrelated mutation.

The distinction between catalytic and structural functions is especially relevant for proteins in multiprotein assemblies. If RuvB is present but cannot hydrolyze ATP normally, a junction-recognition complex may still assemble. If RuvA is absent, the same motor may not be recruited productively. Complementing a mutant with a wild-type copy of the relevant gene can restore the phenotype, but a nonfunctional catalytic variant expressed at the same abundance may not. Appropriate checks of protein abundance and localization reduce the possibility that the supposed catalytic mutant actually fails merely because the protein is unstable.

17. Quantitative reasoning: conditional probabilities, tracts and repair outcomes

A useful calculation in recombination starts by defining what has been counted. A chromatid, a single molecular joint, a repaired break, a viable colony and a complete meiotic tetrad are different observational units. A fraction calculated using one denominator should not be relabeled as a fraction of another without a justified conversion. Most numerical mistakes in this topic arise from confusing these units rather than from difficult arithmetic.

Suppose an experiment records 400 cells with an induced break. Of these, 300 produce an identifiable completed repair product and 60 of those products are classified as crossovers by informative flanking markers. The completion fraction relative to the starting population is 300 divided by 400, or 0.75. The conditional crossover fraction among completed products is 60 divided by 300, or 0.20. The crossover yield relative to the starting population is 60 divided by 400, or 0.15. Multiplying the completion fraction by the conditional crossover fraction gives the last result because the events have been counted as nested categories. The units are fractions of the stated population; percentage values follow by multiplying by one hundred.

Now compare a second culture with the same 400 starting cells, 100 completed products and 40 crossover products. The conditional crossover fraction is 0.40, twice the value of the first culture. However, the absolute crossover yield relative to the starting population is 0.10, lower than the first culture. Thus the second culture does not necessarily initiate more recombination or produce more crossovers. It has a larger crossover share among the smaller number of completed events. A late pathway bias, a survival difference or selective failure of noncrossover products could all contribute to this observation.

Conditional reasoning can also describe a hypothetical mixture of repair pathways. Let f denote the fraction of successfully repaired breaks that enter a double-junction processing route, and let c denote the fraction of products of that route that are crossovers. Assume the remainder are repaired through a route that always yields a noncrossover and that each event is counted once. The total crossover fraction among successful products is then f multiplied by c. If 40 percent enter double-junction processing and 25 percent of those products are crossovers, the overall fraction is 0.40 times 0.25, or 0.10. If an observed overall fraction is 0.10, however, many pairs of f and c could explain it. The product alone does not identify both unknowns.

In a simplified labelled two-junction connectivity model, a local processing decision is represented by a binary variable. A value of zero preserves a specified distal pairing and a value of one switches it. Assume the first and second decisions are independent with probabilities p and q of switching, respectively. The overall crossover occurs when exactly one switch takes place. The probability is p times the quantity one minus q, plus q times the quantity one minus p. If p equals 0.2 and q equals 0.3, the result is 0.14 plus 0.24, or 0.38. If both probabilities are one-half, the result is one-half. These values arise from the assumptions of the model and not from an invariant biological ratio of resolution events. Coordinated processing or alternative pathways would violate the independence assumption.

A similar caution applies to estimates of heteroduplex extent. Suppose a recipient and donor differ at informative positions 20, 40, 60 and 80 base pairs to the right of a defined reference coordinate. The final recipient carries donor-specific alleles at positions 40 and 60, but retains its original alleles at 20 and 80. If conversion is assumed to have occurred in one continuous tract and every informative site within it is assumed to have converted, the tract must include the interval between positions 40 and 60 and cannot include positions 20 or 80. The smallest separation between the converted markers is twenty base pairs; if both endpoint nucleotide positions are counted, the interval contains twenty-one positions. The exact endpoints are not observable between informative markers.

In real data, mismatches within a continuous heteroduplex may be corrected differently at different positions, and donor-specific sequence can also arise by gap filling. The assumption that all informative positions within a heteroduplex convert is therefore not generally justified. Retention of an original marker does not necessarily exclude heteroduplex formation there. The safe conclusion from the example is that the two observed converted markers lie within the region of detected sequence transfer, and any stronger bound depends on the stated tract model. This is a standard illustration of the difference between an observation and a model-dependent inference.

Gene conversion frequencies can be expressed per meiosis or per product. If twelve of two hundred complete tetrads display a three-to-one segregation ratio at a marker, the fraction of tetrads displaying that pattern is 12 divided by 200, or six percent. If every such tetrad arose by conversion of exactly one chromatid and no other conversions occurred, then twelve chromatids out of the eight hundred total meiotic products are converted. That fraction is 1.5 percent. The latter result depends on the explicit assumptions; a different number of converted chromatids per exceptional tetrad or undetected restoration events would change the interpretation.

A single reciprocal crossover between two nonsister chromatids of a four-chromatid meiotic set yields two recombinant chromatids at flanking markers under the usual simple assumptions. Thus if exactly one such crossover occurs in forty percent of meioses and no other crossovers occur in the scored interval, the recombinant chromatid fraction across all meiotic products is 0.40 multiplied by 0.50, or 0.20. This calculation is not a general genetic mapping formula for intervals with multiple exchanges. Multiple crossovers and the identity of participating chromatids can alter the final marker patterns. Detailed mapping belongs to Unit 8, but the event-to-product distinction is essential here.

18. Integrating the pathway with replication, repair and chromosome behavior

The recombination pathway uses knowledge developed earlier in molecular biology. Its dependence on an extendable three-prime hydroxyl follows directly from the chemistry of DNA polymerization. The need for exposed DNA and suitable template orientation follows from antiparallel DNA architecture. The ability to repair a gap by copying a separate molecule follows from complementary base pairing and the conservation of information across related DNA sequences. These are not new chemical principles; recombination organizes familiar principles into a new cellular operation.

A defective DNA polymerase can impair repair synthesis even when recombinase-mediated pairing succeeds. A ligase defect can leave discontinuities after a junction is resolved, preventing full restoration of covalent integrity. A topological defect can prevent efficient separation of DNA molecules even when they have been synthesized to full length. Each of these proteins also has functions outside homologous recombination, which is why broad cellular phenotypes cannot automatically be assigned exclusively to recombination failure. Mechanistic specificity requires examination of the relevant intermediate.

Recombination can support recovery from replication-associated problems because replication forks depend on intact template continuity. A lesion or a break near a fork may create a substrate that cannot be handled by simply continuing ordinary polymerization. The presence of a nearby newly replicated sequence can provide an opportunity for homology-dependent rescue. This connects the principles of fork structure and DNA damage tolerance to homologous repair without making them identical. Translesion synthesis, for example, can bypass certain lesions using specialized polymerases, whereas homologous recombination can use sequence information available on another DNA molecule.

The cell cycle provides another point of integration. The period after chromosome replication supplies sister chromatids as closely related templates, and regulatory processes influence access to resection and downstream homologous repair. During meiosis, the use of homologous chromosomes and promotion of particular crossovers meet a different chromosome-segregation requirement. The existence of a crossover therefore should be interpreted in context: a desirable component of meiotic chromosome behavior may be undesirable in certain mitotic repair settings.

In biotechnology, homologous sequence can be used to direct a genetic change to a corresponding genomic region. A conceptual donor contains the desired sequence flanked by regions corresponding to the recipient target. Productive homology-dependent repair can transfer the intended sequence into the recipient. The intended edit, however, is not guaranteed by detection of a donor-associated marker alone; one must distinguish the expected local sequence from unrelated integration or other repair products. Detailed editing protocols and vector design belong to the methods and biotechnology units.

In agriculture, meiotic exchange between homologous chromosomes helps produce new combinations of existing alleles that plant and animal breeding can select. Gene conversion can also alter local sequence relationships. Neither process directs the organism to produce a useful combination merely because that combination would benefit the breeder. Variation is generated by molecular and chromosomal processes, while selection changes the representation of variants across generations. The distinction between generation of diversity and selection of diversity applies equally to evolutionary reasoning.

The strongest unifying way to solve a novel question is to identify the starting DNA structure, the functional substrate made from it, the protein responsible for the next operation and the measurement used to detect that operation. A free three-prime single strand predicts the potential for recombinase-mediated invasion and template-dependent synthesis. A four-way junction predicts a requirement for appropriate branch processing, not necessarily another homology search. A noncrossover tells us about distal connectivity, not about absence of earlier invasion. A conversion tells us about local information transfer, not necessarily reciprocal exchange. These logical relationships are more powerful than remembering a single pathway diagram in isolation.

Common examination traps

Homology is not complementarity between the same two named strands. The invader matches the displaced donor strand in sequence orientation; it pairs with the complementary donor strand.

Recombination is not synonymous with crossover. Noncrossover repair can involve genuine homologous strand invasion.

A Holliday junction is not a chiasma. A junction is a molecular DNA intermediate; a chiasma is a chromosome-level association reflecting crossover and cohesion.

A D-loop is not necessarily a Holliday junction. The displaced strand and invaded duplex do not automatically create a four-arm covalent junction.

A 3′ tail does not mean removal of the 3′ strand. Standard resection preferentially removes the strand ending 5′ at the break.

RuvABC roles are distinct. RuvA recognizes, RuvB migrates and RuvC resolves.

Cut direction is drawing-dependent. Trace labelled flanks rather than memorizing horizontal versus vertical.

Two cleavage choices do not mean equal biological probability. Enzyme preferences, pathway selection and coordinated processing alter outcome frequencies.

Gene conversion is not necessarily reciprocal. Donor-directed synthesis or mismatch correction may change one recipient while leaving donor unchanged.

A normal 2:2 ratio does not rule out heteroduplex. A mismatch may have been restored, or parental sequence may be identical at the scored locus.

Dissolution is not cleavage-based resolution. It uses convergent migration and topological unlinking to yield noncrossovers.

A high recombinant percentage among survivors may hide lower absolute yield. Report numerator and denominator separately.

Homologous recombination does not always preserve genome organization. Misalignment of repeats can produce rearrangements.

The original Holliday scheme is not the universal initiating mechanism. Distinguish paired nicks from modern break-and-resection pathways.

Active recall

Why is resection directional?

To expose three-prime ends capable of invasion and extension.

Which donor strand pairs with the invader?

The antiparallel complementary strand.

What makes an RPA-coated substrate recombinase competent?

Regulated RAD51 loading by mediators such as BRCA2 in vertebrates.

What is the distinction between branch migration and resolution?

Migration shifts pairing boundaries; resolution introduces cuts that separate joint duplexes.

How can gene conversion occur without a mismatch?

A missing recipient sequence can be copied directly from an alternative donor allele.

How do SDSA and dissolution differ?

SDSA avoids a stable double junction; dissolution removes the topology of an existing double junction.

Why can a crossover cause loss of heterozygosity?

Mitotic crossover plus particular segregation can make distal allele states identical in a daughter.

Why distinguish survival from repair yield?

Survival-based denominators can bias the apparent frequency of repair outcomes.

What makes RecBCD and RecFOR different?

They are model routes adapted especially to broken ends and ssDNA gaps, respectively.

What does a 5:3 octad illustrate?

Persistence of heteroduplex through meiosis followed by segregation in subsequent replication and division.

Answer and explanation

A. Binding and productive homology recognition can be separated

Equal association does not establish equivalent pairing. The stable sequence-dependent product is the informative observation.

8. Experimental — A nuclease cuts synthetic four-way DNA at paired positions but rarely cuts an ordinary duplex. What does this support?
  1. A universal equal crossover probability
  2. DNA replication licensing
  3. Proof of every cellular pathway's initiation
  4. Structure-selective resolving activity
Answer and explanation

D. Structure-selective resolving activity

Substrate preference and paired incisions support resolvase activity; the assay cannot establish in vivo frequency or initiation.

9. Experimental — A slowly migrating gel band increases after adding RecA. What additional control most directly rules out simple protein binding as the sole explanation?
  1. Increase sample volume without changing analysis
  2. Count colonies in a different organism
  3. Analyze DNA after accounting for or removing bound protein and test joint structure
  4. Add a different colored dye only
Answer and explanation

C. Analyze DNA after accounting for or removing bound protein and test joint structure

The band shift alone is ambiguous. Structure-sensitive analysis of the DNA distinguishes a joint product from protein-associated mobility changes.

10. Product tracing — Parental flanks are L1–R1 and L2–R2. Products are L1–R2 and L2–R1. What is the outcome?
  1. Noncrossover
  2. Unresolved junction
  3. Necessarily gene conversion at every marker
  4. Reciprocal crossover
Answer and explanation

D. Reciprocal crossover

Distal associations have switched reciprocally. Local marker conversion cannot be inferred from flanking connectivity alone.

11. Product tracing — Parental AB and ab have internal alleles C and c. Final flanks remain AB and ab but the AB recipient now carries c. What occurred?
  1. Local conversion with noncrossover at the flanks
  2. All four alleles have changed
  3. No information transfer
  4. Obligatory reciprocal crossover
Answer and explanation

A. Local conversion with noncrossover at the flanks

The local allele changed while flanking connectivity was retained. This distinguishes conversion from crossover.

12. Quantitative — Of 500 cells, 300 complete repair and 75 repaired products are crossovers. What is crossover fraction among completed products and yield among starting cells?
  1. 75%; 60%
  2. 25%; 15%
  3. 15%; 25%
  4. 60%; 75%
Answer and explanation

B. 25%; 15%

75/300 = 0.25; 75/500 = 0.15. The two denominators answer different questions.

13. Quantitative — WT: 1000 starting cells, 400 repairs, 100 crossovers. Mutant: 1000 starting, 100 repairs, 50 crossovers. Which is correct?
  1. Both yields equal 50%
  2. WT has larger conditional crossover share
  3. Mutant crossover share rises from 25% to 50%, but yield falls from 10% to 5%
  4. Mutant makes twice as many crossovers
Answer and explanation

C. Mutant crossover share rises from 25% to 50%, but yield falls from 10% to 5%

100/400 = 25%, 50/100 = 50%, while starting-cell yields are 100/1000 and 50/1000.

14. Quantitative — 30% of repaired breaks enter a junction route, and 40% of that route's products are crossovers. Others are noncrossovers. Total crossover fraction?
  1. 12%
  2. 70%
  3. 30%
  4. 40%
Answer and explanation

A. 12%

0.30 × 0.40 = 0.12, assuming nested categories and one product classification per event.

15. Quantitative — In an independent binary-switch model of two junctions, switch probabilities are 0.1 and 0.4. Probability of exactly one switch?
  1. 0.42
  2. 0.04
  3. 0.50
  4. 0.60
Answer and explanation

A. 0.42

0.1 × 0.6 + 0.9 × 0.4 = 0.06 + 0.36 = 0.42. Both switching is a distinct event.

16. Quantitative — An unbiased junction random walk has 225 independent steps of ±1 bp. Root mean squared displacement?
  1. 112.5 bp
  2. 225 bp
  3. 15 bp
  4. 0 bp
Answer and explanation

C. 15 bp

Variance equals 225 bp² and RMS displacement is √225 = 15 bp. Mean signed displacement is zero, not RMS.

17. Quantitative — A hypothetical motor moves 2 bp per completed step at 8 steps/s for 10 s, with no pauses. Net distance?
  1. 160 bp
  2. 80 bp
  3. 16 bp
  4. 20 bp
Answer and explanation

A. 160 bp

2 bp/step × 8 steps/s × 10 s = 160 bp under the stipulated directed-motion model.

18. Quantitative — 20 of 250 tetrads show a single-chromatid conversion each; assume no others. Fraction of tetrads with conversion and fraction of chromatids converted?
  1. 2%; 8%
  2. 20%; 5%
  3. 8%; 8%
  4. 8%; 2%
Answer and explanation

D. 8%; 2%

20/250 = 8%; total chromatids 1000, so 20/1000 = 2%.

19. Quantitative — In 60% of meioses exactly one nonsister crossover occurs between informative flanks; none in others. Fraction of all chromatids recombinant?
  1. 60%
  2. 30%
  3. 15%
  4. 100%
Answer and explanation

B. 30%

Two of four chromatids are recombinant in each affected meiosis: 0.60 × 0.50 = 0.30.

20. Quantitative — A donor has informative differences at positions 10, 30, 50 and 70. Only 30 and 50 convert. Under one continuous fully converted tract assumption, minimum endpoint separation?
  1. 10 bp
  2. 60 bp
  3. 40 bp
  4. 20 bp
Answer and explanation

D. 20 bp

50 − 30 = 20 bp; inclusive positions number 21. Without the assumption, the actual heteroduplex extent is not determined.

21. Segregation — Three meiotic products are A, A and a. The fourth contains unresolved A/a heteroduplex. With one subsequent mitosis and no other changes, octad counts?
  1. 6 A:2 a
  2. 8 A:0 a
  3. 4 A:4 a
  4. 5 A:3 a
Answer and explanation

D. 5 A:3 a

Resolved products give 4 A and 2 a; heteroduplex descendants give one of each, totaling 5:3.

22. Segregation — Two A and two a chromatids start meiosis. One A-bearing chromatid converts to a; no other changes. Tetrad and octad?
  1. 1 A:3 a; 2 A:6 a
  2. 2 A:2 a; 4 A:4 a
  3. 0 A:4 a; 0 A:8 a
  4. 3 A:1 a; 6 A:2 a
Answer and explanation

A. 1 A:3 a; 2 A:6 a

One A becomes a; the tetrad is 1:3 and a subsequent ordinary mitosis duplicates each product.

23. Mechanism — Loss of a dissolution factor raises the crossover fraction, while early joint formation remains unchanged. What is the best inference?
  1. Late pathway partitioning may favor crossover resolution
  2. All cells use site-specific recombination
  3. Every junction cut is now equally probable
  4. Invasion necessarily doubled
Answer and explanation

A. Late pathway partitioning may favor crossover resolution

The initial measured step is unchanged; altered late processing is a plausible explanation, not proof of increased initiation.

24. Mechanism — A resected strand invades donor, extends, dissociates and anneals to the other broken end. Which product pattern is expected?
  1. Obligate reciprocal replacement of donor
  2. Noncrossover, possibly with donor allele conversion
  3. Obligate distal crossover
  4. No DNA synthesis
Answer and explanation

B. Noncrossover, possibly with donor allele conversion

The described route is SDSA; copied local sequence can be retained without exchanging chromosome arms.

25. Comparative — In a bacterial transformation assay, DNA entry is normal but chromosomal integration is severely reduced in a recA mutant. What does this distinguish?
  1. Replication from transcription only
  2. End joining from plasmid copy number only
  3. DNA uptake from homologous integration
  4. Uptake from RNA translation only
Answer and explanation

C. DNA uptake from homologous integration

Incoming DNA can enter without becoming part of the chromosome. RecA supports homologous integration after entry.

26. Rearrangement — Two intramolecular direct repeats surround segment M. A reciprocal exchange between the repeats yields what main chromosome arrangement?
  1. M inverted with both repeats retained necessarily
  2. One repeat remaining with M deleted
  3. No sequence change in any product
  4. Four copies of M
Answer and explanation

B. One repeat remaining with M deleted

With direct repeats and the specified intramolecular configuration, recombination can excise the interval and one repeat from the main molecule.

27. Rearrangement — Two identical chromatids misalign their direct repeats before a reciprocal unequal crossover. Expected complementary outcomes?
  1. Duplication in one, deletion in the other
  2. Inversion in both obligatorily
  3. Both lose the same interval with no gain
  4. Neither changes copy number
Answer and explanation

A. Duplication in one, deletion in the other

Offset pairing changes distal-arm exchange points; one product gains a segment and the reciprocal product loses it.

28. Integrative — A mismatch-repair mutant shows more divergent-sequence recombinants and more postmeiotic segregation. Which explanation accounts for both?
  1. The mutant must have twice as many chromosomes
  2. Reduced heteroduplex rejection and reduced mismatch correction can coexist
  3. All homologous pairing has ceased
  4. Mismatch repair is identical to RuvB translocation
Answer and explanation

B. Reduced heteroduplex rejection and reduced mismatch correction can coexist

Mismatch recognition can oppose inappropriate divergent interactions and correct mismatches after pairing. Separate assays are needed to quantify each effect.
