1. Hormones as chemical instructions rather than raw materials

A multicellular organism contains cells that perform different tasks while sharing a common internal environment. A skeletal muscle fibre can increase its demand for glucose within a short interval, whereas a liver cell may need to release glucose into the circulation. The two responses can occur in the same organism at the same time because each cell interprets signals through its own molecular machinery. A hormone is a chemical messenger produced by one group of cells that alters the activity of responsive cells. In classical endocrine signaling, secretion into the circulation makes communication possible over long distances. A hormone need not supply appreciable material or energy to its target. Its principal contribution is information: it changes the probability that particular molecular processes will occur.

Consider insulin arriving at a muscle cell after a meal. The amount of glucose that enters the cell is not limited to the number of insulin molecules that reach it. Instead, recognition of insulin modifies the behavior of proteins already present in the cell, including the machinery controlling the delivery of glucose transporters to the plasma membrane. The resulting transport uses the glucose concentration gradient; the hormone does not physically carry each glucose molecule across the membrane. This distinction between information and material transport is essential. A single hormone-receptor interaction can initiate several biochemical events, and each event can alter many substrate molecules. The underlying energy for those events comes from cellular resources or pre-existing gradients.

The term receptor refers to a molecule capable of specifically recognizing a signal and coupling that recognition to a cellular response. For the hormone receptor classes considered here, the receptor is a protein. A protein that binds a hormone without transmitting information need not be a signaling receptor. A serum carrier, for example, can transport a hormone to distant tissues but has a different principal role from that of an intracellular transcriptional receptor. Similarly, a membrane transporter that delivers a hormone to the cytoplasm may be essential for hormone action without being the molecular switch that controls gene expression. In examining an unfamiliar pathway, it is therefore necessary to identify both the location of hormone recognition and the molecular function of the binding protein.

The first task of receptor classification is to decide how the ligand reaches the relevant recognition site. The second is to decide how binding is translated into a molecular event. These are related but logically distinct questions. An extracellular peptide can activate a membrane protein that opens an ion pore, stimulates an enzyme or communicates with a separate intracellular regulatory protein. All of these are cell-surface signaling arrangements, yet their initial biochemical outputs differ. Conversely, several chemically distinct small molecules may reach nuclear receptors whose common output is regulation of transcription. Chemical classification of hormones is useful because it predicts accessibility, while structural and functional classification of receptors explains the route of signal transfer.

A target cell is not defined merely by physical proximity to a hormone-producing tissue. It must express an accessible, functional receptor and possess the machinery required to produce the particular response being measured. Even the presence of a receptor does not guarantee that every potential downstream outcome is available. A liver cell and a heart cell may express receptors for the same catecholamine but possess different enzyme systems, ion channels and regulatory proteins. Their responses can consequently differ in magnitude, duration and biological meaning. Hormone specificity arises at several levels rather than being encoded in a single lock-and-key interaction.

2. Chemical origin, polarity and hormone availability

Peptide and protein hormones are constructed from amino acids joined by peptide bonds. The peptide backbone contains polar groups capable of hydrogen bonding with water, and many side chains introduce additional ionizable groups. Although the folded surface of a protein contains both polar and nonpolar regions, transferring an entire peptide hormone through the nonpolar interior of a phospholipid bilayer usually entails a substantial energetic penalty. This is why conventional peptide hormones such as insulin, glucagon and vasopressin principally signal by binding to cell-surface receptors. They do not all use the same kind of receptor merely because they share a chemical origin.

Protein and peptide hormones differ in size, but the division between the two terms has no universal numerical cutoff. Glucagon is a peptide of 29 amino acid residues, whereas mature insulin contains two polypeptide chains connected by disulfide bonds and has 51 amino acid residues in total. The folds, sequences and chemical surfaces of these molecules differ. Their receptors recognize specific three-dimensional features rather than treating the ligands as interchangeable chains of amino acids. A small peptide may bind a G-protein-coupled receptor, a different peptide may bind a membrane guanylyl cyclase, and a larger protein may activate a kinase-associated receptor. A classification based only on length cannot predict these choices.

Many peptide hormones are synthesized as larger precursors. A signal peptide directs a newly synthesized precursor into the secretory pathway, and subsequent processing can remove segments or generate the mature active form. Storage in secretory vesicles allows release in response to an appropriate cellular stimulus. The receptor on the target cell recognizes the biologically relevant hormone structure, so altered processing can impair signaling even when a hormone gene is transcribed normally. The details of protein targeting and maturation are not required to infer the receptor class, but this relationship explains why measuring precursor abundance alone cannot establish that active hormone is present outside the cell.

The name amine hormone refers to a chemical category derived from amino acids. It does not identify a single physical property or receptor mechanism. Epinephrine and norepinephrine are catecholamines derived from tyrosine. Their hydroxyl groups interact with water, and the amino group is predominantly protonated at physiological pH. Despite their small size compared with proteins, this combination favors the aqueous environment over the interior of a lipid bilayer. Their conventional hormonal effects are mediated by adrenergic cell-surface GPCRs. Molecular size by itself is therefore an unreliable predictor of passive passage through the membrane.

Thyroxine, abbreviated T4, and triiodothyronine, abbreviated T3, also originate from tyrosine-derived structures. Their iodinated aromatic groups and other chemical features give them a very different combination of physical properties from catecholamines. Their major genomic actions depend on nuclear thyroid hormone receptors, especially recognition of T3. Yet the membrane crossing step is regulated by transport proteins in many physiological contexts. Monocarboxylate transporter 8, or MCT8, is a notable example. A question that describes both epinephrine and T3 as tyrosine-derived is not providing sufficient evidence that their receptor locations are alike. Chemical origin is not the same classification as receptor accessibility.

Steroid hormones are derived from cholesterol and retain a characteristic framework of fused carbon rings, modified by different functional groups. Cortisol, aldosterone, estradiol, progesterone and testosterone are representative examples. Their relatively hydrophobic character permits access to intracellular receptor populations, commonly through passage across the plasma membrane. Functional groups still matter: two steroids sharing the same general ring framework can have substantially different receptor affinities and biological activities. The shape and polarity of a ligand's surface affect both its interaction with lipid and its molecular recognition by a receptor binding pocket.

A useful distinction is between solubility in the aqueous extracellular environment and accessibility to a membrane. A compound that partitions into membrane lipids may not dissolve well in plasma. Many circulating steroids and thyroid hormones therefore associate reversibly with carrier proteins. Albumin and more selective binding proteins can support transport and influence the relationship between measured total hormone and the concentration of unbound hormone. Binding to a carrier does not mean the carrier-hormone complex normally enters the nucleus as a unit. Hormone becomes available to the target through exchange with the free pool and, where required, through membrane transport mechanisms.

An increase in total hormone concentration is not necessarily accompanied by an equal increase in free hormone. A change in carrier abundance can alter the distribution of hormone between free and bound states. Suppose two samples contain the same total amount of a steroid, but the first sample has more available carrier binding sites. If all other conditions are comparable, a smaller fraction of hormone may be free in that sample. The receptor is responding to the ligand accessible at its binding site, not to the numerical value of total hormone reported by an assay. In a living organism, secretion, clearance and feedback can modify the eventual steady state, so a simple binding argument describes the immediate physicochemical distribution rather than a complete endocrine prediction.

3. The membrane barrier and the meaning of receptor location

The plasma membrane separates two water-rich compartments by a thin hydrophobic layer. Its amphipathic lipid molecules arrange their polar head groups toward the aqueous media and their hydrocarbon chains toward the membrane interior. A solute passing directly through this interior must leave much of its favorable interaction with surrounding water. Molecules with several permanent or readily formed charges face a particularly large cost. Membrane permeability thus reflects not only the size of a solute but also the way its chemical groups interact with water and lipid.

This physical arrangement gives cell-surface receptors an important biological advantage. An extracellular binding domain or accessible extracellular pocket can recognize a water-soluble ligand without requiring the ligand to enter the cytoplasm. A transmembrane segment couples this extracellular recognition to a conformational change or rearrangement on the cytoplasmic side. The signal crosses the boundary as a change in molecular state rather than as movement of the hormone itself. The receptor spans the compartmental separation and makes its two sides functionally connected.

The situation is different when a ligand can reach a receptor inside the cell. In this case the membrane is no longer necessarily the site of primary signal recognition. A steroid molecule can enter the cell and associate with an intracellular receptor that has a ligand-binding domain and, in the case of classical nuclear receptors, a DNA-binding domain. The receptor then affects the expression of selected genes. The hormone is not an enzyme that copies DNA and does not substitute for RNA polymerase. It changes the behavior of a protein capable of recruiting or influencing the transcriptional machinery.

The distinction between cell-surface and intracellular receptors is most accurately defined by the site of biologically relevant ligand recognition in a specified pathway. A membrane receptor that undergoes endocytosis does not automatically become a nuclear hormone receptor. Its original structure, ligand accessibility and mechanism are still those of a membrane receptor; signaling may continue from an internalized membrane compartment. Conversely, a classical nuclear receptor can have a population outside the nucleus before activation. The word nuclear refers in this context to a family of transcription-regulating receptors and their canonical signaling mechanism, rather than asserting that every molecule of every family member is always present in the nucleus.

Some apparently plausible experiments fail to distinguish physical uptake from productive signaling. A fluorescent peptide may be visible inside a cell after incubation because receptor-bound ligand was internalized into vesicles. Fluorescence in the cytoplasmic region of an image does not establish that the ligand is freely dissolved in cytosol. The lumen of an endocytic vesicle remains separated from the cytosol by membrane. To decide whether a hormone can reach an intracellular receptor, an investigator needs information about the subcellular compartment, ligand integrity and the functional dependence of a response on that receptor.

Thyroid hormone transport illustrates why a binary distinction between hydrophobic and hydrophilic hormones should be used as a starting model rather than an inviolable rule. If a cell loses a transporter that provides important T3 access, the nuclear receptor itself may remain fully functional while the response to extracellular T3 falls. Delivering ligand intracellularly by a controlled experimental approach could help distinguish this access defect from a defective receptor. The outcome would depend on the transport and metabolic properties of the model system. The decisive concept is that receptor function and ligand delivery can be separate stages, either of which may be limiting.

4. How evidence separated extracellular recognition from intracellular action

Early experiments on hormone action faced an important problem: a change in tissue metabolism after addition of hormone could be explained either by direct interaction with a metabolic enzyme or by an intervening signaling system. A tissue response alone could not distinguish these possibilities. Work by Earl Sutherland and colleagues on the effects of epinephrine and glucagon in liver preparations provided a way to separate hormone recognition from the metabolic machinery that executes the response. Their investigations established cyclic AMP as an intracellular intermediate, a landmark demonstration that the primary extracellular signal and the intracellular regulatory molecule could be chemically different.

The logic of fractionation is especially informative. A tissue homogenate contains fragments of membranes as well as soluble proteins. Centrifugation can enrich these classes of material into distinct fractions, although no simple fractionation guarantees absolute purity. If hormone-sensitive production of a small regulatory molecule is associated with a membrane-containing fraction, whereas a downstream enzyme responds to that molecule in another fraction, a model involving separated recognition and response becomes plausible. Controls must distinguish a genuine hormone-dependent change from differences in enzyme concentration, substrate availability or sample handling.

In the classical cyclic AMP case, the important conclusion is not that every hormone generates cyclic AMP. Rather, it is that a hormone can control an intracellular process through production of another molecule. Subsequent work distinguished membrane receptors, G proteins and adenylyl cyclases as separate functional components in many pathways. Thus, when an experiment shows increased cyclic AMP after ligand addition, it identifies a downstream output but does not by itself demonstrate the structure of the receptor that initiated it. Additional perturbations or molecular identification are required. This distinction becomes crucial when comparing GPCRs with receptor guanylyl cyclases and other enzyme-linked proteins.

A second line of evidence concerns intracellular hormone receptors. Binding measurements can reveal a finite population of molecules with high affinity for a radiolabeled hormone. Competition with unlabeled hormone can help distinguish selective binding from nonspecific retention in membranes or other cellular material. Fractionation can suggest where the binding sites reside, but the result must be interpreted using markers that test compartment purity and the possibility that receptor distribution changes during sample preparation. A saturable binding site is evidence for selective molecular recognition, not yet evidence that the binding protein regulates transcription.

To establish a receptor's role in transcription, an experimental model can compare a hormone-responsive reporter gene with a reporter lacking the relevant DNA recognition element. If ligand exposure increases reporter transcription only when the appropriate element and functional receptor are present, the outcome supports an element-dependent mechanism. A mutation in the receptor's DNA-binding domain can provide another test. The mutant receptor may retain ligand recognition while losing its effect on the reporter, separating two molecular functions. This is a general experimental reasoning model, not a report of a particular historical dataset.

The strongest mechanistic interpretation often combines evidence of necessity with evidence of sufficiency under controlled conditions. Receptor loss followed by rescue with a normal receptor tests whether that receptor is necessary in a cell system and whether restoration can restore function. Expression of the receptor in a previously unresponsive cell can demonstrate that receptor availability was limiting if the required downstream machinery is already present. Failure of expression to create a response is not conclusive evidence against the receptor's identity; the new cell may lack a cofactor, transporter or suitable gene regulatory environment. These different evidential levels prevent students from treating binding, localization and signaling as interchangeable observations.

5. G-protein-coupled receptors: recognition through a separate molecular switch

A receptor can communicate with the cytoplasm without itself being an enzyme. This is the central architectural principle of a G-protein-coupled receptor, usually abbreviated GPCR. A typical member of this family consists of a polypeptide that crosses the lipid bilayer seven times in the form of alpha helices. Extracellular loops and the extracellularly accessible portion of the receptor contribute to ligand recognition, while intracellular loops and the cytoplasmic ends of the helices participate in communication with cytosolic partners. Ligand binding changes the distribution of receptor conformations. A signaling-competent conformation presents an intracellular surface that interacts productively with an appropriate G protein.

The G protein associated with the conventional pathway is a heterotrimer composed of alpha, beta and gamma subunits. In its inactive signaling state the alpha subunit normally carries GDP. The activated receptor promotes nucleotide exchange on the alpha subunit, allowing GTP to bind from the cellular nucleotide pool. The GTP-bound alpha subunit and the beta-gamma complex can regulate downstream effector proteins. The exact sequence of molecular rearrangements is the subject of the next chapter. For receptor classification, the critical point is that the receptor activates a separate nucleotide-regulated protein rather than directly catalyzing the synthesis of a second messenger or phosphorylating an effector.

The distinction can be understood by comparing an adrenergic receptor with an adenylyl cyclase. Both proteins may be embedded in the same membrane, yet they perform different chemical functions. Epinephrine binds the adrenergic receptor; adenylyl cyclase catalyzes the production of cyclic AMP from ATP. An intervening G protein provides the functional connection in a representative stimulatory pathway. The fact that the final measured response is an increase in cyclic AMP does not justify naming the receptor itself an adenylyl cyclase. The classes are defined by the molecular properties of the receptor, not by a molecule found several steps downstream.

Different GPCRs can couple to different G-protein families, and the direction of a biochemical response therefore cannot be deduced solely from the presence of seven membrane-spanning helices. The beta adrenergic receptors provide familiar examples of stimulatory G-protein coupling, whereas alpha2 adrenergic receptors commonly couple to inhibitory G proteins that reduce adenylyl cyclase activity. Alpha1 adrenergic receptors commonly couple to a G protein that activates phospholipase C. The same catecholamine can therefore initiate more than one class of intracellular messenger response through distinct receptor subtypes. These are representative couplings rather than a claim that signaling in every cell is limited to one possible partner.

Peptide hormone receptors also occur in the GPCR family. Glucagon receptors and receptors for vasopressin offer examples of ligands whose chemical composition differs radically from epinephrine but whose receptors share the seven-transmembrane organizational principle. Some peptide-binding GPCRs use substantial extracellular regions for ligand recognition; many small amines interact with a pocket formed partly within the transmembrane helical bundle. There is no requirement that all GPCR ligands bind in an identical region. Shared coupling architecture can be conserved despite differences in ligand recognition.

In a hypothetical cell expressing a hormone-binding GPCR, consider a mutation in a cytoplasmic region that normally contacts the G protein. The mutant protein could be transported to the plasma membrane and bind hormone at a normal affinity, yet fail to activate its intracellular partner. The predicted result is normal receptor occupancy with a reduced downstream response. A second mutation that disrupts ligand binding could produce the same downstream phenotype by a different route. Assays of surface abundance and ligand binding would help distinguish these failures. The first mutation demonstrates why receptor recognition and receptor coupling must be treated as experimentally separable functions.

The energy bookkeeping also differs from that of an ion channel. Binding is a reversible molecular association, while GTP hydrolysis ultimately helps return the G protein toward its inactive state. The receptor does not provide the GTP itself and need not hydrolyze ATP simply to bind a ligand. Later phosphorylation reactions can consume ATP, and cellular metabolism replenishes nucleotide pools. A hormone acts as a regulatory input into a system already capable of using chemical energy. This principle becomes useful when a signaling-defective cell is also metabolically compromised: impaired downstream output may arise from loss of energy resources without any primary defect in hormone recognition.

6. Receptor tyrosine kinases: combining recognition and catalysis

Receptor tyrosine kinases, or RTKs, solve the problem of membrane-to-cytoplasm communication using a different organization. A conventional RTK has an extracellular ligand-binding region, a transmembrane segment and a cytoplasmic domain with protein tyrosine kinase activity. When an appropriate ligand activates the receptor, the catalytic domain can transfer phosphate from ATP to suitable tyrosine residues in proteins. The same membrane-spanning receptor thus participates in both signal recognition and the initial enzymatic response. The extracellular ligand does not need to contact ATP because catalysis occurs on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane.

Many RTKs are activated through ligand-dependent association or rearrangement of receptor molecules. Bringing intracellular kinase regions into suitable orientations can promote phosphorylation, including phosphorylation of one receptor chain by the catalytic domain of another. The resulting phosphotyrosine residues can alter enzymatic activity or serve as recruitment sites for other proteins. They should not be imagined merely as nonspecific negative charges. Particular signaling proteins recognize phosphotyrosine in the context of surrounding amino acid sequences. This recognition creates selective interactions and helps assemble downstream signaling complexes near an activated receptor.

The epidermal growth factor receptor is a widely used model of an RTK, although its activation involves specific structural features and should not be treated as a universal template. Insulin provides an especially useful endocrine example because the mature insulin receptor is already assembled as a disulfide-linked complex of two alpha and two beta subunits. The alpha subunits are extracellular, and each beta subunit includes a transmembrane region and an intracellular kinase domain. Insulin binding changes the organization and activity of the preassembled receptor. It is therefore incorrect to assert that insulin activates its receptor simply by causing two previously separate receptor monomers to meet for the first time.

The insulin receptor is also a useful example of the difference between receptor phosphorylation and substrate phosphorylation. Phosphorylation of receptor regions contributes to activation and coupling. The receptor subsequently phosphorylates other proteins, including insulin receptor substrates. These proteins serve as links to downstream regulatory systems. A cell may display a phosphorylated insulin receptor yet fail to carry out a normal glucose uptake response if a later component controlling transporter redistribution is defective. Conversely, an intervention that activates a later component independently of the receptor may partially reproduce a downstream response without restoring the complete range of normal insulin effects.

Consider a kinase-inactive mutant of an RTK that reaches the plasma membrane normally. Its extracellular domain may bind ligand, and ligand may induce the expected receptor association, but the kinase cannot transfer phosphate efficiently. If phosphorylation is necessary for recruitment of a downstream signaling protein, that recruitment will fail or be reduced. The immediate defect lies in catalysis, whereas altered proliferation or metabolism measured much later is a consequence of the interrupted pathway. A receptor abundance assay cannot detect this catalytic defect. An assay of the receptor's phosphorylation and kinase function is required.

It is also important to distinguish a receptor tyrosine kinase from an intracellular tyrosine kinase associated with a receptor. In the former case the receptor polypeptide contains an intrinsic catalytic region. In the latter, a separate protein supplies the kinase activity. Both can lead to tyrosine phosphorylation and both can recruit proteins recognizing phosphotyrosine. An immunoblot that detects increased phosphotyrosine after hormone exposure cannot, by itself, decide which architecture is present. Molecular identification of the phosphorylated proteins and analysis of their catalytic domains provide more decisive evidence.

RTK activation can rapidly change existing protein functions, but it can also trigger gene expression and longer-term cellular responses. The biological outcome depends on the substrate proteins and pathways accessible in the particular cell. A receptor class therefore cannot be identified from response timing alone. An early increase in phosphorylation is compatible with an intrinsic kinase receptor, a kinase-associated receptor or a pathway that reaches a kinase indirectly. Structural information and the location of catalytic activity are more reliable criteria. The detailed connections to Ras, MAP kinases and other intracellular signaling branches will be developed in their dedicated chapter.

7. Kinase-associated receptors: catalytic activity belongs to a partner

Some receptors contain extracellular ligand recognition regions and transmembrane segments but lack an intrinsic intracellular kinase domain. A representative family is that of cytokine receptors coupled to Janus kinases, abbreviated JAKs. In this arrangement, the receptor forms the recognition and membrane-spanning platform, while an associated cytoplasmic protein supplies tyrosine kinase activity. The association can exist before ligand stimulation. Ligand binding reorganizes receptor chains and their attached kinases into a signaling-competent state, enabling phosphorylation of suitable substrates. This design separates the catalytic machinery from the polypeptide that recognizes the extracellular ligand.

Growth hormone and prolactin provide endocrine examples of receptors using associated JAK activity. Erythropoietin provides another example of the same broad architectural principle. Although these proteins differ in their biological roles and tissue distributions, their receptors are not classified as receptor tyrosine kinases merely because the first measurable signaling output may be tyrosine phosphorylation. The difference becomes obvious when inspecting the receptor sequence: a receptor lacking a tyrosine kinase catalytic domain requires a separate molecule to perform the transfer of phosphate from ATP.

A standard cytokine receptor pathway can involve phosphorylation of the receptor cytoplasmic region followed by recruitment of STAT proteins, the signal transducers and activators of transcription. Activated STAT proteins participate in transcriptional regulation after reaching the nucleus. This establishes an important conceptual bridge between surface receptor signaling and gene expression. A hormone does not need to bind a nuclear receptor directly in order to alter transcription. Surface signaling can activate transcription factors indirectly through protein modification, localization changes or other forms of regulation.

A mutation that removes the region of a cytokine receptor required for JAK association could permit normal surface expression and hormone binding but prevent efficient phosphorylation. The loss of signal is due to the absence of productive kinase coupling, not necessarily to a missing ligand recognition domain. If a normal receptor is reintroduced and restores signaling, the result supports a role for the missing interaction region. It remains necessary to verify that the mutant was expressed at the same level and reached the appropriate cellular compartment. Otherwise, reduced signaling could simply reflect defective receptor maturation or trafficking.

A complementary experiment would disrupt the associated kinase while leaving the receptor sequence unchanged. An investigator could compare receptor abundance, ligand binding, receptor phosphorylation and a downstream transcriptional reporter before and after restoring functional kinase. The observations would localize the defect if the first two measurements were preserved while the latter two were impaired and rescued. In a cell containing several related kinases, incomplete loss of signaling might reflect compensation. Interpretation should take account of whether the perturbation removes all relevant catalytic capacity or only one contribution to it.

8. Receptor serine/threonine kinases: another intrinsic catalytic class

The chemical reaction of protein phosphorylation is not limited to tyrosine. Serine and threonine side chains also contain hydroxyl groups that can receive phosphate. Receptors of the transforming growth factor beta family use intrinsic kinase domains that predominantly phosphorylate serine and threonine residues. They are therefore called receptor serine/threonine kinases. Like RTKs, their ligand recognition machinery is connected directly to catalytic domains on the intracellular side of the membrane. They are nevertheless a distinct functional class because their typical substrates and signaling organization differ.

A representative TGF-beta pathway involves type II and type I receptors. Ligand promotes formation or reorganization of an active receptor complex, and the type II receptor phosphorylates and activates the type I receptor. The activated type I receptor phosphorylates appropriate receptor-regulated SMAD proteins. These can associate with a common mediator SMAD and influence gene expression. This outline is sufficient to classify the receptor system and to distinguish it from a JAK-associated cytokine receptor; the detailed sequence and regulatory variations belong to the later chapter on cytokine and serine/threonine kinase pathways.

The presence of different receptor types within an active complex demonstrates that a simple diagram of a single receptor molecule is often an abstraction. A ligand-binding complex may have several chains with distinct contributions to binding, catalysis and substrate recognition. When a mutation affects one chain, its consequences depend on that chain's role. A kinase-inactive type II receptor, for instance, would be expected to impair the normal activation of type I in a representative TGF-beta signaling system even if ligand recognition by the complex were preserved. A type I receptor that cannot phosphorylate its appropriate SMAD substrates could interrupt the pathway at a subsequent step.

Phosphorylation of serine or threonine alone is not proof of activation of this receptor family. Protein kinase A, protein kinase C and numerous other intracellular enzymes phosphorylate serine and threonine residues and can act downstream of unrelated receptor classes. Receptor classification requires evidence that the receptor has the relevant intrinsic activity. In a simplified exam problem, a single-pass membrane protein with an extracellular TGF-beta recognition function and a cytoplasmic serine/threonine kinase domain is strong evidence for this class. A cell containing a phosphorylated serine residue on an unrelated enzyme provides much weaker information about the identity of the initiating receptor.

9. Receptor guanylyl cyclases: direct production of a second messenger

A membrane receptor may contain an enzyme that acts on a small nucleotide instead of phosphorylating a protein. Receptor guanylyl cyclases illustrate this architecture. A representative natriuretic peptide receptor contains an extracellular ligand-binding region, a membrane-spanning segment and a cytoplasmic region that includes a guanylyl cyclase catalytic domain. When atrial natriuretic peptide activates its signaling receptor, often termed GC-A or NPR-A, the intracellular catalytic machinery increases the production of cyclic GMP. The receptor is therefore directly coupled to production of the second messenger by virtue of its own enzymatic domain.

The chemical substrate for this reaction is guanosine triphosphate, or GTP. Cyclization yields cyclic guanosine monophosphate and pyrophosphate. This transformation differs from the nucleotide exchange performed on a heterotrimeric G protein. A G protein binds guanine nucleotides to switch between regulatory states, whereas guanylyl cyclase uses GTP as a substrate to generate another molecule. The repeated appearance of GTP in receptor signaling can obscure these distinct roles. A nucleotide may serve as a bound regulatory switch or as a reactant in a catalytic transformation; these functions should not be conflated.

Cyclic GMP can subsequently regulate proteins that recognize it, including cyclic GMP-dependent protein kinase in appropriate cell types. As with cyclic AMP, the messenger concentration is determined not only by synthesis but also by removal. Therefore a rise in cyclic GMP does not uniquely identify a receptor guanylyl cyclase: increased synthesis through a different cyclase or reduced degradation could produce the same measurement. The defining feature of a receptor guanylyl cyclase is that the membrane receptor has a catalytic domain responsible for producing cyclic GMP.

The contrasting example of nitric oxide is particularly useful. Nitric oxide is a small diffusible gas that can activate soluble guanylyl cyclase inside responding cells. Its signaling receptor is not the same as a membrane receptor for a natriuretic peptide. Both systems can increase the concentration of the same second messenger, but they have different ligand accessibility and receptor organization. In one case a peptide remains outside the cell during primary recognition; in the other a small molecule reaches a cytosolic enzyme. A common downstream product does not imply a common receptor topology.

Even within the family of receptors that bind natriuretic peptides, not every binding protein functions as a guanylyl cyclase. NPR-C is notable for lacking the guanylyl cyclase catalytic domain and contributes to clearance and signaling in relevant contexts. Consequently, a question identifying a protein solely by the fact that it binds a natriuretic peptide is underspecified if it asks whether the protein has intrinsic cyclase activity. Identification of NPR-A or NPR-B, or direct evidence of the relevant catalytic region, gives a more defensible answer. The distinction prevents the broad term natriuretic peptide receptor from being mistaken for a single invariant biochemical mechanism.

10. Ligand-gated ion channels: the receptor controls a physical pathway

Ligand-gated ion channels differ from enzyme-linked and G-protein-coupled receptors because ligand recognition is directly connected to regulation of an ion-conducting pore. A conventional channel receptor can exist in conformations in which the pore is relatively inaccessible or accessible to particular ions. Binding of the appropriate ligand changes the probabilities of these states. Ions can then move through the open pore according to their electrochemical gradients. The receptor does not need to synthesize a cyclic nucleotide or phosphorylate a substrate as an obligatory intermediate in this direct gating process.

The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor provides a standard example of a ligand-gated cation channel. Acetylcholine is usually discussed as a neurotransmitter in this setting rather than a circulating endocrine hormone. Its inclusion is justified by the broader cell-surface receptor classification. Nicotinic receptors differ from muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, which belong to the GPCR family. The same chemical ligand can thus be recognized by receptors with fundamentally different architectures. One class directly regulates a pore, while the other communicates with intracellular partners. Classification based on the name of the ligand alone would fail.

The direction of ion movement is not determined simply by the fact that a channel has opened. It depends on ion selectivity, the prevailing concentration gradients and the electrical potential difference across the membrane. For a cation channel at a typical resting membrane potential, opening may cause net inward positive current and depolarization. Under a different voltage or ion composition the current can change direction or become negligible. Detailed electrochemical calculations belong to the membrane physiology chapters. Here the relevant distinction is that the channel regulates an existing gradient rather than pumping ions uphill through a catalytic transport cycle.

Ligand binding is likewise not equivalent to successful channel opening. A mutation in the pore or the structural elements coupling ligand recognition to gating may retain agonist binding while abolishing the electrical response. Measuring ligand binding, surface expression and membrane currents provides three different types of evidence. A receptor with normal binding but no detectable current could be defective in gating or conduction. If the recording conditions provide no electrochemical driving force, however, a functioning open channel could also produce little net current. A conclusion about channel function requires appropriate voltage and ionic controls.

Ligand-gated channels often enable particularly rapid responses because the change in permeability is closely coupled to the binding event. This does not mean that all consequences of opening a channel are immediate or purely electrical. Calcium entering through an appropriate channel can regulate enzymes and eventually alter gene expression. The rapid initial output and the slower biological outcome are distinct stages. The channel is identified by direct ligand-regulated ion conduction, not by the final cellular response or by an arbitrary time threshold.

11. Nuclear hormone receptors: ligand recognition and gene regulation in one protein

A nuclear receptor couples chemical recognition to the regulation of gene expression through a molecular architecture different from that of a membrane receptor. In the conventional genomic pathway, the hormone reaches a protein inside the cell. This protein recognizes the ligand and can influence transcription through direct or indirect association with chromosomal DNA. The receptor does not need a membrane-spanning segment to communicate with the nucleus, because the signaling protein itself participates in gene regulation. The two activities of ligand recognition and transcriptional regulation are therefore functionally connected within one receptor molecule.

A typical nuclear receptor contains several distinguishable regions. Near its amino terminus is a variable region that can participate in transcriptional regulation. A centrally located DNA-binding domain recognizes appropriate nucleotide sequences. A flexible hinge connects this domain to a ligand-binding domain that contributes to ligand selectivity, conformational regulation and interactions with other proteins. The precise dimensions and organization vary across the family. Thinking of these regions as functional modules explains how mutations can selectively alter particular aspects of receptor action rather than necessarily destroying the entire protein.

The DNA-binding domain of classical nuclear receptors contains two zinc-binding structures often described as C4 zinc fingers. Each coordinates zinc through four cysteine residues. The metal ions help stabilize the folded structures, enabling the protein to make appropriate contacts with DNA and, in some cases, with a receptor partner. Zinc is not used as a freely diffusing second messenger in this mechanism. Its structural role helps establish the geometry required for molecular recognition. A mutation substituting a critical cysteine could compromise DNA recognition even if the ligand-binding domain remains functional.

A hormone response element is a DNA sequence that contributes to regulation by the corresponding receptor. It is not necessarily located immediately next to the start of transcription. Regulatory elements can reside in promoters or at considerable distances, and chromatin organization influences their ability to participate in transcriptional control. Different nuclear receptor systems recognize related sequence motifs arranged in characteristic orientations and spacings. A receptor pair may recognize two half-sites rather than a single isolated short sequence. The strength of the response depends on the receptor, DNA sequence, accessibility of that sequence and other regulatory molecules present in the cell.

It is tempting to regard hormone binding as a process that causes the receptor to stick more tightly to every relevant DNA sequence. This is not a reliable general model. Some receptors show changes in DNA occupancy upon activation, whereas others already occupy regulatory elements in the absence of ligand. Hormone binding may change the receptor's interactions with transcriptional coregulators rather than primarily changing its attachment to DNA. Consequently, the term activation must specify what property is changing: localization, DNA association, partner recruitment, transcriptional activity or some combination of these.

A transcriptional coactivator is a protein that helps a DNA-associated regulator increase transcription in a given context. A corepressor helps establish or maintain a state of reduced transcription. These terms describe functional contributions rather than implying that the proteins always have opposite effects in every possible cellular setting. Nuclear receptors can interact with complexes that alter chromatin structure, modify histones or influence components of the transcriptional machinery. By changing the accessibility and regulatory environment of a gene, a relatively small hormone-receptor complex can influence the production of many RNA molecules over time.

The ligand-binding region is not merely a cavity that holds a hormone. Binding stabilizes particular conformations, and conformational changes can influence the surface with which coregulators interact. Two molecules may bind the same receptor but differ in their ability to stabilize a transcriptionally active state. The distinction between affinity and activation is therefore especially important for understanding agonists and antagonists at a conceptual level. A ligand that occupies a receptor without producing the conformational state required for activation can reduce the response to an activating ligand by competing for access. Detailed pharmacological classification is unnecessary to appreciate this mechanism.

The receptor acts on genes rather than directly manufacturing their protein products. After changes in transcription, RNA must be processed as appropriate, and changes in protein abundance generally require translation. Protein stability and existing cellular machinery further determine the observed phenotype. A hormone might induce transcription of an enzyme, repress transcription of an alternative enzyme or influence a regulator that subsequently affects several genes. The direct transcriptional targets of a receptor and the broader set of genes whose expression eventually changes should therefore be distinguished. Early response measurements and tests of direct regulatory element dependence can help establish that distinction.

One advantage of a modular receptor is that its different functions can be investigated separately. If a mutant receptor binds hormone but cannot recognize a hormone response element, a loss of reporter expression would support the importance of DNA recognition. If a different mutant binds hormone and associates with DNA but fails to recruit an essential coactivator, reporter expression may still be impaired. The two mutants give similar final outputs but interrupt different stages. Measurements restricted to total receptor abundance or even DNA association would not discriminate between them. This is why molecular reasoning requires a sequence of intermediate observations.

12. Steroid receptors: cytoplasmic and nuclear starting states

Steroid hormone receptors provide familiar examples of ligand-dependent transcription. The glucocorticoid receptor can be used to understand a common activation sequence. In many unstimulated cells, a substantial receptor population is associated with chaperone-containing complexes in the cytoplasm. These proteins help establish and maintain receptor conformations compatible with ligand recognition. An incoming glucocorticoid can bind the receptor, alter the composition and behavior of the complex, and promote nuclear accumulation of the activated receptor. The receptor can then participate in the regulation of glucocorticoid-responsive genes.

A chaperone is not an inert cover that always detaches completely as soon as hormone is present. Chaperone interactions can stabilize receptor folding and can influence receptor trafficking and activation. The important introductory principle is that the absence of ligand does not necessarily mean the receptor is an isolated inactive polypeptide. Its functional state depends on interactions with other proteins. If chaperone activity is disrupted, the response may decline because receptor maturation, stability or ligand-binding competence is affected. Such a result would not by itself indicate that the hormone has failed to enter the cell.

Movement of a receptor into the nucleus is governed by the same general distinction between nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments that applies to other proteins. Small steroid molecules and receptor proteins do not traverse the nuclear envelope in identical ways. Steroid entry into the cell is determined by chemical accessibility, whereas nuclear localization of a large receptor protein depends on protein transport machinery and regulatory sequences. Ligand-dependent changes in receptor localization can result from changes in exposure of localization signals and from altered interactions with cellular transport and retention systems. The direction of receptor redistribution is thus a property of the signaling protein as well as the ligand.

Activated glucocorticoid receptors can bind appropriate DNA response elements, often through receptor dimerization in conventional textbook models. Receptor binding may increase transcription of particular genes, but the net transcriptional effects of glucocorticoids include both increases and decreases in gene expression. Some effects involve interactions with other transcription regulators rather than the simplest model of a receptor dimer binding a canonical response element. It would therefore be wrong to conclude that a steroid receptor can only turn transcription on. Ligand-dependent transcriptional regulation is a broader category than transcriptional activation.

Estradiol receptors provide a useful contrast because classical estrogen receptor populations are frequently nuclear even before hormone exposure. They can also redistribute among cellular compartments, and additional membrane-associated signaling functions occur in appropriate contexts. The glucocorticoid receptor example must not become a universal statement that all steroid hormone receptors reside exclusively in the cytoplasm before ligand binding and then move to the nucleus. For examination purposes, a cytoplasmic chaperone-associated receptor moving toward the nucleus is a representative model; the exact initial localization must be assigned to a particular receptor and cell type.

Steroid hormone specificity reflects more than a shared origin from cholesterol. Different steroids carry distinct patterns of functional groups, and receptors contain ligand-binding pockets with different chemical environments. Changes in hydrogen bonding, steric fit and other molecular interactions affect the relative stability of ligand-bound conformations. Cortisol and aldosterone, for example, share substantial structural similarity, and their receptor selectivity in particular tissues can also be shaped by local hormone metabolism. This shows why cellular response cannot always be inferred from binding preferences measured with purified receptors in isolation.

The same reasoning extends to developmental differences between cells. Two cells exposed to identical estradiol concentrations can differ in the expression of estrogen receptor isoforms, the availability of coactivators and the regulatory DNA elements accessible in chromatin. As a result, the active receptor may regulate different genes. Identical genomic DNA sequence in two differentiated cells does not guarantee identical hormone-responsive transcription. Differential chromatin state and protein expression can create different accessible regulatory programs. Thus, receptor distribution explains which cells can initiate a pathway, while the differentiated state explains much of the selectivity of its output.

A practical way to study the genomic response is to follow a hormone-sensitive reporter over time. If reporter RNA increases before reporter protein, the order is compatible with transcription followed by translation. A transcription inhibitor might prevent accumulation of newly produced reporter RNA, and a translation inhibitor might prevent accumulation of reporter protein while some RNA induction remains. These perturbations have broad cellular effects, so conclusions should be limited to the measured process and supported with controls. A change in the amount of receptor protein during the experiment could confound either comparison, particularly if the receptor is unstable.

A rapid response to a steroid should not automatically be dismissed as experimentally impossible. Steroid-dependent signaling can include rapid actions associated with membrane-localized or cytoplasmic signaling machinery. In a given system a response measured within seconds may be difficult to explain by new protein synthesis, but that observation does not identify the receptor by itself. Tests of dependence on membrane accessibility, the classical steroid receptor, other candidate receptors and the downstream proteins are needed to distinguish mechanisms. The common genomic pathway remains central to receptor classification without implying that it is the only route available to every steroid.

13. Thyroid hormone receptors: access to a pre-existing nuclear regulatory complex

The classical thyroid hormone pathway illustrates a different starting arrangement for nuclear receptor signaling. Thyroid hormones circulate predominantly in protein-bound forms, and target tissue exposure depends on exchange with the available hormone pool. Transport proteins mediate cellular entry and exit in important tissues. Intracellular enzymes called deiodinases can modify iodothyronines, including conversion of T4 to the more active receptor ligand T3 in suitable cells. Thus, several molecular decisions occur before the ligand engages the transcriptional receptor. Hormone concentrations in blood cannot completely describe the intracellular supply of T3 in every tissue.

Many thyroid hormone receptors associate with DNA regulatory elements together with retinoid X receptor, abbreviated RXR. Such an arrangement is called a heterodimer because the two receptor partners are different proteins. In a standard positively regulated thyroid hormone target gene, the receptor complex can occupy a regulatory element even without bound T3. In the unliganded state, interactions with corepressor machinery can contribute to reduced transcription. T3 binding changes the receptor's regulatory interactions, favoring a different state that can recruit coactivators and increase transcription. The same DNA-associated complex can therefore participate in repression and activation depending on the ligand and cellular context.

This model shows why receptor localization is not a sufficient measure of receptor activation. If immunofluorescence detects thyroid hormone receptor in the nucleus both before and after T3 exposure, the receptor could still undergo a major functional change. If a chromatin association assay detects similar receptor occupancy at a target element under both conditions, the associated coregulator composition or transcriptional state may nevertheless change. A reporter or RNA measurement is needed to assess regulatory output. A protein's location and its activity are separate properties even when location is important for the activity.

A DNA element containing a recognition sequence does not respond equally in every experimental context. Local chromatin accessibility, regulatory partners and promoter organization influence transcription. Moreover, not every thyroid hormone-responsive gene follows the simple positively regulated model. Some physiological effects involve gene repression or indirect changes in expression. The standard unliganded corepressor to liganded coactivator transition provides a strong conceptual starting point, but it should not be imposed indiscriminately on all hormone-regulated genes. When interpreting a data figure, the sign of a change in RNA abundance should come from the actual measurements.

Comparing T4 and T3 reveals an important distinction between a circulating precursor and an intracellular receptor ligand. If two cells receive equal extracellular T4, but one can convert it efficiently to T3 and the other cannot, their transcriptional responses may differ. This does not necessarily imply different thyroid receptor affinity or expression. Providing T3 instead of T4 may bypass the conversion requirement, provided entry and receptor function remain adequate. This is a valuable experimental design for locating a defect in hormone metabolism rather than receptor function. A failure of T3 rescue, however, could be caused by defective transport as well as receptor or downstream problems.

A transporter defect and a receptor defect can produce a superficially similar phenotype. Suppose a cultured cell shows low expression of a thyroid hormone-responsive reporter when exposed to T3. The reporter may be intact, but the concentration of intracellular T3 may be inadequate. Measuring T3 uptake and receptor expression in parallel helps separate the possibilities. Restoring a missing transporter could rescue the response if transporter-mediated access was limiting. Restoring a receptor could rescue the response if the receptor was defective. An informative experiment controls the expression level of each introduced protein and avoids interpreting rescue caused by nonspecific changes in cellular condition.

The role of nuclear thyroid receptors can also be distinguished from the broader category of intracellular receptors. Soluble guanylyl cyclase is an intracellular receptor for nitric oxide, but its direct output is cyclic GMP production. It is not a member of the nuclear hormone receptor family and does not function by the same DNA-binding architecture. Thus, all classical nuclear receptors are intracellular in their canonical genomic pathway, but not all intracellular signal receptors are nuclear transcription regulators. Exam questions often exploit this difference between the location-based category and the molecular-function category.

A coherent classification now emerges. Catecholamines and thyroid hormones are related in their amino acid origin but differ in access and receptor mechanisms. Steroids and thyroid hormones have different chemical origins yet share the broad principle of nuclear receptor-mediated gene regulation. Peptide hormones share a broad restriction on passive membrane entry but can activate several unrelated classes of surface receptors. These relationships are easier to understand if chemical origin, membrane accessibility, receptor topology and primary output are considered four independent descriptors rather than four interchangeable names.

14. Response timing: distinguish initial signal from final biological effect

Cellular responses can be measured on many timescales because different molecular events require different numbers of intermediate steps. A ligand-gated ion channel can change conductance as soon as ligand binding alters its gating behavior. A GPCR can influence an enzyme through a G protein and change the concentration of a messenger within a short interval. An intrinsic kinase receptor can rapidly alter the phosphorylation state of itself and its substrates. These early events frequently involve proteins that are already synthesized and localized. They do not require the cell to first produce an entirely new set of proteins.

A transcription-dependent increase in protein abundance requires additional stages. A regulatory protein must influence transcription, newly synthesized RNA must become available for translation, and the new protein must accumulate enough to change the measured cellular output. If the measured outcome is the activity of an enzyme that is induced by a nuclear hormone receptor, the time to increase RNA abundance and the time to increase enzyme activity need not be identical. The difference reflects production, processing and turnover of the relevant molecules. Even when hormone recognition is fast, a downstream change in protein abundance may require a much longer interval.

A useful comparison distinguishes the onset, peak and persistence of a response. Onset is the earliest time at which a particular measurement becomes detectably different from baseline. The peak occurs when the measured change is maximal. Persistence describes how long the change remains after the initiating stimulus has declined or been removed. A response can have a rapid onset and short duration, or a rapid onset with prolonged downstream consequences. Conversely, a delayed response may disappear rapidly if it depends on an unstable product. Timing is shaped by multiple molecular processes, not simply by receptor location.

Consider an extracellular peptide that activates a cytokine receptor. The initial receptor-associated kinase response can be rapid, followed by accumulation of particular transcripts and proteins. If the only measurement is an induced protein several hours after treatment, the response might appear similar to a conventional nuclear receptor pathway. Yet the hormone may never enter the cytosol during primary recognition. It is the activated intracellular transcription factor, rather than the hormone itself, that reaches and regulates nuclear targets. Consequently, a delayed transcriptional response is not adequate evidence for a nuclear hormone receptor.

Now consider a rapid steroid response that modifies existing ion channels or enzymes. A requirement for new RNA synthesis is unlikely to explain an effect occurring before new transcripts could produce sufficient protein. A reasonable hypothesis would be a pathway involving existing proteins, possibly including non-genomic steroid signaling. The conclusion requires attention to experimental resolution. If a response is recorded only at ten-minute intervals, it is impossible to determine from those observations whether the initial event occurred after one second or nine minutes. A time series must be designed to resolve the expected stages.

The term genomic signaling describes a response involving regulation of gene expression through the relevant receptor pathway. The term non-genomic is commonly applied to actions that do not require the conventional ligand-dependent transcriptional response for the immediate effect. These categories need not describe mutually isolated networks. An early signaling event can later modify transcription, and a gene-regulatory pathway can change the abundance of components controlling future rapid responses. The same cell can integrate both kinds of regulation. The distinction is most informative when the measured endpoint and the time interval are specified.

An experiment involving brief hormone exposure followed by washout can reveal how long a response persists after removal of extracellular ligand. A persistent response might reflect slow hormone dissociation, continued signaling from an intracellular compartment, slow turnover of a modified protein or the accumulation of a stable newly synthesized protein. Washout by itself cannot distinguish these alternatives. Assays of receptor occupancy, phosphorylation, messenger concentration, RNA and protein over the same period can help determine which state persists. A mechanistic explanation should identify a molecular property that can retain the signal after the original input has diminished.

The energy required for each stage must also be distinguished from the speed of the stage. Direct gating can use an electrochemical gradient that was established before ligand binding. Kinase signaling consumes ATP in phosphorylation reactions, but these reactions can still occur rapidly. RNA and protein synthesis consume cellular resources and require multiple coordinated processes, yet the first transcriptional changes may be detected much earlier than a final phenotype. Describing an immediate response as energy-free or all transcriptional responses as uniformly slow would obscure these differences. Receptor architecture indicates the first biochemical event; the time course reveals how that event propagates through the cell.

15. Receptor distribution and the identity of the responding cell

Hormones entering the circulation can encounter many tissues, but encountering a cell and regulating its activity are different events. Imagine a hormone passing through a blood vessel supplying muscle, liver and connective tissue. The hormone does not contain a chemical address that restricts its physical presence to one target tissue. Selective action instead emerges from the distribution of receptor proteins and from the accessibility of those proteins to the hormone. A cell that lacks a particular receptor may be directly insensitive to the corresponding hormone even though neighboring cells respond strongly.

A receptor transcript is an indication that a cell has the capacity to synthesize a particular receptor, but the amount of transcript does not specify how many functional receptor molecules are present. Translation, folding, post-translational modification, assembly and turnover intervene between RNA and active protein. For a membrane receptor, trafficking to the surface is another necessary stage. A receptor protein retained in the endoplasmic reticulum may be detectable in a whole-cell extract yet unavailable to extracellular ligand. For an intracellular receptor, ligand access and localization to the appropriate compartment must also be considered. Gene expression data, total protein measurements and accessible binding-site measurements answer distinct questions.

Receptor subtype expression can cause two tissues to interpret the same hormone differently. Catecholamines illustrate this principle particularly clearly. One population of cells may express a beta adrenergic receptor that commonly stimulates adenylyl cyclase through a stimulatory G protein. Another population may express alpha2 adrenergic receptors that commonly reduce cyclase activity through inhibitory G proteins. Under suitable conditions, the same external hormone can cause opposing changes in the same intracellular messenger. These differences do not require that the hormone itself change chemical identity on entering the two tissues. They arise because its information is interpreted by different receptor-coupling systems.

Differences in cellular machinery can also produce divergent physiological outcomes when the same receptor subtype or messenger is involved. In a cardiac muscle cell, stimulation of beta adrenergic signaling can modify proteins that control contraction. In some smooth muscle contexts, beta adrenergic signaling can instead favor relaxation through the proteins available in that cell. It would be an error to equate an increase in cyclic AMP with contraction as a universal rule. A messenger influences the substrates and regulatory proteins accessible to it, and the consequences of modifying those targets depend on their physiological roles. The messenger conveys an instruction, but the cell supplies the set of operations that the instruction can regulate.

Vasopressin provides a peptide example of receptor subtype diversity. V1a receptors commonly couple to signaling that involves phospholipase C, whereas V2 receptors commonly couple to signaling that increases cyclic AMP. In appropriate kidney cells, the latter pathway regulates the availability of water channels at the apical membrane. The peptide itself does not become a component of the water channel, and it does not physically escort water into the cell. In a different tissue, activation of another receptor subtype can influence cellular contraction instead. A defect that selectively affects V2 receptors would not necessarily abolish all responses to vasopressin elsewhere.

The distribution of intracellular receptor isoforms can likewise contribute to tissue specificity. Thyroid hormone receptors arise from distinct receptor genes and transcript variants, and their expression varies among cell types. The local abundance of transporters and deiodinases can additionally change ligand availability. Thus, an experiment comparing T3 responses between tissues should not assume that receptor binding is the only variable. Two tissues with comparable nuclear receptor abundance may differ in intracellular T3 concentration or in the regulatory elements and cofactors through which the receptor acts. A complete interpretation separates hormone availability, receptor function and transcriptional competence.

The role of cellular context also explains why receptor expression in a heterologous model is powerful but not universally decisive. A scientist might introduce a receptor gene into cultured cells that lack a detectable response to the hormone. If an appropriate response appears, the result supports a limiting role for the receptor under those experimental conditions. If no response appears, the introduced receptor may not have folded correctly, may be trapped in an inaccessible compartment or may lack a compatible signaling partner. The absence of rescue is not automatically evidence that the receptor cannot recognize the hormone. The experiment needs a positive control demonstrating that the relevant downstream machinery can respond when stimulated by another route.

Hormone signaling can also alter the sensitivity of a cell over longer periods. Changes in receptor production, turnover and distribution can modify how a subsequent hormone exposure is interpreted. Such changes need not occur in all receptor systems or in the same direction. A receptor initially abundant at the surface may become less available after repeated stimulation, whereas a different hormone-regulated transcriptional program may increase the abundance of a receptor in a particular tissue. The details of desensitization and feedback are treated later, but the immediate implication is that receptor expression is a regulated state rather than an unchanging label assigned to a cell.

16. Quantitative comparison of receptor abundance, affinity and hormone access

Quantitative measurements help prevent apparently similar responses from being attributed to the same molecular cause. A minimal model considers a receptor R that reversibly binds one ligand L to form a complex RL. At equilibrium the rate of association equals the rate of dissociation, and the dissociation constant Kd equals the product of free receptor concentration and free ligand concentration divided by the complex concentration. All concentrations must be expressed in compatible units. This is an equilibrium binding model, not a universal description of all receptor systems, and assumes one class of independent, equivalent binding sites.

Let Rtotal represent the total concentration of binding sites, equal to free R plus RL. Substitution of free R as Rtotal minus RL into the equilibrium definition gives Kd multiplied by RL equal to free L multiplied by the difference between Rtotal and RL. Collecting the terms containing RL on one side gives RL multiplied by the sum of Kd and free L equal to Rtotal multiplied by free L. Dividing by the total receptor concentration and by the sum gives the occupied fraction, theta, equal to free L divided by the sum of Kd and free L. The equation has no units because both the numerator and denominator are concentrations in the same units.

When free ligand concentration equals Kd, the model predicts half occupancy. A receptor with a lower Kd has a higher equilibrium affinity than a receptor with a higher Kd when compared under the same conditions. This statement concerns the fraction occupied at a specified free ligand concentration, not the magnitude of the biological response. If the two receptors have different levels of expression or different efficiency of downstream coupling, the same fractional occupancy can lead to different responses. The distinction is particularly important when comparing hormone-sensitive tissues rather than purified proteins.

Consider a hypothetical pair of receptor populations with the same Kd of 2 nanomolar. Cell A has 20,000 accessible receptor binding sites, while cell B has 5,000 accessible sites. If the free hormone concentration is maintained at 6 nanomolar, theta equals 6 divided by 8, or 0.75. The two cells have the same fractional occupancy, but A has 15,000 occupied sites while B has 3,750. If each occupied site produced the same initial signaling output and the system remained in a linear range, A could generate four times as much initial output. This last prediction requires explicit assumptions; downstream components may saturate or become limiting.

Now reverse the comparison and consider two receptor types in a cell population with the same accessible receptor abundance. One has a Kd of 1 nanomolar, and the other has a Kd of 9 nanomolar. At 3 nanomolar free ligand, their fractional occupancies are 3 divided by 4 and 3 divided by 12, respectively. This gives 75 percent and 25 percent occupancy. With 8,000 accessible sites of each type, the predicted occupied counts are 6,000 and 2,000. Higher affinity produces more occupied sites in this particular comparison because the abundance is held constant. It does not establish that the first receptor's downstream output will be threefold greater. The receptors may couple to different effectors altogether.

The value of Kd must not be confused with EC50, the ligand concentration giving half of the maximal measured response in a defined dose-response experiment. The former is a binding parameter, and the latter is a functional parameter. If a system has substantial amplification, a half-maximal output can occur when fewer than half of its receptor sites are occupied. For example, a hypothetical receptor with Kd of 10 nanomolar would be about 9.1 percent occupied at 1 nanomolar free ligand. If its signaling system gives half of maximal output at that concentration, the measured EC50 would be 1 nanomolar, not 10 nanomolar. The difference does not violate the binding model because response and occupancy are distinct measured variables.

This separation is useful when examining receptor mutants. Suppose a mutant has the same equilibrium binding curve as the normal protein but a right-shifted functional dose-response curve and a lower maximum output. One possible explanation is impaired signal coupling rather than altered ligand recognition. A reduction in accessible receptor number could also change the functional response if the binding curves were normalized to each population's own maximum. Absolute binding capacity must therefore be examined along with normalized affinity. A graph containing only percentages of maximal binding can conceal large changes in the number of sites available.

Another quantitative complication occurs when hormones bind abundant extracellular carrier proteins. A simple model considers a ligand L that binds a single class of carrier sites C to produce CL. The dissociation constant Kc equals free carrier multiplied by free ligand divided by bound complex concentration. If the free ligand concentration is far below Kc and the carrier remains far from saturation, most carrier sites remain unoccupied and free carrier concentration can be approximated by total carrier concentration. Rearranging the equilibrium equation then gives bound hormone approximately equal to free hormone multiplied by the ratio of total carrier concentration to Kc.

The total hormone concentration in this model is the sum of free and carrier-bound hormone. Substituting the approximate expression for bound hormone gives total hormone approximately equal to free hormone multiplied by one plus total carrier concentration divided by Kc. The free fraction is therefore approximately one divided by that bracketed factor. This is a deliberately simplified model for illustrating the effects of carrier binding. It ignores other carriers, ligand depletion of binding sites, tissue uptake and physiological feedback. The approximation should be rejected if a large fraction of the available carrier sites becomes occupied.

For a hypothetical calculation, take total carrier concentration as 900 nanomolar and Kc as 100 nanomolar. Under dilute, unsaturated conditions, the free fraction is one divided by ten, or 0.10. A total hormone concentration of 2 nanomolar then corresponds to approximately 0.2 nanomolar free hormone. If carrier abundance increases to 1,900 nanomolar without changing total hormone or Kc, the approximate free fraction falls to one divided by twenty, or 0.05, corresponding to 0.1 nanomolar free hormone. Both free concentrations are much lower than the assumed Kc, and the bound concentrations are very small relative to carrier abundance, supporting the approximation in this example.

Suppose an intracellular receptor is accessible to a ligand in equilibrium with the free extracellular pool, and for the sake of the example the receptor-site free ligand concentration equals the calculated free concentration. With a receptor Kd of 0.2 nanomolar, the first condition would give half occupancy and the second would give one-third occupancy. The number of total hormone molecules measured in the extracellular samples has not changed, yet the predicted fraction of receptor sites occupied has. In real thyroid hormone signaling, transport and local metabolism may invalidate the assumption that extracellular free ligand concentration equals the concentration at the nuclear receptor. This is a reason to state the simplifying assumption clearly rather than to transfer the numerical prediction directly to a physiological situation.

A final issue is ligand depletion. The simple occupancy equation uses the free concentration of the ligand, not necessarily the amount initially added to a sample. If receptors or carrier sites bind a substantial fraction of the ligand present, the remaining free concentration may be considerably smaller than the nominal starting concentration. Adding more binding sites can then change both the number of occupied sites and the fraction occupied. The examples above stipulated a maintained free ligand concentration or dilute carrier conditions to avoid that complication. Whenever a question gives total rather than free ligand, the student must decide whether it is reasonable to approximate one by the other before substituting into an equation.

17. Comparing the first molecular event and the source of energy

The receptor classes can be organized by a single question: what happens immediately after recognition becomes productive? For a ligand-gated ion channel, the probability of an ion-conducting state changes. For a conventional GPCR, the receptor promotes activation of a separate G protein. For an intrinsic kinase receptor, a receptor catalytic domain alters the phosphorylation state of appropriate proteins. A kinase-associated receptor relies on a separate enzyme to carry out a similar chemical transfer. A receptor guanylyl cyclase changes the rate of cyclic nucleotide formation through its own catalytic region. A classical nuclear receptor changes its regulatory interactions with DNA and associated proteins.

Each event must be distinguished from the final physiological effect. If a receptor guanylyl cyclase initiates a rise in cyclic GMP, the messenger may activate a kinase that modifies proteins. Detecting that later phosphorylation does not transform the original receptor into a protein kinase. A receptor tyrosine kinase may indirectly change the concentration of a lipid-derived intracellular messenger without catalyzing its synthesis itself. A ligand-gated calcium-permeable channel can alter transcription through calcium-dependent proteins without becoming a nuclear receptor. It is possible to trace a common downstream output backward through multiple distinct architectures. The earliest class-defining event is therefore an anchor for causal interpretation.

The relevant energy sources differ as well. Opening an ion channel permits movement along an electrochemical gradient. The gradient may have been created or maintained by energy-consuming pumps, but the channel does not normally hydrolyze an ATP molecule for every ion that flows through it. In a kinase pathway, phosphorylation consumes ATP as a phosphate donor. In a GPCR pathway, the G-protein cycle uses guanine nucleotide binding and hydrolysis, while further downstream reactions can consume other substrates. In guanylyl cyclase signaling, GTP is converted into cyclic GMP and pyrophosphate. Transcription and subsequent synthesis of new proteins require nucleotide and other energy resources, but the nuclear receptor is not itself the enzyme that drives RNA chain elongation.

This comparison helps distinguish a signal from a source of chemical energy. The hormone may remain bound only transiently and may eventually dissociate without being chemically transformed by its receptor. The cellular response can nevertheless involve large changes in metabolic activity. The receptor controls the use of pre-existing chemical substrates and gradients. If a hormone-triggered reaction requires ATP, the ATP does not ordinarily originate in the hormone molecule. It comes from the target cell. This principle explains how low concentrations of signaling molecules can cause substantial biological effects.

In experiments involving metabolic inhibitors, interpretation must account for this organization. A severe reduction in cellular ATP could compromise phosphorylation, membrane potential maintenance, protein synthesis and transport at the same time. A reduced hormone response after such a treatment is not specific evidence for a kinase receptor. To establish a requirement for intrinsic receptor catalysis, a more selective perturbation, such as a receptor mutation verified to preserve ligand binding and surface expression, is more informative. Global disruption of energy supply can show that the response depends on cellular function but seldom identifies the particular molecular architecture of the receptor.

There is also a difference between a receptor's catalytic capacity and the strength of a measured output. A receptor enzyme can turn over multiple substrate molecules after activation, but its rate depends on substrate concentration, catalytic state and inhibitory regulation. A ligand-gated channel can permit many ions to cross during an open interval, but the net flux depends on the driving force and conductance. Both systems can amplify a ligand-binding event in a functional sense without using the same molecular operation. A question about amplification should specify whether it refers to messenger molecules, modified substrates, transported ions or an eventual cellular response.

18. Experimental strategies for identifying an unknown receptor class

An unknown extracellular signal can be investigated through a series of experiments that distinguish ligand access, binding, receptor structure and signal transmission. The first question is whether the cell can respond when the signal is restricted to the extracellular environment. A ligand attached to a sufficiently large impermeant carrier can sometimes be used to test this idea. If the modified ligand retains biological activity without entering the cell, a surface-accessible recognition site becomes plausible. The result is informative only if the modification preserves the features necessary for recognition and if the preparation contains no appreciable unmodified hormone.

This qualification is essential in comparing steroid and peptide signals. Attaching a steroid to a bulky carrier changes its chemistry and can interfere with receptor binding or expose the cell to a small amount of free steroid released from the conjugate. A negative response may simply mean that the attached ligand no longer interacts effectively with its receptor. A positive response may reflect unmodified ligand contamination or internalization of the conjugate. An impermeant conjugate is therefore a useful experimental tool only with controls for chemical integrity, surface accessibility and ligand release. It is not an infallible test for the location of the functional receptor.

Surface labeling provides a complementary approach. A reagent that reacts with exposed extracellular protein groups without crossing intact membranes can label accessible surface proteins. After cell lysis, an investigator can determine whether a candidate receptor was present in the labeled population. A control cytoplasmic protein should remain unlabeled if the reagent truly stayed outside intact cells. A membrane receptor detected in a total extract but absent from the surface-labeled fraction may have been retained in an internal compartment. If ligand cannot reach that compartment, the reduction in signaling could reflect accessibility rather than altered affinity or intrinsic activity.

An antibody recognizing an extracellular receptor epitope can also be used to examine receptor localization in intact, nonpermeabilized cells. In a parallel sample, permeabilization allows access to internal epitopes. Comparing the two conditions helps distinguish total receptor abundance from the accessible surface population. Antibodies must be validated for specificity, and an absence of extracellular labeling might arise from a masked or inaccessible epitope rather than the complete absence of receptor from the membrane. A reliable interpretation often combines labeling with independent ligand-binding and functional assays.

One can move from localization to the biochemical identity of the receptor by examining its molecular sequence and domain organization. A protein containing a predicted seven-transmembrane helical bundle is a candidate for a GPCR, but seven membrane-spanning segments alone do not demonstrate G-protein coupling. Some membrane proteins with comparable topology perform other functions. Similarly, a single-pass membrane protein may be a receptor kinase, a cyclase, a kinase-associated receptor or another kind of membrane protein. Domain sequence and experimentally established activity provide the additional evidence needed to classify it.

The next step is to identify the earliest response that depends on the candidate protein. An ion current that begins shortly after ligand application suggests changes in membrane conductance. Measurement of G-protein nucleotide exchange can reveal coupling to the GPCR class. Phosphorylation of a defined receptor substrate by a purified receptor catalytic domain supports an intrinsic kinase mechanism, whereas loss of phosphorylation upon removal of an associated kinase supports a partner-dependent mechanism. Direct formation of cyclic GMP from GTP by the candidate receptor's intracellular catalytic region supports guanylyl cyclase activity. Each test addresses a class-defining function rather than merely measuring a distant shared output.

A study of an intracellular transcriptional receptor must establish more than the presence of hormone-binding protein in a nuclear fraction. A candidate protein with appropriate DNA-binding and ligand-binding domains may be tested for hormone-dependent effects on a reporter containing the relevant regulatory element. Alteration of the element, disruption of receptor binding or modification of a receptor domain can reveal which components are necessary. Measurements of RNA abundance offer a closer readout of transcription than enzyme activity measured several hours later, although RNA stability also influences steady-state abundance. When exact transcription rates are required, assays focused on newly produced RNA are more informative than total RNA alone.

Loss-of-function and rescue experiments connect candidate identity to cellular necessity. If removal of the receptor eliminates the response and reintroduction of a normal receptor restores it, the simplest interpretation is that the receptor was required in that system. A well-designed experiment includes cells receiving an empty vector and verifies that expression of the reintroduced receptor is appropriate. Excessive overexpression can create abnormal interactions or change signaling thresholds. A rescue performed at unrealistically high receptor abundance may demonstrate potential for signaling without showing that the same organization operates at normal abundance.

An instructive hypothetical dataset concerns a peptide and two candidate surface proteins, A and B. Removing A eliminates hormone binding and phosphorylation of B. Removing B preserves hormone binding to A but eliminates the later response. These observations suggest that A is upstream of B. They do not establish whether A has intrinsic kinase activity, whether B supplies associated kinase activity, or whether a third molecule connects them. A direct biochemical assay of A and B, combined with their domain organization, is required to distinguish these models. This example teaches why genetic ordering and biochemical receptor classification are complementary but not identical.

Finally, a claim that a receptor is sufficient to produce a biological response should name the experimental conditions. Expression of a ligand-binding GPCR in a recipient cell that already contains compatible G proteins and downstream effectors can create a response that was absent before. That result does not imply that the receptor alone, purified away from every cellular partner, can carry out all steps. The term sufficiency is relative to the system in which a component is introduced. It is the addition of the receptor to an existing molecular environment that proves sufficient in this example, not the receptor's ability to substitute for that entire environment.

19. Interpreting perturbations across receptor classes

A powerful approach to a signaling problem is to construct a small causal chain in which each step produces a measurable state. For a representative GPCR pathway, the chain might contain extracellular hormone recognition, G-protein activation and production of an intracellular messenger. For a receptor tyrosine kinase pathway, it might contain ligand binding, receptor kinase activation and phosphorylation of a downstream substrate. For a nuclear receptor pathway, the chain might contain hormone access, receptor binding, DNA-associated regulation and accumulation of a target transcript. The appropriate measurements differ among pathways, but the reasoning strategy is shared.

Suppose a hypothetical peptide triggers phosphorylation of an intracellular substrate in cultured cells. The normal receptor binds ligand and has an intracellular domain containing a predicted kinase sequence. A mutation in this domain preserves binding and receptor surface abundance but abolishes substrate phosphorylation. A second construct restores the normal catalytic sequence and rescues the response. This is strong evidence that the intracellular region is necessary for productive signaling. To establish intrinsic kinase activity, a direct assay of the candidate catalytic domain would be especially useful. The mutation could theoretically disrupt a partner interaction rather than the catalytic reaction itself unless its biochemical effects were characterized.

Now suppose another peptide produces the same substrate phosphorylation but binds a receptor that lacks a catalytic kinase domain. The response requires an associated kinase, and an interaction-deficient receptor mutant loses phosphorylation despite normal hormone recognition. These data support a kinase-associated receptor architecture. A selective kinase inhibitor might suppress both receptor phosphorylation and downstream substrate modification. Nevertheless, inhibition alone does not locate the enzyme relative to the receptor. The domain and interaction evidence are what distinguish the associated arrangement from an intrinsic receptor kinase.

An analogous comparison can be made for cyclic nucleotide signaling. Suppose ligand X stimulates accumulation of cyclic GMP in a cell expressing a single-pass receptor with a guanylyl cyclase domain. A catalytic-domain mutation preserves ligand binding but prevents cyclic GMP production. Ligand Y stimulates cyclic GMP accumulation in another cell even when the membrane receptor is absent, and its response depends on soluble guanylyl cyclase. The messenger is the same in the two cases, but the routes differ. If phosphodiesterase activity is also modified, the measured cyclic GMP concentration may change independently of receptor activation. An experiment measuring the rate of nucleotide formation can help separate synthesis from degradation.

A different hypothetical case concerns thyroid hormone-sensitive cells with an intact reporter and a verified functional nuclear receptor. Cell line A responds to both extracellular T4 and T3. Cell line B responds to T3 but not T4, while uptake measurements show that both hormones enter the cell. This pattern is compatible with a defect in T4-to-T3 conversion. An assay of the relevant deiodinase activity would test the hypothesis directly. If cell line C responds to neither external hormone but responds when T3 is made available inside the cell, impaired cellular access becomes more plausible. In each case the downstream reporter must be verified to respond to an appropriate positive control.

The word rescue does not necessarily mean that a normal pathway has been fully reconstructed. An experimentally delivered second messenger or an independently activated downstream protein may bypass a defective receptor and restore one measured endpoint. This demonstrates that the downstream machinery remains capable of producing that endpoint. It does not establish restoration of the receptor's normal specificity, regulation or response timing. A bypass experiment is particularly useful for locating a lesion relative to the rescued step, but it should not be mistaken for complete physiological correction.

The direction of an effect is also informative when comparing receptor subtypes. Consider two cell types that both express catecholamine-binding receptors. After hormone addition, cyclic AMP rises in one and falls in the other. A simple explanation is that the receptors couple to different G proteins, but a change in cyclic AMP removal could also contribute to the observed pattern. Measurements of cyclase activity and messenger degradation, together with identification of the receptor and its associated G protein, would test the competing explanations. The data demonstrate opposing net responses; they do not automatically demonstrate the exact mechanisms generating them.

Experimental interpretation becomes more difficult when observations are made at a single late time point. A hormone could have produced a brief early phosphorylation event that has already disappeared, while changes in target RNA or protein remain. A second hormone might produce sustained phosphorylation but little change in the selected transcript. Neither pattern can be described accurately by comparing only the final protein concentration. A time course of proximal and distal endpoints provides information about both the initiation of signaling and the propagation of its effects.

When comparing the timing of different receptor classes, it is also important to control the dose relative to receptor affinity and functional sensitivity. An extracellular peptide applied far below its effective concentration may produce a delayed or weak measurable response, while a readily accessible intracellular ligand applied at an effective dose may induce detectable transcription relatively early. This would not reverse the basic architectural distinction between the pathways. It would demonstrate that assay timing depends on how the experiment stimulates and measures the system. Comparisons should use clearly specified concentrations, appropriate controls and matched definitions of onset.

These experimental principles are broadly applicable to examination problems because they reduce the temptation to infer a complete pathway from one observation. The most reliable inference is the narrowest one that accounts for the measurements and survives the stated controls. A binding assay can establish recognition, a localization assay can establish accessibility, a biochemical assay can establish a catalytic capability, and a rescue experiment can establish that a defect lies upstream of a restored output under defined conditions. Together, these measurements make it possible to reason about receptor mechanism rather than merely recall the hormone's name.

20. Integrating hormone classes with cell organization and gene expression

Hormone signaling provides a bridge between concepts that are often studied separately in molecular biology and physiology. The membrane's physical barrier explains why a water-soluble peptide requires an accessible receptor. Protein folding explains why receptor mutations can alter ligand recognition without necessarily changing protein abundance. Enzyme chemistry explains why different catalytic receptors consume different substrates. Nuclear transport and gene regulation explain why intracellular receptors can alter the production of selected cellular proteins. These principles come together in the question of how a chemical instruction changes cell behavior.

Consider a mutation that impairs trafficking of a membrane receptor. The relevant first failure belongs to protein localization, even though the observed phenotype may appear endocrine. A mutation that disrupts the active site of a receptor kinase primarily affects catalysis. A mutation in a nuclear receptor's DNA-binding region primarily affects recognition of a regulatory sequence. All can cause reduced cellular responses to a hormone, but the appropriate experiments for investigating them differ. The mechanistic level of the defect is more informative than the broad label of hormone resistance.

A similar distinction helps in comparing signaling across organisms. Steroid-derived ecdysteroids in insects provide examples of small lipophilic signaling molecules whose canonical actions involve nuclear receptor complexes regulating developmental gene expression. The precise receptor partners and physiological functions differ from mammalian glucocorticoid pathways. The conserved principle is that ligand recognition can be coupled directly to transcriptional regulation by a receptor family, not that every steroid hormone produces the same response or binds the same receptor. A comparative statement must identify the shared molecular logic without erasing organism-specific features.

Plant hormone signaling also illustrates the limitations of imposing mammalian categories on every organism. Some plant hormone receptors operate inside cells and regulate transcription through changes in the stability or activity of other regulatory proteins, rather than using the classical animal nuclear receptor architecture. Other plant hormone responses involve membrane-associated receptor systems. The relevant point here is that intracellular localization is not synonymous with membership in the nuclear hormone receptor superfamily. Detailed plant hormone mechanisms require their own physiological and signaling context.

A learner faced with an unfamiliar receptor can now proceed systematically. First identify the ligand's chemical properties and the evidence for how it reaches the receptor. Next examine the receptor's topology and molecular domains. Then distinguish the first biochemical event from the eventual cellular phenotype. Determine whether a catalytic domain belongs to the receptor or to a separate partner, whether a nucleotide is a switch or substrate, and whether transcriptional regulation is direct or mediated through downstream factors. Finally consider receptor abundance, ligand availability, cellular machinery, time and experimental limitations. This framework links the hormone-receptor classification to the mechanistic pathways developed in subsequent chapters.

Part B conceptual practice

Original questions for NET/SET preparation.

1. Which feature explains the usual need for a cell-surface receptor for glucagon?

  1. It cannot bind any intracellular protein.
  2. Its receptor must be an ion channel.
  3. Its peptide backbone and polar groups make passive passage through the bilayer unfavorable.
  4. It supplies phosphate directly to intracellular enzymes.
Answer and explanation

C. Hydration of the polar peptide disfavors direct bilayer traversal. The chemical class does not specify a channel, nor exclude binding to an intracellular protein under artificial conditions. Phosphate donors come from the target cell.

2. Which pair illustrates different receptor locations despite a common tyrosine-derived chemical origin?

  1. Epinephrine and T3
  2. Insulin and glucagon
  3. Cortisol and aldosterone
  4. Growth hormone and prolactin
Answer and explanation

A. Catecholamine signaling typically uses membrane GPCRs; T3 acts mainly through nuclear receptors after regulated cellular access. The other pairs share a broad chemical family but do not illustrate this particular contrast.

3. Which property directly defines an intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase?

  1. Association with a separate JAK protein.
  2. Binding a guanine nucleotide as a regulatory switch.
  3. Activation of a nuclear transcription factor.
  4. A cytoplasmic domain that transfers phosphate from ATP to tyrosine residues.
Answer and explanation

D. The receptor itself supplies catalytic activity. JAK-associated receptors use a separate kinase. GTP switches characterize G proteins, while nuclear output can occur through several classes.

4. Which statement about the mature insulin receptor is correct?

  1. It is a monomeric seven-pass receptor that activates Gs.
  2. It is a preassembled disulfide-linked alpha2 beta2 complex with kinase domains on beta subunits.
  3. Its alpha chains form the cytoplasmic kinase domains.
  4. It lacks intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity and uses JAK exclusively.
Answer and explanation

B. The extracellular alpha chains participate in recognition, and membrane-spanning beta chains carry the kinase domains. It is neither a GPCR nor a kinase-free cytokine receptor.

5. What is the initial class-defining event for a conventional GPCR?

  1. Promotion of nucleotide exchange on a separate heterotrimeric G protein.
  2. Direct conversion of GTP to cGMP by the receptor.
  3. Direct opening of a ligand-gated pore.
  4. Direct phosphorylation of tyrosine by the receptor.
Answer and explanation

A. A GPCR promotes GDP release and GTP binding on G alpha. Cyclases, ionotropic receptors and intrinsic kinases perform the other operations.

6. Which pair is most appropriate for comparing intrinsic and associated tyrosine kinase signaling?

  1. Glucagon receptor and beta adrenergic receptor
  2. Nicotinic and muscarinic acetylcholine receptors
  3. Insulin receptor and growth hormone receptor
  4. Cortisol receptor and thyroid hormone receptor
Answer and explanation

C. The insulin receptor has an intrinsic kinase; the growth hormone receptor uses associated JAK. The other comparisons involve GPCRs, channel versus GPCR, or nuclear receptors.

7. Which substrate is directly converted by a receptor guanylyl cyclase?

  1. ATP
  2. GTP
  3. GDP-bound G alpha
  4. A receptor tyrosine residue
Answer and explanation

B. GTP is cyclized to cGMP with release of pyrophosphate. ATP is the substrate for adenylyl cyclase and a phosphate donor for kinases, not the cyclase substrate in question.

8. Which is a canonical ligand for a receptor guanylyl cyclase?

  1. Nitric oxide acting at soluble guanylyl cyclase
  2. Insulin acting at insulin receptor
  3. Cortisol acting at glucocorticoid receptor
  4. Atrial natriuretic peptide acting at NPR-A
Answer and explanation

D. NPR-A is a membrane cyclase receptor for ANP. NO acts on a soluble cyclase; insulin and cortisol use other receptor architectures.

9. Which event directly follows opening of a ligand-gated ion channel when a driving force exists?

  1. ATP hydrolysis by the pore for each transported ion
  2. Obligatory transcription of a new channel gene
  3. Synthesis of cAMP by the receptor catalytic domain
  4. Ion movement down the relevant electrochemical gradient
Answer and explanation

D. A gated channel changes permeability and uses an existing gradient. Pumps may establish that gradient, but the channel is not a pump or nucleotide cyclase.

10. Which receptor for acetylcholine is ionotropic?

  1. Nicotinic receptor
  2. Muscarinic M2 receptor
  3. Muscarinic M3 receptor
  4. All muscarinic receptor subtypes
Answer and explanation

A. Nicotinic receptors contain a ligand-regulated cation pore. Muscarinic receptors are GPCRs, showing that a ligand name does not determine receptor architecture.

11. Which region of a classical nuclear receptor most directly recognizes a response element?

  1. Ligand-binding pocket alone
  2. Transmembrane domain
  3. DNA-binding domain containing zinc-coordinating structures
  4. Intrinsic guanylyl cyclase domain
Answer and explanation

C. The DNA-binding domain establishes sequence-specific contacts. The ligand-binding domain regulates conformation and coregulators; conventional nuclear receptors lack the membrane and cyclase domains proposed.

12. What is a typical feature of positively regulated thyroid hormone target genes in the textbook model?

  1. T3 must activate a receptor tyrosine kinase in the plasma membrane.
  2. TR/RXR may be DNA-associated before T3 and change coregulator interactions after binding.
  3. Unliganded TR is always excluded from the nucleus.
  4. T4 directly catalyzes transcription without a protein receptor.
Answer and explanation

B. The canonical model includes an already DNA-associated receptor complex and ligand-dependent regulatory changes. Nuclear localization alone does not establish activation.

13. Which comparison of free and total steroid hormone is correct?

  1. Total hormone includes carrier-bound and free pools; accessibility depends partly on the free pool.
  2. All carrier-bound hormone immediately crosses into the cytosol intact.
  3. Free hormone is necessarily equal to total hormone.
  4. Carrier proteins are the same functional class as nuclear receptors.
Answer and explanation

A. Carrier binding changes the free fraction. Transport and signaling proteins have different functions; equal free and total concentrations require negligible binding.

14. Which receptor architecture is characteristic of TGF-beta family signaling?

  1. Seven-pass GPCR coupled to Gs exclusively.
  2. Ligand-gated sodium pore with no kinase activity.
  3. Nuclear receptor directly binding TGF-beta after passive diffusion.
  4. Type I and type II receptor kinases with characteristic serine/threonine phosphorylation.
Answer and explanation

D. TGF-beta receptor complexes use intrinsic kinases and signal through SMAD proteins. Their primary architecture differs from the other three classes.

15. Why can insulin and glucagon produce different cellular effects despite both being peptides?

  1. All peptide hormones activate the same receptor but have different molecular masses.
  2. Their receptors and downstream regulatory pathways differ.
  3. Only glucagon can bind any cell-surface receptor.
  4. Insulin passively crosses the bilayer and binds DNA.
Answer and explanation

B. Insulin is recognized by an RTK, glucagon by a GPCR. Chemical origin predicts access more readily than downstream output.

16. Which statement about MCT8 is correct in canonical thyroid signaling?

  1. It is the T3-binding nuclear transcription factor.
  2. It is an extracellular cGMP-producing receptor.
  3. It contributes to cellular iodothyronine transport; TR is the nuclear regulatory receptor.
  4. Its main function is to phosphorylate DNA-bound TR.
Answer and explanation

C. MCT8 facilitates access of thyroid hormone to cellular compartments. Its role is distinct from that of TR and from cyclase or kinase catalysis.

17. A receptor is present in a nuclear fraction without ligand. What can be concluded from that observation alone?

  1. It is necessarily activating all its target genes.
  2. It must be a thyroid receptor rather than any other protein.
  3. The ligand has already entered the nucleus.
  4. Nuclear presence is compatible with an inactive or active regulatory state.
Answer and explanation

D. Localization gives no direct measurement of ligand occupancy, molecular identity or transcriptional activity. Unliganded nuclear receptors can be regulatory proteins.

18. Which observation is most directly compatible with receptor subtype-dependent effects of vasopressin?

  1. Vasopressin becomes a steroid in renal cells.
  2. V1a and V2 receptors can couple to different proximal signaling systems.
  3. All vasopressin receptors are ion pores.
  4. V2 signaling requires vasopressin to bind DNA directly.
Answer and explanation

B. V1a commonly uses Gq-linked signaling, V2 uses Gs-linked signaling. Both are surface GPCRs, not steroid or ion channel receptors.

19. What distinguishes soluble guanylyl cyclase from classical nuclear receptors?

  1. It is an intracellular enzyme whose ligand-regulated direct output is cGMP.
  2. It necessarily has seven membrane-spanning helices.
  3. It directly recognizes a hormone response element through two zinc fingers.
  4. It is a receptor for ANP located at the plasma membrane.
Answer and explanation

A. Soluble cyclase illustrates that intracellular receptors need not be DNA-binding nuclear receptors. The last choice instead describes a particulate receptor cyclase.

20. What is the strongest general reason that response timing alone cannot identify receptor class?

  1. All receptors have identical rates of ligand binding.
  2. Only nuclear receptors use ATP anywhere in their pathway.
  3. Different receptor classes can regulate both existing proteins and later gene expression.
  4. All surface receptor signals are completed within milliseconds.
Answer and explanation

C. Time depends on the measured endpoint and intermediate steps. The other statements impose unjustified universal rates or energy requirements.

Part C analytical practice

All experimental and numerical scenarios below are hypothetical educational examples, not official previous-year questions.

21. A peptide-binding receptor mutant has normal surface abundance and Kd, but ligand no longer stimulates G alpha nucleotide exchange. Which lesion is best supported?

  1. Loss of the extracellular ligand-binding site
  2. Defective receptor–G protein coupling
  3. Complete loss of membrane insertion
  4. A defect exclusively in nuclear DNA binding
Answer and explanation

B. The first two measurements rule out the most direct evidence for binding and trafficking defects. G-protein activation is the missing proximal event, supporting impaired coupling; a downstream DNA-binding defect cannot explain loss of that proximal event.

22. In an artificial assay, cell A has 20,000 sites and cell B 5,000 sites. Both Kd values are 2 nM and free ligand is 6 nM. What are occupied sites A and B?

  1. 10,000 and 2,500
  2. 20,000 and 5,000
  3. 3,750 and 15,000
  4. 15,000 and 3,750
Answer and explanation

D. Fractional occupancy = 6/(6+2) = 0.75. Multiply by the site numbers. Half occupancy would require free ligand = 2 nM; full occupancy is not predicted at this finite concentration.

23. A receptor with Kd = 10 nM gives half-maximal measured output at free ligand = 1 nM. Which interpretation follows?

  1. EC50 is 1 nM; occupancy at that dose is about 9.1%, compatible with amplification.
  2. Kd must actually equal 1 nM.
  3. Exactly 50% of receptor sites are occupied.
  4. The binding equation proves the response assay is erroneous.
Answer and explanation

A. The functional half-maximal concentration is not the binding dissociation constant. Occupancy is 1/(1+10) = 0.0909. Signal coupling can make functional and binding curves differ.

24. T3 reporter activity is low in cells lacking a transporter. Direct intracellular T3 delivery restores the response. What is the most economical inference?

  1. The nuclear receptor has lost all DNA recognition.
  2. The reporter cannot respond to T3.
  3. The defect is consistent with limited hormone access upstream of an intact receptor-to-reporter pathway.
  4. Transporter necessarily functions as a nuclear transcription factor.
Answer and explanation

C. Bypassing entry restores the output, supporting an upstream access limitation. A defective reporter or absent receptor DNA function would not be rescued merely by providing ligand, and transporter identity is not established as a transcription factor.

25. Cell lines receive T4 and T3 separately. Line B takes up both hormones but responds only to T3. Which additional assay is most discriminating?

  1. Measure only total receptor transcript.
  2. Measure conversion of T4 to T3 and restore the relevant deiodinase if impaired.
  3. Block all RNA synthesis and compare late protein levels.
  4. Measure plasma membrane potential alone.
Answer and explanation

B. The pattern suggests a conversion problem with preserved T3 response. Direct testing of the conversion step is more discriminating than transcript abundance or unrelated/global interventions.

26. A receptor fragment purified away from other proteins converts GTP to cGMP. The intact protein is a peptide-binding single-pass receptor. Which class is best supported?

  1. Receptor guanylyl cyclase
  2. GPCR coupled to Gs
  3. JAK-associated cytokine receptor
  4. Nicotinic ion channel
Answer and explanation

A. Direct catalytic activity on GTP plus surface receptor topology identifies a receptor cyclase. G proteins use GTP as a switch; JAKs phosphorylate proteins and ionotropic receptors conduct ions.

27. A cell retains hormone binding but loses phosphorylation after removal of a separate cytoplasmic kinase. The receptor sequence lacks a catalytic kinase domain. Which model is most supported?

  1. An intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase with no catalytic domain
  2. An obligatory ligand-gated ion channel
  3. A receptor whose direct output is cGMP synthesis
  4. A kinase-associated receptor
Answer and explanation

D. Both the domain information and the kinase dependency support an associated kinase. The phosphorylation result alone would not establish this, but the additional sequence evidence is decisive in the stated comparison.

28. A TGF-beta receptor system has inactive type II kinase but functional type I kinase when tested separately. What is the predicted proximal defect?

  1. Increased direct phosphorylation of SMAD by ligand
  2. Inability of type I to bind ATP in every assay
  3. Reduced normal activation of type I receptor and thus reduced downstream SMAD phosphorylation
  4. Obligatory absence of all receptors at the surface
Answer and explanation

C. Type II normally activates type I in this representative system. The downstream defect does not imply type I is catalytically incapable in isolation or absent from the cell surface.

29. A nuclear receptor binds hormone normally and reaches the nucleus, but a DNA-binding cysteine mutation prevents reporter induction. Which follow-up best localizes the defect?

  1. Compare only extracellular hormone concentrations.
  2. Measure cyclic AMP production by a different receptor.
  3. Compare response-element association by normal and mutant receptor under matched expression.
  4. Measure total ATP without testing DNA recognition.
Answer and explanation

C. The mutation plausibly disrupts the zinc-coordinated DNA-binding architecture. Directly measuring element association tests that step; extracellular ligand and unrelated messenger assays do not.

30. TR/RXR occupancy at a positive regulatory element is unchanged after T3 treatment, but corepressor binding decreases and target RNA rises. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. TR must have entered the nucleus only after T3 binding.
  2. T3 changes regulatory interactions of an already DNA-associated receptor complex.
  3. The receptor did not respond because DNA occupancy was unchanged.
  4. The target RNA necessarily rose by direct ligand-dependent RNA stabilization alone.
Answer and explanation

B. Coregulator and RNA observations are compatible with a transcriptional change without increased receptor occupancy. Nuclear entry is not required by these observations; RNA stabilization alone is not demonstrated.

31. Cells contain a normal number of surface receptors and bind ligand. Phosphorylation is absent, but introducing an activated downstream effector restores one reporter. What follows?

  1. The complete normal receptor pathway has been repaired.
  2. The ligand must be defective chemically.
  3. The receptor must be a nuclear receptor.
  4. The downstream reporter machinery can operate; the original defect lies upstream of the bypassed step in this model.
Answer and explanation

D. A bypass restores an endpoint, not the full normal pathway. Preserved ligand binding argues against loss of recognition, while receptor class is not identifiable from this rescue alone.

32. Two cells express the same receptor, but one has half the surface binding capacity. Both normalized binding curves are identical. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Affinity may be unchanged even though accessible site number differs.
  2. Both cells necessarily have identical occupied receptor counts at every ligand concentration.
  3. The lower-capacity cell must have a higher Kd.
  4. The receptor in the lower-capacity cell must be intracellular.
Answer and explanation

A. Normalization conceals absolute binding capacity. Under maintained equal free ligand, the fractional occupancy can match while the occupied counts differ. Neither higher Kd nor intracellular topology follows.

33. In an unsaturated carrier model, Kc = 100 nM, total carrier = 900 nM and total hormone = 2 nM. What is the approximate free hormone concentration?

  1. 1.8 nM
  2. 2 nM
  3. 0.02 nM
  4. 0.2 nM
Answer and explanation

D. Free fraction is approximately 1/(1+900/100)=0.1. Free hormone = 2×0.1 = 0.2 nM; approximately 1.8 nM is bound. This uses dilute, unsaturated carrier assumptions.

34. An investigator finds a fluorescent peptide in endocytic vesicles. Which conclusion is justified without additional evidence?

  1. The peptide necessarily acts through a nuclear receptor.
  2. All peptide receptors are soluble proteins.
  3. Internalization has occurred, but access to free cytosol and intracellular receptors is not established.
  4. The fluorescent peptide must have crossed the vesicular membrane.
Answer and explanation

C. Vesicular lumen and cytosol remain separated by membrane. Imaging of intracellular puncta does not show cytosolic release or identify a nuclear receptor.

35. A receptor pore binds its ligand but produces no current at a single holding potential. Which control is most informative before concluding that gating has failed?

  1. Test current at other appropriate holding potentials and ionic gradients.
  2. Remove all extracellular ligand permanently.
  3. Measure transcription of an unrelated reporter.
  4. Replace the channel with a nuclear receptor.
Answer and explanation

A. An open channel can have zero net current at its reversal potential. Changing driving force while controlling ligand and channel expression distinguishes this possibility from gating/conduction failure.

36. A cell-surface peptide receptor produces phosphorylation in 20 seconds and new reporter protein in 3 hours. What is the appropriate inference?

  1. The peptide necessarily crosses into the nucleus before 20 seconds.
  2. A rapid surface-initiated event can be followed by delayed gene-regulatory output.
  3. The three-hour response proves a classical nuclear receptor.
  4. The two events must be independent because their times differ.
Answer and explanation

B. The delay is compatible with propagation to transcription and translation. Timing alone cannot establish nuclear ligand recognition or independence of the events.

37. A steroid–carrier conjugate induces a rapid response in a cell. Which control addresses the most important alternative to a surface receptor mechanism?

  1. Test for free steroid contamination or release and confirm conjugate accessibility.
  2. Assume conjugation always preserves affinity.
  3. Measure only total cellular protein.
  4. Infer surface localization solely from the response time.
Answer and explanation

A. Unmodified steroid may enter the cell and explain a positive response. Conjugation can also change ligand binding, so integrity and functional controls are essential.

38. Two different ligands raise cGMP. Loss of a membrane receptor eliminates response to ligand A only; removal of soluble cyclase eliminates response to ligand B only. Which pair best fits?

  1. Nitric oxide and ANP-like peptide, respectively
  2. Insulin and glucocorticoid, respectively
  3. Glucagon and a nicotinic receptor agonist, respectively
  4. ANP-like peptide and nitric oxide, respectively
Answer and explanation

D. A membrane guanylyl cyclase can respond to natriuretic peptides; NO activates soluble cyclase. Reversing the ligands conflicts with the specified dependencies. The other pairs do not identify these direct cGMP routes.

39. A cell engineered to express a known GPCR binds its hormone but does not produce the expected messenger. The effector enzyme responds normally to independent activation. Which next measurement is most useful?

  1. Assume the GPCR must itself be the effector enzyme.
  2. Infer that the ligand is membrane-permeant.
  3. Determine whether the appropriate G protein is present and activated by the receptor.
  4. Measure only unrelated nuclear receptor occupancy.
Answer and explanation

C. Ligand recognition and effector catalytic competence are preserved. Testing the intervening coupling component directly addresses a missing link; the alternatives do not localize that link.

40. At 3 nM free ligand, receptor X has Kd 1 nM and receptor Y has Kd 9 nM; each has 8,000 sites. Which is correct?

  1. X has 2,000 and Y 6,000 occupied sites.
  2. X has 6,000 occupied sites and Y 2,000; their output ratio cannot be deduced without coupling information.
  3. Each has 4,000 occupied sites because abundance is equal.
  4. X necessarily generates exactly three times the final biological response.
Answer and explanation

B. Occupancies are 3/(3+1)=0.75 and 3/(3+9)=0.25. Multiplication gives 6,000 and 2,000. The ratio of binding does not fix the ratio of nonlinear or distinct downstream outputs.

  3. Assertion true, reason false.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and explanation

A. JAK-associated cytokine receptors exemplify this causal relationship through STAT activation. The external ligand need not enter the nucleus to alter transcription.

43. Assertion–Reason — Assertion: A cell with normal hormone binding can have defective hormone signaling. Reason: The equilibrium dissociation constant alone measures all downstream catalytic and coupling functions.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. Assertion true, reason false.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and explanation

C. Recognition and downstream coupling are separable. Kd is an equilibrium binding parameter and cannot measure kinase activity, G-protein coupling or transcriptional competence.

44. Multiple statements — Select all correct statements: I. Insulin receptor beta subunits carry kinase domains. II. Growth hormone receptor has intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase activity. III. NPR-A forms cGMP from GTP. IV. Ligand-gated channels directly synthesize ATP.

  1. I and III only
  2. I, II and III only
  3. II and IV only
  4. III and IV only
Answer and explanation

A. I and III are correct. Growth hormone receptor requires associated JAK, and ion channel gating changes permeability without direct ATP synthesis.

45. Multiple statements — Select the correct statements: I. The ligand-binding and DNA-binding domains of a nuclear receptor are distinct. II. All nuclear receptors must enter the nucleus only after ligand binding. III. TR/RXR can occupy an element before ligand binding. IV. Coregulator recruitment can change without a large change in DNA occupancy.

  1. I and II only
  2. II and III only
  3. I, II, III and IV
  4. I, III and IV only
Answer and explanation

D. II is false: some nuclear receptor complexes are already DNA-associated. I, III and IV are consistent with nuclear receptor architecture and transcriptional regulation.

46. Matching — Match (P) insulin receptor, (Q) growth hormone receptor, (R) NPR-A, (S) nicotinic acetylcholine receptor with (1) associated kinase, (2) ion pore, (3) intrinsic tyrosine kinase, (4) guanylyl cyclase.

  1. P–1, Q–3, R–4, S–2
  2. P–3, Q–1, R–2, S–4
  3. P–3, Q–1, R–4, S–2
  4. P–4, Q–2, R–1, S–3
Answer and explanation

C. The insulin receptor phosphorylates tyrosines, growth hormone receptor couples to JAK, NPR-A produces cGMP and nicotinic receptors directly gate a pore. The alternative pairings swap these class-defining functions.

47. Matching — Match (P) T3, (Q) ANP, (R) glucagon, (S) TGF-beta with (1) GPCR, (2) nuclear receptor, (3) receptor guanylyl cyclase, (4) receptor serine/threonine kinase.

  1. P–2, Q–3, R–1, S–4
  2. P–3, Q–2, R–4, S–1
  3. P–2, Q–1, R–3, S–4
  4. P–4, Q–3, R–2, S–1
Answer and explanation

A. Canonical T3 action is mediated by nuclear TR, ANP by NPR-A, glucagon by a GPCR and TGF-beta by intrinsic type I/II receptor kinases.

48. Numerical — In an unsaturated carrier model, total carrier is 1,900 nM, Kc = 100 nM and total hormone is 2 nM. Assume free intracellular ligand equals free extracellular ligand and receptor Kd = 0.2 nM. What is receptor fractional occupancy?

  1. Approximately 0.05
  2. Approximately 0.333
  3. Approximately 0.50
  4. Approximately 0.90
Answer and explanation

B. Free fraction = 1/(1+1900/100)=0.05, giving free ligand = 0.1 nM. Occupancy = 0.1/(0.2+0.1)=1/3. The free fraction is not the receptor occupancy. This is an explicitly simplified hypothetical model.

49. Experimental interpretation — Hypothetical reporter values (relative units): normal receptor + hormone = 8, mutant receptor + hormone = 1, normal receptor without hormone = 1, mutant receptor + downstream bypass = 7. Both receptors bind ligand equally. What is most justified?

  1. The mutant is defective between ligand recognition and the bypassed step, but the exact molecular lesion is unresolved.
  2. The mutant lacks all ligand binding.
  3. The downstream reporter is incapable of responding.
  4. Bypass restored every normal receptor function.
Answer and explanation

A. Binding is preserved, while bypass almost restores the measured reporter. These observations locate an upstream functional defect but cannot distinguish trafficking, coupling or catalysis unless additional measurements are supplied.

50. Integrative reasoning — A candidate hormone receptor is a single-pass membrane protein. Ligand raises intracellular cGMP. A mutation in its cytoplasmic catalytic site abolishes cGMP synthesis by the isolated domain without altering surface binding. Which interpretation best integrates topology and biochemical evidence?

  1. A conventional GPCR directly converts GTP into cGMP.
  2. A nuclear hormone receptor regulates cGMP only by new transcription.
  3. An ion channel necessarily produces cGMP as its first product.
  4. A receptor guanylyl cyclase directly couples extracellular recognition to intracellular nucleotide cyclization.
Answer and explanation

D. Single-pass topology alone is nonspecific, but direct catalytic conversion and mutation evidence support receptor cyclase activity. G-protein switching, transcription and ion permeation are different molecular processes.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. All amine hormones bind the same receptor class. Amino acid origin sounds like a common physical category. Catecholamines and iodothyronines differ substantially in polarity, membrane access and receptor architecture. Use chemical properties and receptor evidence, not origin alone.
  2. Small ligands always diffuse freely through membranes. Small size reduces one barrier but does not eliminate the energetic cost of transferring charged groups into lipid. Epinephrine is small yet conventionally binds surface receptors.
  3. Thyroid hormone always enters cells by unrestricted passive diffusion. Its relatively lipophilic properties suggest membrane access, but transport proteins such as MCT8 regulate that access in important tissues. Separate uptake, metabolism and receptor recognition.
  4. Peptide hormones all signal through GPCRs. Many familiar peptides do, but insulin receptors are RTKs, growth hormone receptors use JAK, and ANP activates a receptor cyclase. Identify the first class-defining event.
  5. Insulin receptor activation requires initial dimerization of separate monomers. This is an appealing generalization from many RTK examples. The mature insulin receptor is already a disulfide-linked alpha2 beta2 complex and undergoes activation-related rearrangement.
  6. All hormone-induced tyrosine phosphorylation establishes an RTK. A separate JAK can phosphorylate receptor and substrate proteins. Determine whether catalytic activity is intrinsic to the receptor.
  7. cGMP production proves activation of a cell-surface receptor. ANP-sensitive receptor cyclases and NO-sensitive soluble cyclase both form cGMP. Changes in degradation also influence the messenger concentration.
  8. GTP binding and GTP conversion into cGMP are equivalent. G alpha uses GDP/GTP states as a regulatory switch. Guanylyl cyclase chemically converts GTP into cGMP and pyrophosphate.
  9. An open ion channel always gives a large inward current. An open channel provides a conductive pathway; current depends on driving force and selectivity. Near reversal potential the net current can be small or zero.
  10. Every steroid receptor starts exclusively in the cytoplasm. The glucocorticoid receptor provides a useful cytoplasmic model, but estrogen receptor populations are often nuclear before stimulation, and localization varies with system.
  11. Every nuclear receptor begins binding DNA only after hormone arrives. TR/RXR can already occupy an element and respond through altered coregulator association. DNA occupancy is not identical to transcriptional output.
  12. A slow response proves that the hormone binds an intracellular receptor. Surface pathways can regulate transcription and protein synthesis. Identify the immediate event and ligand recognition site, not just the final time point.
  13. Kd and EC50 are interchangeable. Kd describes equilibrium binding under a defined model; EC50 describes half-maximal functional output. Amplification, receptor abundance and coupling can separate them.
  14. Normal ligand binding proves a receptor is functional. Coupling, catalytic activity, localization to a productive compartment and downstream components may fail independently of binding.
  15. Downstream bypass establishes full pathway recovery. A restored endpoint establishes competence of the remaining machinery under the test conditions, not normal receptor regulation, timing or every physiological output.

Revision and active recall

Essential definitions

Ligand
A molecule that binds to a specified recognition site.
Hormone receptor
A protein linking hormone recognition to a biological signaling mechanism.
Receptor occupancy
Fraction of accessible binding sites occupied by ligand.
Intrinsic catalytic receptor
A receptor that contains its own catalytic domain.
Kinase-associated receptor
A receptor that depends on a distinct associated kinase for catalytic signaling.
Hormone response element
A DNA regulatory sequence contributing to receptor-dependent transcription.
Coregulator
A protein or complex that modifies transcriptional regulation by another regulatory protein.
Free hormone
Hormone not associated with carrier or other binding molecules in the specified pool.

Concept map and high-yield mechanism summary

Hormone chemistry → cellular access → receptor topology → earliest biochemical event → downstream effectors → measured response. At each arrow ask whether the evidence establishes necessity, sufficiency or only association.

Peptides and catecholamines typically bind surface-accessible receptors. Steroids commonly reach intracellular receptors; iodothyronines require attention to transport and local metabolism. Surface receptor output may be electrical, nucleotide-mediated or phosphorylation-mediated. Nuclear receptor output changes transcriptional regulatory interactions.

Recall before revealing answers

  1. Why is peptide uptake into a vesicle not proof of access to a nuclear receptor?
    Recall answer

    The vesicle lumen remains separated from cytosol; uptake does not establish cytosolic release.

  2. What distinguishes insulin receptor from growth hormone receptor?
    Recall answer

    Intrinsic tyrosine kinase on insulin receptor beta chains versus associated JAK on the kinase-free growth hormone receptor.

  3. What distinguishes NPR-A from soluble guanylyl cyclase?
    Recall answer

    NPR-A is a cell-surface peptide receptor; soluble cyclase responds to intracellularly accessible NO.

  4. How can T3 regulate a DNA-associated receptor without moving it into the nucleus?
    Recall answer

    Ligand binding can change TR/RXR coregulator recruitment and transcription.

  5. Why can two receptors with equal Kd give different responses?
    Recall answer

    Accessible receptor abundance and downstream coupling can differ.

  6. What control is needed before interpreting no channel current as failed gating?
    Recall answer

    Test an appropriate electrochemical driving force and verify channel abundance.

  7. What does restoring a reporter by bypass establish?
    Recall answer

    The downstream output machinery works under bypass conditions; the normal upstream route remains unresolved.

  8. How do catecholamines and thyroid hormones refute classification by origin alone?
    Recall answer

    Both derive from tyrosine but their typical receptor location and access mechanisms differ.

Formula sheet

One-site equilibrium: Kd = [R][L]/[RL]; Rtotal = [R] + [RL]; θ = [L]/(Kd + [L]); occupied sites = θ × total accessible sites. Use free ligand, compatible concentration units and the independent equivalent-site equilibrium model.

Dilute unsaturated carrier approximation: Kc = [C][L]/[CL]; free fraction ≈ 1/(1 + Ctotal/Kc); free ligand ≈ total ligand × free fraction. This assumes negligible carrier saturation, one carrier-site class and no other relevant binding pools.

Functional concentration: EC50 is the ligand concentration yielding half the maximal measured functional response in a specified assay, not automatically Kd.

Chemical distinction: GTP → cGMP + PPi (guanylyl cyclase); protein + ATP → phosphorylated protein + ADP (protein kinase, simplified); G alpha cycles between GDP- and GTP-bound regulatory states.
