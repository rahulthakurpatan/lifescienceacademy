From inheritance patterns to visible chromosomes

A pedigree allows a geneticist to infer the transmission of a trait without directly observing the hereditary material. Linkage analysis adds evidence about the relative positions of loci. Neither method necessarily reveals whether a chromosome is missing, whether a segment has moved to another chromosome, or whether a person has inherited an extra copy of a large genomic region. Cytogenetics addresses these questions by studying the number, organization and physical properties of chromosomes. The central intellectual task is to infer the genomic constitution of cells from observations at an appropriate spatial resolution.

Consider two cells whose DNA sequences differ. In the first, a single nucleotide substitution changes the coding sequence of an enzyme. In the second, an additional chromosome contributes hundreds of extra gene copies. Both changes can affect development or cellular function, but their scale differs enormously. A conventional microscope cannot resolve an individual nucleotide in a human chromosome. It may readily reveal a chromosome present in three copies. Conversely, sequencing one small gene cannot establish that every chromosome is structurally intact. A geneticist therefore chooses a method according to the size and kind of change that the evidence suggests.

Cytogenetics connects the observable structure of chromosomes with the principles of transmission genetics. A physical structure can be followed through cells, its identifying features compared with those of homologous chromosomes, and an unusual arrangement investigated by molecular methods. A cytogenetic observation can support a hypothesis about gene dosage or the redistribution of genetic material. It does not by itself specify the function of every affected gene. Distinguishing an observation from its biological interpretation is especially important when a chromosome abnormality is associated with a phenotype.

A karyotype describes the chromosome constitution, including number and identifiable structural properties. A karyogram is an ordered representation of the chromosomes of a particular preparation. An ideogram is a schematic representation, often showing characteristic bands and landmarks. In ordinary usage the word karyotype is also applied to the displayed chromosome image. In reasoning questions, distinguish the biological constitution from the picture and from a standardized verbal description. An error in ordering a karyogram is not equivalent to a biological rearrangement. A well-drawn ideogram is not an independent observation of an individual's cells.

A chromosome complement can be considered at several levels. Counting establishes whether an expected number of chromosomes is present. Recognizing individual chromosomes determines which chromosome has been gained or lost. Comparing patterns within chromosomes may reveal an altered structure. Locating a selected sequence can test a much more specific hypothesis. A measure of relative abundance of DNA across the genome can identify smaller regional gains or losses. These observations are complementary because a change can alter one property while leaving another unchanged.

This chapter treats chromosome findings as evidence in genetic problems. The examples describe hypothetical cells and simplified patterns rather than actual diagnostic cases. Clinical interpretation requires information beyond a diagram or isolated test result. The objective here is to understand why different observations permit different conclusions, and why a scientifically defensible answer often includes a statement about what has not yet been established.

The human diploid complement and the cell cycle

The usual chromosome complement of a diploid human somatic cell contains 46 nuclear chromosomes. Twenty-two types are autosomes, each ordinarily represented twice. Two additional chromosomes belong to the sex chromosome complement. Common constitutions are 46,XX and 46,XY. These descriptions state observed chromosome composition; they are not complete descriptions of sexual development or every feature of a person. Mitochondrial DNA is hereditary material but is not included in the conventional count of 46 nuclear chromosomes.

A haploid gamete ordinarily contains 23 nuclear chromosomes, consisting of one representative of each autosomal type and one sex chromosome. At fertilization, the two haploid complements combine. This numerical relationship explains how a diploid chromosome number can remain stable across generations. If an exam problem uses a gamete with 24 chromosomes and another with 23, the immediate predicted zygotic number is 47, provided the complements combine without additional changes. The chromosome identity of the extra element is still necessary to determine the particular trisomy.

The two members of an autosomal pair are homologues. They usually contain corresponding genes in corresponding regions, although their sequence variants may differ. The chromosomes contributed by the two gametes become members of homologous pairs in the resulting cell. The term homologous does not mean identical at every nucleotide. It means that the two chromosomes correspond in their major organization and evolutionary origin. A cytogenetic comparison relies on this relationship: homologues usually show corresponding band patterns, while changes in the order or length of these patterns may indicate a structural difference.

Homologous chromosomes must not be confused with sister chromatids. Following DNA replication, each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids held together by cohesion. Each chromatid contains a DNA double helix associated with proteins. Replication increases the DNA content of the cell without immediately increasing the number of independently segregating chromosomes. The familiar X-shaped image of a condensed replicated chromosome represents two sisters, not the two homologues of a pair.

For a diploid cell with a usual chromosome complement, the unreplicated G1 state contains 46 chromosomes and 46 nuclear chromosomal DNA molecules. At the end of S phase the cell has 46 replicated chromosomes and 92 chromatids. Its DNA content has doubled from 2C to 4C while its ploidy remains diploid. At mitotic anaphase, after sister separation, the dividing cell transiently contains 92 daughter chromosomes distributed toward two poles; each future daughter nucleus receives 46 in ordinary segregation. These statements concern nuclear chromosomes and exclude mitochondrial genomes.

The distinction between ploidy and DNA content prevents a common examination error. A metaphase human cell is not tetraploid merely because it contains four haploid genome equivalents of DNA. It is diploid with replicated chromosomes. Tetraploidy means four complete chromosome sets rather than two replicated sets. If a tetraploid cell is in G1, it also has 4C DNA relative to a normal haploid reference, but its independently segregating chromosome number is 92 rather than 46. Thus DNA amount alone cannot distinguish all cell-cycle and ploidy states.

The metaphase stage is useful for cytogenetics because chromosomes are relatively compact and can be individually distinguished when adequately spread. During interphase, DNA is organized in chromatin and chromosomes occupy nuclear territories, but their individual boundaries are not usually recognizable as separate stained rods. A chromosome has not disappeared when it becomes less condensed. The change is in physical organization and visual detectability, rather than absence of the underlying DNA.

Some human cells are unsuitable for routine metaphase counting. Mature circulating erythrocytes lack nuclei; they cannot provide a nuclear karyotype. Other differentiated cells may contain nuclei but may not be dividing at the time of observation. The genetic constitution inferred from a particular preparation is a statement about the cells sampled and successfully analyzed. This distinction becomes especially important when different cell lineages carry different chromosome complements.

Suppose a student sees 46 replicated chromosomes arranged in 23 pairs and counts 92 arms that extend downward from the centromeres. This observation cannot be translated into 92 chromosomes. Both sisters of every replicated chromosome share the relevant centromeric region before separation. The unit counted depends on the cell-cycle state and the conventional chromosome-counting rule. Identifying that unit before counting is more reliable than counting every visible line in a simplified drawing.

How the correct chromosome number was established

Early cytologists faced a measurement problem. Human chromosomes are numerous, relatively small, and difficult to distinguish when superimposed. Earlier investigators commonly accepted a diploid number of 48. A persistent count in the literature was not enough to guarantee accuracy because different investigators could share the same limitations of preparation and interpretation.

The work of Joe Hin Tjio and Albert Levan established the usual human diploid number as 46, reported in 1956. Their finding depended on improved preparations that permitted separate recognition of chromosomes. The important lesson is methodological: a biological quantity may appear well established until a technique permits more reliable observation. Improved chromosome spreading made it possible to count individual units without depending on ambiguous crowded images. The historical account reviewed by Harper describes both technical changes and the influence of prior expectations.

A scientist comparing rival hypotheses of 46 and 48 should not choose between them by averaging uncertain observations from poorly resolved preparations. Instead, the experiment should improve the quality of the evidence. The expected observation under the 46 model is a reproducible count of 46 in appropriately prepared diploid cells. Under a genuine 48 model, well-resolved preparations should reproducibly contain 48. Repeated observations of independent cells and independent confirmation reduce the possibility that the result is peculiar to one preparation.

An apparent discrepancy between two cells requires further reasoning. One spread may have chromosomes overlapping or lying outside the examined area. A different cell may genuinely possess an altered complement. These alternatives predict different patterns when more well-resolved cells are examined. Repeated detection of the same extra identifiable chromosome supports a biological explanation more strongly than irregular deficits of unrelated chromosomes in poor preparations.

The establishment of a reference chromosome number made comparative human cytogenetics possible. A numerical observation could now be evaluated against a reliable baseline. In 1959, the presence of an extra chromosome associated with Down syndrome was reported. This did not mean every characteristic of the condition had been explained by the microscope. It established a connection between a recognizable pattern of clinical features and a chromosome-level alteration, providing a basis for subsequent investigation of dosage and gene function.

Early unbanded preparations were better suited to detecting large numerical differences than to assigning every chromosome identity confidently. Chromosomes of similar lengths could be difficult to distinguish. Later banding techniques offered repeatable internal landmarks and permitted much more detailed comparisons. This historical progression illustrates a general experimental principle: measurement improves not only when objects become visible, but also when they acquire distinguishable features.

The scientific importance of the historical transition is therefore greater than memorizing the numbers 48 and 46 or a publication date. Cytogenetics became a method capable of distinguishing between competing explanations of inheritance. A visible alteration could be related to a genetic phenotype, and later molecular methods could test what segment or locus was involved. Modern methods still depend on the same logic of reproducibility, adequate controls and recognition of the limits of observation.

Chromosome morphology, centromeres and arms

A condensed chromosome is recognized partly by its length and the position of its centromere. The centromere is a specialized chromosomal domain involved in chromosome segregation. The kinetochore assembles on centromeric chromatin and provides an interface with spindle microtubules. The visible primary constriction often corresponds to this region. It is not necessary for a centromere to lie at the geometrical centre of a chromosome.

The centromere divides the chromosome into a short arm, designated p, and a long arm, designated q. The end of each arm contains a telomere. Telomeric structures contribute to the stability and protection of chromosome ends. The distinction between p and q gives a direction for describing locations along a chromosome. It is useful to imagine a map whose origin is the centromere and whose two directions run toward the ends of the arms.

A metacentric chromosome has approximately equal arms. A submetacentric chromosome has visibly unequal arms because its centromere is displaced from the midpoint. An acrocentric chromosome has a centromere located near one end and a relatively small short arm. A telocentric chromosome would have its centromere at or extremely near the terminal end, depending on the morphological terminology used. The usual human complement does not contain true telocentric chromosomes. These classes describe positions and relative arm lengths rather than specific chromosome numbers.

The human acrocentric autosomes are chromosomes 13, 14, 15, 21 and 22. Their short arms contain repetitive regions, including nucleolar organizer regions with ribosomal DNA arrays. Stalks and small distal satellites may be distinguished in appropriate preparations. A satellite in this morphological sense is not an independent chromosome; nor is the word identical in meaning to the broader molecular term satellite DNA. Recognizing the distinction prevents an apparent extra small chromosome from being inferred simply because a small region is visible beyond a narrow stalk.

One way to compare morphology quantitatively is to calculate a centromeric index, the short-arm length divided by total arm length, multiplied by 100. Let p and q denote measured arm lengths in the same units. The index is 100p/(p+q). If p is 3 micrometres and q is 7 micrometres in a hypothetical chromosome image, the index is 30 percent. The q/p arm ratio is 7/3, approximately 2.33. These values express the relative displacement of the centromere; they do not identify the chromosome on their own. Exact category boundaries can depend on the classification convention.

Now imagine two chromosomes of equal total length. The first has a centromeric index near 50 percent; the second has an index near 15 percent. The first is much more symmetric than the second. This evidence can help distinguish them, even if their overall sizes are identical. Conversely, two different chromosomes can have similar arm ratios, so one should also inspect band patterns. A length measurement may vary with chromatin condensation or image scaling. Ratios can reduce the effect of uniform scaling but cannot remove the effect of distorted or poorly resolved regions.

During routine comparison, one orients chromosomes consistently with the short arm toward the top. This is a representational convention rather than an intrinsic upward direction in the nucleus. The same chromosome rotated through 180 degrees has not undergone an inversion. A biological inversion changes the order of a segment relative to its surrounding chromosome. A rotated image changes only the orientation of the representation. The correct inference depends on relative landmarks, not the screen position of a chromosome.

A centromere location can appear different after a structural rearrangement, especially if the relative lengths of the arms have changed. But a difference in relative arm lengths does not prove an inversion. A deletion on one arm, an unequal exchange or a rearrangement involving another chromosome may produce a similar gross silhouette. The more specific the proposed mechanism, the more informative the evidence required to distinguish it from alternatives.

Karyogram construction and chromosome banding

A metaphase spread is a microscopic view of condensed chromosomes from one dividing cell. To construct a karyogram, the observer identifies individual chromosomes and orders them according to a conventional arrangement. Autosomes are placed by chromosome number, followed by the sex chromosome complement. Matching homologues requires more than sorting chromosomes from longest to shortest. Centromere position, arm lengths and internal band patterns jointly support identification.

Conventional G-banding produces reproducible alternating dark and light regions along appropriately prepared chromosomes. The banding pattern results from differential properties of chromatin and its interaction with staining after treatment. Dark G-bands tend to be relatively AT-rich and less gene-dense, and often replicate later than light G-bands; light bands tend toward the opposite pattern. These are broad associations, not absolute definitions. A dark band does not imply that every nucleotide in the region is an A or T, nor does it mean that the region contains no genes.

The value of banding is positional identification. A chromosome can be compared with its homologue and with a reference idiogram. If the expected pattern contains a dark-light-dark sequence and one copy instead lacks part of the light segment, an alteration may be suspected. A credible interpretation must consider whether the image quality permits a reliable comparison. Apparent missing bands can result from excessive condensation, overlapping chromosomes or weak contrast.

Chromosome addresses use the chromosome number, arm, region, band and, where appropriate, subdivisions. The numbering proceeds outward from the centromere along each arm. For example, 7q31.2 refers to chromosome 7, the long arm, region 3, band 1, sub-band 2. It is not a position of 31.2 million base pairs, nor a recombination distance. Cytogenetic addresses are hierarchical landmarks derived from visible patterns. Sequence coordinates are another coordinate system and require an appropriate reference assembly.

Less condensed chromosomes can exhibit finer band subdivision than highly condensed chromosomes. An approximately 400-band and an approximately 550-band preparation differ in the number of bands distinguishable in the haploid complement. The larger number means finer visual subdivision; it does not imply more DNA or more genes. The practical ability to detect a change depends on resolution, contrast, region and the nature of the alteration. A single fixed minimum detectable size for all preparations would therefore be misleading.

Other banding approaches emphasize different features. C-banding highlights constitutive heterochromatin, often near centromeres. R-banding gives a pattern broadly complementary to G-banding. Q-banding uses fluorescent staining and produces characteristic banding patterns with relationships to G-bands. Their exam relevance lies in distinguishing how chromosomes may be identified or particular chromatin regions emphasized, not memorizing specialized laboratory recipes.

A karyogram records chromosome structure at the visual level. Suppose an image contains 46 chromosomes, and each autosomal type appears twice. Two homologues of chromosome 4 differ in the order of recognizable bands. The chromosome count is typical, but structural identity is not established by number alone. An altered pattern supports a structural hypothesis. Additional characterization may be needed to determine whether material was lost, gained or reordered.

An ordered image also hides some information about sampling. Chromosomes in the picture usually come from one chosen spread. The figure may illustrate a predominant constitution, but it does not automatically summarize every cell that was examined. In mosaicism, a separate description of more than one observed line may be required. Reading a single karyogram as if it were a count of all tissues overstates the observation.

The ideal examination strategy is to identify the chromosome count, inspect the sex chromosome complement, compare homologous autosomes by recognizable landmarks, and then classify any evident numerical or structural difference. Finally, state which additional observations would be necessary if the pattern is ambiguous. This sequence separates the actual evidence from the inference and avoids treating a visually attractive diagram as complete genomic information.

Interpreting simplified human karyotype notation

Standardized chromosome nomenclature compresses information into a compact symbolic description. In a simple human karyotype, the first number is the total chromosome count, followed by a sex chromosome description and, when necessary, additional abnormalities. The notation 46,XX specifies a count of 46 with an XX complement and no further listed abnormality at the stated level of analysis. The notation 46,XY conveys the corresponding XY complement. Neither notation excludes variants that were below the resolution of the method used.

The notation 47,XY,+21 specifies 47 chromosomes, an XY complement and an extra chromosome 21. One ordinarily expects 44 autosomes and two sex chromosomes. Here the total includes 45 autosomes and two sex chromosomes. The plus sign is linked to chromosome identity, not a count of extra DNA molecules after replication. A typical example of free trisomy 21 contains three separately recognizable chromosome 21 homologues.

The notation 45,X represents a total of 45 chromosomes with a single X and no second sex chromosome listed. It is incorrect to write 45,XO as if O were a chromosome identity in formal notation. A zero may appear in informal teaching diagrams to indicate absence, but the notation describes the chromosomes actually observed. The total and the sex chromosome complement must be interpreted together.

In 47,XXY, the autosomal complement may be present in the usual count of 44 while the sex chromosome complement consists of two X chromosomes and a Y chromosome. In 47,XXX, the three sex chromosomes are X chromosomes. In 47,XYY, there is one X and two Y chromosomes. Equal total chromosome numbers do not imply identical genomic composition. A meaningful interpretation of 47 therefore requires the remaining symbols.

The notation 46,XX,del(5)(p15.2) is a simplified example of a terminal deletion involving chromosome 5 with a stated breakpoint at 5p15.2. The chromosome count may remain 46 because a chromosome that has lost a segment is still counted as one chromosome. A regional deletion changes the copy number of genes in that segment; it need not produce monosomy of the entire chromosome. The distinction between segmental and whole-chromosome loss is fundamental.

Similarly, a reciprocal translocation can preserve the number of recognizable chromosomes. A notation such as 46,XY,t(2;8)(q21;q24) describes a translocation involving two chromosomes and illustrative breakpoints on their long arms. The symbols specify chromosome identities and the observed rearrangement; they do not by themselves guarantee molecular balance, establish gene expression at the breakpoints or determine the phenotype.

A Robertsonian translocation is a useful counterexample to the idea that a count of 45 must imply monosomy of a full chromosome. Fusion of the long arms of two acrocentric chromosomes can reduce the number of separate chromosome elements by one while retaining most of their long-arm genetic material. A balanced carrier may therefore have 45 chromosomes. In a simplified example, 45,XX,rob(14;21)(q10;q10) is consistent with a Robertsonian rearrangement involving chromosomes 14 and 21. Counting the separate 21 chromosomes without considering material on the derivative chromosome would misrepresent dosage.

These examples establish a strategy for interpreting nomenclature. Read the total, then the sex chromosomes, then the type of change, the chromosomes involved and the location information. Ask whether the change alters chromosome number, DNA copy number, organization, or some combination. Detailed current reporting standards contain additional conventions beyond these teaching examples. The goal is to reason correctly about representative cytogenetic descriptions rather than reproduce a complete professional nomenclature manual.

Numerical abnormalities and the meaning of dosage

Numerical abnormalities involve a difference in the number of chromosomes or chromosome sets from an appropriate reference complement. Two terms are essential. Aneuploidy describes a deviation involving individual chromosomes rather than an entire additional or missing set. Polyploidy describes a complement with more than two complete sets. Trisomy 21 is an example of aneuploidy. A triploid complement containing 69 chromosomes is an example of polyploidy. The difference is not simply a matter of how many chromosomes have been added; it concerns whether a whole set is represented.

A normal diploid autosomal locus is generally represented by two genomic copies. If a whole autosome is present three times, most loci on that chromosome have three copies rather than two. The relative dosage at such a locus is 3/2, or 1.5 times the reference genomic dosage. A corresponding monosomy gives 1/2, or 0.5 times the reference dosage. These numerical ratios refer to DNA copy number, not necessarily to RNA abundance, protein abundance or phenotypic severity. Regulatory compensation, cellular feedback, tissue-specific expression and other mechanisms can change the relation between genomic dosage and gene product.

Why can a change affecting many genes be biologically important even if every DNA sequence on the extra chromosome is individually functional? Cells depend on the relative abundance of molecular components. Suppose two proteins assemble into a complex with one copy of each. Increasing the supply of one subunit without a corresponding increase in the other may not increase the number of complete complexes proportionally. Excess unassembled subunits may be degraded or interact differently. An imbalance can also affect a regulatory network if a transcription factor and its targets are not altered in the same proportion. The consequences depend on the particular genes, the cellular context and the degree of compensation.

In a simplified numerical problem, the usual number of human chromosomes is 2n = 46. A trisomic cell has 2n + 1 = 47, and a monosomic cell has 2n minus 1 = 45. Two distinct trisomies, each contributing one additional chromosome, would give 48 if no other changes occurred. Tetrasomy of a single chromosome could also give 48. Therefore the numerical count of 48 does not specify the distribution of the excess chromosomes. An observer needs chromosome identities to distinguish these hypotheses.

For an illustrative case, a karyogram displays three copies of chromosome 18, two copies of the other autosomes and an XX complement. Its notation is 47,XX,+18. A second image shows three copies of chromosome 13 with an XY complement, described as 47,XY,+13. The numerical class is the same in both, but the genomic regions whose dosage differs are different. Neither is equivalent to 47,XXY, in which the extra element is a sex chromosome.

A usual human autosomal monosomy involving an entire chromosome is generally much more disruptive than the loss of some small regions. It removes one copy of numerous genes. The observation that certain chromosome constitutions occur among live-born individuals does not mean that every conceivable chromosome imbalance is equally compatible with development. Viability and ascertainment affect the spectrum of findings that scientists observe. An absent class in a sample can reflect developmental selection rather than an inability of segregation errors to produce that class.

Sex chromosome dosage provides an important exception to simple copy-number intuition. In cells with more than one X chromosome, X-chromosome inactivation can reduce expression from additional X copies for many genes. Inactivation is incomplete, and some genes escape silencing. The X and Y chromosomes also contain regions of shared homology. Consequently, a change in X chromosome number cannot be treated as a uniformly proportional change in every X-linked product. This explains why counting copies and predicting expression are related but different problems.

A chromosome complement of 69,XXY represents triploidy, not three independent trisomies. Most autosomal loci would ordinarily have three copies, but the relationship among whole chromosome sets differs from a single autosomal trisomy. An important analytical consequence is that a test based purely on relative abundance among autosomal regions may not recognize a uniform change affecting the entire genome if the data are normalized to that genome. An observation of equal relative dosage across chromosomes is not always equivalent to proof that two absolute copies are present.

Nondisjunction is the failure of chromosome partners or sisters to segregate appropriately during division. It can result in daughter cells or gametes with unequal complements. A simple example is a gamete receiving both homologues of a chromosome that would ordinarily contribute only one. After fusion with a gamete carrying the usual single copy, the zygote has three copies. This accounts for one possible origin of a free trisomy. Detailed distinctions between meiotic stages and the complete analysis of segregation products belong to Subunit 8.F.

A cytogenetic observation of trisomy alone usually does not establish when the segregation error occurred. A chromosome gain could have arisen in the formation of a gamete or during a mitotic division after fertilization. Exam problems that ask for the precise stage of origin need additional evidence, such as informative allele patterns or the distribution of chromosome complements among cells. It is important not to assign a causal history just because the final state is recognizable.

Representative human chromosome syndromes are useful because they demonstrate that identity and dosage matter. Free trisomy 21 is associated with Down syndrome, trisomy 18 with Edwards syndrome and trisomy 13 with Patau syndrome. An X monosomy is a common chromosome finding in Turner syndrome, and 47,XXY is associated with Klinefelter syndrome. These examples are not a complete description of each condition, which can involve additional forms of chromosome alteration and considerable biological variation. The current chapter emphasizes the cytogenetic findings rather than clinical prediction.

Structural abnormalities: count, order and genetic balance

Structural abnormalities change the arrangement or amount of material within chromosomes. A chromosome can retain a centromere and remain recognizable as one element even after a large segment is lost or moved. This is why the total chromosome count can be 46 in a cell that carries a significant structural abnormality. The observer must examine chromosome identity and internal organization, not merely the total.

A deletion removes a segment from a chromosome. If the corresponding region of the homologous chromosome remains intact, the cell may have only one copy of the deleted region rather than two. A terminal deletion removes material extending toward an original chromosome end; an interstitial deletion removes an internal segment. The distinction concerns the location of the missing material. In a simplified band pattern A–B–C–D–E, removal of D–E produces a terminal loss, while removal of C produces an interstitial loss. The sequence of letters represents consecutive genomic intervals, not individual genes.

A duplication creates an additional copy of a region. If one chromosome retains an ordinary copy of a region and its homologue carries two, the diploid cell has three copies of that region. The rest of the chromosome can remain present in the usual two copies. A duplication may be adjacent to the original copy or located elsewhere. Relative DNA abundance can reveal an extra copy, but abundance alone does not establish the location or orientation of that additional copy.

An inversion changes the orientation of a chromosomal segment. If a chromosome segment B–C–D is reversed within a schematic arrangement A–B–C–D–E, the resulting order is A–D–C–B–E. If the rearrangement does not gain or lose material, the dosage of those intervals can remain unchanged. A paracentric inversion does not include the centromere; a pericentric inversion does. The detailed effects on homologous pairing and crossover products are studied later. Here, the essential point is that the order of landmarks, not merely their total abundance, identifies an inversion.

A reciprocal translocation involves an exchange of material between two chromosomes. Imagine chromosome A and chromosome B each containing a proximal region and a distal region. If their distal regions are exchanged without a detectable loss or gain, the two derivative chromosomes may together retain the same set of intervals. The physical positions of these intervals have changed. Their genomic copy numbers can remain approximately unchanged. A copy-number assay is therefore not expected to reveal the exchange if the balance is genuine at its detection scale.

The word balanced must be used with care. A rearrangement can appear balanced at the level of visible chromosome bands while involving small losses or gains below the available resolution. Even a rearrangement with no net change in DNA amount can interrupt a gene at a breakpoint, separate a gene from regulatory elements or place a gene in a different chromosomal environment. Balance is a statement about the retention of material, not a guarantee that all genomic functions are preserved. Apparent balance should be distinguished from independently confirmed molecular balance.

An unbalanced rearrangement changes the amount of material in one or more intervals. It may combine a deletion of part of one chromosome with duplication of part of another. If a derivative chromosome is inherited without its complementary derivative, a gamete or zygote may have unequal dosage despite carrying a typical number of separate chromosome elements. This explains why a chromosome count of 46 cannot exclude segmental aneuploidy.

Robertsonian translocations concern the acrocentric chromosomes. Fusion involving two long arms can form a derivative chromosome that replaces two separate elements. In an illustrative balanced 14;21 carrier, one ordinary chromosome 14, one ordinary chromosome 21 and one derivative bearing long-arm material from both are present. There are two principal long-arm copies of chromosome 14 and two of chromosome 21. The count is reduced to 45 because the derivative is a single chromosome rather than two independent elements. Repetitive short-arm material can be lost in this type of rearrangement, so balance refers to the major functional genomic material rather than strict equality of every base.

Contrast this with a hypothetical cell carrying the same derivative along with an additional ordinary chromosome 21. The number of chromosome elements is now 46, but chromosome 21 long-arm material occurs three times: twice on ordinary chromosomes 21 and once on the derivative. This is a form of translocation trisomy 21. The visible chromosome count differs from free trisomy 21, although both produce increased dosage of chromosome 21 long-arm material. A number-only classification would miss the shared dosage mechanism.

These examples show the difference between the count of structures and the count of particular sequences. For every rearrangement problem, write down the material represented by each chromosome. Sum the copies of the relevant interval across ordinary and derivative chromosomes. A derivative name is not an instruction to ignore the material it carries. Conversely, a chromosome name does not guarantee that all of its original material remains on that physical element.

A ring chromosome may arise when chromosome ends are lost or otherwise rearranged and the remaining material forms a circular structure. It can be associated with loss of terminal material and may show instability during division, although the details depend on the particular ring. An isochromosome has two copies of one arm instead of the usual distinct short and long arms, producing an imbalance of arm content. Both examples reinforce the idea that the shape and composition of a chromosome, rather than the count alone, are informative. They are included here as recognition examples; their mechanistic complexity is not needed to interpret basic karyotype questions.

The strongest conclusion from a structural observation often combines description and uncertainty. For instance, one may observe a shortened chromosome with an altered band pattern, infer that a deletion is plausible, and propose a copy-number test to determine which interval is missing. If the alternative hypothesis is an exchanged segment of similar length, locus-specific positional evidence may be needed. Such reasoning is preferable to naming a rearrangement solely from one feature of a schematic image.

Mosaicism, sampling and the interpretation of cell counts

A multicellular individual develops from a founding cell whose descendants form many lineages. If a genetic change occurs in one descendant rather than in the founding cell, only some of the later cells may inherit it. Mosaicism refers to the presence of two or more genetically distinct cell populations derived from a single zygote. In cytogenetics the distinguishing feature may be a chromosome count or structural alteration. Mosaicism is not synonymous with the presence of two different alleles in a diploid cell. A heterozygous genotype can be shared by every cell, whereas mosaicism involves differences between cells.

Consider a zygote with the usual chromosome complement. If one mitotic division produces a descendant with an extra chromosome and that lineage persists, a tissue may eventually contain cells with both the ordinary and altered chromosome constitution. Alternatively, an early embryo with a trisomic complement can generate a lineage in which the extra chromosome has been lost. Both routes can create the observation of a mixture of diploid and trisomic cells. The mixture alone does not necessarily identify which route occurred.

A chimera contains genetically distinct cell populations derived from more than one zygote. Mosaicism and chimerism can produce superficially similar mixed genetic observations, but their developmental origins differ. Distinguishing them requires evidence about the genetic relationships of the cell populations. A chromosome count from a mixed sample does not automatically reveal how many founding zygotes contributed to the organism.

Simplified chromosome nomenclature can indicate more than one observed cell line. A teaching example is mos 47,XX,+21[8]/46,XX[22], which specifies eight scored cells of one constitution and twenty-two of the other. The brackets give observed counts. They do not state the exact percentage of abnormal cells in every organ. A tissue sample containing 30 scored cells is a finite observation of a biological system with potentially different lineage proportions at different sites.

The observed fraction in that example is 8/30 = 0.2667, or approximately 26.7 percent. If the cells were sampled independently from a homogeneous population and each constitution was recognized without error, the fraction would estimate the underlying proportion in that population. The word estimate matters because another sample of thirty cells could yield a different number. Sampling variability is not necessarily a sign that the initial test was incorrect.

A useful simplified probability model treats the detection of an altered cell as a Bernoulli event. If p is the true altered-cell fraction in a representative sample and N independently selected cells are examined, the probability of seeing no altered cells is (1 − p) raised to the power N. This follows because each cell has probability 1 − p of belonging to the other population and independence permits the probabilities to be multiplied. The probability of detecting at least one altered cell is therefore 1 − (1 − p) to the power N.

Suppose p = 0.10 and N = 20. The probability of observing no altered cells is 0.9 to the power 20, approximately 0.1216. Hence the probability of seeing at least one is about 0.8784, or 87.8 percent. The model shows why failing to observe an altered cell in a small sample does not demonstrate that the alteration is absent. The chance of a missed population depends jointly on its fraction and the number of observations.

For a second example, consider p = 0.05. With 20 cells, the probability of no altered cells is 0.95 to the power 20, approximately 0.3585. With 60 cells it is 0.95 to the power 60, approximately 0.0461. Increasing the number of independent observations makes the low-frequency population more likely to appear. These values are illustrative calculations, not validated detection limits of any particular clinical cytogenetic method.

One can rearrange the same expression to ask how many cells would be needed for a target probability of detecting at least one. For a 95 percent target, require (1 − p) to the power N to be at most 0.05. Taking natural logarithms gives N at least ln(0.05)/ln(1 − p), with rounding upward to a whole cell. For p = 0.05 the quotient is about 58.4, so at least 59 independent observations are required under this idealized model. The direction of the inequality reverses when dividing by the negative logarithm of 1 − p.

This model does not capture every real sampling problem. Cells analyzed after growth in culture may represent lineages with different growth or survival properties. A single cell can produce multiple descendants in culture, violating the assumption of independent observations. The tissue originally sampled may differ from other tissues. Technical artifacts may also produce unusual chromosome counts. More sophisticated inference therefore depends on the actual design, controls and scoring rules. The binomial model is a useful starting point for examination reasoning, not a substitute for validated laboratory interpretation.

A particularly informative observation is the recurrence of the same structurally distinctive abnormality in several independently evaluated cells. Random loss of chromosomes during preparation can create apparent hypodiploidy, but it need not repeatedly remove the same chromosome. Evidence across well-resolved cells helps distinguish a coherent abnormal lineage from preparation artifacts. The reverse caution is equally important: a low-frequency line may be genuine even if it is seen only rarely, and should not be dismissed solely because the predominant complement looks usual.

A mixed population can also influence a bulk DNA measurement. If a fraction f of cells has three copies of a locus and the remainder has two, the idealized mean copy number is 3f + 2(1 − f) = 2 + f. At f = 0.20 the mean is 2.20, only a 10 percent increase relative to the diploid reference, although the altered cells individually have a 50 percent increase. The averaged measurement therefore contains a weaker dosage signal than a pure trisomic population. Detectability depends on signal noise, assay design and normalization, not just on the fact that three copies are present in some cells.

The comparison between single-cell observations and bulk measurements explains why methods may produce different-looking results without being logically inconsistent. A metaphase analysis can identify distinct cell lines if they are represented among scored cells. Interphase FISH can count selected targets across individual nuclei. A microarray typically measures an aggregate signal from DNA extracted from a population. Different methods observe different properties of a mixed sample, and interpretation must preserve that distinction.

From a biological sample to a chromosome observation

A chromosome analysis starts with a population of cells, not with a photograph of isolated DNA. Conventional chromosome banding depends on the ability to recognize individual condensed chromosomes. During ordinary interphase the DNA occupies chromatin territories, and the boundaries of individual chromosomes are not generally resolved by routine light microscopy. During metaphase the chromosomes are sufficiently condensed to produce distinguishable structures. A method that increases the number of suitable dividing cells therefore increases the opportunity to examine complete chromosome complements.

Peripheral blood lymphocytes are one familiar source of cells for constitutional chromosome analysis. Many circulating lymphocytes are not actively dividing. Stimulation under suitable culture conditions can induce some of them to enter the cell cycle. Other cell sources can have different requirements. The choice of tissue matters because a constitutional finding and a tissue-restricted acquired finding are different biological questions. It also matters whether the cell population remains representative after culture. The preparation is designed to reveal chromosomes while preserving enough of their structure for identification.

An agent that disrupts spindle function can increase the fraction of cells observed in a metaphase-like arrested state. A common examination example is colcemid or colchicine, which interferes with microtubule dynamics. The important mechanistic distinction is that arrest does not ordinarily create a new complete diploid complement for the investigator to count. It makes the already replicated chromosomes accessible at an appropriate stage. If a cell at metaphase possesses 46 chromosomes, each normally consists of two sister chromatids. The two chromatids together are counted as one chromosome until the sisters separate. Counting each visible chromatid as an independent chromosome would incorrectly report 92 for the metaphase.

A hypotonic treatment can cause cells to swell and help separate chromosomes spatially. Fixation helps preserve the material during preparation. When cells are placed onto a microscope slide and the chromosomes spread apart, an investigator can seek fields in which the chromosomes are sufficiently separated and complete. The final image is a product of the biological starting material and the preparation process. Overlap can obscure one chromosome behind another. Excessive scattering can place an element outside the region assumed to belong to a single cell. Chromosomal breaks can complicate the distinction between an intact chromosome and a fragment.

These limitations make the selection of suitable metaphases a critical step rather than a cosmetic one. A clear banding pattern on an incomplete spread cannot establish the chromosome number. A complete spread with poorly resolved bands may support a count but not a fine structural interpretation. Investigators seek observations that are appropriate to the question. In a problem about a suspected additional small chromosome, the completeness and separation of the spread are especially important. In a problem about a possible inversion, the order and orientation of identifiable bands are more important than the visual attractiveness of the entire image.

After staining and imaging, chromosomes can be identified using features such as relative length, centromere position and characteristic banding patterns. The chromosomes are then arranged into a karyogram. This arrangement is not the position in which they existed inside the living nucleus. The positions are deliberately reorganized to facilitate comparison of homologues. A learner should distinguish the biological chromosome complement, the metaphase spread and the arranged karyogram. The karyogram is an informative representation of the complement, rather than a direct map of spatial nuclear organization.

Several cells are generally examined to establish whether a finding recurs and whether additional cell populations are evident. The required number is not a single universal value independent of the question. The method, sample type, quality of preparations, suspected mosaicism and relevant laboratory standards all affect the appropriate analysis. For an exam calculation that explicitly assumes random sampling, the binomial model provides a useful idealized estimate. In a real biological interpretation, the limitations of culture and sample selection must remain explicit.

A useful quality-control exercise is to examine the difference between reproducibility and representativeness. Suppose ten metaphases from one cultured colony all exhibit the same chromosome change. That result may be technically reproducible but less informative about the diversity of the starting population than ten independent metaphases from distinct lineages. Conversely, a study of many cells from an irrelevant tissue may be technically excellent while failing to answer the biological question. Both the quality of individual observations and the relation of the observed cells to the population of interest matter.

At the interpretation stage, the investigator records the chromosome complement according to cytogenetic nomenclature. A notation summarizes an observation at the resolution and scope of the analysis. It should not be treated as a complete inventory of every DNA sequence or epigenetic state. The statement that no visible abnormality was detected by an adequately performed chromosome analysis is a statement about the method and its observations, not proof that the entire genome is unaltered.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization: testing a positional hypothesis

Fluorescence in situ hybridization, abbreviated FISH, connects cytogenetic structure to sequence specificity. A probe consists of a nucleic acid sequence capable of hybridizing to complementary target DNA. The probe can be visualized by a fluorescent label or through a detection system associated with that label. The chromosome or nuclear preparation provides a spatial context. Unlike a conventional band, a probe signal is intended to identify a selected DNA region rather than the general staining behavior of a broad chromosomal segment.

Hybridization depends on complementary base pairing. The target and probe must be accessible in a form that allows complementary sequences to associate. Conditions are chosen to favor sufficiently specific binding, and unbound or weakly bound probe material is removed or distinguished from useful signal. A fluorescence microscope then reveals the positions of the signals relative to the chromosomal or nuclear structures. A probe signal is not a literal picture of an individual DNA base. It is an optical indication of hybridization to an interval of DNA or repeated sequence represented by the probe.

There are two broad contexts in which cytogenetic FISH is often considered. In metaphase FISH, signals are assigned to condensed chromosomes whose identities and positions can be examined. In interphase FISH, selected signals are scored within nuclei even though individual chromosome bodies are not readily distinguished. Metaphase analysis is particularly helpful when the physical placement of a target is the question. Interphase analysis can permit scoring of many nuclei without requiring every observed cell to be at metaphase. The forms are complementary rather than identical.

A locus-specific probe is chosen to investigate a particular region. Consider a probe targeting a locus on an autosome that normally occurs twice in a diploid G1 nucleus. A simple teaching diagram would show two signals in the usual state, one signal when one copy of the target is deleted, and three signals when the target is present in three copies. These are idealized expectations. Actual nuclei may show replicated targets, partially overlapping signals, split signals, nuclear truncation in certain preparations or technical signal failure. The expected scoring rules depend on the probe and specimen. A raw signal count is not automatically identical to a chromosome count.

A centromere-associated enumeration probe targets sequences associated with a selected centromeric region. It can help assess the number of copies of that region. A useful distinction is that three centromere probe signals for chromosome 18 and three locus-specific signals for a gene on chromosome 18 address related but different questions. In a straightforward free trisomy of chromosome 18, both may be expected to increase. In a duplication of a region that contains the locus but not the centromere, only the locus-specific probe may show additional copies. The interpretation depends on the actual rearrangement.

A whole chromosome painting probe uses a collection of sequences associated with one chromosome to distinguish its material from other chromosome material. In an idealized reciprocal translocation, a painted region may appear on a derivative chromosome that otherwise contains material from another chromosome. Such an observation addresses the origin and placement of chromosomal material. It is not a sensitive method for identifying every small intrachromosomal inversion or deletion. The visual resolution and the genomic coverage of the probes remain limiting factors.

Consider the case of a suspected balanced reciprocal translocation involving chromosomes 2 and 8. Two probes, one specific to a region on chromosome 2 and the other specific to a region on chromosome 8, may be selected to investigate whether regions have changed physical locations. In metaphase preparations, the positions of the signals relative to the recognizable derivative chromosomes can help distinguish an exchange from an ordinary pair of homologues. If both selected sequences remain present twice but occupy altered positions, the observation is compatible with an exchange without loss of those selected targets. It does not establish that every interval throughout both chromosomes is retained.

A dual-color break-apart probe provides a different kind of positional evidence. Imagine two differently labeled probes placed on opposite sides of a genomic region of interest. In an unrearranged configuration, the signals are expected to appear near one another. If a rearrangement separates the flanking sequences sufficiently, the signals may be resolved apart. In a simplified heterozygous example, one allele retains the adjacent signal pattern and the other shows separated colors. The observation supports a rearrangement affecting the region between or near the probe targets. It does not necessarily identify the partner chromosome or the exact nucleotide breakpoint.

A fusion probe strategy can investigate whether two selected genomic regions have become adjacent. Under a suitable design, the two regions have differently colored labels and a particular juxtaposition supports a rearrangement hypothesis. The phrase fusion signal refers to an optical appearance consistent with close proximity of the labeled targets. It should not be confused with direct proof of a functional fusion transcript. Establishing the expression and precise sequence of a fusion may require additional molecular evidence. The distinction is especially useful in problems that ask whether a structural result alone demonstrates production of an altered protein.

Interpretation also requires appropriate positive and negative controls. A negative target signal could indicate absence of the target, but it could also result from insufficient hybridization, an inaccessible target, or a failed preparation. A reference probe at an unaffected region can help distinguish a target-specific absence from a broader technical problem. For example, if a nucleus shows no signal for either the target or a suitable reference, concluding that both chromosome regions are deleted would be unjustified without further evidence. If the reference shows the expected pattern and a target repeatedly shows one instead of two signals in suitable cells, a region-specific loss becomes a stronger hypothesis.

A probe may fail to bind or produce the expected signal even when the broader chromosome is present. The probe interrogates a region rather than the entirety of the chromosome. Conversely, a signal may be detected from a sequence carried on an unexpected chromosome. This is precisely why metaphase positional evidence can be informative. A question that asks whether a signal represents a particular chromosome should specify how the chromosome itself is recognized and what genomic target the probe recognizes.

FISH is often described as a targeted test. This does not mean that it is useful only when an investigator already knows the exact molecular change. A hypothesis about a chromosome or interval may be sufficient to choose an informative probe. However, a test limited to several selected regions cannot exclude arbitrary changes elsewhere in the genome. If a probe for chromosome 21 gives the expected two signals, the observation says little about an untested region of chromosome 7. A correct explanation should always relate the negative finding to the genomic scope of the assay.

For examination reasoning, the most effective approach is to draw the proposed chromosome arrangement, mark the sequences recognized by each probe and predict signal number and location. Then compare the predicted pattern with the observations, taking into account whether the preparation is metaphase or interphase. This method prevents the common mistake of interpreting every abnormal signal as a whole-chromosome gain or loss. It also makes it easier to distinguish a question about dosage from one about physical organization.

Chromosomal microarrays: relative DNA dosage across the genome

Chromosomal microarrays examine a different property of the genome. Rather than observing entire chromosome shapes or counting fluorescent spots within separate cells, they measure the behavior of DNA at many selected genomic sites. One major purpose is to detect copy-number alterations, meaning genomic intervals present in more or fewer copies than the reference state. The spatial order of those sites in the data is established by their known genomic coordinates, not by seeing each chromosome as an intact physical structure.

In array comparative genomic hybridization, commonly called array CGH, sample DNA and reference DNA are compared across many targets. A simplified description is that the relative hybridization signals reflect the relative amount of complementary material in the two DNA samples. Different experimental designs and normalization procedures are used in practice. The conceptual output for a teaching problem can be represented as a ratio of sample to reference signal for a genomic interval. A sustained deviation across neighboring targets is more persuasive evidence of a regional copy-number alteration than an isolated noisy value.

The base-two logarithm of a ratio is useful because a ratio of one becomes zero, gains become positive and losses become negative. If a pure population has three copies of an autosomal interval and a diploid reference has two, the ideal ratio is 3/2. The ideal log2 ratio is approximately +0.585. A hemizygous deletion relative to a two-copy autosomal reference gives a ratio of 1/2 and an ideal log2 ratio of minus one. These values assume a simple relationship between copy number and signal and an appropriate normalization. Observed values in real experiments may deviate for technical or biological reasons.

Suppose a hypothetical array plot contains many consecutive targets spanning a region of chromosome 5. The log2 ratios remain near minus one across that region, while adjacent regions have ratios near zero. This pattern is consistent with a one-copy loss in a pure diploid cell population. If the region is small relative to the practical resolution of a metaphase banded preparation, the karyotype may show no obvious alteration. The two results are not contradictory. The array is sensitive to a change in relative DNA abundance at the assayed positions, whereas conventional chromosome analysis depends on visual recognition of structural differences.

In a mixed population the ratio is smaller in magnitude than that expected for a pure altered population. If a fraction f of cells carries a one-copy deletion and the rest carry two copies, the average dosage is f + 2(1 − f), which equals 2 − f. With f = 0.4, the mean copy number is 1.6. Relative to two copies, the ratio is 0.8, and the ideal log2 ratio is approximately minus 0.322. A learner can compare this value with minus one for a pure deletion. The diluted signal illustrates how mosaicism can reduce detection power.

Similarly, if forty percent of cells carry a third copy and sixty percent retain two copies, the mean copy number is 2.4, the ratio is 1.2 and the ideal log2 ratio is approximately +0.263. The values for a gain and loss in equally large fractions are not symmetric on the log scale because the logarithm acts on different ratios. This is a mathematical property of the transformation rather than evidence that a gain is necessarily less biologically important. Data interpretation should retain the distinction between signal magnitude and functional consequences.

The size of a detectable interval depends on factors including the density and distribution of probes, the signal quality and the analysis criteria. A region densely represented by informative probes may be resolved more effectively than a sparsely represented region of the same physical length. There is therefore no universal base-pair limit that applies to all chromosome microarrays. A small alteration outside adequately interrogated regions may not be detected. The resolution of a reported result should be related to the actual analytical platform.

An array can identify an increase in copy number without necessarily determining whether the additional sequence is located immediately beside the original or inserted elsewhere in the genome. For example, a gain of a chromosome 12 interval could be consistent with a tandem duplication on chromosome 12 or the insertion of additional material into another chromosome. The array profile could be similar because the amount of sequence is similar. A positional method such as metaphase FISH or a suitable structural sequencing method could help distinguish the arrangements.

Likewise, a genuinely balanced reciprocal translocation may leave the abundance of the assayed DNA unchanged. The probe targets from both chromosomes remain represented in the expected copy number, even though their physical positions differ. An array measuring dosage alone would not be expected to identify that exchange. A banded karyotype can be more informative in this case if the rearrangement is large enough to alter recognizable band patterns. This comparison is central to choosing a test according to the genetic question rather than assuming that the most molecular method is always superior.

Single nucleotide polymorphism arrays add information about allelic patterns at selected polymorphic sites as well as copy number. At an ordinary diploid locus, possible genotype classes include AA, AB and BB. In idealized allelic signal data, these correspond to B-allele fractions near zero, one-half and one. A region with three copies may produce additional patterns such as AAB or ABB, with ideal fractions near one-third and two-thirds. These patterns can provide information that is not available from dosage-only measurements.

Long contiguous regions of homozygosity in SNP array data can occur without a change in total copy number. Such a result can be consistent with inheritance of highly similar chromosomal segments or, in an appropriate context, some forms of uniparental disomy. The result alone does not always identify the mechanism or parental origin. Parental genotypes and additional evidence can be important. A normal diploid copy-number profile does not establish that both parental homologues have contributed in the ordinary way.

This comparison also explains why triploidy is an important exam exception. A dosage-only method based on relative normalization can have difficulty identifying a uniformly increased genomic complement because all chromosome regions change together. SNP allelic patterns may reveal the presence of three copies through genotype clusters not expected in ordinary diploidy. A conventional karyotype, if suitable metaphases are available, can directly establish a chromosome count of 69. Whether a specific assay recognizes triploidy depends on its design and analysis, not merely on whether it is called a microarray.

A chromosome microarray generally reports evidence of DNA dosage or allelic pattern rather than directly specifying the clinical effect of an alteration. Some copy-number differences are tolerated; others affect dosage-sensitive genes or regulatory sequences. A result requires attention to interval boundaries, gene content, evidence of inheritance and other biological information. For this chapter, the key skill is to interpret the molecular signal correctly before drawing functional conclusions. A visually abnormal ratio should not automatically be translated into a particular disease or developmental outcome.
