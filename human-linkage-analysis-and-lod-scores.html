Why human families provide evidence of genetic linkage

Imagine a family in which an inherited trait occurs in several generations. A genetic marker can be detected at a known chromosomal position, and one of its alleles seems to be inherited by many of the relatives who express the trait. This observation raises a question different from the one asked in a simple pedigree analysis. The pedigree may suggest that a single dominant allele influences the trait, but can it also tell us where that allele is located? If the trait locus and the marker locus occupy nearby positions on the same chromosome, they may be transmitted together more often than expected for two independently assorting loci. Human linkage analysis turns this biological possibility into a quantitative test.

A locus is a position in the genome; an allele is a sequence variant at that position. A genetic marker is an observable DNA variant, such as a single nucleotide polymorphism, that can identify the inheritance of a chromosomal region. A marker need not cause the trait being investigated. Its usefulness arises from the ability to distinguish chromosomal copies and follow their passage between generations. When a marker lies close to an unknown trait locus, the marker can serve as a signpost for a chromosomal segment that tends to accompany the trait allele.

Classical genetic mapping in experimental organisms may use intentionally chosen parents, controlled crosses and large groups of descendants. Such a strategy is neither feasible nor ethically acceptable for human genetics. Family structures already exist, the number of children is usually limited, and some relatives may be unavailable for genetic analysis. A human pedigree also provides phenotypes rather than direct observations of all the gametes that generated the children. The investigator must therefore distinguish inheritance events that can be inferred confidently from those that remain ambiguous.

The central idea is to compare predictions. A close linkage model predicts that nonrecombinant transmissions occur frequently and recombinant transmissions occur less frequently. An unlinked model predicts that the two categories occur equally often. Neither model generally predicts one unique family pattern. Rather, each assigns probabilities to many possible family patterns. The same observations may be consistent with both models, but may be much more probable under one of them. Likelihood analysis uses this difference to measure evidence.

To see why this is necessary, consider a family with three informative children, all receiving a marker allele together with a trait allele. If the loci assort independently, the probability that three specified transmissions are nonrecombinant is one eighth. This is not impossible. An unlinked pair can produce a perfectly concordant small family just by chance. When the same type of concordance is observed in many independent transmissions, its probability under independent assortment falls rapidly. The number and informativeness of observed transmissions matter, not merely the appearance of a pedigree.

Newton E. Morton's 1955 paper on sequential linkage tests formalized a way to accumulate evidence from families and assess linkage statistically. Instead of regarding every pedigree as a separate yes-or-no experiment, the approach assigns a numerical evidence measure that can be combined under stated assumptions. Modern linkage methods extend this principle to more complicated pedigrees and multiple markers. For examination purposes, the foundational case is a two-locus model in which the recombination fraction is varied and its likelihood is compared with that of independent transmission.

Chromosomes, haplotypes and phase

Consider a diploid parent heterozygous for both a trait locus and a marker locus. Denote the alleles at the trait locus by D and d and those at the marker by M and m. Writing DdMm does not tell us which marker allele is carried on the chromosome bearing D. Two configurations are possible: DM/dm, in which D and M occupy the same homolog, and Dm/dM, in which D and m occupy the same homolog. The arrangement of alleles on individual homologous chromosomes is called phase. A set of alleles on one chromosomal segment is a haplotype.

In the first configuration, DM and dm are parental haplotypes. A gamete carrying Dm or dM is recombinant relative to those parental haplotypes. In the second configuration, Dm and dM are parental, whereas DM and dm are recombinant. The very same observed pair of alleles can therefore be recombinant in one family and nonrecombinant in another. A marker allele is not inherently recombinant; the designation depends on how the transmitting parent's chromosomes were organized before meiosis.

During meiotic prophase, exchange between nonsister chromatids of homologous chromosomes can produce new combinations of alleles. If a chromosome transmitted to a child has a new combination at the two observed loci, that transmission is recombinant for the interval. The molecular number of exchanges is not directly revealed by this two-marker observation: multiple exchanges may produce a chromosome with a parental combination at the ends. Hence a recombinant transmission identifies a changed endpoint combination rather than counting every physical crossing-over event.

Define the recombination fraction θ as the probability that a gamete from the relevant heterozygous parent is recombinant between the two loci. For the ordinary two-point linkage model, θ lies between zero and one half. If θ is 0.10, ten percent of transmissions are expected to be recombinant across many comparable meioses. Under ordinary reciprocal segregation, the two recombinant classes each have probability θ/2, and the two nonrecombinant classes each have probability (1−θ)/2.

At θ equal to zero, the model predicts complete linkage for the sampled transmissions. At θ equal to one half, the two categories are equally probable. Loci on distinct autosomes ordinarily assort independently and have θ equal to one half. Widely separated loci on a single chromosome may also behave nearly independently because the endpoint information becomes randomized by repeated exchanges. Therefore an estimated recombination fraction close to one half cannot establish that the loci occupy different chromosomes.

Recombination fraction is dimensionless. Genetic map distance is usually expressed in centimorgans, and physical distance is measured in base pairs. For short intervals, one percent recombination approximately corresponds to one centimorgan. That approximation progressively loses validity for larger intervals because endpoint classifications do not reveal all crossover events. Two regions of equal physical length may also have different genetic distances owing to variation in recombination activity. A linkage study estimates transmission relationships, not a universal number of base pairs.

Recognizing informative meioses in a pedigree

An informative meiosis is one whose available observations allow the transmitted allelic combination to contribute information about recombination. In the simplest calculation, phase is known and both loci can be tracked in the offspring without ambiguity. This permits classification of a transmission as recombinant or nonrecombinant. Human pedigrees often fall short of this ideal, so informative data must be recognized before counting events.

Suppose a parent carries DM/dm and the other parent carries dm/dm. Assume D causes a fully penetrant dominant trait, there are no phenocopies, and the marker genotype is measured without error. The other parent contributes d and m to every child. An affected child with marker genotype Mm must have received DM from the heterozygous parent. An unaffected child with genotype mm must have received dm. Both transmissions are nonrecombinant. An affected child with genotype mm received Dm, while an unaffected child with genotype Mm received dM; these transmissions are recombinant. This is the human counterpart of a simplified testcross, but its assumptions must be stated.

If the marker is homozygous in the transmitting parent, its two chromosomal copies cannot be distinguished by that marker allele alone. A child inheriting M reveals no information about which of the parent's homologs supplied it. Consequently, the marker cannot classify the crossover status of this transmission. The same problem arises when the child's marker genotype could have been generated by either parent in more than one way. Additional relatives or nearby markers may sometimes resolve the ambiguity.

An affected individual is not necessarily a carrier of a particular marker allele, and an unaffected individual is not always known to lack a trait-associated allele. With incomplete penetrance, an individual carrying a disease-associated allele may lack the phenotype. With phenocopies, individuals without that allele may have a similar phenotype for another reason. Such observations do not prove a recombinant event. Their probabilities must instead be evaluated under the inheritance model. Genotype information can improve inference, but a measured genotype also requires appropriate quality checks.

Phase can sometimes be determined from a transmitting parent's parents. If the marker alleles differ between the grandparents and the trait allele is traceable through one lineage, the two alleles inherited together can be established with considerable confidence. However, uncertainty about the grandparental genotype or phenotype may leave more than one phase possible. Assigning phase to maximize apparent concordance and then treating it as if known in advance introduces bias. The likelihood must account for all admissible phases with their proper weights.

Meioses must also be distinguished from individuals. A large family may include many people whose genotypes are uninformative for the interval, whereas a smaller family may supply several clear transmissions. When a pedigree includes more than one generation, the observed alleles are related through ancestral chromosomes. The complete pedigree likelihood accounts for these relationships and unknown genotypes; it is not obtained simply by counting every affected person as a separate concordant event.

From the probability of a family to the LOD score

Probability describes the frequency with which an observation could arise if a particular model were true. Likelihood uses the same numerical expression but treats the observations as fixed and varies a model parameter. Suppose that a family has nine nonrecombinant and one recombinant transmission. We do not yet know θ. We ask how probable this observed pattern would be for θ equal to 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 or other candidate values. The candidate value that makes the observed data most probable has the greatest likelihood among those examined.

Assume that the phase is known and that n informative transmissions are independent conditional on a common θ. Let R denote the number of recombinant transmissions and N the number of nonrecombinant transmissions, so that n equals R + N. The probability of any particular ordered sequence containing those classes is θ raised to R multiplied by (1−θ) raised to N. If only the counts are observed, a binomial coefficient also appears. That coefficient depends on the observed counts but not on θ, so it cancels in a ratio of likelihoods for the same data.

The unlinked model has θ equal to 0.5. Every specified sequence of n binary transmission classes has probability (0.5) raised to n under this model. Consequently, the likelihood ratio comparing a candidate θ with independent transmission is θ to the power R times (1−θ) to the power N, divided by 0.5 to the power n. When a specific recombinant or parental gamete rather than a class is recorded, an additional factor of one half for each observed gamete cancels from the ratio under the ordinary reciprocal model.

The logarithm of odds, or LOD score, is the base-ten logarithm of this likelihood ratio. Because the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms, the expression becomes N times log base ten of 2(1−θ), plus R times log base ten of 2θ. A positive score indicates that the observed data are more probable under the specified linked model than under the unlinked model. A negative score indicates that they are less probable. At θ equal to 0.5 the score is always zero, because the numerator and denominator represent the same model.

Logarithms are especially useful when a pedigree contains many informative transmissions. Products of many probabilities can become very small even when the underlying model predicts the data reasonably well. Taking a ratio compares relative support rather than the absolute size of these products. Taking a logarithm permits stable calculations and makes it easy to combine independent evidence. A score of 2 means a likelihood ratio of 100; a score of −2 means a ratio of one hundredth. The signs and the logarithmic base therefore matter.

At θ equal to zero, any unequivocally recombinant transmission has probability zero under the model and produces a LOD of negative infinity. If no recombinant transmission is observed, the recombinant factor is absent and the score is N log base ten of 2. It is a common mathematical error to write zero times the logarithm of zero without recognizing that the absent factor contributes nothing. The correct boundary value follows from the likelihood itself or from a limit as θ approaches zero.

LikelihoodL(θ) ∝ (1−θ)NθR
Null likelihoodL(0.5) ∝ 0.5N+R
LODZ(θ) = log10[L(θ)/L(0.5)]
Expanded formZ(θ) = N log10[2(1−θ)] + R log10(2θ)

Worked calculations and maximum likelihood

Consider a hypothetical family with nine nonrecombinant and one recombinant informative transmission. At θ equal to 0.1, the likelihood ratio is 1.8 raised to the ninth power times 0.2. The LOD is nine times log base ten of 1.8 plus log base ten of 0.2. The resulting score is approximately 1.598. The score is positive, so a recombination fraction of 0.1 fits these observations better than the unlinked model, but the score does not reach the conventional threshold of 3 for strong linkage evidence.

If the same data are evaluated at θ equal to 0.25, the score becomes nine times log base ten of 1.5 plus log base ten of 0.5, or approximately 1.284. The candidate model still fits better than no linkage, but it fits less well than the model with θ equal to 0.1. In contrast, a model with θ equal to zero cannot explain the recombinant transmission under the assumed error-free known-phase model. The best-fitting candidate is neither necessarily zero nor necessarily the one most distant from one half.

The maximum-likelihood estimate is the parameter value at which the likelihood is highest. Since taking a logarithm preserves the order of positive values, the maximum of the LOD curve occurs at the same point as the maximum of the likelihood curve. To derive the estimate, it is simpler to differentiate the natural logarithm of the likelihood, which is N ln(1−θ) + R ln θ plus a constant independent of θ. The derivative is −N/(1−θ) + R/θ. Equating it to zero gives R(1−θ) = Nθ and therefore θ = R/(N+R). The second derivative is negative for interior values when there are observations, identifying a maximum.

This result is valid for the simplified model in which all classifications are known and the same recombination fraction applies. The estimate must also satisfy the biological parameter restriction from zero to one half. If the observed recombinant proportion is larger than one half and phase was specified correctly in advance, the constrained maximum occurs at θ equal to one half. This is not evidence of a recombination fraction of 0.7. Instead, the linked models within the assumed phase do not fit better than the null model. A high apparent recombinant fraction may suggest a phase assignment problem or ordinary sampling variation.

For nine nonrecombinants and one recombinant, the estimated θ is 1/10. Yet an estimate and the strength of evidence are separate properties. A family with ninety nonrecombinants and ten recombinants has the same estimated θ, but its maximum LOD is ten times greater, approximately 15.985. The larger family supplies far more evidence because the observed ratio is much less easily attributed to sampling chance under independence.

With all ten informative transmissions nonrecombinant, the maximum occurs at θ equal to zero and the LOD is ten times log base ten of two, approximately 3.010. This crosses the conventional threshold in the idealized known-phase model. Nevertheless, zero observed recombinants does not demonstrate that the loci are at an identical physical position. A small positive true recombination fraction can yield no observed recombinants, and additional transmissions may change the estimate.

A plot of LOD versus θ summarizes two important aspects of inference. Its peak shows the best-fitting value of θ, while its height measures the evidence relative to the null model. The width of the peak reflects how sharply the data discriminate nearby values under the chosen model. A broad peak means that several candidate values fit almost equally well. It is inappropriate to interpret a single best estimate as a precisely known recombination fraction without considering uncertainty.

Combining families and interpreting evidence thresholds

A useful property of the logarithmic likelihood ratio is additivity. Suppose family A and family B have independent observations under a common model and a common θ. The probability of their combined observations is the product of the two family likelihoods. Their joint likelihood ratio is therefore the product of their separate likelihood ratios, and the joint LOD is the sum of their individual LODs at that same θ. One family can support linkage and another can oppose it; both contribute to the combined result.

Consider family A with twelve nonrecombinant and two recombinant transmissions, and family B with six nonrecombinant and two recombinant transmissions. At θ equal to 0.2, the respective scores are 1.654 and 0.429. Their combined score is 2.082. In this simplified example, pooling the counts gives exactly the same score because all transmissions have known phase and share a common parameter. In a general pedigree model, adding likelihood scores at a common θ is valid under independence, but pooling counts may not represent the uncertainty in each pedigree.

Individual families can have different maximum-likelihood estimates. The correct combined maximum is obtained by summing the separate curves at every candidate θ and finding the peak of the resulting curve. Adding each family's separate maximum may exaggerate the support for a single shared recombination fraction, because the maxima may occur at different positions. The sum of independent maxima answers a model allowing each family its own unconstrained estimate, not the same question as a shared-θ linkage test.

The traditional interpretation of a LOD of at least +3 is strong support for linkage under the specified testing framework: the observations are at least one thousand times as likely under that candidate model as under θ equal to one half. A LOD of −2 or less is conventionally used to exclude linkage at the tested recombination fraction, because that model gives a likelihood at most one hundredth of the null likelihood. Scores between those benchmarks are generally inconclusive in the conventional two-point framework. These rules are evidence conventions, not mathematical guarantees of truth.

Exclusion is local to the tested parameter or interval. For example, a recombinant observation excludes θ equal to zero under an error-free model, but may be compatible with θ equal to 0.1 or 0.2. A curve may fall below −2 near zero while remaining positive elsewhere. A student should never convert the exclusion of complete linkage into a statement that the loci are definitely on different chromosomes. A marker may provide little evidence for tight linkage yet still be consistent with more distant linkage.

Several conditions qualify threshold interpretation. Genome-wide searches test many potential locations; uncertainty about the genetic model, selection of pedigrees, errors and locus heterogeneity can affect the distribution of scores. Modern linkage studies sometimes use different significance criteria suited to the design. The traditional numbers remain important in elementary two-point linkage questions, but they should not be represented as universal cutoffs for every statistical genetic method.

Uncertain phase, sex-specific recombination and linkage versus association

When the phase of a parent is unknown, the same series of transmissions has alternative explanations. Consider six transmissions that appear to carry D with M and one that appears to carry D with m. If the parent's phase is DM/dm, this corresponds to six nonrecombinants and one recombinant. If its phase is Dm/dM, the classification is reversed. If the two phase arrangements have equal prior weights and the rest of the data do not favor either, the likelihood for the sequence under the first phase is weighted by one half and added to the likelihood under the second phase weighted by one half. The likelihood is then compared with the similarly evaluated null likelihood.

Phase uncertainty can substantially reduce the apparent evidence from a small family. With only one informative child and two equally probable phases, either observed endpoint pattern has the same marginal probability regardless of θ. The child cannot alone distinguish close linkage from independent assortment without additional phase information. With more children, patterns shared among siblings can provide evidence about phase and linkage jointly. An investigator must not independently reassign the parent's phase for every child; phase is a property of the parent's inherited chromosomes.

Recombination rates vary between genomic regions and can differ between male and female meioses. If a pedigree has known transmissions from both types of parent, a common θ model may serve as a simplifying assumption for an examination problem. When separate parameters are justified, the likelihood factors for male transmissions use θm, while those for female transmissions use θf. The null model sets both to one half for the ordinary autosomal comparison. Combining the two classes without acknowledging their distinct parameters can conceal differences in the expected segregation pattern.

Another complication arises when distinct genetic causes produce similar observed traits in different families. This is locus heterogeneity. A marker may lie near a causal locus in one family but not in another family where a variant in a different gene contributes to the phenotype. A strongly negative LOD from the latter family does not necessarily refute the former family's relationship; rather, it may indicate that the shared genetic model is inadequate. At the NET and SET level, the essential reasoning is to distinguish the data from the assumptions used to combine them.

Linkage is a relationship between loci revealed by their transmission through meiosis. Association is a statistical relationship between observed alleles or between a marker allele and a phenotype in a sample of individuals. A marker allele can be associated with a trait because it is in linkage disequilibrium with a causal allele, but population structure, selection and other mechanisms can also generate an association. Conversely, different families may carry different marker alleles near the same disease locus, so a strong family linkage signal need not correspond to a universal marker allele shared among all affected people.

Finally, a likelihood ratio is not a posterior probability. A LOD of 3 converts to a likelihood ratio of one thousand, not directly to a 99.9 percent probability that linkage exists. Under a simplified comparison of two fixed models, posterior odds equal prior odds multiplied by the likelihood ratio. If the prior odds are one to one thousand, a ratio of one thousand produces posterior odds of one to one, corresponding to probability one half. If the prior odds are one to one, the same ratio produces posterior odds of one thousand to one. The data have not changed; the assumptions about prior plausibility have changed. In a composite linkage model, posterior inference also requires accounting for uncertainty in θ rather than simply equating a maximum likelihood ratio to a Bayes factor.

Initial examination practice

1. B — What does θ represent in ordinary two-point linkage analysis?

Answer and explanation

The probability of a recombinant transmission between the two loci, not the number of crossover events or a physical distance.

2. B — What is Z(0.5) for any dataset under the stated comparison?

Answer and explanation

Zero, because both likelihoods describe the identical parameter value.

3. B — Which gamete is recombinant for phase DM/dm: DM or Dm?

Answer and explanation

Dm, because the alleles originate from different parental haplotypes.

4. B — Does a LOD of −2 mean the marker is on a different chromosome?

Answer and explanation

No. It conventionally excludes the tested recombination fraction under the assumed model.

5. C — A known-phase family has 8 nonrecombinants and 2 recombinants. Estimate θ and calculate its maximum LOD.

Answer and explanation

θ = 2/10 = 0.2; Z = 8 log10(1.6) + 2 log10(0.4) = 0.837. The evidence is positive but below +3.

6. C — Family A has its maximum LOD at θ = 0.05 and family B at θ = 0.25. Can their maximum values simply be added to obtain the maximum of a shared-θ analysis?

Answer and explanation

No. Sum their scores at each common candidate θ and then find the maximum. The separate maxima permit different parameters.

7. C — Ten known-phase nonrecombinant transmissions are observed. Does this establish θ = 0 with certainty?

Answer and explanation

The maximum occurs at zero and Z(0) = 3.010, but small positive values can still generate ten nonrecombinants. The estimate is not certainty.

8. C — Under equal phase probabilities, one child provides an observed marker-trait combination. Explain why it may provide no marginal information about θ.

Answer and explanation

The combination is nonrecombinant under one phase and recombinant under the other. Their equally weighted probabilities sum to 0.5 for the binary class, regardless of θ.

9. SET — Contrast linkage and association.

Answer and explanation

Linkage concerns co-transmission of loci in families; association concerns statistical dependence of alleles or phenotypes in sampled populations.

10. SET — A fixed-model likelihood ratio is 100 and prior odds are 1:100. What are posterior odds?

Answer and explanation

One to one, yielding posterior probability 0.5 for the first model in the two-model comparison.
