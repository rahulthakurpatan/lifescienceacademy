1. The problem of reconstructing inheritance

A family history is a record of outcomes rather than a controlled genetic cross. Human geneticists generally cannot choose the parents of a future generation, dictate the number of children or deliberately introduce a genetic variant. Instead, they ask whether naturally observed family relationships and phenotypes are consistent with particular mechanisms of transmission. This reverses the direction of a conventional Mendelian problem. In a pea cross, the parental genotypes may be known and the offspring ratios predicted. In a human pedigree, the observed offspring help constrain the possible parental genotypes. It is an inference problem: several explanations may fit the same observations, and an explanation must survive every informative relationship in the family.

The phenotype of interest must be defined before any transmission model is tested. For example, two relatives described as having impaired hearing may have completely different underlying causes. A shared symptom is not proof of a shared allele. Conversely, a relative without an apparent symptom may carry the same variant as an affected family member, especially when the trait appears late in life or is incompletely penetrant. The useful question is not merely which symbols are shaded but what the shading actually represents. Age at observation, diagnostic criteria and reliability of the family report can change the interpretation substantially.

A diploid autosomal locus normally contains one allele inherited from each biological parent. Meiosis separates the two homologous chromosomes so that a heterozygote produces gametes bearing either allele, in an idealized model with equal probability. Fertilization combines one allele from each parent. When the locus is on the non-pseudoautosomal part of the X chromosome, the path differs because a typical XY father passes his X to daughters and Y to sons. Mitochondrial DNA follows yet another route, through the cytoplasm of the egg. Pedigree patterns are therefore consequences of cellular mechanisms, not arbitrary visual rules. The scope of this chapter assumes conventional XX and XY transmission in textbook crosses unless otherwise specified; actual families can have chromosome complements and biological circumstances that require more detailed models.

A pedigree represents biological relatedness as a set of constrained paths. It is useful to separate three layers of information: known relationships, observed phenotypes and inferred genotypes. The first two are observations or reports; the third is a hypothesis. If a model requires a father to transmit an ordinary non-pseudoautosomal X allele to his son, the model contradicts chromosome segregation, no matter how visually attractive the pedigree looks. If a model merely predicts that approximately half of children will be affected but the family has three affected children and none unaffected, the prediction is not logically contradicted. A probability can be small without being zero.

Classical studies of inherited human traits long predate DNA sequencing. Archibald Garrod's work on alkaptonuria linked the inheritance of an unusual metabolic phenotype to Mendelian segregation and proposed that inherited differences in metabolism could explain the condition. The enduring lesson is that family transmission and biochemical phenotype can inform one another. Modern sequence data can add a third kind of evidence: an identified variant that segregates with a phenotype. Segregation by itself still does not establish causation because neighboring variants may travel together. Molecular evidence, functional understanding and a sufficiently informative family may all be necessary. In examination problems, the goal is usually more limited: determine a plausible transmission model and compute its consequences.

2. Pedigree notation and reliable family records

Standard pedagogical pedigrees commonly show a square for a male, a circle for a female and a diamond when sex is unspecified or not recorded. A filled symbol denotes the defined affected phenotype. A horizontal line connects reproductive partners and a vertical line descends to the sibship line, from which each child's symbol is connected. Generations are labelled using Roman numerals, and people within a generation are numbered from left to right. Thus II-3 identifies the third numbered individual in generation II, not necessarily the third child in a particular sibship. The arrow identifying a proband indicates the individual through whom the family came to attention; that individual is not necessarily the founder of the variant.

A diagonal slash through a symbol denotes death rather than the presence or absence of the phenotype. Carrier markings vary according to the notation standard and the condition. Some educational diagrams use a dot or partial fill, but a legend must explicitly distinguish a proven heterozygote from a person whose genotype is inferred, and distinguish genotype information from observed phenotype. A person whose phenotype is unknown must not be drawn as unambiguously unaffected without an appropriate annotation. The standardized pedigree nomenclature recommendations discussed by Bennett and colleagues emphasize consistent recording and communication. For an exam diagram, follow its supplied key rather than silently importing a different convention.

Full siblings share two biological parents; half siblings share one. When people are connected by multiple ancestral paths, a single individual may be relevant to more than one branch of the probability calculation. A double partner line commonly indicates consanguinity. It is not a symbol for a genetic disease. Adoption, donor conception, uncertain parentage and other relevant relationships can require specific annotations in a formal pedigree. In the simple mathematical models used here, the lines represent the stated genetic relationships. The absence of a line is not evidence that a person has no siblings; it only means that the diagram does not show them.

To extract evidence systematically, first identify each affected individual and list the phenotype of both parents where known. Next follow affected fathers separately from affected mothers. Ask whether daughters and sons are represented and whether the phenotype occurs in consecutive generations. Then annotate obligatory genotypes under each candidate model. For instance, an unaffected parent of a child with a fully penetrant autosomal recessive phenotype must have contributed a recessive allele if the simple single-locus model is correct. If both parents are unaffected they are both heterozygotes under that model. Their genotypes are deduced from the child, not from a visual carrier symbol.

The number of children is a crucial part of the evidence. A father with one unaffected daughter and no sons provides a very different test of X-linked dominant transmission from a father with five unaffected daughters. A missing daughter is not an unaffected daughter. Similarly, in a family of two sons, the absence of affected girls provides no information about the risk among daughters. When a question says that both sexes are equally affected, decide whether it is describing an expected probability from a model or the actual observed counts. These statements cannot be used interchangeably.

3. Autosomal dominant inheritance

In a simple autosomal dominant model, denote the phenotype-producing allele by D and the alternate allele by d. Both Dd and DD individuals are affected when penetrance is complete and both genotypes are viable, whereas dd individuals are unaffected. Dominance is a relationship between alleles and a specified phenotype, not a measure of how common an allele is or how severe a disease is. A dominant variant can be rare because it arises infrequently or reduces reproductive success. A molecular change can act dominantly by reducing the functional product below a necessary threshold, by creating an abnormal activity, or by interfering with the normal gene product. The pedigree predicts the inheritance relationship; additional evidence is required to choose the molecular mechanism.

For Dd crossed with dd, the affected parent produces D-bearing and d-bearing gametes in equal proportions. The other parent contributes only d. Thus the possible offspring genotypes are Dd and dd with probabilities one half each. Since the locus is autosomal, these genotype probabilities are the same when conditioned on a son or a daughter, provided chromosome segregation and sex are independent. An affected father can transmit the allele to a son, a relationship that is particularly useful when comparing this model with X-linked inheritance. But father and son both being affected does not, by itself, prove that the father transmitted the variant. The son might have inherited a separate allele from his mother or have an unrelated cause of the phenotype.

A dominant trait often produces a vertical pedigree in which affected individuals appear in successive generations. The explanation is that a single transmitted allele can produce the phenotype. However, the vertical appearance is only a tendency. If a heterozygote has two children, the probability that neither inherits D is one quarter. If the branch continues through one of those noncarriers, the familial variant cannot be transmitted through that branch under the ordinary model. Dominant inheritance does not require every generation or every individual in a family to be affected. A completely unaffected person cannot transmit a fully penetrant dominant allele in the idealized model, but age-dependent expression, germline mosaicism and incorrect phenotype assignment can invalidate that deduction.

When both parents are heterozygotes, the genotypes DD, Dd and dd have respective probabilities one quarter, one half and one quarter. With complete penetrance and equal survival, the phenotype probability is three quarters affected. If DD is lethal before the stage at which the offspring are scored, the observed surviving fraction affected is instead two thirds because only Dd and dd survivors contribute to the denominator. This calculation distinguishes the probability at fertilization from the probability among surviving children. Selection of the observation stage is an important part of many genetics problems, and the same issue can arise in recessive or X-linked models.

4. Autosomal recessive inheritance

For a fully penetrant autosomal recessive condition, write A for an allele sufficient for the ordinary phenotype and a for a disease-associated allele. AA and Aa have the unaffected phenotype in this model; aa is affected. The heterozygous individual is a carrier. This simplified notation can also stand for two different disease-associated variants in the same gene, one inherited from each parent, when the combination causes loss of adequate function. Such an affected person is a compound heterozygote rather than a homozygote at the sequence level. The common educational cross Aa by Aa assumes that a denotes any relevant nonfunctional allele at the locus, not necessarily a single DNA substitution.

Each carrier makes A and a gametes in equal proportions. Multiplying the probabilities gives AA with probability one quarter, Aa with probability one half and aa with probability one quarter. If a child is already known to be unaffected, the aa outcome must be removed from the possible genotype states under complete penetrance. Among the remaining three equally weighted Punnett-square entries, two correspond to Aa. Therefore the conditional carrier probability is two thirds. The fact that one sibling is affected does not alter the genotype distribution of a further child when parental genotypes are already known; the pregnancies are independent conditional on those parental genotypes. Observing a new affected child can nevertheless change our knowledge of the parental genotypes if those genotypes were originally uncertain.

Recessive conditions commonly produce a horizontal pattern, with affected siblings and unaffected parents. This is not an exclusive property of recessive traits, and recessive inheritance can occur in consecutive generations. Suppose aa mates with Aa. The affected parent can only transmit a. The carrier parent produces A or a, giving Aa and aa with equal probability. Half the children are expected to be affected; every unaffected child is an obligate carrier. This cross can mimic dominant inheritance visually and is often called pseudodominant transmission. The clue is not the pattern's vertical appearance but whether the assigned genotypes satisfy all relationships.

Consanguinity raises the chance that two biological relatives share an allele inherited from a common ancestor. It does not generate a recessive mutation automatically. Under a simple rare-allele model, suppose two first cousins share a grandparent known to be heterozygous while all other founders are assumed noncarriers. Each cousin has probability one quarter of receiving that grandparent's allele: one half for transmission to the intervening parent and one half for transmission to the cousin. Under independent segregation down the two distinct branches, the probability that both cousins carry this allele is one sixteenth. If both carry it, their child has probability one quarter of receiving it twice, giving a joint probability of one sixty-fourth under the explicitly stated model. This is not a general clinical risk for first-cousin couples; population allele frequencies, other founders and the actual family history change the result.

5. X-linked and Y-linked inheritance

A typical XY male has one copy of most X-linked loci, whereas a typical XX female has two. Hemizygosity describes the former state; it does not mean homozygosity because there is no second allele at that ordinary X locus. For a recessive X-linked variant Xr, a male XrY expresses the phenotype under complete penetrance. A heterozygous female XRXr is often unaffected in a simplified exam model. In real biological examples, X inactivation and tissue-specific expression can produce manifestations in heterozygous females, so the idealization must be stated rather than used as a universal clinical rule.

Consider XRXr crossed with XRY. A son receives Y from his father and either XR or Xr from his mother. His conditional probability of being affected is one half. A daughter receives XR from her father and XR or Xr from her mother, so half the daughters are carriers and none is affected under the simple recessive model. If the probabilities of a son and daughter are equal, an affected son is one quarter of all children. Many examination mistakes arise from confusing the probability among sons with the joint probability of being a son and affected.

An affected male XrY with a noncarrier female XRXR has no affected offspring under that model, but all daughters inherit Xr from him and become carriers. His sons inherit his Y and a normal X from their mother. If a carrier daughter later has children with an unaffected noncarrier male, her sons each have one-half risk of the recessive phenotype. This produces the classic appearance of a variant passing through unaffected female relatives to affected male descendants. A family can lack affected women because the combination of two disease-associated X alleles is rare, but female expression remains biologically possible.

In an X-linked dominant model, Xd denotes the dominant phenotype-producing allele. An affected father XdY with an unaffected mother XnXn has daughters XdXn and sons XnY. With complete penetrance all daughters and no sons express the trait. A heterozygous affected mother crossed with an unaffected father transmits Xd to half her daughters and half her sons. This asymmetry between maternal and paternal transmission follows the different gamete chromosome complements. The absence of affected daughters in a large family of an affected father contradicts a fully penetrant simple X-linked dominant model if the daughter relationships and phenotype reports are reliable. Some X-linked conditions do not fit the binary dominant and recessive labels cleanly.

For a variant restricted to the male-specific part of the Y chromosome, a typical father passes the Y chromosome to all sons and no daughters. With complete penetrance and no new mutation, all sons of an affected fertile father therefore carry and express the relevant allele. A pattern of affected males alone is not sufficient to infer Y linkage. X-linked recessive inheritance, sex-limited autosomal expression and chance can also produce male-only affected symbols. Pseudoautosomal loci differ because homologous regions on X and Y can recombine; the ordinary prohibition of father-to-son X-linked transmission is intended for non-pseudoautosomal loci.

6. Mitochondrial transmission and competing models

Most human mitochondrial genomes are inherited from the egg. Thus a mother carrying an mtDNA variant may transmit it to both sons and daughters, whereas a father carrying an mtDNA variant does not ordinarily transmit it. The difference is not a result of the sex of the offspring but of the origin of the organelles. In a simple model where every mitochondrial genome in an egg carries the variant, all offspring inherit it. However, genotype transmission must be separated from disease expression. A person may have a mixture of mitochondrial genomes with and without a variant, termed heteroplasmy. During oogenesis and development, changes in the proportion of those genomes can create different variant burdens among siblings and tissues.

A variant burden that impairs function in one tissue may be tolerated in another because energy demands and cellular context differ. A threshold model represents this principle by assuming that dysfunction becomes apparent when the proportion of variant genomes exceeds some tissue-dependent level. The model does not imply that every mitochondrial disorder has one universal threshold. A mildly affected mother can have a more severely affected child, and a carrier may have no observable phenotype. Consequently, maternal transmission of an mtDNA variant does not justify assigning every child a one hundred percent probability of clinical expression. In this course the organelle bottleneck is a prerequisite concept from 8.A.08; we use it to explain why a human pedigree cannot generally be reduced to a simple dominant Punnett square.

A maternally clustered pedigree could also arise from a nuclear allele, shared exposure or an ascertainment artifact. Many proteins necessary for mitochondrial function are encoded by nuclear genes. A disease involving mitochondrial physiology may consequently be inherited as autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant or X-linked rather than through mitochondrial DNA. The phenotype's organ system does not identify the genome harboring the causal variant. A strong test compares transmission from affected males with transmission from affected females and then uses molecular data to identify whether the variant actually resides in mtDNA.

Distinguish three statements that are sometimes collapsed into one: a variant is located within mitochondrial DNA; its transmission is usually maternal; and its phenotype can be observed in a person. The first concerns genomic location, the second inheritance, and the third physiological expression. Only when all relevant assumptions are specified can a numerical recurrence prediction be made. The same three-level analysis applies to chromosomal variants. It is especially useful for interpreting unaffected individuals who might nevertheless transmit an allele.

7. Conditional probability and Bayesian updating

A pedigree problem often asks for the probability of a hidden genotype after observing a phenotype. Let H denote the hypothesis that a woman is a carrier of an autosomal recessive variant, and E denote the evidence that she is unaffected. The conditional probability P(H given E) equals P(H and E) divided by P(E). Bayes' rule rewrites this as P(E given H) times P(H) divided by P(E). If the two hypotheses H and not-H exhaust the possibilities, the denominator is P(E given H)P(H) plus P(E given not-H)P(not-H). The denominator normalizes the two weighted hypotheses so that their posterior probabilities sum to one.

Suppose the parents are known carriers and their daughter is unaffected. Before observing her phenotype, the genotype probabilities are AA one quarter, Aa one half and aa one quarter. Under complete penetrance the probability of being unaffected is three quarters. The joint probability of being a carrier and unaffected is one half. Dividing yields two thirds. In a second example, assume that this unaffected daughter has children with a confirmed carrier. The risk of an affected child is two thirds times one quarter, or one sixth, because only the event that she is a carrier permits an aa child. This is a mixture of genotype scenarios, not a new Mendelian segregation ratio.

Now suppose the woman has two unaffected children with the confirmed carrier partner. For a noncarrier woman AA, the probability that both children are unaffected is one. For a carrier woman Aa, each child has probability three quarters of being unaffected, so the likelihood of two unaffected children is nine sixteenths, assuming independent meioses and complete penetrance. Multiplying the prior carrier probability two thirds by nine sixteenths gives three eighths; multiplying the noncarrier prior one third by one gives one third. The total probability of the two observations is seventeen twenty-fourths, and the updated carrier probability is nine seventeenths. The conditional risk for a further child is nine seventeenths times one quarter, or nine sixty-eighths. The declining risk reflects revised information about the woman's genotype, not a biological compensation mechanism after unaffected births.

A similar update applies to X-linked recessive transmission. Suppose a woman has prior carrier probability one half, her partner is unaffected, and her two sons are unaffected. Under the carrier hypothesis the likelihood of the observations is one quarter; under the noncarrier hypothesis the likelihood is one. The two unnormalized posterior weights are one eighth and one half. Their sum is five eighths, so her posterior carrier probability is one fifth. The next son has one tenth probability of being affected under these assumptions. If the question asks for the next child regardless of sex, the probability of an affected son is one twentieth, given equal sex probabilities and the assumption that daughters are unaffected.

For a dominant allele with penetrance f, the probability of a phenotype need not equal the allele transmission probability. If a known Dd parent mates with dd and carriers have penetrance 0.8 while dd individuals never express the trait, the affected probability is 0.5 times 0.8, or 0.4. If the offspring is observed to be unaffected, the probability of carrying D is the joint probability of receiving D and failing to express, 0.5 times 0.2, divided by the total unaffected probability 0.6. The answer is one sixth. This person can still transmit the variant. Penetrance is a conditional probability of expression given a genotype and relevant context; expressivity describes the extent or character of expression among people with the genotype.

The likelihood approach compares models by asking how probable the actual observations are under each. Suppose an affected father and unaffected mother have three daughters, all affected, and two sons, all unaffected. Conditional on known offspring sexes, a fully penetrant X-linked dominant model with the usual parental genotypes assigns likelihood one to the phenotype sequence. An autosomal dominant heterozygote model assigns likelihood one thirty-second. This comparison favors X-linked dominance over the particular autosomal model by a factor of thirty-two. It does not establish a posterior probability of thirty-two thirty-thirds unless equal model priors and an exhaustive two-model comparison are separately justified. This distinction prepares the logic of the next node on LOD scores.

8. Exceptions, evidence and model discrimination

De novo variation can produce an affected individual in a family with no prior affected relatives. If a variant arises in a gamete or at an early developmental stage, the person may carry it even though neither parent shows the corresponding phenotype. A dominant pedigree can therefore start with an apparently isolated case. Germline mosaicism means that a parent has a variant in a subset of germ cells, potentially absent or below detection in a sampled somatic tissue. Repeated transmission from an unaffected parent cannot automatically be ruled out simply because a blood test does not detect the variant. The fraction of germ cells carrying it cannot generally be inferred from one observed birth.

Phenocopy refers to an observed phenotype resembling a genetically determined one but arising from a different cause, often an environmental influence. In a pedigree study it may produce an affected symbol at a position incompatible with an otherwise consistent segregation model. Genetic heterogeneity creates a related problem: similar phenotypes may result from variants at distinct loci or from distinct alleles of the same locus. A model that assumes every affected family member has the identical genetic cause can fail for either reason. In exam questions, assume a single locus and no phenocopies only if the wording states that model or the intended simplification is clear.

Reduced penetrance can mimic skipped generations in dominant inheritance. A young relative with an age-dependent trait may be classified as unaffected at observation but manifest later. Thus penetrance should sometimes be interpreted as conditional on age. Variable expressivity differs: two people carrying the same allele may express different severity or combinations of signs. The distinctions matter when a family record uses a binary affected/unaffected symbol to compress a spectrum of physiological effects. Scoring criteria that classify mild expression as unaffected can create apparent nonpenetrance even if the person has measurable manifestations.

An orderly method for model discrimination is to write a candidate genotype for each individual, propagate obligatory alleles through parent-child links, reject assignments that require impossible transmission, and then compare the likelihoods of remaining assignments. Do not reject a model merely because an expected ratio is not observed in a small sibship. For example, a heterozygous autosomal dominant parent and noncarrier partner have probability one eighth of producing three unaffected children. That outcome is not unusual enough to establish nontransmission. In contrast, a confirmed non-pseudoautosomal X-linked allele cannot follow a direct X chromosome route from a typical XY father to his XY son. The distinction between zero probability under a specified model and a low nonzero probability is the foundation of reliable pedigree analysis.

Actual human genetics involves more than these simplified mathematical models. Family histories can be incomplete, variants may have uncertain significance, and risks can depend on allele frequencies, ancestry, penetrance, environmental influences and clinical evidence. The worked examples here are hypothetical, intended to teach segregation and conditional inference. Their numerical answers apply to their stated assumptions, not to an individual's medical circumstances. The next step in the Academy curriculum is linkage analysis, which uses segregation of markers and disease-associated alleles to compare likelihoods across recombination fractions.

9. Reconstructing genotypes across three generations

The most productive way to read a multigenerational family is to move repeatedly between individuals rather than starting with the oldest generation and following a single path. Information may travel backward as well as forward in an inference. Consider two unaffected parents with an affected daughter in a fully penetrant autosomal recessive model. Although neither parent initially has a known genotype, the daughter's genotype aa fixes the contribution from each parent. Both parents must be Aa. We can now propagate these assignments forward to another child and sideways to relatives whose carrier status was initially uncertain. This backward reasoning is a constraint imposed by Mendelian transmission, not a claim that a child's genotype physically determines the genotype of its parents.

Suppose one of these parents, II-2, has an unaffected brother II-3. Their parents in generation I are known to be AA and Aa. II-2 is a carrier because of the affected daughter. Before this evidence, II-3 has probability one half of being Aa. Knowing that II-2 is Aa does not change this probability when the two grandparental genotypes are fixed. The two children received their respective gametes independently. If the grandparents' genotypes were uncertain before observing II-2's transmission, the same evidence could alter the brother's probability by changing what we believe about the grandparents. Distinguish independent segregation events conditional on a parental genotype from unconditional dependence when that genotype is unknown.

We can extend this family to a third generation. If II-3 has a partner known to be AA, his child has probability one quarter of carrying a: first II-3 must carry a, with probability one half, and then he must pass it to the child, again with probability one half. That child cannot be aa under the assumptions because the other parent supplies A. If II-3 is subsequently demonstrated to be a carrier, the child's carrier probability is revised to one half. The model has not changed its rule of meiosis. It has changed the probability of the state on which the transmission event depends. This distinction between a hidden state and an observable event is especially important when working through longer pedigrees.

An obligate carrier is an individual whose genotype can be deduced from the available evidence under an explicitly specified model. For autosomal recessive inheritance, the unaffected parents of an affected child are examples. In X-linked recessive problems, a daughter of an affected father and a confirmed noncarrier mother is an obligate heterozygote. In both cases the designation is conditional on the assumed mechanism, family relationships and absence of new mutations. A carrier who is merely inferred to have a probability of one half is not an obligate carrier. Label the latter with a numerical probability rather than filling the carrier symbol as if a molecular result had established the genotype.

Consider a dominant allele instead. If an affected person D? has an unaffected child with an unaffected dd partner, the affected person must be Dd under complete penetrance and ordinary Mendelian segregation. A DD parent cannot produce a d gamete. This conclusion does not require knowledge of the affected person's own parents. When a problem explicitly states that the homozygous dominant genotype is lethal, the viability constraint may establish Dd even before a child is observed. Conversely, when no such information is given and the person has only affected children, both Dd and DD can remain possible. Do not silently assume that every affected dominant parent is heterozygous unless rarity or the pedigree evidence justifies it.

A practical genotype table can list each individual, observed phenotype, possible states and evidence for elimination. For a recessive pedigree, assign aa to each affected person first, then check unaffected parents and partners of affected individuals. For an X-linked pedigree, separately track the paternal and maternal sex chromosome routes. For mitochondrial inheritance, follow the chain of egg-derived genomes rather than assign two alleles at a nuclear locus. This disciplined approach is more robust than memorizing a picture of a typical family. It also reveals which additional observations would reduce uncertainty, a useful skill in data-interpretation problems.

10. Families with uncertain founder genotypes

Founder genotypes often cannot be deduced simply from shading. An unshaded outsider in a rare autosomal recessive problem may be assumed to be AA as a convenient approximation, but that is an assumption about allele frequency and available evidence rather than an inference from the phenotype. A person with one disease-associated allele and one functional allele is usually unaffected in the simple recessive model. Treating all outsiders as noncarriers would incorrectly make some legitimate recurrence questions impossible. Before calculation, determine whether the exam gives a carrier frequency, says a condition is very rare, or explicitly provides a partner genotype. If none of these are available, a numerical answer may be impossible without an assumption.

Consider an affected man aa and an unrelated unaffected woman whose carrier probability is given as 1/50. Under the simple model her alternative state is AA. If she is AA, their children are all Aa and unaffected. If she is Aa, each child has a probability one half of being aa. The overall affected probability is therefore 1/50 multiplied by 1/2, or 1/100. The probability that an unaffected child is a carrier is one, because the father contributes a in either maternal genotype scenario. These are distinct questions: the uncertain maternal state changes the probability that a child is affected but does not change the allele that the affected father passes to every child.

Now suppose two unrelated unaffected partners have carrier probabilities 1/20 and 1/30. Assume independence of their carrier states. The probability that both are carriers is 1/600. If both are carriers, the chance of an affected child is 1/4, so the marginal risk is 1/2400. If they are related through a common ancestor, the independence assumption can fail. The relevant expression is P(both carriers) times 1/4, not necessarily the product of the two marginal carrier probabilities. Even among nominally unrelated people, population structure may matter if the probabilities are not conditional on their ancestry. An examination will often supply an independence assumption; a scientific interpretation must say when that assumption is being used.

Consider two offspring of the same uncertain carrier couple. If their carrier probabilities are each one half, the probability that both parents are carriers is one quarter. The chance that both children are affected is one quarter multiplied by the probability of two aa outcomes given Aa by Aa, namely one sixteenth. The result is one sixty-fourth. A frequent mistaken method computes the marginal probability for one affected child, one sixteenth, and squares it to obtain one two-hundred-and-fifty-sixth. This treats the two children as if their parental genotypes were independently redrawn before each conception. In reality the hidden carrier states of the shared parents create dependence among the offspring outcomes. Once both parents are known to be carriers, the segregation events are independent under the standard model.

The same reasoning explains why an affected child can dramatically change the inferred risk in a family. Before birth, two uncertain parents may have a low joint carrier probability. If an affected child is observed and the model excludes phenocopies and new mutations, both parents become obligate carriers. The next child has risk one quarter, not the previous marginal risk calculated for the uncertain states. The affected birth provides evidence about a shared cause; it does not change the alleles in the parents. This is a general statistical pattern in which an observation predicts future observations by revealing information about an underlying variable.

A more complex example uses one unaffected child as evidence. Suppose an unaffected woman has a one-half prior probability of being an autosomal recessive carrier, and her unaffected partner independently has a one-quarter prior probability. Their probability of both being carriers is one eighth. Under the both-carrier hypothesis, an unaffected child has probability three quarters. Under the complementary hypothesis the probability is one. The posterior probability that both parents are carriers after one unaffected child is therefore (1/8 × 3/4) divided by [(1/8 × 3/4) + (7/8 × 1)], which equals 3/31. The risk for a further child is 3/124. We used a joint hypothesis rather than two separate carrier updates because the observation is jointly informative about the two parental genotypes. Their posterior states need not remain independent.

For an autosomal dominant condition, an analogous problem may involve an unaffected founder whose carrier state is uncertain because penetrance is incomplete. Suppose the prior chance of Dd is 1/10, penetrance is 0.6 and dd never produces the phenotype. An unaffected observation has probability 0.4 under Dd and one under dd. The posterior carrier probability is 0.04 divided by 0.94, or 2/47. If that founder has a child with a dd partner, the child has probability 1/47 of inheriting D and 3/235 of expressing the phenotype under the same penetrance assumption. The final multiplication by penetrance is essential if the question asks about expression rather than inheritance.

11. Probability distributions and the evidence of a sibship

Pedigree ratios are probability models. The expected number of affected individuals in a family is not a demand that each family exhibit exactly that count. Suppose a heterozygous dominant parent and a noncarrier partner have four children and penetrance is complete. Each child has affected probability p = 1/2. The probability of exactly k affected children among n independent births is the binomial expression C(n,k)p^k(1-p)^(n-k), where C(n,k) counts the distinct ways to choose k affected positions from the n children. For n = 4 and k = 2, the six possible arrangements give probability 6/16 = 3/8. The other possible counts are zero, one, three and four, with probabilities 1/16, 4/16, 4/16 and 1/16, respectively.

The binomial coefficient is necessary when order is not specified. If the question describes the first two children as affected and the last two as unaffected, only one particular sequence is allowed and the probability is 1/16. If the question asks for two affected children without specifying positions, six different sequences satisfy the observation. Some students multiply by a combinatorial coefficient even when the diagram fixes every individual's status. Decide whether the observation is a count or a specific family arrangement. For comparing models using a fully specified pedigree, the observed individuals and their phenotypes are usually fixed.

For two autosomal recessive carriers the affected probability is one quarter. In a family of three children, the probability of no affected child is (3/4)^3 = 27/64. The probability of at least one affected child is its complement, 37/64. The probability of exactly one affected child is 3 × 1/4 × (3/4)^2 = 27/64. Although the expected affected count is three quarters of one child, no actual family can contain a fractional affected individual. The expected value describes the mean over repeated families with the same genotype model, whereas the observed count is an integer random outcome.

If the sex of each child matters, condition on what the question specifies. Under X-linked recessive inheritance with a heterozygous mother and an unaffected father, if the children are known to be two sons and one daughter, only the sons can express the phenotype in the elementary model. The probability that at least one son is affected is 1 - (1/2)^2 = 3/4. If instead the three children's sexes are unspecified and each birth has probability one quarter of being an affected son, the probability of at least one affected son is 1 - (3/4)^3 = 37/64. These two problems are not equivalent because the first conditions on a particular sex composition. In pedigree model comparison, it is often appropriate to condition on observed sexes rather than to include the same probability of those sexes in each model's likelihood.

Now consider ascertainment: how did the family enter the sample? Suppose researchers recruit families only if at least one child has a recessive disease. For known carrier parents with two children, the unconditioned count probabilities for zero, one and two affected children are 9/16, 6/16 and 1/16. In the recruited sample the zero-affected families are excluded. Conditional on recruitment, the probabilities of one and two affected children become 6/7 and 1/7. The expected affected proportion among recruited two-child families is therefore (6/7 × 1/2) + (1/7 × 1) = 4/7, much larger than one quarter. This does not demonstrate a violation of segregation. The sample is enriched for families satisfying the recruitment criterion.

A related issue occurs when a pedigree is collected around a proband. If the proband's affected status is the reason the family was studied, treating this observation as if it arose in a random sample can exaggerate evidence for an inheritance model. At NET level, understand that likelihood must correspond to the actual observation and sampling scheme. More detailed statistical methods correct for ascertainment in ways appropriate to the study design, but the conceptual point can be derived from elementary conditional probability. When comparing a hypothetical family's models, do not introduce ascertainment adjustments unless the problem specifies recruitment or asks explicitly about selection.

The variance of a binomial count is np(1-p), which gives a mathematical explanation of why small families may look unusual. For four children with p = 1/2, the variance is one and the standard deviation is one child. For one hundred independent observations under the same model, the proportion's standard deviation is the square root of p(1-p)/100, or 0.05. Larger samples produce more stable proportions, but a family of one hundred siblings is not a realistic human study design. Data are instead accumulated across families, and those families may differ in genotype, penetrance and ascertainment. Pooling counts without accounting for these differences can obscure the actual inheritance mechanism.

12. Solving sex-linked pedigrees beyond the elementary cross

Track an X chromosome by asking which parent provides it rather than memorizing a rule about affected sons. In a typical family, a male receives his single non-pseudoautosomal X from his mother. Thus if a boy is affected by a fully penetrant X-linked recessive allele and new variants are excluded, the mother must carry at least one disease-associated X. If she is unaffected, she is heterozygous under the idealized model. His father may be affected or unaffected without changing the origin of the boy's X chromosome. An affected father and affected son may therefore occur in an X-linked recessive family if the mother is a carrier. What is excluded is direct paternal transmission of that X allele, not the coexistence of the two affected males.

An affected father and a heterozygous mother provide a useful exercise because all four offspring genotype categories are possible. Let the father be XrY and the mother XRXr. Among daughters, half inherit XR from the mother and Xr from the father, becoming heterozygous, while half inherit Xr from both parents and are affected. Among sons, half inherit XR and are unaffected, while half inherit Xr and are affected. With equal sex probabilities each of the four outcomes occurs with probability one quarter. Thus half of all children are affected, and the affected proportion is the same in sons and daughters. The familiar statement that X-linked recessive traits primarily affect males is a population tendency for rare alleles, not an invariant property of every possible cross.

Suppose an unaffected daughter of this cross is shown in a pedigree. Her paternal X must be Xr. Since she is unaffected under the simple model, her maternal X must be XR. She is an obligate heterozygote. Her unaffected brother, however, must be XRY and cannot carry the same X-linked recessive variant in the hemizygous state under full penetrance. The identical unshaded appearance of two siblings can conceal completely different genotype constraints. This is why the sex of each individual and the relationship to the affected father are essential to a pedigree calculation.

The mechanism of X inactivation explains why the phenotype of heterozygous females is not always identical to the elementary model. In many mammalian cells one X becomes largely transcriptionally inactive early in development. Different cells in a heterozygous individual may retain different active X chromosomes, yielding a mosaic of gene expression. If the fraction of cells expressing the normal allele is insufficient for adequate function in a relevant tissue, manifestations may occur. The simple genotype probabilities for transmission are unchanged; what changes is the map from genotype to phenotype. X-inactivation patterns also vary among tissues, and some genes escape inactivation. This is sufficient mechanistic context for evaluating a pedigree without introducing specialist assays.

For a fully penetrant X-linked dominant condition, an affected heterozygous woman and an unaffected man produce affected and unaffected offspring of both sexes in equal proportions within each sex category. This cross has the same basic phenotype distribution as a Dd by dd autosomal dominant cross. The two models cannot be distinguished from that cross alone, even if a very large number of children precisely fit the one-half ratio. Evidence from affected fathers or molecular localization is required. This example demonstrates non-identifiability: different models make the same predictions for a particular observation, so collecting more of the same type of observation cannot distinguish them.

If a variant is X-linked dominant but affected males have a substantially reduced probability of survival to birth, the observed pedigree may contain mostly affected females even when transmission at conception is symmetric from a heterozygous mother. As a simple teaching model, suppose affected males do not survive to the scoring stage and all other genotypes do. The four equal-probability conception categories are unaffected female, affected female, unaffected male and affected male. Removing the last category leaves three equally weighted surviving categories. One third of survivors are affected females, two thirds are unaffected, and the ratio of females to males among survivors is two to one. This is a conditional survival calculation, not a change in the probability of transmitting the mother's variant. Do not apply it to an actual disease without evidence that this viability model fits.

Finally consider Y-linked inheritance alongside an autosomal sex-limited phenotype. Both models can show affected fathers and sons but no affected females. In the ordinary Y-linked model, every son receives the relevant paternal Y chromosome. In an autosomal dominant male-limited model, a heterozygous affected father transmits the allele to half his sons and half his daughters. Daughters may carry it without expressing the male-limited phenotype and can transmit it to sons. An unaffected daughter who later has an affected son can therefore provide evidence for the autosomal sex-limited model if the son's paternal line lacks the variant. The word sex-limited describes expression, while sex-linked describes genomic location. Confusing them erases a biologically important difference.

13. Interpreting family segregation alongside molecular observations

Pedigree evidence is strongest when it makes predictions that can be independently tested. A family with an apparently dominant trait may contain a sequence variant in an autosomal gene. If the variant is causal and penetrance is complete, affected individuals should carry it and unaffected individuals should not, with the usual qualifications about recombination, phenotype accuracy and new mutation. If a reliably unaffected older person carries it, the result challenges the full-penetrance model but does not by itself establish that the variant is irrelevant. If a well-characterized affected person lacks the variant, the same variant cannot explain that individual's phenotype under the model. The researcher should examine possibilities such as heterogeneity, phenocopy or inaccurate initial assignment.

Imagine a hypothetical family with six informative relatives. Four people express the phenotype and all four have variant V. Two unaffected people lack V. This is evidence of cosegregation but not proof that V changes gene function. A second nearby variant W could be inherited with V in the family because recombination has not separated them. If both variants have identical genotype patterns among the six relatives, the pedigree cannot choose between them. Additional independent evidence may include observing a recombinant relative, identifying other families with distinct variants in the same gene, or demonstrating a plausible functional consequence. The detailed logic of marker cosegregation belongs to 8.D.02; here the key is that transmission evidence is one part of causal inference.

An illustrative biochemical example clarifies the distinction. A recessive metabolic phenotype may result when enzyme activity falls below a physiological threshold. Imagine a teaching experiment in which individuals AA have activity near 100 arbitrary units, Aa individuals near 50 units, and aa individuals near 5 units. Suppose the phenotype appears when activity is below 20 units. The heterozygotes are phenotypically unaffected even though enzyme activity differs from that of AA. At the molecular level the dosage effect can be measurable, while the binary phenotype exhibits recessive inheritance. If an examination question asks whether the enzyme activity itself is recessive, do not assume that the classification for the disease phenotype applies to every biochemical variable.

The same framework can model a dominant effect. Imagine a protein complex whose activity requires two correctly functioning subunits, with equal production of normal and abnormal subunits and random assembly. In an illustrative Dd cell where the abnormal subunit disrupts any complex containing it, the probability that both subunits are normal is (1/2)^2 = 1/4. If a tissue needs more than half of ordinary activity, the heterozygote may express a phenotype despite producing one normal allele's protein. The assumptions of random assembly and equal expression are simplifications rather than general facts. The point is to connect a dominant-negative mechanism, introduced in 8.B.04, to a dominant pedigree without assuming that all dominant variants act in this manner.

Researchers must also separate the cause of the underlying disease from factors that alter its appearance. A modifier allele might change age of onset while the principal variant still determines susceptibility. If a modifier segregates independently, the pedigree of severe manifestations may differ from the pedigree of all manifestations. A phenotypic classification based on severity can thus make the same family appear to fit different models. Students should examine the operational definition of the affected state and distinguish the segregation of the causal allele from the probability of crossing a diagnostic threshold.

For a recessive phenotype, finding one disease-associated allele in an affected person does not automatically establish the required two-allele mechanism. The individual could have a second relevant allele not detected by the assay, a different cause, or a more complicated mechanism. Where two variants occur in the same gene, their phase matters: variants on opposite homologs can disrupt both copies, while two variants on the same homolog leave the other copy potentially functional. Family information can help infer phase by tracking which parent transmitted each variant. In a simplified example, one variant found in the father and another in the mother, both transmitted to the child, supports opposite-homolog phase. The relevant details are conceptual here, not a clinical interpretation of a real genetic test.

14. Identity by descent and consanguineous pedigrees

The genealogical relationship between two people is relevant to recessive inheritance because the same ancestral allele can be transmitted along two lines and reunite in a descendant. In this context, identity by descent means that the two alleles descend from the same ancestral copy. It is different from identity by state, in which alleles have the same sequence or the same marker state without necessarily sharing a recent ancestral origin. Two unrelated people can have identical sequence variants, while a pair of relatives can inherit different copies of a locus from their common ancestors. The distinction allows a pedigree to be used quantitatively rather than merely treated as a picture of relatives.

A child receives two autosomal copies at a locus. The inbreeding coefficient F is the probability that those two copies are identical by descent with respect to a specified set of founders, under the assumed segregation model. It is not the proportion of all genes that are harmful and is not a diagnosis. For two first cousins with a single shared pair of non-inbred grandparents and no other relationship paths, F for their child is 1/16. To derive the result, start with one shared grandparent. Each cousin is two transmissions away from this ancestor. For the child's two alleles to trace to this grandparent, both parental gametes must carry a copy descended from that grandparent. Along each side there are three transmissions from grandparent to the child's inherited allele, giving probability 1/8 for inheritance of a specified copy along either path. For a specified ancestral copy the probability that it reaches both sides is 1/64. Merely tracing to the individual is not the same as tracing to the same allele copy. There are two possible copies in that grandparent, each with probability 1/64 of contributing through both sides. Thus one shared grandparent contributes 1/32; the other contributes another 1/32. The total is 1/16. The paths are assumed independent and the founding copies distinct.

One can also derive this using the path rule. For an independent common-ancestor path of length n1 and n2 from each parent to a common ancestor, the contribution to F is (1/2)^(n1+n2+1)(1+FA), where FA is the inbreeding coefficient of the ancestor. First cousins are each two generations distant from a shared grandparent, so one contribution is (1/2)^5 = 1/32 when FA is zero. There are two shared grandparents. This rule counts independent paths; overlapping paths must not be counted as if they introduced entirely new ancestral copies. At NET level simple pedigrees are sufficient to understand the origin of the exponent. Two of the factors of one half represent the transmission from each parent to the child's inherited copy, and the common ancestor's two alternative copies are summed.

The coefficient F modifies the expected genotype probabilities of a random offspring under a simple population model. Let q be the frequency of a recessive allele a and p = 1 - q. With probability F the two copies are identical by descent. Conditional on that event, the probability that the shared ancestral copy is a is q. With probability 1 - F they are not identical by descent; in the elementary model their allelic states are independent draws, giving probability q squared of aa. Therefore P(aa) = Fq + (1-F)q squared, or q squared + Fpq. By a parallel argument P(AA) = p squared + Fpq and P(Aa) = 2pq(1-F). Adding the three terms gives one because the increase in the two homozygote classes equals the decrease in heterozygotes.

For example, take q = 0.01 in a hypothetical population and F = 1/16 for a given offspring class. Under the simple model P(aa) becomes 0.0001 + (1/16 × 0.99 × 0.01), or 0.00071875. Without the inbreeding term the corresponding probability is 0.0001. The absolute difference is 0.00061875, while the ratio of probabilities is approximately 7.19. The ratio appears large because the baseline is small. This is an illustrative single-locus calculation using a specified allele frequency, not an estimate of the total genetic risk of any real couple. The population model is also distinct from the known-founder example in section 4, where we knew a particular grandparent was a heterozygote and assumed all other founders were noncarriers. In the latter case the ancestral state is conditioned on evidence, whereas in the population calculation it is averaged over an assumed distribution.

An important examination distinction follows from these models. When two relatives are known to be carriers of the same fully penetrant recessive condition, their chance of an affected child is one quarter under ordinary segregation regardless of their genealogical relationship. Consanguinity changes the prior chance that the parents share a recessive allele, not the conditional segregation ratio of two established heterozygotes. If students calculate a higher recurrence fraction for an Aa by Aa cross simply because the parents are relatives, they have mixed the process of determining parental genotypes with the process of transmitting alleles from known parental genotypes.

In a founder pedigree, careful tracing may identify a specific ancestral variant rather than averaging over all alleles in the population. Suppose a common ancestor is aa, and each first cousin descends through one of that ancestor's children, whose other parent is AA. Both intervening parents must be Aa, and each cousin has probability one half of being a carrier if their unrelated other parent is AA. The cousins' child then has probability one half times one half times one quarter, or one sixteenth, of being affected. Compare this with the one sixty-fourth risk in the earlier heterozygous-grandparent model. The difference arises because the first transmission from the homozygous ancestor is certain rather than probabilistic. These examples show why a genealogy alone is insufficient for an exact family-specific risk: ancestral genotypes are essential.

15. Modelling penetrance, age and phenocopies quantitatively

The observed phenotype of a person is not always a deterministic label of the genotype. Penetrance models express this uncertainty in a way that can be combined with Mendelian segregation. Let G denote the event that a person has a particular susceptibility genotype, and D denote the event that the phenotype is observed at the relevant age or examination stage. Write f = P(D given G) and b = P(D given not-G). The first quantity is penetrance in the specified population and context. The second represents the probability of an observed similar phenotype in those without the genotype, which may reflect background causes. If g is the prior probability of the genotype, the total probability of the phenotype is fg + b(1-g). This expression follows by dividing the sample space into people with and without G and summing the two disjoint ways in which D can occur.

Suppose a heterozygous dominant parent and a noncarrier partner produce a child whose probability of receiving the allele is one half. In a teaching model let penetrance be 0.7 and the background phenotype probability be 0.1. The probability that the phenotype is observed is 0.7 × 0.5 + 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.4. If the child is affected, the probability of carrying the susceptibility allele is 0.35/0.4 = 7/8. Thus even an affected child is not guaranteed to have inherited D in this model. If the child is unaffected, the probability of carrying D is 0.3 × 0.5 divided by 0.6, or one quarter. Both affected and unaffected categories contain some uncertainty about genotype. This would be impossible in the strict full-penetrance, no-phenocopy model.

Now imagine that two unaffected relatives each have the same confirmed susceptibility genotype. The fact that neither has developed the phenotype does not necessarily mean penetrance is zero. If f = 0.7 and their expression outcomes are independent given the genotype and context, the probability that both are unaffected is 0.3 squared = 0.09. If they share modifiers, exposures or similar ages, independence of expression may not hold, even though their alleles were transmitted independently. The assumption of independent segregation concerns gametes; independence of phenotypic expression concerns biological factors after inheritance. They are mathematically separate. In simple examination questions, independence of phenotypes is often assumed, but it should not be generalized to every real family.

Age is a particularly informative context because some traits are not present from birth. Suppose penetrance for a dominant susceptibility genotype is 0.2 by age 20 and 0.8 by age 60 in a hypothetical dataset. A child of a known heterozygote and noncarrier has prior genotype probability one half. If unaffected at age 20, the posterior genotype probability is (0.5 × 0.8)/(0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5) = 4/9. If unaffected at age 60 under the same model, the posterior probability becomes (0.5 × 0.2)/(0.5 × 0.2 + 0.5) = 1/6. An unaffected young person provides relatively weak evidence against the genotype, whereas an unaffected older person provides stronger evidence. In an age-dependent condition the same unshaded symbol in a pedigree can represent different degrees of evidence depending on the person's age.

Be careful not to multiply cumulative penetrance values across ages as though an individual were a new independent observation at every birthday. If a carrier has cumulative penetrance 0.2 at age 20 and 0.8 at age 60, the probability of remaining unaffected by age 60 is 0.2. The probability of developing the phenotype between the two ages, conditional on being unaffected at age 20 and on carrying the genotype, is (0.8 - 0.2)/(1 - 0.2) = 0.75. This calculation assumes that the phenotype event is cumulative and does not reverse. It illustrates the distinction between a cumulative probability and a conditional probability over a later interval. More advanced survival analysis is outside the present node.

Variable expressivity can also be represented by multiple outcome categories. Suppose in an illustrative carrier group the probabilities of no visible manifestation, mild manifestation and severe manifestation are 0.2, 0.5 and 0.3. If the pedigree labels any visible manifestation as affected, its penetrance is 0.8. If it labels only severe manifestation as affected, the apparent penetrance becomes 0.3. Neither probability changes the allele transmission fraction. A model that compares pedigrees across studies must account for different scoring definitions. This is why a question asking which family has higher penetrance cannot be answered merely by comparing the severity of illness among affected relatives; one needs the number of carriers and the defined phenotype.

A penetrance estimate is a population or model quantity, not a property that can be read directly from one affected parent and one unaffected child. A family with four confirmed carriers and three affected members gives an observed expression proportion of 3/4. This proportion is an estimate and may be affected by sampling, relatedness and age distribution. The family does not establish that every carrier everywhere has exactly a 75 percent probability of expression. If the examination gives penetrance as 75 percent, use it as an assumed parameter; if it asks to estimate penetrance from an illustrative sample, describe the limitations of that estimate. The interpretation of incomplete penetrance is supported by the distinction between genotype and phenotype made in standard human genetics references, including MedlinePlus Genetics.

16. Distinguishing mitochondrial, maternal-effect and parent-of-origin patterns

A common difficulty in pedigree questions is that different biological mechanisms can produce asymmetric maternal and paternal effects. Maternal inheritance of mitochondrial DNA is a transmission mechanism: the genetic material itself normally arrives via the egg. A maternal effect from a nuclear gene is a mechanism of phenotypic determination in which the mother's nuclear genotype influences the offspring through products deposited in the egg or through other maternal biological processes. Genomic imprinting is a parent-of-origin difference in expression of a nuclear allele. These mechanisms can generate superficially similar families but make different predictions when the variant and the phenotype are followed through several generations.

Consider a hypothetical nuclear maternal-effect locus where an aa mother produces affected offspring regardless of their own genotype, and all AA or Aa mothers produce unaffected offspring. If an aa mother mates with an AA father, every child is Aa, and every child is affected in this generation by the stated maternal-effect rule. If one of the Aa daughters later mates with an AA male, none of her children is affected by this maternal effect because her own genotype is Aa. The nuclear allele can still segregate into half her offspring. Compare this with an mtDNA model where the same maternal lineage continues to transmit the organellar variant. The two processes can differ even when the first generation looks the same. The maternal-effect model here is a hypothetical teaching construction, not a claim about a particular human disease.

Imprinting can create another asymmetry. Suppose a nuclear locus has a phenotype-producing variant whose effect is expressed only when it is inherited from the father, while the maternal copy is silenced for the relevant function. A heterozygous father can transmit an expressed variant to half his children regardless of sex. A heterozygous mother can transmit the same sequence variant to half her children, but it is not expressed in this simplified model. A son carrying a maternally inherited silent variant can later transmit it as a paternally inherited variant, with expression in the next generation. This pattern differs from ordinary mitochondrial inheritance, because the nuclear allele can travel through both egg and sperm. Imprints are established and reset according to germline processes; the sequence's parent-of-origin expression state need not remain the same forever.

A sex-limited nuclear phenotype provides yet another example. A variant may be inherited by both sexes but produce an observable trait only in one sex. If the family records only affected individuals, the phenotype may seem to travel along paternal or maternal lines depending on which sex expresses it. Molecular evidence can reveal unaffected carriers of the other sex. Students should distinguish the sex of the parent transmitting an allele from the sex of the child expressing the allele. Mitochondrial inheritance is principally about the transmitting parent, whereas sex-limited expression is principally about the offspring's physiological context.

For mitochondrial pedigrees, the best conceptual model often treats the variant burden as a continuous or multi-category variable rather than as the binary genotype AA or aa. Suppose the measured fraction of variant mtDNA in the same type of tissue from a hypothetical mother and three children is 40%, 15%, 45% and 85%, respectively. If the illustrative threshold is 60% in that tissue, only the third child exceeds it. All three children have some variant mtDNA in the sample, even though two are below the assumed functional threshold. The observations are consistent with maternal transmission accompanied by changes in variant proportions. They do not establish that the maternal tissue measurement equals the burden of each oocyte. They also do not establish the variant level in other tissues, which could differ through segregation and selection.

A basic sampling model helps explain how variability arises. If a small effective population of organellar genome copies is sampled from a maternal pool with variant fraction h, and each draw is assumed independent with that same probability, the number of variant copies in a sample of N has binomial mean Nh and variance Nh(1-h). The sampled proportion has variance h(1-h)/N. In a hypothetical N = 20 and h = 0.4 example, the expected number of variant copies is eight, while the standard deviation of the sampled proportion is the square root of 0.24/20, approximately 0.110. This is an intuition-building sampling model rather than an estimate of an actual human mitochondrial bottleneck size. Actual transmission can involve nonrandom selection and developmental changes, and the effective number of segregating units may not equal the literal number of DNA molecules. The point for pedigree interpretation is that siblings need not inherit identical proportions even from the same mother.

A hypothetical study comparing a mtDNA marker and a nuclear marker across a family illustrates the logic of distinguishing mechanisms. If every child of a variant-carrying mother inherits a particular mtDNA sequence while none of the children of variant-carrying fathers does, the data support ordinary maternal transmission of that marker. But if a nuclear variant cosegregates with disease and affected fathers transmit it to sons, a nuclear model is favored for the disease phenotype. The mtDNA marker could be inherited maternally without causing the trait. Genetic transmission of a marker is not equivalent to proof that it causes the observed phenotype. A proper explanation must connect marker location, segregation, phenotype and independent evidence about function.

17. Bayes reasoning with hypothetical marker and test observations

The probability of a carrier genotype can be updated using any observation for which conditional probabilities are specified. The earlier examples used offspring phenotypes. A marker observation or an educational test result can be treated in the same mathematical way. Define sensitivity s as the probability of a positive result when the target genotype is present, and specificity t as the probability of a negative result when the genotype is absent. These definitions describe a simplified binary model, not the interpretation of an actual clinical genetic test. A molecular assay may detect particular variants rather than every possible cause of a condition, and its performance can depend on which variants and samples are considered.

Consider an unaffected sibling of an affected person in a fully penetrant autosomal recessive family with two confirmed carrier parents. The sibling's prior carrier probability is two thirds. Suppose a hypothetical carrier assay has sensitivity 0.9 and specificity 1 for the specified carrier state. A negative observation has probability 0.1 under the carrier hypothesis and one under the noncarrier hypothesis. The posterior carrier probability is (2/3 × 0.1)/[(2/3 × 0.1) + (1/3 × 1)] = 1/6. If the partner is a confirmed carrier, the modeled recurrence probability becomes one sixth times one quarter, or one twenty-fourth. A negative result reduces but does not eliminate the probability of the target genotype. The conclusion depends on the deliberately supplied assay characteristics; it must not be used to infer the sensitivity or residual risk of any actual assay.

Now consider a rare genotype with prior probability 1/100 and a hypothetical test with sensitivity 0.95 and specificity 0.95. Out of 10,000 modeled people, the expected number with the genotype is 100, of whom 95 test positive. Of the 9,900 without it, 495 test positive because the false-positive probability is 0.05. The probability of carrying the genotype among positive results is 95/(95+495), approximately 0.161. Although both stated test characteristics are high, the posterior probability after a positive result is relatively low because the prior state is uncommon. This example introduces the base-rate effect. It is deliberately unrelated to a specific genetic test, and it illustrates how Bayes' rule prevents interpreting P(positive given genotype) as P(genotype given positive).

The same calculation can use odds. Prior odds for H are P(H)/P(not-H). A likelihood ratio for evidence E is P(E given H)/P(E given not-H). Posterior odds equal prior odds multiplied by the likelihood ratio. In the rare-genotype example, prior odds are 1:99. The positive likelihood ratio is 0.95/0.05 = 19, so posterior odds are 19:99. Converting these odds to probability gives 19/118, the same result as the frequency calculation. This form is particularly useful for repeated evidence when the observations are independent conditional on each hypothesis. It also prepares the distinction between likelihood ratios and posterior probabilities in human linkage analysis.

If two different hypothetical tests produce evidence E1 and E2, multiplying their separate likelihood ratios is valid only when the evidence is conditionally independent under both hypotheses. Two assays that fail to detect the same class of variants may have correlated negative results. A family report and a molecular observation may also depend on the same ascertainment or classification process. When evidence is dependent, the relevant ratio is P(E1 and E2 given H)/P(E1 and E2 given not-H). One can factor this using conditional probabilities, but cannot simply substitute the two marginal likelihoods. This general lesson applies to observations about relatives with shared hidden genotype states as well.

For an exam example of independent evidence, suppose an individual has prior carrier probability one half and two specified independent observations each have likelihood ratio one third in favor of the carrier hypothesis. The prior odds are one. After both observations the posterior odds are one ninth, which corresponds to carrier probability one tenth. If the individual's partner is a confirmed autosomal recessive carrier, the modeled risk of an affected child is one fortieth. Notice the conversion from odds to probability before multiplying by the segregation fraction. Multiplying the posterior odds directly by one quarter would produce a quantity without the intended probability interpretation.

A useful approach to any Bayes problem is to write two columns for carrier and noncarrier states, a row for the prior, a row for each likelihood, a row for the product of applicable weights, and a final row dividing each weight by their total. The products do not need to sum to one before normalization. They represent joint probabilities of the respective hypotheses and observations. If the answer is a recurrence probability, carry the normalized genotype probability into the transmission calculation only at the end. This organization keeps the distinction between the probability of a hidden state and the probability of future offspring explicit.

18. From family observations to a biological explanation

Garrod's 1902 work on alkaptonuria offers an instructive historical example of how pedigree evidence can direct a biochemical question. The unusual feature was that urine containing accumulated homogentisic acid darkened under suitable conditions. Such a distinctive chemical phenotype could be observed in family members even before investigators understood its molecular origin. Its occurrence among siblings, including children of related parents, supported an inherited recessive interpretation in the context of the rediscovery of Mendelian genetics. Garrod subsequently developed the idea that inherited differences could alter steps of normal metabolism. The original report is available as a historical reprint, and modern accounts distinguish the early family-based inference from later biochemical and molecular identification.

A recessive inheritance model makes several predictions for this observation. If both parents carry a relevant allele without showing the characteristic phenotype, each child has a chance of receiving both alleles. Affected children can occur in either sex because an autosomal locus does not require a particular sex chromosome. Familial recurrence among siblings and the occurrence in families with related parents are consistent with this model. However, such observations do not reveal which enzyme is affected. A purely environmental explanation or another genetic model would require its own predictions. The historical contribution was to connect the recurring chemical feature with a pattern of inheritance and to motivate a biological mechanism, rather than to identify the DNA sequence directly.

The biochemical interpretation was later connected to deficient homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase activity in the pathway that degrades tyrosine. Homogentisic acid is a substrate in that pathway. Reduced activity at this step leads to accumulation of the compound. A 1996 molecular study by Fernández-Cañón and colleagues reported mapping of the relevant gene, missense variants segregating with disease, and functional evidence of loss of activity for at least one variant. This evidence connects three distinct observations: a family transmission pattern, a measurable biochemical abnormality and an alteration affecting the enzyme. No individual observation substitutes for the other two. The family study predicts how the allele is inherited; the biochemical study identifies the disturbed reaction; the molecular study connects a particular DNA change with loss of function.

This historical sequence illustrates why a pedigree is not merely an exercise in identifying symbols. When an apparently recessive phenotype involves a metabolic block, a reasonable mechanistic hypothesis is that heterozygotes retain enough enzyme activity for ordinary function, whereas individuals carrying two sufficiently disruptive alleles do not. Testing that hypothesis requires measuring activity or relevant biochemical consequences and comparing genotypes, with appropriate controls. It also requires avoiding the assumption that every variant in the gene completely eliminates activity. Some variants retain residual activity, and the phenotype can depend on additional factors. The pedigree itself supplies no direct estimate of an enzyme's catalytic rate.

Consider how a student might design a conceptual comparison using an illustrative family with known genotypes and enzyme measurements. The null expectation under no association between genotype and activity is that the activity distribution is similar across the genotype groups. A functional loss model predicts reduced activity in the biallelic group. One should compare measurements made in comparable sample types and conditions, account for biological variation, and distinguish a difference in mean activity from complete separation of the groups. If the carriers show intermediate activity but lack the overt phenotype, the data can support a threshold interpretation. Such a teaching design does not require students to learn a specialist clinical assay or laboratory protocol. It teaches how genotype, biochemical evidence and phenotype constrain a causal model.

A second source of evidence arises from independent families with different variants in the same gene. If unrelated families show recessive transmission, variants that disrupt the same molecular function, and the same characteristic biochemical defect, the agreement strengthens the proposed relationship. Yet even this evidence must be interpreted at the correct level. It supports the causal role of the gene and particular well-supported variants; it does not establish that every sequence difference at the locus is pathogenic. Human genes commonly have multiple alleles, many of which do not cause a defined disease. The earlier Unit 8 work on allelic series and complementation helps explain why variant classification is not identical to identifying the gene.

Pedigree records themselves improved through the development of standardized nomenclature. A consistent symbol key allows independent readers to reproduce the relationships used in an analysis. Bennett and colleagues' published recommendations illustrate the importance of accurately recording information and distinguishing genotype-related annotations from phenotypic markings. The scientific strength of a pedigree depends on clear communication: a diagram that confuses unrecorded status with a confirmed unaffected observation can lead to an erroneous model rejection. Standardization is therefore an evidence-quality issue, not a decorative convention.

19. Comparing inheritance hypotheses using complete observations

A comprehensive pedigree solution needs to account for every relationship, not merely the most visually distinctive part of the family. Suppose a hypothetical affected woman has an unaffected partner, one affected son and one unaffected daughter. Assume complete penetrance, no new mutation and clearly defined phenotypes. Under an autosomal dominant model she could be Dd and the partner dd. Conditional on the children's observed sexes, the phenotype likelihood is one quarter. Under an X-linked dominant model she could be XdXn and her partner XnY, with the same likelihood of one quarter. Under an autosomal recessive model she could be aa and the partner Aa, again giving likelihood one quarter. Under a recessive X-linked model she would be XrXr and the partner XRY; all sons would be affected and all daughters unaffected carriers, giving likelihood one. Under a simplified mitochondrial model with uniform transmission and complete expression, an unaffected daughter is inconsistent with the mother's phenotype being caused by the transmitted variant. If heteroplasmy or incomplete expression is allowed, the model could remain plausible. The ranking of models depends on explicitly stated genotype possibilities and assumptions.

The recessive X-linked model in this example has a larger likelihood conditional on its required parental states, but those states may have different prior probabilities. If the variant is extremely rare, an affected homozygous female might be much less probable than a heterozygous autosomal dominant female. A full comparison would average over possible founder genotypes with appropriate prior weights and account for why the family was studied. At examination level, some questions intentionally ask only which model is compatible, or which model is most consistent under rare-allele assumptions. Read the question carefully. A model that fits the observed children is not necessarily the most probable explanation in the population.

Consider a family in which an affected father with an unaffected mother has two daughters who are affected and two sons who are unaffected. The simple X-linked dominant model predicts all four phenotype assignments conditional on the given sexes. An autosomal dominant heterozygote model has probability 1/16 for this specified assignment. If a fifth child is a son and is affected, the strict X-linked dominant model with a noncarrier mother is now contradicted, while the autosomal dominant model still assigns a positive likelihood. One observation can therefore be qualitatively more informative than several observations agreeing with a model. The affected son's existence does not prove a paternal X-linked transmission route; instead it shows that this particular simple X-linked dominant family model is insufficient.

A model can be made more flexible by allowing phenocopies or incomplete penetrance, but those additions must be biologically justified and quantitatively represented rather than invoked only after a contradiction. Suppose the previous X-linked dominant model allowed each child without the variant a background affected probability of 0.02 and variant carriers a penetrance of 0.95. The likelihood of two affected daughters, two unaffected sons and one affected son, given observed sexes and independent expression conditional on genotype, would be 0.95 squared × 0.98 squared × 0.02. This is about 0.0173. The autosomal dominant fully penetrant heterozygote model would assign 1/32 or 0.03125. Neither model is impossible once the first is modified, and the comparison changes. Introducing more adjustable parameters makes a model capable of accommodating more patterns, but does not automatically make it better supported.

A useful qualitative table lists observations as required, possible, or incompatible under a defined model. In fully penetrant autosomal dominant inheritance with a heterozygous parent and unaffected partner, affected children of either sex are possible but not required. In fully penetrant paternal X-linked dominance with a noncarrier mother, affected daughters are required and affected sons are incompatible. In the ordinary Y-linked model, sons inherit the paternal Y and daughters do not. In the simple mtDNA model, paternal transmission is not expected, while maternal transmission can affect both sexes. The practical advantage of the categories is that they prevent confusing an expected tendency with a strict mechanistic requirement.

To choose the next informative observation in a study, identify where candidate models predict different outcomes. For example, a family containing only affected mothers and their children may be insufficient to distinguish autosomal dominant from X-linked dominant inheritance because heterozygous maternal transmission can appear identical. A family branch headed by an affected father with multiple daughters and sons is especially informative for that comparison. If an unaffected carrier is suspected because of reduced penetrance, genotype information can help separate variant transmission from expression. If a maternally clustered pattern suggests mtDNA but affected fathers are not represented, molecular localization may provide evidence unavailable from family structure alone. The aim is not to collect the largest number of shaded symbols, but to acquire observations that distinguish the predictions of competing explanations.

Some patterns are simply not identifiable from a small family. An isolated affected child may be compatible with autosomal recessive inheritance, a de novo dominant variant, mitochondrial expression with an unaffected mother, or a non-genetic cause. A single affected son can also fit an X-linked recessive model. Without more observations, assigning a unique inheritance type would overstate the evidence. A scientifically sound answer can say that several models remain possible and then identify which additional relationships, genotypes or biochemical observations would discriminate among them. Part C questions often reward this reasoning more than memorization of a textbook pedigree shape.

The next node will formalize an analogous distinction in the setting of linkage: given marker and disease segregation observations, compare their likelihood under different recombination fractions. Here the relevant ideas are already established. One must specify the hidden genotype states, the process producing observable data, the assumptions of conditional independence, and the precise observation whose probability is being evaluated. A likelihood is evidence relative to a model. It becomes a posterior probability only after prior probabilities and an exhaustive set of hypotheses have been considered.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Dominant means common

Dominance describes the effect of genotype on a specified phenotype, not allele frequency. A rare dominant allele is entirely possible; distinguish frequency from expression.

2. All unaffected people lack the variant

Recessive carriers and nonpenetrant dominant carriers may be unaffected. Assign possible genotypes before eliminating a transmission route.

3. One affected child raises the known Aa × Aa risk

With both carrier states fixed, the next segregation risk remains 1/4. An observation changes uncertainty about unknown parental genotypes, not the fixed segregation law.

4. Every family follows the expected ratio exactly

Small sibships sample a probability distribution. Use the binomial model rather than demand an exact 3:1 or 1:1 count.

5. Affected father and affected son prove autosomal inheritance

They can coexist in X-linked inheritance through independent maternal transmission to the son. Distinguish affected status from confirmed direct allele transmission.

6. All male-only pedigrees are Y-linked

X-linked recessive and sex-limited autosomal models can resemble Y linkage. Look for transmission through daughters and for informative sons.

7. All daughters of an X-linked recessive affected father are affected

They receive his variant X, but expression depends on their other X and context. Track genotype before phenotype.

8. Half the sons affected means half all offspring affected

For an unaffected father and carrier mother, affected-son probability among all children is 1/4 with equal sex probabilities. Check whether the denominator is sons or all children.

9. Mitochondrial disease always means maternal inheritance

Nuclear genes also encode mitochondrial proteins. Identify the locus and transmission evidence.

10. Maternal transmission implies uniform phenotype

Heteroplasmy and thresholds can change expression. Keep variant inheritance separate from phenotype risk.

11. Consanguinity creates the mutant allele

It changes identity-by-descent probabilities. Do not change a confirmed Aa × Aa cross ratio simply because the parents are related.

12. Carrier probability among unaffected Aa × Aa children is 1/2

Conditioning on unaffected makes it 2/3. Remove the affected genotype and renormalize.

13. Penetrance equals expressivity

Penetrance measures occurrence; expressivity measures manifestations. Specify phenotype threshold and relevant age.

14. A likelihood is already a posterior

Bayes requires prior weights and normalization. Do not interpret a likelihood ratio of 32 as a posterior of 32/33 without assumptions.

15. Unknown phenotype is unaffected

Missing data are not a negative observation. Do not reject a model using unrecorded status.

16. A de novo variant makes future recurrence impossible

Parental germline mosaicism can permit recurrence. Recognize that somatic observations do not determine all germline genotypes.

17. Cosegregation proves a variant is causal

Linked variants can travel together. Combine family data with independent functional or recombination evidence.

18. Pedigree counts are unbiased population frequencies

Affected-proband recruitment changes the observation distribution. Distinguish an ascertained sample from an unselected family.

Part B: concept and mechanism

Part B 1. Which conventional pedigree symbol identifies an affected male?

  1. Filled square
  2. Open square
  3. Filled circle
  4. Double horizontal line
Answer and explanation

A. Filled square — The square identifies the conventional male symbol and filling identifies the recorded phenotype. An open square lacks the recorded phenotype; the circle conventionally identifies a female; the double line indicates consanguinity.

Part B 2. What identifies the proband?

  1. Oldest affected person
  2. Person through whom a family comes to attention
  3. Person with the first mutation
  4. Any obligate carrier
Answer and explanation

B. Person through whom a family comes to attention — The proband is the index person for ascertainment. This does not establish chronological onset, the ancestral origin of a variant, or carrier status.

Part B 3. In Aa × Aa with complete recessive penetrance, what fraction of all conceptions is Aa?

  1. 1/4
  2. 2/3
  3. 1/2
  4. 3/4
Answer and explanation

C. 1/2 — The two heterozygous gamete combinations contribute 2/4. The 2/3 value refers to conditioning on an unaffected offspring; 1/4 refers to either homozygote.

Part B 4. Which pedigree observation makes both unaffected parents obligate carriers in the ideal autosomal recessive model?

  1. An unaffected son
  2. A daughter who is Aa by chance alone
  3. One parent has an affected cousin
  4. An affected aa child
Answer and explanation

D. An affected aa child — The aa child received a from each parent; unaffected parents cannot be aa under full penetrance. The other observations alone do not establish both parental genotypes.

Part B 5. A typical XY male receives his non-pseudoautosomal X from whom?

  1. Mother
  2. Father
  3. Either parent equally
  4. Paternal grandfather directly
Answer and explanation

A. Mother — An egg supplies the X, while the sperm supplies Y to a typical XY offspring. Paternal X transmission to a typical son is excluded in this model.

Part B 6. A fully penetrant X-linked dominant affected father and a noncarrier mother have what outcome among daughters?

  1. None affected
  2. All affected
  3. One quarter affected
  4. Only the eldest affected
Answer and explanation

B. All affected — Every daughter receives the father's variant X. Sons receive his Y; birth order is irrelevant.

Part B 7. Hemizygosity at an ordinary X locus in an XY person means:

  1. Both alleles identical
  2. Two different alleles
  3. Only one allele copy
  4. No copy of the gene
Answer and explanation

C. Only one allele copy — The locus has a single X-linked copy in this chromosome complement. Homozygosity and heterozygosity require two copies.

Part B 8. What is the usual human mtDNA transmission route?

  1. Paternal to sons only
  2. Paternal to daughters only
  3. Autosomal segregation
  4. Egg-derived, to offspring of either sex
Answer and explanation

D. Egg-derived, to offspring of either sex — Mitochondrial DNA ordinarily follows egg cytoplasm. This route does not imply that all recipients manifest a mitochondrial phenotype.

Part B 9. Penetrance refers to:

  1. Probability of phenotype conditional on genotype and context
  2. Severity among affected people only
  3. Allele frequency among all people
  4. Recombination fraction
Answer and explanation

A. Probability of phenotype conditional on genotype and context — Penetrance concerns whether a specified phenotype occurs among genotype carriers. Severity is expressivity; population frequency and recombination are separate quantities.

Part B 10. Variable expressivity describes:

  1. Different gene locations
  2. Variation in manifestations among individuals with a relevant genotype
  3. Failure of chromosome segregation
  4. The proportion of carriers who are unaffected exclusively
Answer and explanation

B. Variation in manifestations among individuals with a relevant genotype — Expressivity concerns degree or form of phenotype. Nonpenetrance concerns whether it appears at all under the scoring definition.

Part B 11. Consanguinity chiefly increases what for rare recessive inheritance?

  1. The segregation ratio in every Aa × Aa cross
  2. The mutation rate of every gene
  3. The chance of shared ancestral alleles
  4. Paternal inheritance of mtDNA
Answer and explanation

C. The chance of shared ancestral alleles — Related parents can share alleles by descent. Once two carrier genotypes are established, their ordinary one-quarter recessive risk does not change merely because of kinship.

Part B 12. A recessive aa affected person mates with AA. What genotype is expected in all offspring?

  1. AA
  2. aa
  3. DD
  4. Aa
Answer and explanation

D. Aa — The aa parent contributes a and the AA parent contributes A. The resulting Aa children are carriers under the usual full-penetrance recessive model.

Part B 13. Which statement about dominance is correct?

  1. It describes the phenotype relationship of allele combinations
  2. It guarantees high population frequency
  3. It guarantees more severe disease
  4. It requires homozygosity
Answer and explanation

A. It describes the phenotype relationship of allele combinations — Dominance is defined relative to a specified phenotype. Neither allele frequency nor clinical severity follows from dominance alone.

Part B 14. Which phenomenon can make an inherited dominant allele appear to skip a generation?

  1. Independent assortment alone
  2. Incomplete penetrance
  3. Hemizygosity at all autosomal loci
  4. Obligate maternal mtDNA transmission
Answer and explanation

B. Incomplete penetrance — A carrier may lack the recorded phenotype and still transmit the dominant allele. The other processes do not by themselves explain this apparent skip.

Part B 15. Which term describes a new variant not inherited as an existing allele from either parent?

  1. Phenocopy
  2. Expressivity
  3. De novo variant
  4. Consanguinity
Answer and explanation

C. De novo variant — A de novo variant originates in a gamete or during development. A phenocopy concerns similar phenotype from another cause, not a newly arisen sequence variant.

Part B 16. What does a double partner line commonly signify?

  1. Two children
  2. An affected pair
  3. Identical twin status
  4. Consanguinity
Answer and explanation

D. Consanguinity — A double relationship line marks a biological relationship between partners. It is not itself a phenotype or genotype designation.

Part B 17. In the elementary non-pseudoautosomal Y-linked model, who receives an affected father's Y allele?

  1. All sons
  2. All daughters
  3. Half his daughters
  4. Half all offspring of either sex
Answer and explanation

A. All sons — A typical father supplies his Y to sons. Daughters receive his X; the statement concerns allele transmission, not penetrance.

Part B 18. Which finding alone cannot establish that a condition is due to mtDNA?

  1. Molecular localization of a causal variant to mtDNA
  2. The affected organ is mitochondrion-dependent
  3. Evidence of egg-derived transmission of the variant
  4. Direct sequence evidence from mtDNA
Answer and explanation

B. The affected organ is mitochondrion-dependent — Mitochondrial function also depends on nuclear genes. Organellar dysfunction alone does not identify the genomic location.

Part B 19. Which genotype can produce the fully penetrant recessive phenotype in a compound heterozygote?

  1. Two functional alleles
  2. Two variants on the same chromosome and a fully functional other copy
  3. Two disease-associated alleles in trans at the same gene
  4. One functional autosomal allele only in every condition
Answer and explanation

C. Two disease-associated alleles in trans at the same gene — Two disruptive alleles on opposite homologs can cause a recessive phenotype. Variants on the same homolog may leave the other copy functional.

Part B 20. What is a phenocopy?

  1. A duplicate chromosome
  2. An obligate heterozygote
  3. A recombinant haplotype
  4. A similar phenotype from a different, often environmental, cause
Answer and explanation

D. A similar phenotype from a different, often environmental, cause — A phenocopy can make a family member resemble an inherited phenotype without carrying its causal allele.

Part B 21. Which quantity is a likelihood of observation E under hypothesis H?

  1. P(E given H)
  2. P(H given E)
  3. P(H) alone
  4. P(E and not-H) only
Answer and explanation

A. P(E given H) — Likelihood evaluates observed data under a specified model. Posterior probability reverses the conditioning and requires prior information.

Part B 22. Under a simple Aa × Aa model, is an unaffected child necessarily AA?

  1. Yes, always
  2. No, the child may be Aa
  3. Yes, if a sibling is aa
  4. Yes, if the parents are unrelated
Answer and explanation

B. No, the child may be Aa — Both AA and Aa are unaffected under full recessive penetrance. Neither sibling status nor unrelatedness eliminates heterozygosity.

Part B 23. What is the definition of heteroplasmy?

  1. Two autosomal alleles in one nucleus
  2. Presence of X and Y
  3. A mixture of mitochondrial genome types
  4. A pedigree with affected siblings only
Answer and explanation

C. A mixture of mitochondrial genome types — Heteroplasmy is coexistence of different mitochondrial genome states in a sample or cell. It is not ordinary diploid heterozygosity.

Part B 24. What is an obligate carrier in a stated pedigree model?

  1. Anyone who is unaffected
  2. Anyone with a probability of 1/2
  3. Any member of an affected family
  4. An individual whose carrier genotype is required by the evidence and assumptions
Answer and explanation

D. An individual whose carrier genotype is required by the evidence and assumptions — Obligate carrier status follows from constraints on transmission. A probabilistic carrier assignment or family membership is not sufficient.

Part C: analytical and quantitative reasoning

Part C 1. An unaffected sister of an aa individual has two confirmed Aa parents. She mates with a confirmed Aa person. What is the probability of an affected child?

  1. 1/6
  2. 1/4
  3. 2/3
  4. 1/12
Answer and explanation

A. 1/6 — Conditioning on unaffected removes aa, giving P(Aa)=2/3. Multiply by the conditional Aa × Aa risk 1/4. The result is 1/6, not the carrier probability or the known-carrier cross risk.

Part C 2. The woman in the previous independently specified scenario has two unaffected children with her confirmed Aa partner. What is her posterior carrier probability?

  1. 2/3
  2. 9/17
  3. 9/16
  4. 1/3
Answer and explanation

B. 9/17 — Weights: (2/3)(9/16)=3/8 and (1/3)(1)=1/3. Normalizing gives (3/8)/(17/24)=9/17. The likelihood 9/16 is not the posterior.

Part C 3. A woman has prior X-linked recessive carrier probability 1/2. Her two sons are unaffected, with full penetrance. What is the risk for her next son?

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/5
  3. 1/10
  4. 1/20
Answer and explanation

C. 1/10 — Two unaffected sons have likelihood 1/4 if carrier and 1 otherwise. Posterior carrier probability is 1/5; transmission to a son is 1/2. The 1/20 option conditions on an unspecified-sex next child, not a son.

Part C 4. A Dd parent mates with dd; penetrance in Dd is 0.8, dd has no background phenotype. What is P(Dd given unaffected)?

  1. 1/2
  2. 1/5
  3. 2/5
  4. 1/6
Answer and explanation

D. 1/6 — P(Dd and unaffected)=0.5×0.2=0.1; total unaffected probability is 0.6. The conditional result is 1/6.

Part C 5. Two known Aa parents have three children. Probability of at least one affected child?

  1. 37/64
  2. 27/64
  3. 1/4
  4. 3/4
Answer and explanation

A. 37/64 — The complement is all three unaffected, (3/4)^3=27/64. Subtract from one to obtain 37/64.

Part C 6. For the same Aa × Aa model, probability of exactly one affected among three children?

  1. 1/64
  2. 27/64
  3. 37/64
  4. 9/64
Answer and explanation

B. 27/64 — There are three positions for the affected child: 3×(1/4)×(3/4)^2=27/64. The 37/64 includes families with two or three affected children.

Part C 7. An affected father and noncarrier mother have three affected daughters and two unaffected sons. Relative likelihood of a complete-penetrance X-linked dominant model to a heterozygous autosomal dominant model, conditional on the observed sexes?

  1. 1
  2. 5
  3. 32
  4. 1/32
Answer and explanation

C. 32 — X-linked dominant assigns probability one to the particular pattern. Autosomal dominant assigns (1/2)^5=1/32. The ratio is 32, not a posterior probability.

Part C 8. In an X-linked recessive XrY × XRXr cross, an unaffected daughter later mates with an unaffected XRY male. Probability her next child is an affected son, assuming equal sex probabilities?

  1. 0
  2. 1/2
  3. 1/8
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

D. 1/4 — The daughter must be XRXr because her father contributes Xr. Half her sons inherit Xr; half of all children are sons. Joint risk is 1/4.

Part C 9. Two unrelated unaffected people have independent autosomal recessive carrier probabilities 1/20 and 1/30. Risk of an affected child?

  1. 1/2400
  2. 1/600
  3. 1/1200
  4. 1/50
Answer and explanation

A. 1/2400 — Both must be carriers, probability 1/600. Conditional risk is 1/4, so the product is 1/2400.

Part C 10. Two partners each independently have prior carrier probability 1/2. For two children, probability both are affected by the same full-penetrance recessive condition?

  1. 1/256
  2. 1/64
  3. 1/16
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

B. 1/64 — Both parents must be carriers (1/4), then both offspring aa (1/16). Multiply to obtain 1/64. The 1/256 option wrongly squares a marginal risk over shared parental states.

Part C 11. A child of Dd × dd has carrier penetrance 0.7 and noncarrier background phenotype probability 0.1. P(Dd given affected)?

  1. 0.4
  2. 0.7
  3. 7/8
  4. 1/2
Answer and explanation

C. 7/8 — The total affected probability is 0.35+0.05=0.4. The carrier contribution is 0.35, so the posterior is 7/8.

Part C 12. Two first cousins share a single non-inbred grandparent pair, with no other relationship paths. What is F for their offspring?

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/8
  3. 1/32
  4. 1/16
Answer and explanation

D. 1/16 — Each common grandparent contributes (1/2)^(2+2+1)=1/32. Summing the two independent contributions gives 1/16.

Part C 13. Given p=0.99, q=0.01 and F=1/16, what is modeled P(aa)?

  1. 0.00071875
  2. 0.0001
  3. 0.0099
  4. 0.0625
Answer and explanation

A. 0.00071875 — The probability is q²+Fpq=0.0001+0.00061875. F is an identity-by-descent probability, not the recessive genotype frequency.

Part C 14. An unaffected sibling of an aa individual has confirmed carrier parents. A hypothetical genotype assay has sensitivity 0.9 and specificity 1. Following a negative result, posterior carrier probability?

  1. 1/10
  2. 1/6
  3. 2/3
  4. 1/3
Answer and explanation

B. 1/6 — Prior carrier odds 2:1 multiply by negative likelihood ratio 0.1. Posterior odds 1:5 correspond to probability 1/6.

Part C 15. An affected mother XdXn has children with XnY. An illustrative viability model removes all XdY conceptions before birth. What fraction of survivors is affected?

  1. 1/2
  2. 2/3
  3. 1/3
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

C. 1/3 — Three equally weighted categories survive: XdXn, XnXn, XnY. One is affected. The conception probability differs from the survivor probability.

Part C 16. Known Aa parents have two children and the family is selected only if at least one is affected. P(both affected given selection)?

  1. 1/16
  2. 1/4
  3. 1/2
  4. 1/7
Answer and explanation

D. 1/7 — P(both)=1/16; P(at least one)=7/16. Their ratio is 1/7. The selected sample no longer has the unconditional count distribution.

Part C 17. A hypothetical dominant allele has penetrance 0.2 at age 20 and 0.8 at age 60. For confirmed carriers unaffected at 20, probability of onset by 60?

  1. 3/4
  2. 3/5
  3. 4/5
  4. 1/5
Answer and explanation

A. 3/4 — Assuming cumulative onset, the interval event has probability (0.8−0.2)/(1−0.2)=0.75. The cumulative probability 0.8 is not the conditional interval probability.

Part C 18. A heterozygous dominant founder and dd partner have four children. Probability of exactly two affected with complete penetrance?

  1. 1/16
  2. 3/8
  3. 1/2
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

B. 3/8 — Choose two affected positions in six ways, each probability 1/16. The result is 6/16=3/8.

Part C 19. A mtDNA heteroplasmy sample has hypothetical h=0.4 and N=20 independent segregating units. Approximate SD of sampled variant fraction?

  1. 0.24
  2. 0.4
  3. 0.110
  4. 0.012
Answer and explanation

C. 0.110 — Variance of fraction is h(1−h)/N=0.012. Its square root is approximately 0.1095. This is a sampling model, not a human bottleneck estimate.

Part C 20. A recessive aa father and an unaffected partner with carrier probability 1/50 have a child. If the child is unaffected, what is its probability of being Aa?

  1. 1/50
  2. 1/100
  3. 1/2
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

D. 1 — The father always transmits a; under full penetrance the unaffected child must receive A from the mother. Her uncertainty affects the disease risk but not the conditional carrier state.

Part C 21. An affected father, unaffected mother, all four daughters affected and all three sons unaffected. Which further observation directly contradicts the strict X-linked dominant model with that father and noncarrier mother?

  1. An additional unaffected daughter
  2. An additional affected daughter
  3. An additional unaffected son
  4. An affected paternal grandmother
Answer and explanation

A. An additional unaffected daughter — The father must transmit the variant X to every daughter; under full penetrance an unaffected daughter contradicts the model. The other observations can be consistent.

Part C 22. Two models predict exactly the same outcome probabilities for a specified family structure. What follows?

  1. The dominant model must be selected
  2. Repeating only that same type of observation cannot discriminate them
  3. Both are false
  4. Both have posterior probability one
Answer and explanation

B. Repeating only that same type of observation cannot discriminate them — Identical likelihood predictions imply no evidence favoring one over the other from those data. Other family structures or molecular localization may distinguish them.

Part C 23. An unaffected woman has carrier prior 1/2, her independent partner 1/4. One child is unaffected. Posterior probability both carry the recessive allele?

  1. 1/8
  2. 3/32
  3. 3/31
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

C. 3/31 — Joint carrier prior 1/8, likelihood 3/4. Alternative prior 7/8, likelihood one. Normalize 3/32 against 28/32 to obtain 3/31.

Part C 24. A rare genotype has prior probability 1/100. A hypothetical assay has sensitivity and specificity each 0.95. P(genotype given positive)?

  1. 0.95
  2. 0.05
  3. 0.5
  4. 19/118
Answer and explanation

D. 19/118 — Positive likelihood ratio is 19; prior odds are 1:99. Posterior odds 19:99 give probability 19/118. Sensitivity alone cannot answer the question.

Part C 25. A woman has prior carrier probability 1/2. Two conditionally independent observations each have likelihood ratio 1/3 favoring carrier over noncarrier. Partner is a confirmed autosomal recessive carrier. Risk of affected child?

  1. 1/40
  2. 1/36
  3. 1/10
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

A. 1/40 — Prior odds 1, posterior odds 1/9; probability 1/10. Multiplying by 1/4 gives 1/40. Odds are not probability.

Part C 26. A dominant variant's prior probability in an offspring is 1/2. At age 20 penetrance is 0.2 and noncarriers never express. What is carrier probability given unaffected at that age?

  1. 1/5
  2. 4/9
  3. 1/2
  4. 1/6
Answer and explanation

B. 4/9 — Weights are 0.5×0.8 and 0.5×1. Normalizing gives 0.4/0.9=4/9. At age 60 the answer could differ.

Part C 27. Hypothetical genotype groups AA, Aa and aa exhibit enzyme activities 100, 50 and 5 units. A trait appears below 20 units. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Enzyme activity is identical for AA and Aa
  2. The trait is dominant
  3. The threshold phenotype is recessive despite intermediate carrier activity
  4. The variant is necessarily X-linked
Answer and explanation

C. The threshold phenotype is recessive despite intermediate carrier activity — Only aa falls below threshold. The quantitative activity phenotype is not identical to the binary clinical classification.

Part C 28. A nuclear variant is expressed only if inherited from the father; a heterozygous woman transmits it to her son, who later transmits it to a child. Which statement fits the simplified imprinting model?

  1. It cannot travel through the son
  2. It must be mtDNA
  3. The child must inherit it from the grandmother directly
  4. It can be silent in the son and expressed in his child if transmitted
Answer and explanation

D. It can be silent in the son and expressed in his child if transmitted — Nuclear sequence transmission occurs through both sexes, while parent-of-origin expression can reset between generations. This differs from mtDNA inheritance.

Core summary

Read a pedigree by separating relationship, observed phenotype and inferred genotype. Track the parental origins of nuclear and mitochondrial alleles, reject impossible transmissions only under explicit assumptions, and compare the likelihood of plausible alternatives. Conditional probability updates uncertainty about genotype; penetrance and background expression alter the link between genotype and phenotype.

Important relationships

  • Conditional probability: P(H given E)=P(H and E)/P(E).
  • Bayes: posterior weights = prior × likelihood; normalize over competing states.
  • Known carriers Aa × Aa: 1/4 affected, 1/2 heterozygous, 2/3 carrier among unaffected.
  • Binomial count: C(n,k)p^k(1-p)^(n-k), conditional on fixed assumptions.
  • Inbreeding coefficient: P(aa)=q²+Fpq under the elementary population model.
  • Expression probability: fg+b(1-g) with penetrance f, background probability b and genotype prior g.
  • Posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio.

  2. Draw and label an affected male, an unaffected female and a proband arrow.
  3. Derive the 2/3 conditional carrier probability.
  4. Explain pseudodominance using aa × Aa.
  5. Track the X chromosomes in XrY × XRXr.
  6. Contrast paternal X-linked dominant and Y-linked transmission.
  7. Explain why mitochondrial heteroplasmy weakens phenotype predictions.
  8. Derive Bayes' rule for an unaffected observation.
  9. Compute P(at least one affected) for three known-carrier births.
  10. Distinguish phenotype penetrance from expressivity.
  11. Explain how ascertainment modifies the observed affected fraction.
  12. Derive F for a first-cousin offspring and state assumptions.
  13. Explain why a likelihood ratio is not automatically a posterior probability.
  14. State what evidence distinguishes a nuclear maternal effect from mtDNA transmission.
  15. Explain why confirmed direct variant transmission matters more than shared affected status.

Mastery checklist

  • I can read generation and individual labels without treating missing data as unaffected.
  • I can justify genotype assignments under each specified transmission model.
  • I can distinguish sex-conditioned risk from joint offspring risk.
  • I can update carrier probability after independent and shared-state evidence.
  • I can explain how penetrance, phenocopies, de novo variation and heteroplasmy complicate patterns.
  • I can compare likelihoods without treating them as posterior probabilities.
  • I can state the assumptions and limitations of theoretical recurrence examples.
