1. The problem of reconstructing inheritance

A family history is a record of outcomes rather than a controlled genetic cross. Human geneticists generally cannot choose the parents of a future generation, dictate the number of children or deliberately introduce a genetic variant. Instead, they ask whether naturally observed family relationships and phenotypes are consistent with particular mechanisms of transmission. This reverses the direction of a conventional Mendelian problem. In a pea cross, the parental genotypes may be known and the offspring ratios predicted. In a human pedigree, the observed offspring help constrain the possible parental genotypes. It is an inference problem: several explanations may fit the same observations, and an explanation must survive every informative relationship in the family.

The phenotype of interest must be defined before any transmission model is tested. For example, two relatives described as having impaired hearing may have completely different underlying causes. A shared symptom is not proof of a shared allele. Conversely, a relative without an apparent symptom may carry the same variant as an affected family member, especially when the trait appears late in life or is incompletely penetrant. The useful question is not merely which symbols are shaded but what the shading actually represents. Age at observation, diagnostic criteria and reliability of the family report can change the interpretation substantially.

A diploid autosomal locus normally contains one allele inherited from each biological parent. Meiosis separates the two homologous chromosomes so that a heterozygote produces gametes bearing either allele, in an idealized model with equal probability. Fertilization combines one allele from each parent. When the locus is on the non-pseudoautosomal part of the X chromosome, the path differs because a typical XY father passes his X to daughters and Y to sons. Mitochondrial DNA follows yet another route, through the cytoplasm of the egg. Pedigree patterns are therefore consequences of cellular mechanisms, not arbitrary visual rules. The scope of this chapter assumes conventional XX and XY transmission in textbook crosses unless otherwise specified; actual families can have chromosome complements and biological circumstances that require more detailed models.

A pedigree represents biological relatedness as a set of constrained paths. It is useful to separate three layers of information: known relationships, observed phenotypes and inferred genotypes. The first two are observations or reports; the third is a hypothesis. If a model requires a father to transmit an ordinary non-pseudoautosomal X allele to his son, the model contradicts chromosome segregation, no matter how visually attractive the pedigree looks. If a model merely predicts that approximately half of children will be affected but the family has three affected children and none unaffected, the prediction is not logically contradicted. A probability can be small without being zero.

Classical studies of inherited human traits long predate DNA sequencing. Archibald Garrod's work on alkaptonuria linked the inheritance of an unusual metabolic phenotype to Mendelian segregation and proposed that inherited differences in metabolism could explain the condition. The enduring lesson is that family transmission and biochemical phenotype can inform one another. Modern sequence data can add a third kind of evidence: an identified variant that segregates with a phenotype. Segregation by itself still does not establish causation because neighboring variants may travel together. Molecular evidence, functional understanding and a sufficiently informative family may all be necessary. In examination problems, the goal is usually more limited: determine a plausible transmission model and compute its consequences.

2. Pedigree notation and reliable family records

Standard pedagogical pedigrees commonly show a square for a male, a circle for a female and a diamond when sex is unspecified or not recorded. A filled symbol denotes the defined affected phenotype. A horizontal line connects reproductive partners and a vertical line descends to the sibship line, from which each child's symbol is connected. Generations are labelled using Roman numerals, and people within a generation are numbered from left to right. Thus II-3 identifies the third numbered individual in generation II, not necessarily the third child in a particular sibship. The arrow identifying a proband indicates the individual through whom the family came to attention; that individual is not necessarily the founder of the variant.

A diagonal slash through a symbol denotes death rather than the presence or absence of the phenotype. Carrier markings vary according to the notation standard and the condition. Some educational diagrams use a dot or partial fill, but a legend must explicitly distinguish a proven heterozygote from a person whose genotype is inferred, and distinguish genotype information from observed phenotype. A person whose phenotype is unknown must not be drawn as unambiguously unaffected without an appropriate annotation. The standardized pedigree nomenclature recommendations discussed by Bennett and colleagues emphasize consistent recording and communication. For an exam diagram, follow its supplied key rather than silently importing a different convention.

Full siblings share two biological parents; half siblings share one. When people are connected by multiple ancestral paths, a single individual may be relevant to more than one branch of the probability calculation. A double partner line commonly indicates consanguinity. It is not a symbol for a genetic disease. Adoption, donor conception, uncertain parentage and other relevant relationships can require specific annotations in a formal pedigree. In the simple mathematical models used here, the lines represent the stated genetic relationships. The absence of a line is not evidence that a person has no siblings; it only means that the diagram does not show them.

To extract evidence systematically, first identify each affected individual and list the phenotype of both parents where known. Next follow affected fathers separately from affected mothers. Ask whether daughters and sons are represented and whether the phenotype occurs in consecutive generations. Then annotate obligatory genotypes under each candidate model. For instance, an unaffected parent of a child with a fully penetrant autosomal recessive phenotype must have contributed a recessive allele if the simple single-locus model is correct. If both parents are unaffected they are both heterozygotes under that model. Their genotypes are deduced from the child, not from a visual carrier symbol.

The number of children is a crucial part of the evidence. A father with one unaffected daughter and no sons provides a very different test of X-linked dominant transmission from a father with five unaffected daughters. A missing daughter is not an unaffected daughter. Similarly, in a family of two sons, the absence of affected girls provides no information about the risk among daughters. When a question says that both sexes are equally affected, decide whether it is describing an expected probability from a model or the actual observed counts. These statements cannot be used interchangeably.

3. Autosomal dominant inheritance

In a simple autosomal dominant model, denote the phenotype-producing allele by D and the alternate allele by d. Both Dd and DD individuals are affected when penetrance is complete and both genotypes are viable, whereas dd individuals are unaffected. Dominance is a relationship between alleles and a specified phenotype, not a measure of how common an allele is or how severe a disease is. A dominant variant can be rare because it arises infrequently or reduces reproductive success. A molecular change can act dominantly by reducing the functional product below a necessary threshold, by creating an abnormal activity, or by interfering with the normal gene product. The pedigree predicts the inheritance relationship; additional evidence is required to choose the molecular mechanism.

For Dd crossed with dd, the affected parent produces D-bearing and d-bearing gametes in equal proportions. The other parent contributes only d. Thus the possible offspring genotypes are Dd and dd with probabilities one half each. Since the locus is autosomal, these genotype probabilities are the same when conditioned on a son or a daughter, provided chromosome segregation and sex are independent. An affected father can transmit the allele to a son, a relationship that is particularly useful when comparing this model with X-linked inheritance. But father and son both being affected does not, by itself, prove that the father transmitted the variant. The son might have inherited a separate allele from his mother or have an unrelated cause of the phenotype.

A dominant trait often produces a vertical pedigree in which affected individuals appear in successive generations. The explanation is that a single transmitted allele can produce the phenotype. However, the vertical appearance is only a tendency. If a heterozygote has two children, the probability that neither inherits D is one quarter. If the branch continues through one of those noncarriers, the familial variant cannot be transmitted through that branch under the ordinary model. Dominant inheritance does not require every generation or every individual in a family to be affected. A completely unaffected person cannot transmit a fully penetrant dominant allele in the idealized model, but age-dependent expression, germline mosaicism and incorrect phenotype assignment can invalidate that deduction.

When both parents are heterozygotes, the genotypes DD, Dd and dd have respective probabilities one quarter, one half and one quarter. With complete penetrance and equal survival, the phenotype probability is three quarters affected. If DD is lethal before the stage at which the offspring are scored, the observed surviving fraction affected is instead two thirds because only Dd and dd survivors contribute to the denominator. This calculation distinguishes the probability at fertilization from the probability among surviving children. Selection of the observation stage is an important part of many genetics problems, and the same issue can arise in recessive or X-linked models.

4. Autosomal recessive inheritance

For a fully penetrant autosomal recessive condition, write A for an allele sufficient for the ordinary phenotype and a for a disease-associated allele. AA and Aa have the unaffected phenotype in this model; aa is affected. The heterozygous individual is a carrier. This simplified notation can also stand for two different disease-associated variants in the same gene, one inherited from each parent, when the combination causes loss of adequate function. Such an affected person is a compound heterozygote rather than a homozygote at the sequence level. The common educational cross Aa by Aa assumes that a denotes any relevant nonfunctional allele at the locus, not necessarily a single DNA substitution.

Each carrier makes A and a gametes in equal proportions. Multiplying the probabilities gives AA with probability one quarter, Aa with probability one half and aa with probability one quarter. If a child is already known to be unaffected, the aa outcome must be removed from the possible genotype states under complete penetrance. Among the remaining three equally weighted Punnett-square entries, two correspond to Aa. Therefore the conditional carrier probability is two thirds. The fact that one sibling is affected does not alter the genotype distribution of a further child when parental genotypes are already known; the pregnancies are independent conditional on those parental genotypes. Observing a new affected child can nevertheless change our knowledge of the parental genotypes if those genotypes were originally uncertain.

Recessive conditions commonly produce a horizontal pattern, with affected siblings and unaffected parents. This is not an exclusive property of recessive traits, and recessive inheritance can occur in consecutive generations. Suppose aa mates with Aa. The affected parent can only transmit a. The carrier parent produces A or a, giving Aa and aa with equal probability. Half the children are expected to be affected; every unaffected child is an obligate carrier. This cross can mimic dominant inheritance visually and is often called pseudodominant transmission. The clue is not the pattern's vertical appearance but whether the assigned genotypes satisfy all relationships.

Consanguinity raises the chance that two biological relatives share an allele inherited from a common ancestor. It does not generate a recessive mutation automatically. Under a simple rare-allele model, suppose two first cousins share a grandparent known to be heterozygous while all other founders are assumed noncarriers. Each cousin has probability one quarter of receiving that grandparent's allele: one half for transmission to the intervening parent and one half for transmission to the cousin. Under independent segregation down the two distinct branches, the probability that both cousins carry this allele is one sixteenth. If both carry it, their child has probability one quarter of receiving it twice, giving a joint probability of one sixty-fourth under the explicitly stated model. This is not a general clinical risk for first-cousin couples; population allele frequencies, other founders and the actual family history change the result.

5. X-linked and Y-linked inheritance

A typical XY male has one copy of most X-linked loci, whereas a typical XX female has two. Hemizygosity describes the former state; it does not mean homozygosity because there is no second allele at that ordinary X locus. For a recessive X-linked variant Xr, a male XrY expresses the phenotype under complete penetrance. A heterozygous female XRXr is often unaffected in a simplified exam model. In real biological examples, X inactivation and tissue-specific expression can produce manifestations in heterozygous females, so the idealization must be stated rather than used as a universal clinical rule.

Consider XRXr crossed with XRY. A son receives Y from his father and either XR or Xr from his mother. His conditional probability of being affected is one half. A daughter receives XR from her father and XR or Xr from her mother, so half the daughters are carriers and none is affected under the simple recessive model. If the probabilities of a son and daughter are equal, an affected son is one quarter of all children. Many examination mistakes arise from confusing the probability among sons with the joint probability of being a son and affected.

An affected male XrY with a noncarrier female XRXR has no affected offspring under that model, but all daughters inherit Xr from him and become carriers. His sons inherit his Y and a normal X from their mother. If a carrier daughter later has children with an unaffected noncarrier male, her sons each have one-half risk of the recessive phenotype. This produces the classic appearance of a variant passing through unaffected female relatives to affected male descendants. A family can lack affected women because the combination of two disease-associated X alleles is rare, but female expression remains biologically possible.

In an X-linked dominant model, Xd denotes the dominant phenotype-producing allele. An affected father XdY with an unaffected mother XnXn has daughters XdXn and sons XnY. With complete penetrance all daughters and no sons express the trait. A heterozygous affected mother crossed with an unaffected father transmits Xd to half her daughters and half her sons. This asymmetry between maternal and paternal transmission follows the different gamete chromosome complements. The absence of affected daughters in a large family of an affected father contradicts a fully penetrant simple X-linked dominant model if the daughter relationships and phenotype reports are reliable. Some X-linked conditions do not fit the binary dominant and recessive labels cleanly.

For a variant restricted to the male-specific part of the Y chromosome, a typical father passes the Y chromosome to all sons and no daughters. With complete penetrance and no new mutation, all sons of an affected fertile father therefore carry and express the relevant allele. A pattern of affected males alone is not sufficient to infer Y linkage. X-linked recessive inheritance, sex-limited autosomal expression and chance can also produce male-only affected symbols. Pseudoautosomal loci differ because homologous regions on X and Y can recombine; the ordinary prohibition of father-to-son X-linked transmission is intended for non-pseudoautosomal loci.

6. Mitochondrial transmission and competing models

Most human mitochondrial genomes are inherited from the egg. Thus a mother carrying an mtDNA variant may transmit it to both sons and daughters, whereas a father carrying an mtDNA variant does not ordinarily transmit it. The difference is not a result of the sex of the offspring but of the origin of the organelles. In a simple model where every mitochondrial genome in an egg carries the variant, all offspring inherit it. However, genotype transmission must be separated from disease expression. A person may have a mixture of mitochondrial genomes with and without a variant, termed heteroplasmy. During oogenesis and development, changes in the proportion of those genomes can create different variant burdens among siblings and tissues.

A variant burden that impairs function in one tissue may be tolerated in another because energy demands and cellular context differ. A threshold model represents this principle by assuming that dysfunction becomes apparent when the proportion of variant genomes exceeds some tissue-dependent level. The model does not imply that every mitochondrial disorder has one universal threshold. A mildly affected mother can have a more severely affected child, and a carrier may have no observable phenotype. Consequently, maternal transmission of an mtDNA variant does not justify assigning every child a one hundred percent probability of clinical expression. In this course the organelle bottleneck is a prerequisite concept from 8.A.08; we use it to explain why a human pedigree cannot generally be reduced to a simple dominant Punnett square.

A maternally clustered pedigree could also arise from a nuclear allele, shared exposure or an ascertainment artifact. Many proteins necessary for mitochondrial function are encoded by nuclear genes. A disease involving mitochondrial physiology may consequently be inherited as autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant or X-linked rather than through mitochondrial DNA. The phenotype's organ system does not identify the genome harboring the causal variant. A strong test compares transmission from affected males with transmission from affected females and then uses molecular data to identify whether the variant actually resides in mtDNA.

Distinguish three statements that are sometimes collapsed into one: a variant is located within mitochondrial DNA; its transmission is usually maternal; and its phenotype can be observed in a person. The first concerns genomic location, the second inheritance, and the third physiological expression. Only when all relevant assumptions are specified can a numerical recurrence prediction be made. The same three-level analysis applies to chromosomal variants. It is especially useful for interpreting unaffected individuals who might nevertheless transmit an allele.

7. Conditional probability and Bayesian updating

A pedigree problem often asks for the probability of a hidden genotype after observing a phenotype. Let H denote the hypothesis that a woman is a carrier of an autosomal recessive variant, and E denote the evidence that she is unaffected. The conditional probability P(H given E) equals P(H and E) divided by P(E). Bayes' rule rewrites this as P(E given H) times P(H) divided by P(E). If the two hypotheses H and not-H exhaust the possibilities, the denominator is P(E given H)P(H) plus P(E given not-H)P(not-H). The denominator normalizes the two weighted hypotheses so that their posterior probabilities sum to one.

Suppose the parents are known carriers and their daughter is unaffected. Before observing her phenotype, the genotype probabilities are AA one quarter, Aa one half and aa one quarter. Under complete penetrance the probability of being unaffected is three quarters. The joint probability of being a carrier and unaffected is one half. Dividing yields two thirds. In a second example, assume that this unaffected daughter has children with a confirmed carrier. The risk of an affected child is two thirds times one quarter, or one sixth, because only the event that she is a carrier permits an aa child. This is a mixture of genotype scenarios, not a new Mendelian segregation ratio.

Now suppose the woman has two unaffected children with the confirmed carrier partner. For a noncarrier woman AA, the probability that both children are unaffected is one. For a carrier woman Aa, each child has probability three quarters of being unaffected, so the likelihood of two unaffected children is nine sixteenths, assuming independent meioses and complete penetrance. Multiplying the prior carrier probability two thirds by nine sixteenths gives three eighths; multiplying the noncarrier prior one third by one gives one third. The total probability of the two observations is seventeen twenty-fourths, and the updated carrier probability is nine seventeenths. The conditional risk for a further child is nine seventeenths times one quarter, or nine sixty-eighths. The declining risk reflects revised information about the woman's genotype, not a biological compensation mechanism after unaffected births.

A similar update applies to X-linked recessive transmission. Suppose a woman has prior carrier probability one half, her partner is unaffected, and her two sons are unaffected. Under the carrier hypothesis the likelihood of the observations is one quarter; under the noncarrier hypothesis the likelihood is one. The two unnormalized posterior weights are one eighth and one half. Their sum is five eighths, so her posterior carrier probability is one fifth. The next son has one tenth probability of being affected under these assumptions. If the question asks for the next child regardless of sex, the probability of an affected son is one twentieth, given equal sex probabilities and the assumption that daughters are unaffected.

For a dominant allele with penetrance f, the probability of a phenotype need not equal the allele transmission probability. If a known Dd parent mates with dd and carriers have penetrance 0.8 while dd individuals never express the trait, the affected probability is 0.5 times 0.8, or 0.4. If the offspring is observed to be unaffected, the probability of carrying D is the joint probability of receiving D and failing to express, 0.5 times 0.2, divided by the total unaffected probability 0.6. The answer is one sixth. This person can still transmit the variant. Penetrance is a conditional probability of expression given a genotype and relevant context; expressivity describes the extent or character of expression among people with the genotype.

The likelihood approach compares models by asking how probable the actual observations are under each. Suppose an affected father and unaffected mother have three daughters, all affected, and two sons, all unaffected. Conditional on known offspring sexes, a fully penetrant X-linked dominant model with the usual parental genotypes assigns likelihood one to the phenotype sequence. An autosomal dominant heterozygote model assigns likelihood one thirty-second. This comparison favors X-linked dominance over the particular autosomal model by a factor of thirty-two. It does not establish a posterior probability of thirty-two thirty-thirds unless equal model priors and an exhaustive two-model comparison are separately justified. This distinction prepares the logic of the next node on LOD scores.

8. Exceptions, evidence and model discrimination

De novo variation can produce an affected individual in a family with no prior affected relatives. If a variant arises in a gamete or at an early developmental stage, the person may carry it even though neither parent shows the corresponding phenotype. A dominant pedigree can therefore start with an apparently isolated case. Germline mosaicism means that a parent has a variant in a subset of germ cells, potentially absent or below detection in a sampled somatic tissue. Repeated transmission from an unaffected parent cannot automatically be ruled out simply because a blood test does not detect the variant. The fraction of germ cells carrying it cannot generally be inferred from one observed birth.

Phenocopy refers to an observed phenotype resembling a genetically determined one but arising from a different cause, often an environmental influence. In a pedigree study it may produce an affected symbol at a position incompatible with an otherwise consistent segregation model. Genetic heterogeneity creates a related problem: similar phenotypes may result from variants at distinct loci or from distinct alleles of the same locus. A model that assumes every affected family member has the identical genetic cause can fail for either reason. In exam questions, assume a single locus and no phenocopies only if the wording states that model or the intended simplification is clear.

Reduced penetrance can mimic skipped generations in dominant inheritance. A young relative with an age-dependent trait may be classified as unaffected at observation but manifest later. Thus penetrance should sometimes be interpreted as conditional on age. Variable expressivity differs: two people carrying the same allele may express different severity or combinations of signs. The distinctions matter when a family record uses a binary affected/unaffected symbol to compress a spectrum of physiological effects. Scoring criteria that classify mild expression as unaffected can create apparent nonpenetrance even if the person has measurable manifestations.

An orderly method for model discrimination is to write a candidate genotype for each individual, propagate obligatory alleles through parent-child links, reject assignments that require impossible transmission, and then compare the likelihoods of remaining assignments. Do not reject a model merely because an expected ratio is not observed in a small sibship. For example, a heterozygous autosomal dominant parent and noncarrier partner have probability one eighth of producing three unaffected children. That outcome is not unusual enough to establish nontransmission. In contrast, a confirmed non-pseudoautosomal X-linked allele cannot follow a direct X chromosome route from a typical XY father to his XY son. The distinction between zero probability under a specified model and a low nonzero probability is the foundation of reliable pedigree analysis.

Actual human genetics involves more than these simplified mathematical models. Family histories can be incomplete, variants may have uncertain significance, and risks can depend on allele frequencies, ancestry, penetrance, environmental influences and clinical evidence. The worked examples here are hypothetical, intended to teach segregation and conditional inference. Their numerical answers apply to their stated assumptions, not to an individual's medical circumstances. The next step in the Academy curriculum is linkage analysis, which uses segregation of markers and disease-associated alleles to compare likelihoods across recombination fractions.
