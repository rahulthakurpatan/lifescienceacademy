1. When a protective immune response causes injury

The skin, blood vessels, respiratory passages and internal organs depend on an immune system that can detect danger without destroying the structures that sustain life. A neutrophil attacking a bacterium releases reactive chemicals and digestive enzymes. A cytotoxic T lymphocyte can induce death in a virus-infected cell. An antibody can identify a microbial surface for removal by other immune components. None of these activities is intrinsically harmless. Their usefulness depends on their localization, intensity, timing and target. When the same activity occurs against a harmless substance or an important host structure, the protective mechanism can become a cause of disease.

Immune-mediated damage is injury produced substantially by the immune response itself. It must be distinguished from damage caused directly by the original stimulus. Consider a respiratory virus that kills epithelial cells during replication. The death of a cell due to viral replication is direct pathogen-associated damage. The death of a different infected cell induced by a cytotoxic lymphocyte is immune-mediated damage. Both can occur in the same infection. The latter may limit the spread of infection and still impose a physiological cost. Whether the net outcome is beneficial depends partly on whether sufficient functional tissue survives and whether regeneration is possible.

Hypersensitivity is an inappropriate or harmful immune reaction to an antigen, conventionally understood in relation to the underlying immune mechanism. An antigen is a structure that an adaptive immune receptor can recognize. A harmless pollen protein may become a target of an immune response in a susceptible person; in that context it is called an allergen. An autoantigen is a molecular structure belonging to the host that becomes a target of self-reactive immune recognition. These labels refer to distinct properties of the target or response. A protein does not become an allergen simply because it enters an airway, and the presence of a self antigen in a lymph node does not necessarily produce autoimmune disease.

It is useful to separate recognition from activation and activation from injury. An antigen-binding site can interact with a molecular surface without necessarily triggering an effector response. A lymphocyte that recognizes a peptide may fail to proliferate in the absence of a suitable secondary signal. An antibody may bind its target without activating complement if its class or the spatial organization of its Fc regions is unsuitable. Finally, even an activated effector may cause little net injury if the relevant target is inaccessible or the tissue has effective local protective mechanisms. In a mechanistic question, identifying the earliest changed event is more informative than simply naming an inflammatory cell observed at the end.

The adaptive repertoire is generated before the organism can know which antigens it will encounter. Gene rearrangement and subsequent selection produce a diverse set of receptor specificities. This strategy permits recognition of new pathogens but creates an unavoidable risk of recognizing host molecules. Central tolerance removes or redirects many developing self-reactive lymphocytes, while peripheral tolerance limits the activation of cells that escape. The existence of these successive barriers reveals why self-recognition is not synonymous with autoimmunity. Disease usually requires an additional failure, such as inappropriate activation, insufficient regulation or repeated access to an important self target.

An equally important distinction concerns the spatial distribution of injury. A harmful reaction at a small area of skin may release substantial local inflammatory mediators without producing a dangerous systemic disturbance. The same mediators released simultaneously near many blood vessels can alter the distribution of fluid in the body and interfere with circulation. A small antibody-mediated change in a receptor can produce a large physiological effect if the receptor controls an endocrine feedback loop. The severity of immune damage therefore cannot be inferred merely by counting inflammatory cells or measuring antibody concentration.

In the four-part Gell–Coombs framework, the mechanistic identity of the response depends on which immune effector connects antigen recognition to damage. The first class involves IgE associated with high-affinity receptors on cells that release inflammatory mediators. The second involves antibody directed against a target that is already fixed to a cell or tissue. The third involves complexes formed between antibodies and soluble antigens, followed by local formation or deposition in tissues. The fourth depends principally on antigen-specific T cells. These categories are not a ranking of severity and do not represent four chronological steps in one response. More than one can contribute to the same disease.

The same four-part scheme can be applied to self-directed injury. It is nevertheless incorrect to define every hypersensitivity as autoimmune, because the initiating antigen may be foreign. It is also incorrect to classify every autoimmune disorder as type II simply because the patient has autoantibodies. An antibody detected in the blood may be a marker of an ongoing T-cell-mediated process rather than the principal cause of tissue destruction. The task is to identify the causal effector, the physical nature of its antigen and the tissue process that fails.

2. How immunologists discovered harmful immunity

Early work on immunization established that previous exposure to an antigen could confer subsequent protection. The discovery of anaphylaxis was striking because it demonstrated that a later exposure could instead cause a harmful response. In the early twentieth century, Charles Richet and Paul Portier observed severe reactions on repeated exposure to material obtained from marine animals. Their findings did not imply that immunity had ceased to obey biological principles. Rather, they established that exposure could change the host in ways that made future encounters dangerous. Richet received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1913 for work on anaphylaxis.

A central question followed: did the altered response depend on a substance circulating in the blood, or was it restricted to a particular population of tissue cells? In 1921, observations associated with Prausnitz and Küstner showed that an immediate allergen-specific skin response could be transferred with serum from a sensitized person. This provided evidence for a soluble transferable factor. The historical experiment predates modern ethical and safety standards and is not a method to reproduce. Its conceptual importance lies in the separation of the source of specificity from the local tissue that generated the visible response.

Serum transfer does not automatically identify the molecular factor responsible. Serum contains antibodies, complement proteins, metabolites and numerous other substances. Further investigation was needed to associate transferable allergic sensitivity with a particular immunoglobulin. In the 1960s, work by Kimishige and Teruko Ishizaka and independently by researchers including Johansson and Bennich established the existence of the immunoglobulin now known as IgE. Its biological importance is much greater than one might predict from its relatively low concentration in normal serum. Binding to specific receptors allows it to accumulate functionally at the interface between antigen exposure and a cellular mediator response.

The four-class scheme developed by Gell and Coombs grouped immune injury according to underlying mechanisms. Its enduring value is that experimental evidence can distinguish whether the relevant activity is transferred by antibody, whether the antigen is attached to a cell, whether immune deposits form and whether sensitized lymphocytes are sufficient to initiate injury. Later discoveries revealed considerable overlap among effector pathways, but the original organization remains a useful starting point for examination reasoning. A response should not be assigned to a category by symptoms alone.

The study of self-tolerance underwent a complementary change in emphasis. A purely deletion-based theory would predict that the mature repertoire contains no potentially harmful self-reactive cells. Evidence that regulatory lymphocytes actively restrain other lymphocytes demonstrated why this expectation was too simple. In experiments reported in 1995, Sakaguchi and colleagues showed that depletion of a CD4 population expressing CD25 could permit autoimmune manifestations in mice, and that the regulatory population could limit such responses. Modern identification of FOXP3-expressing regulatory T cells provides additional molecular context. CD25 by itself is not a unique regulatory marker, because activated conventional T cells can also express it.

The problem of how developing thymocytes encounter antigens ordinarily associated with specialized peripheral tissues was illuminated by work on AIRE. In 2002, Anderson and colleagues reported that this protein helps medullary thymic epithelial cells express many tissue-associated antigens. This creates opportunities to tolerize developing T cells before they encounter the corresponding peripheral organs. The finding connects gene regulation to organism-level protection: a change in thymic antigen expression can alter immune responses elsewhere in the body even when the peripheral tissue itself is genetically normal.

These discoveries illustrate a shared experimental principle. Transfer of a response establishes that some component is sufficient under the tested conditions, whereas selective removal of a component can establish that it is required under those conditions. Neither approach alone establishes the complete mechanism. The serum experiments showed transferability of specificity; later molecular evidence identified an immunoglobulin. Regulatory-cell experiments identified a protective population; subsequent characterization explained additional features of that population. A strong mechanistic conclusion is built by combining different kinds of evidence.

Two additional historical observations helped separate the harmful effects of immune recognition from those of the original foreign material. In 1903, Arthus described an intense local reaction in previously exposed animals challenged with the same antigen. In 1905, von Pirquet and Schick described serum sickness associated with foreign therapeutic serum. The distinction between a local reaction and a systemic response encouraged investigation of where antigen and antibody meet. Although the molecular understanding of complement and immune complexes developed later, these early observations made the anatomical distribution of the response a question in its own right.

In the 1940s, work by Landsteiner and Chase established that certain forms of acquired skin sensitivity could be transferred by cells rather than reproduced by serum alone. These results provided a counterexample to the assumption that every antigen-specific hypersensitivity reaction must depend on a circulating antibody. The later identification of T lymphocytes clarified the origin of this cellular specificity. This history illustrates how transfer experiments first established distinct biological categories before researchers could identify all of the underlying molecules.

3. Type I hypersensitivity: sensitization and immediate effector activation

A person may encounter a protein from pollen without developing symptoms. In another person, a first series of encounters produces an adaptive response that prepares tissue mast cells to respond rapidly to subsequent exposure. The immunological preparation is called sensitization. The later activation of the prepared effector cells is the elicitation phase. Distinguishing these phases explains how an adaptive response that requires days to develop can nevertheless give rise to tissue changes within minutes.

Mast cells are long-lived tissue-resident immune cells commonly located near epithelial surfaces and blood vessels. Their cytoplasm contains secretory granules that store biologically active molecules. Basophils are circulating granulocytes that share certain features with mast cells, including expression of the high-affinity IgE receptor FcεRI. The effector cells do not generate their own immunoglobulin binding specificity. Rather, secreted antibody produced by B-cell descendants attaches to receptors on these cells. A single mast cell can carry IgE molecules with different antigen-binding specificities, because FcεRI recognizes the antibody constant region rather than the antigen-binding site.

During sensitization, antigen is captured by antigen-presenting cells and processed for presentation to appropriate CD4 T lymphocytes. The local signals and the antigenic context influence the differentiation of activated helper cells. In a type 2 immune environment, cytokines including interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 favor the development or maintenance of responses associated with IgE production. A B cell that recognizes the native antigen can internalize it and present derived peptides to a cognate helper cell. Contact-dependent CD40 signaling, together with appropriate cytokine signaling, supports class-switch recombination toward the epsilon heavy-chain constant region. The specificity of a selected variable region is retained during the switch, although additional affinity maturation may occur independently.

IgE secreted by plasma cells circulates and binds FcεRI through its Fc region. The antigen-binding Fab regions remain available to interact with an allergen. Because the receptor has high affinity for IgE, occupancy can persist when soluble IgE is comparatively scarce. Receptor-bound IgE does not by itself guarantee degranulation. The cell is primed in the sense that its surface now contains antigen-specific recognition molecules, but it generally requires an activating event that brings an appropriate group of receptors together.

At a subsequent encounter, an allergen bearing suitable antigenic sites can bind more than one receptor-associated IgE molecule. This cross-linking changes the spatial organization of FcεRI and promotes phosphorylation of receptor-associated signaling motifs. Src-family kinases and Syk participate in signal propagation, leading to intracellular calcium mobilization and additional pathways that support granule fusion with the cell membrane. The increase in cytosolic calcium is not an antigen-specific recognition event. It is part of the common downstream machinery through which antigen-specific receptor clustering is converted into secretion.

Preformed granule mediators include histamine and proteases such as tryptase. Histamine can influence endothelial permeability, vascular tone, sensory nerve activity and smooth muscle in a tissue-dependent manner. Granule exocytosis releases molecules that have already been synthesized, so their effects can begin quickly. Activated mast cells also generate lipid-derived mediators such as cysteinyl leukotrienes and prostaglandin D2. Production of these substances depends on enzymatic metabolism of membrane-derived lipids, rather than simple emptying of a preexisting histamine store. Their effects can extend the response and differ across target cells.

The vascular response is particularly instructive. A change in endothelial barrier properties allows more plasma fluid to move toward the interstitial compartment. Local swelling can develop without any increase in the number of red blood cells at that site. Increased local blood flow can produce redness, while fluid accumulation separates tissue structures. In a narrow airway, an increase in wall thickness reduces the radius through which air moves. Smooth-muscle contraction can compound this obstruction. Under a simplified laminar-flow model, resistance is strongly dependent on radius, although actual airways have branching geometry and flow that need not satisfy the model's assumptions.

The immediate phase of a type I reaction is commonly followed by a later inflammatory phase. Newly expressed cytokines and chemokines help recruit additional cells, including eosinophils in many type 2 responses. Eosinophils possess granule proteins that can be useful against large parasites but may also injure the host epithelium. This recruitment requires changes in vascular adhesion and migration, and it does not occur on exactly the same time scale as secretion from a preloaded granule. A late-phase reaction remains compatible with type I hypersensitivity because the initiating antigen-specific effector mechanism was IgE-dependent activation. Delay alone does not make a response type IV.

Anaphylaxis illustrates the importance of distribution. If mediator release is widespread, systemic vascular changes can reduce effective circulating volume and disturb blood pressure, while respiratory effects can compromise ventilation. Localized mast-cell activation in the skin and systemic mediator release have a related molecular foundation but substantially different physiological consequences. Some anaphylactic reactions involve non-IgE mechanisms, so the clinical term anaphylaxis is not a sufficient demonstration of an IgE-dependent pathway. In a textbook problem, the presence of allergen-specific IgE, receptor-dependent activation and appropriate controls establishes the classical mechanism more directly.

Type 2 immunity also provides a useful evolutionary comparison. Responses involving IgE, mast cells and eosinophils participate in defense against certain multicellular parasites. A parasite too large to be engulfed by a phagocyte presents a different physical challenge from a bacterium. Local secretion of mediators and changes in epithelial physiology may assist expulsion or containment. The harmful response to an innocuous allergen uses components that serve useful functions in other contexts. It is not necessary to claim that all allergy has a single evolutionary explanation.

An IgE antibody molecule may bind a monovalent soluble ligand without efficiently aggregating FcεRI. By contrast, a multivalent ligand can promote clustering when its epitopes are accessible and suitably spaced. The distinction between affinity and valency matters because binding to isolated sites is not equivalent to assembly of a signaling cluster. At very high concentrations, certain model ligands can occupy individual sites in ways that reduce bridging; this behavior depends on the ligand and receptor geometry and is not a universal rule for all allergens. A graph of IgE occupancy cannot automatically be interpreted as a graph of mediator release.

An experiment comparing mast cells exposed to a model antigen before and after incubation with specific IgE can distinguish priming from nonspecific cell activation. If antigen alone causes little secretion but antigen after IgE loading causes a large response, the pattern supports a requirement for antigen-specific sensitization in that system. An irrelevant IgE control checks whether specificity matters. A condition that bypasses receptor signaling and directly raises cellular calcium can help determine whether the cell remains capable of secretion when a receptor-associated pathway is blocked. A viability measurement is also needed, because release of intracellular contents from dead cells would not demonstrate regulated degranulation.

At the molecular level, an IgE molecule contains two antigen-binding arms and an epsilon heavy-chain constant region. Unlike the receptor that originally selected a B-cell clone, secreted IgE is no longer coupled to that B cell's membrane signaling proteins. Its Fc region instead associates with receptors on another cell. FcεRI on mast cells contains a ligand-binding alpha subunit and signaling subunits, including a gamma-chain pair carrying immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs. The beta subunit in the familiar mast-cell receptor complex contributes to signaling. Thus a molecule produced by one differentiated lymphocyte can supply antigen specificity to a cell of a different lineage.

Receptor aggregation brings associated signaling machinery into a configuration that favors phosphorylation and downstream activation. If an IgE molecule remains attached to FcεRI but the allergen cannot bridge receptor-associated antibodies, the high-affinity Fc interaction can remain intact while secretion is weak. If a downstream signaling component is defective, allergen binding and even aggregation may occur without the normal response. These distinctions are useful for identifying whether a mutation affects recognition, organization or intracellular signal transmission.

Comparative biology adds a limit to the terminology. Cells with mast-cell-like features occur in several vertebrate lineages, whereas the mammalian IgE system represents a particular evolutionary specialization. Antibody classes and receptor arrangements vary among vertebrates. It would therefore be inaccurate to assume that the exact human IgE–FcεRI mechanism is universal in animals, or that plant defense responses use the same adaptive antibody machinery. The classical hypersensitivity framework in this chapter describes vertebrate adaptive effector responses, especially those well characterized in mammals.

4. Type II hypersensitivity: antibodies against fixed targets

The second hypersensitivity class is best understood by following the position of the antigen before antibody binds. A red blood cell presents molecules on its outer membrane. A basement membrane contains extracellular matrix proteins that remain fixed in a tissue. A receptor on the surface of an endocrine cell is part of a biological signaling system. When an antibody recognizes an epitope in any of these locations, its recognition is directed toward a target already associated with a cell or matrix. The antibody can therefore focus other immune processes on that structure. This spatial relationship distinguishes the classical type II mechanism from the deposition of complexes initially formed with soluble antigen.

The term cytotoxic hypersensitivity is commonly applied to this class, but it does not describe every possible outcome. An antibody may cause actual destruction of a cell, inflammation around an extracellular matrix, or abnormal activity of an otherwise living cell. The antigen-binding region specifies the target, while the constant region and the arrangement of antibodies on the target influence recruitment of additional effectors. Some antibodies directly alter receptor function without requiring complement or a cytotoxic cell. These different mechanisms are grouped together because the primary antibody target is a fixed structure, not because every response ends in membrane lysis.

Consider the surface of a red blood cell bearing an antigen that is absent from the recipient's own cells. If the recipient has a corresponding antibody, its Fab regions can attach to the donor cell. An appropriately arranged IgM molecule or a suitable set of adjacent IgG Fc regions can provide a binding surface for C1q, the recognition component of the classical complement pathway. The cascade produces enzymes that cleave additional complement components. C3b can become attached to nearby surfaces, marking them for recognition by cells possessing complement receptors. The activation event is amplified because each active convertase can process multiple substrate molecules.

If complement activation proceeds efficiently to formation of the terminal membrane attack complex, susceptible red blood cells may undergo intravascular hemolysis. However, complement-dependent damage is not synonymous with terminal pore formation. Cells coated with C3 fragments or IgG can be removed by phagocytes even if their membranes remain intact while circulating. Macrophages in organs including the spleen and liver encounter such tagged cells and recognize exposed antibody Fc regions or complement fragments. The phagocyte does not have to recognize the original blood-group antigen through a rearranged antigen receptor. The antibody supplies specificity, while the phagocyte supplies the machinery for removal.

The anatomical location of removal affects the predicted evidence. Cell destruction in the circulation can release intracellular hemoglobin directly into plasma. Removal of antibody-coated cells by macrophages is a different process, often described as extravascular hemolysis. These outcomes can coexist, and the balance depends on the antibody, complement activation, target density and target-cell susceptibility. For an examination question, it is unsafe to infer that every antibody-coated red blood cell must be lysed by C5b–9. Opsonization may be the dominant outcome even when terminal complement activity is weak.

ABO incompatibility provides a familiar model. A recipient may possess antibodies against carbohydrate structures corresponding to absent ABO antigens. Such antibodies often include IgM capable of activating complement efficiently, although the isotype composition varies. An incompatible donor red blood cell becomes a surface on which antibody recognition is converted into complement activation and cell removal. The biological lesson is not the clinical selection of blood products; it is the connection between a membrane-associated antigen and an effector response focused on the membrane bearing that antigen. The fact that antibodies and antigens form molecular complexes does not turn this event into type III hypersensitivity, because the target was already fixed to the cell.

Maternal–fetal red-cell alloimmunization illustrates a distinct route to injury. Maternal IgG can cross the placenta through FcRn-mediated transport and bind fetal red-cell antigens inherited from the other parent. IgM does not normally cross the placenta efficiently in the same manner. Thus antibody class influences access to the target independently of antigen-binding specificity. The recognition event is alloimmune rather than autoimmune: the antigen belongs to another individual of the same species. It still illustrates the fixed-target mechanism. A distinction between the identity of the antigen and the class of effector injury prevents a common conceptual error.

A tissue-fixed antigen may be impossible for a phagocyte to engulf. In a basement membrane, for example, antibodies can bind to components of a continuous extracellular scaffold. Complement fragments and Fc-dependent signals recruit inflammatory cells to the site. Neutrophils can become activated at the interface, yet cannot internalize the entire sheet of matrix. Their released proteases and reactive oxidants can injure adjacent host structures. This is sometimes described as frustrated phagocytosis. The same enzymatic machinery that is useful inside a phagosome can damage tissue when released outside it. Therefore tissue injury need not involve the direct destruction of an antigen-bearing cell by antibody alone.

In antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, an effector cell recognizes Fc regions of IgG attached to a target. Natural killer cells can use the Fc receptor CD16 to engage appropriately coated cells and trigger cytotoxic granule release. This combines antibody specificity with an innate cell's killing machinery. It differs from the direct recognition of peptide–MHC by a conventional cytotoxic T cell. A target cell lacking the relevant peptide–MHC complex may still be susceptible to antibody-dependent killing if the surface antigen and Fc-mediated interaction remain intact. Conversely, a target expressing peptide–MHC can be killed by a specific CTL even if no antibody binds it.

Some fixed-target antibodies act as molecular antagonists. At a synapse, a receptor must recognize a transmitter and participate in an appropriate downstream response. An antibody that occupies or alters the extracellular region of that receptor may interfere with normal signaling; antibody-mediated receptor internalization and complement-mediated injury can produce additional loss of function. In myasthenia gravis associated with antibodies to the acetylcholine receptor, these mechanisms contribute to impaired neuromuscular transmission. The number of surviving muscle cells need not fall in proportion to the loss of muscle performance. Physiological failure can occur because communication between surviving cells is disrupted.

Other antibodies behave as agonists. In Graves disease, a stimulating antibody can activate the thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor and increase hormone production. This illustrates how an immune recognition event can bypass the normal logic of an endocrine control system. Elevated thyroid hormone normally suppresses pituitary secretion of TSH through negative feedback. The autoantibody is not produced by pituitary cells and is therefore not directly removed by that feedback. A low TSH concentration can coexist with strong receptor stimulation. This is a particularly useful integrated example because it requires a distinction between hormone concentration, receptor occupancy and downstream physiological activity. Some teaching schemes label receptor-stimulating antibodies as type V, but within the conventional four-category model they are a functional subtype of type II.

Experimental reasoning can distinguish these possibilities. Suppose an antibody binds equally well to cells in two conditions, but the cells die only in the presence of active complement. The finding suggests that binding alone is insufficient and a heat-labile serum effector may be important. Heat inactivation alone is not a perfectly specific complement perturbation, so additional evidence from complement-deficient serum and restoration with the missing component would strengthen the interpretation. If removal of Fc regions preserves antigen binding but prevents killing by macrophages, an Fc-dependent mechanism is implicated. If an isolated antigen-binding fragment instead retains an inhibitory effect on a receptor, interference with receptor function may occur without Fc-dependent killing.

These comparisons show why a positive antibody-binding measurement cannot establish the entire mechanism of tissue damage. Binding may represent an early step, while cell removal, local inflammation, receptor antagonism or receptor agonism are alternative outputs. Appropriate assays must measure the proposed output separately from binding. This reasoning applies equally to naturally occurring antibody responses and to engineered antibodies designed to modify a biological target.

5. Type III hypersensitivity: immune complexes and tissue deposition

Soluble antigens can encounter antibodies in extracellular fluid. If they present multiple accessible epitopes, an antibody may connect one antigen molecule to another, producing aggregates of varying composition. Immune complex formation is not inherently abnormal. It contributes to the removal of soluble antigen by phagocytes and other clearance systems. Harm occurs when the rate, location or physical properties of complexes allow them to persist or accumulate where complement activation and inflammatory cell recruitment injure the host.

The distinction from type II begins with the original physical state of the target. In a classical type II reaction, antibody finds its antigen already attached to a cell or extracellular matrix. In a classical type III reaction, soluble antigen and antibody combine to produce complexes whose distribution is not simply the distribution of a preexisting fixed tissue antigen. The complexes may deposit from the circulation or form locally in tissue. Once deposited they are indeed physically associated with a structure, but classification depends on the route by which that association developed. Merely observing antibody on a tissue section is insufficient to determine which route occurred.

The geometry of antigen–antibody binding helps explain the properties of the complexes. An antibody with two available sites can bridge antigens with appropriate epitopes. An antigen with several accessible sites can connect multiple antibodies. Under suitable conditions these interactions produce a lattice. When antigen and antibody are present in proportions favorable to bridging, large complexes may form. At extreme antigen excess or antibody excess, many binding sites can be occupied without extensive interconnection. Thus the largest aggregate need not form at the highest concentration of one reactant. This is analogous to the zone of equivalence in a precipitation experiment, although molecular geometry, affinity, concentration and solvent conditions all influence the outcome.

A precipitation tube is not the same environment as a circulating blood vessel. Blood flow continually transports complexes, new molecules enter and leave the circulation, and immune proteins interact with many surfaces. Large aggregates may be removed efficiently, whereas certain smaller or intermediate complexes persist and can contribute to pathological deposition. It would be misleading to assert that every small complex is dangerous or that a particular antigen-to-antibody ratio universally causes maximal tissue damage. The biological outcome depends on production, clearance and tissue access rather than complex size alone.

Complement can participate in both protection and injury in this context. Recognition of appropriate antibody arrangements by C1q can initiate classical-pathway activation. Complement fragments attached to complexes can promote their removal. In humans, complement receptor 1 on erythrocytes helps transport complement-tagged complexes toward phagocytic clearance in organs such as the liver and spleen. Erythrocytes are not specialized phagocytes; they serve as carriers in this process. This function helps explain why a deficiency affecting early classical complement components can increase susceptibility to immune-complex autoimmunity even though complement activation is capable of causing inflammation.

If complexes accumulate in a tissue, complement activation can generate inflammatory mediators including C5a. This peptide can contribute to neutrophil recruitment and activation. Fc receptors on inflammatory cells provide another route by which deposited IgG-containing complexes influence the cells. Neutrophils arriving at the site may engage material that is too firmly associated with the tissue to be cleared by ordinary engulfment. Extracellular release of destructive products can damage the surrounding matrix and vascular structures. The target of the final injury may therefore be a tissue that did not originally synthesize the antigen.

The renal glomerulus is a useful example of a vulnerable anatomical site. Its capillary network and filtration barrier expose particular tissue interfaces to plasma proteins under conditions of fluid movement and selective filtration. Immune deposits can occur in different glomerular compartments, depending on the nature of the antigens, antibodies and local tissue properties. Local complement activation and inflammation can interfere with the filtration barrier. A functional consequence may include increased passage of proteins into the filtrate. The connection between immune deposition and impaired physiological selectivity illustrates how molecular binding events become organ-level abnormalities.

The presence of immunoglobulin and complement in a kidney section does not by itself prove a single deposition mechanism. Antibody directed against an intrinsic basement-membrane antigen may give a comparatively linear staining pattern along that membrane, whereas immune complexes often produce granular deposits. These are useful classic associations, not absolute diagnostic rules. A hypothetical imaging experiment can combine the distribution of antigen, antibody and complement with knowledge of the antigen's biochemical identity. The pattern is one observation in a causal argument, not a substitute for identifying the target.

Serum sickness is a classical systemic example. Historically, recipients of certain foreign antisera could develop immune responses against the introduced foreign proteins. When the recipient begins producing antibodies while foreign antigen remains available, antigen and antibody can coexist and form complexes. If those complexes persist and deposit in susceptible tissues, widespread inflammatory effects may follow. This time course helps explain why a response after first substantial exposure may require days, whereas a previously immunized organism may form complexes more rapidly. It is the accumulation and inflammatory handling of complexes, rather than the mere time elapsed, that defines the mechanism.

The Arthus reaction illustrates a more localized version of complex-mediated injury. In a previously immunized individual or experimental animal, specific circulating IgG can encounter antigen introduced at a particular site. Complex formation in or near local vascular structures can trigger inflammation through complement and Fc-dependent mechanisms. The local site may become markedly inflamed even when systemic antigen concentrations remain low. The Arthus and serum-sickness models show that type III reactions can differ dramatically in spatial extent while sharing a mechanistic link between soluble antigen, antibody complexes and inflammation.

A simple balance model makes the reasoning more precise. Let the quantity of circulating complexes be C, measured in an arbitrary but consistent concentration unit, and suppose they are formed at rate F and cleared at rate kC, where k is a first-order clearance constant in reciprocal time. The rate of concentration change is then F minus kC. If F and k are constant and no other loss routes are considered, a steady state occurs at C equal to F divided by k. A decrease in k raises the steady-state concentration even if antibody and antigen production do not change. This is a deliberately simplified model of a heterogeneous biological population, not a law of immune-complex disease. Tissue deposition and nonlinear clearance would change the equation.

For example, when complexes are formed at a hypothetical rate of 12 concentration units per hour and cleared with a first-order constant of 0.6 per hour, the predicted steady state is 20 units. If the clearance constant falls to 0.3 per hour while formation remains unchanged, the predicted steady state doubles to 40 units. This result identifies a mechanism by which impaired disposal can increase burden without enhanced antibody secretion. It does not establish that tissue injury doubles, because tissue entry, complement activation and inflammatory regulation may not scale linearly with circulating concentration.

The model also clarifies how a time-series experiment might separate production from removal. If formation stops, the simplified equation predicts exponential decay, with a half-life equal to the natural logarithm of two divided by k. Following the decline of a labeled complex population after its source is removed would provide information about disappearance. Disappearance from the sampled blood, however, can reflect uptake by clearance organs or deposition in tissues. A faster decline in circulation does not automatically indicate safer clearance. An experimental design that separately measures tissue accumulation and degradation would support stronger interpretation.

Systemic lupus erythematosus provides an important bridge to autoimmunity because some of its autoantigens are components of nuclei or nucleoprotein assemblies that become available outside cells. Antibodies directed against these materials can form complexes that participate in inflammatory injury. Normal disposal of cell debris and nucleic acids therefore matters for tolerance as well as tissue housekeeping. This bridge will be developed further when considering the persistence of self antigens. For the moment, the essential point is that autoimmunity describes the target's origin, whereas type III describes a particular route from antigen recognition to tissue injury.

An instructive physical contrast emerges if we imagine removing all unattached molecules from a tissue surface before adding antibody. In a fixed-target model, the relevant antigen remains because it is part of the surface itself. The antibody can still bind specifically. In an immune-complex model, removing soluble antigen before it associates with antibody would prevent new complexes of that antigen from forming, assuming no further antigen supply. Once a complex has deposited, however, a wash may no longer distinguish the models. The time at which the intervention occurs therefore changes the information supplied by the experiment.

An antibody directed against a surface can bind multivalently there, and a soluble immune complex can subsequently attach firmly to a surface. These similarities explain why looking at the final chemical composition alone is not enough. A useful classification requires a description of the initial location of the antigen and the causal pathway that concentrated inflammatory activity at that location. In a mixed lesion, distinct antigens and antibody populations can contribute through both routes.

6. Type IV hypersensitivity: antigen-specific cellular injury

The fourth class differs from the preceding classes in the identity of the principal antigen-specific effector. Rather than using secreted antibody to provide specificity to an inflammatory process, the classical type IV response depends on sensitized T lymphocytes. T cells can alter tissue function indirectly by secreting cytokines that recruit and activate other cells, or directly by inducing death in target cells. These mechanisms have a normal protective role when directed against infected cells or intracellular pathogens. They can also produce substantial local injury when their activation is excessive or directed against inappropriate targets.

A conventional CD4 T cell recognizes an antigen-derived peptide displayed by an appropriate MHC class II molecule. A CD8 T cell generally recognizes a peptide associated with MHC class I. Neither type recognizes intact soluble protein in the same way as a typical antibody. This restriction has consequences for the spatial development of inflammation. A tissue containing antigen may not directly activate a T cell unless relevant peptides are presented. Antigen processing, the abundance of peptide–MHC complexes and the activation state of the responding cells all influence the reaction. In many type IV responses, a local antigen-presenting cell connects the antigen in the tissue to the specific T-cell receptor.

During sensitization, a naive T cell encounters antigen in an appropriate activation context, proliferates and differentiates into cells with effector or memory capabilities. On later encounter, sensitized T cells can respond more readily to antigen presentation. The development of a visible inflammatory lesion nevertheless takes time. Cytokine secretion alters the local environment, endothelial cells express recruitment signals, circulating leukocytes adhere and migrate, and the incoming cells contribute additional mediators. This sequence explains why a classical delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction often develops over one or more days rather than reaching maximal intensity within minutes.

The Th1–macrophage relationship is a central example. An antigen-specific helper T cell can release interferon-gamma after recognizing its cognate peptide–MHC complex. Interferon-gamma changes the functional state of macrophages, favoring antimicrobial activities and enhancing aspects of antigen presentation. Contact-dependent interactions and other cytokines can reinforce the response. The macrophage may kill ingested microbes more effectively, which is advantageous during appropriate defense. Yet increased production of reactive intermediates, inflammatory mediators and proteolytic enzymes can also damage nearby host structures. T cells provide specific recognition; macrophages can amplify the local destructive response.

The tuberculin response illustrates this principle without requiring live pathogen replication at the site of antigen challenge. A previously sensitized person can mount a local cellular response to mycobacterial antigens. Recruitment and accumulation of inflammatory cells produce a palpable area of induration. A response typically assessed around 48 to 72 hours illustrates the delayed cellular process. Redness alone is not identical to induration, and the response does not independently establish active tuberculosis. The relevant immunological concept is that antigen-specific memory T cells can initiate inflammation upon reencounter with antigen even in the absence of a replicating microorganism at that location.

Persistent antigen can sustain T-cell and macrophage interaction. Under certain circumstances, aggregates of activated macrophages and other cells form a granulomatous structure that helps contain material difficult to eliminate. This is not a universal requirement for type IV hypersensitivity, and granulomas can arise through several routes. It is an example of how cellular immunity organizes tissue when antigen persists. The same architecture that confines an agent can reduce normal tissue function or contribute to damage. Detailed immunity against tuberculosis belongs to the subsequent chapter; here granulomatous organization demonstrates a possible consequence of prolonged cellular effector activity.

CD8-mediated injury has a different proximate mechanism. A cytotoxic T lymphocyte recognizing appropriate peptide–MHC I on a target can form a contact interface and deliver perforin and granzymes, or engage death-receptor pathways such as Fas–FasL. These processes trigger programmed cell death in a susceptible target. Cytokine production and recruitment can occur alongside direct killing. The effect depends on the identity of the presenting cell and the relevant peptide, not merely on the presence of a large concentration of antibody. If the target loses its MHC I presentation of a relevant peptide, recognition by that particular conventional CTL can decrease, although other immune mechanisms may respond differently.

Contact hypersensitivity provides a useful link to antigen chemistry. Certain small chemicals can bind host proteins and generate modified structures that are immunologically recognized. Skin dendritic cells can acquire or process relevant antigen and promote T-cell sensitization in draining lymphoid tissue. Subsequent skin exposure to the chemical can activate local or recruited antigen-specific T cells, resulting in inflammation. The reaction may involve CD8 and CD4 effector populations and is not adequately described as a single macrophage pathway. A small chemical does not have to behave as a complete protein antigen before entering the body; chemical modification can generate new antigenic contexts.

The distinction between a direct irritant and a sensitizer is biologically important. A corrosive or otherwise irritating material can damage skin cells without prior antigen-specific immune memory. A sensitized T-cell response depends on the history and specificity of adaptive immunity. Both mechanisms may produce inflammation and redness. An experiment distinguishing them would compare previously exposed and unexposed groups, include a vehicle control and determine whether responses depend on lymphocytes with relevant antigen specificity. A visible lesion by itself does not distinguish direct chemical injury from delayed immune injury.

Not all T-cell-mediated injury is limited to Th1 cells and macrophages. Th17-associated signals can promote neutrophil recruitment, and cytotoxic lymphocytes can kill targets independently of macrophage activation. Modern classifications describe additional cellular patterns, but the core exam distinction is the T-cell dependence of the classical type IV category. Similarly, antibodies can be present during an infection or autoimmune disease in which the dominant tissue injury is cellular. Their presence does not invalidate classification if experiments identify T cells as the principal cause of the particular injury being considered.

The traditional contrast between immediate and delayed hypersensitivity is valuable when interpreted mechanistically. Rapid mediator release from primed mast cells requires little time for cellular recruitment. A delayed inflammatory lesion often reflects new cytokine production and accumulation of cells. However, type I reactions can have late inflammatory phases, type III reactions can take time to develop, and direct killing by a previously activated CTL may begin relatively rapidly. Time is supporting evidence rather than a complete classification criterion. The stronger question is which antigen-specific component is indispensable for producing the observed injury.

An adoptive-transfer comparison illustrates the distinction. Suppose a response to a defined antigen can be transferred from a sensitized donor by viable T cells but cannot be reproduced by serum alone. Appropriate antigen challenge, MHC compatibility and recipient conditions must be maintained. This pattern would support a cellular mechanism. A failure to transfer with serum does not prove that antibodies never contribute to any aspect of the original response; transfer can fail for many technical reasons. Likewise, loss of a reaction after T-cell depletion must be interpreted in relation to the timing of depletion, because T cells also help generate antibody responses. Removing T cells during sensitization may prevent an antibody-mediated reaction without making its effector phase type IV.

7. Why self-tolerance is an active biological achievement

The idea of self-tolerance begins with a paradox. An adaptive immune system must generate receptors capable of recognizing molecules it has never encountered, including molecules from rapidly evolving pathogens. It cannot accomplish this by keeping a fixed list of all foreign molecules and excluding every self molecule at the moment a receptor gene is assembled. Instead, it generates diverse receptors and then tests the cells carrying them. Some receptors produced during this process will interact with self antigens. The safety of the mature immune system therefore depends on selection, regulation and the conditions under which antigen recognition can lead to effector differentiation.

The term central tolerance refers to processes operating while lymphocytes develop in the primary lymphoid organs. For T cells, this involves the thymus; for conventional B cells, important early selection takes place in the bone marrow. The two cell types face related but distinct problems. A T-cell receptor recognizes an antigen-derived structure in association with an appropriate presenting molecule, usually peptide–MHC for conventional T cells. A B-cell receptor can recognize accessible native molecular structures. The availability of a particular self antigen to the developing cell thus depends on different forms of display. A protein that is effectively presented as a peptide in the thymus need not be present in the same native form in the bone marrow.

Developing thymocytes must acquire receptors that can interact productively with self MHC molecules, because conventional mature T cells use those molecules to survey tissues for antigen. Positive selection favors survival of cells whose receptors make suitable interactions with self peptide–MHC. Negative selection removes many developing cells that respond too strongly to the self complexes they encounter. It would be incorrect to describe positive selection as a test of recognition of foreign antigen. At the time of selection, the relevant peptide–MHC complexes are predominantly derived from the host. A useful mature T-cell repertoire must retain the capacity to recognize appropriate MHC without remaining dangerously reactive to the self peptides encountered during development.

These processes do not divide all possible T-cell receptors into perfectly separate classes with a universal binding-energy threshold. The developmental outcome depends on receptor signaling, peptide availability, antigen-presenting cell identity and the stage of the developing lymphocyte. Some thymocytes responding to self antigens enter a regulatory lineage rather than simply being deleted. A schematic graph of increasing signal strength may be helpful to introduce selection, but it cannot predict every fate from a single numerical affinity. The same receptor can respond differently when ligand density, duration of contact and accessory signals change.

A special difficulty arises because many self proteins are normally expressed only in specialized organs. If the thymus could present only proteins made by ordinary thymic cells, developing lymphocytes might never encounter important peptide sequences derived from the pancreas, skin or other tissues. Medullary thymic epithelial cells express an unusually broad collection of tissue-associated genes. AIRE, the autoimmune regulator, promotes expression of many such antigens, providing opportunities for their presentation to developing T cells. Thymic dendritic cells can contribute to presentation of material acquired from other cells. Consequently, a relatively small organ can influence tolerance to proteins whose main physiological functions lie elsewhere.

A mutation that reduces AIRE function can alter this educational environment without altering the target protein in a peripheral organ. If an important peptide is poorly represented during selection, a self-reactive clone may escape deletion or other tolerance-inducing outcomes. The clone may subsequently encounter that peptide in the peripheral tissue or in antigen-presenting cells that have acquired material from the tissue. The prediction is not that every T cell becomes autoreactive, because many self antigens are displayed through AIRE-independent mechanisms and peripheral regulation remains possible. Rather, the mutation changes which specificities are effectively controlled, thereby increasing the chance of organ-directed immune damage.

B-cell central tolerance involves a different set of choices. A developing B cell that recognizes an accessible self structure strongly may undergo further light-chain gene rearrangement, called receptor editing. Replacement of the light chain can alter the combining site and reduce self-reactivity while allowing the cell to survive. Other cells may be deleted, or they may enter a state of reduced responsiveness called anergy. The exact outcome depends on developmental stage and the form and strength of antigen engagement. Receptor editing should not be confused with somatic hypermutation. Editing can replace receptor components during development, whereas somatic hypermutation generates changes in variable regions during activated B-cell responses.

A major consequence of the B-cell strategy is that elimination of self-reactive immature cells cannot ensure permanent absence of self-reactivity. During later affinity maturation, mutations can change the specificity of an antibody. A clone initially selected for a foreign antigen might acquire stronger binding to a self structure. Germinal-center selection and regulation of helper-cell support therefore contribute to tolerance even after the central developmental stage has ended. This is another reason why a mature immune system requires continued surveillance of activation rather than relying only on the developmental checkpoint.

Peripheral tolerance refers to mechanisms limiting responses by mature lymphocytes outside the primary lymphoid organs. A naive T cell can recognize its peptide–MHC ligand on a presenting cell without receiving an adequate costimulatory signal. In many contexts this encounter fails to produce productive activation and can contribute to anergy, deletion or other tolerance outcomes. This relationship protects tissues in which ordinary turnover continually produces self material for presentation. Antigen presentation itself is not proof that the immune system has interpreted that antigen as a threat.

Inhibitory receptors provide additional control. CTLA-4 and PD-1 are useful examples, but their physiological roles are not interchangeable. CTLA-4 can limit costimulatory interactions involving CD80 or CD86, including through regulatory T-cell activity. PD-1 signaling can reduce aspects of T-cell activation and effector function following engagement with appropriate ligands. At the level of an examination question, inhibition acts as a brake on activation rather than removing all antigen-recognition capability. Loss of inhibitory signaling may increase the response to a given peptide–MHC stimulus even when the T-cell receptor and its target are unchanged.

Regulatory T cells are another layer of protection. Many conventional regulatory T cells express CD4, FOXP3 and high levels of CD25. Their suppressive influence can involve changes in antigen-presenting cell function, competition for IL-2 and cytokine-dependent control, including context-dependent roles for IL-10 and transforming growth factor beta. The regulatory cell need not kill every conventional T cell that it restrains. By altering the activation environment, it can reduce expansion and activity of multiple potentially damaging cells. This helps explain why depletion of a relatively small regulatory population can have effects in several organs.

The significance of IL-2 in tolerance may initially appear contradictory because it is also a growth-promoting cytokine for activated T cells. Different populations depend on it in different ways. Regulatory cells expressing the high-affinity IL-2 receptor can use externally available IL-2 to support survival and function. Conventional activated T cells can produce IL-2 and respond to it. Therefore a reduction in IL-2 signaling does not necessarily produce a uniformly reduced tendency toward autoimmune injury. If regulatory populations are particularly affected, the balance between regulation and effector activity may shift in an unfavorable direction. Predicting the outcome requires information about the cells affected and the timing of the perturbation.

A final protective condition is limited antigen access. Some sites in the body restrict particular inflammatory processes, and some structures are not readily accessible to circulating immune components. The concept of immune privilege captures the observation that certain tissues have specialized anatomical and regulatory arrangements that reduce immune injury. It does not imply that these tissues contain no immune cells or cannot be recognized by the immune system. Tissue injury, altered barriers or inflammatory changes may expose previously poorly accessible material. Whether that exposure generates disease depends on the remaining tolerance mechanisms and the activation context.

Together these processes form a distributed control system rather than a single molecular switch. Central tolerance modifies the available repertoire; peripheral tolerance modifies how that repertoire is used; local tissue properties influence the consequences of immune activation. Failure at one point can sometimes be compensated by protection at another. Conversely, several individually modest changes can cooperate to create a substantial disturbance. This framework is more useful than the statement that the immune system simply mistakes self for foreign, because it specifies testable stages at which protection can fail.

8. How genetic predisposition and environmental context interact

Autoimmune disease can develop even when no single tolerance mechanism is completely absent. Most common autoimmune conditions reflect susceptibility distributed across several genes and environmental influences. A risk allele is a variant associated with a higher probability of a defined outcome in a particular population. It is not equivalent to a disease-causing mutation with complete penetrance. The biological effect of an allele may depend on the other alleles carried by an individual, the antigens encountered and the state of regulatory networks. An association between a genetic variant and a disease therefore raises a mechanistic question rather than answering it.

The major histocompatibility complex provides a clear example of how inherited variation can influence adaptive immune specificity. In humans, classical HLA molecules are highly polymorphic. Variation in the peptide-binding groove can influence which peptide sequences are bound, how stable a peptide–MHC complex is and which surfaces are presented to T-cell receptors. An allele may change thymic selection, peripheral antigen presentation or both. Its effect can therefore operate before exposure to a suspected environmental trigger as well as during subsequent inflammation. The same broad category of peptide presentation participates in both protective immunity and autoimmune susceptibility.

Suppose two HLA class II variants are equally abundant on antigen-presenting cells but differ in their ability to bind a particular self-derived peptide. The variant producing more stable complexes may make the peptide available for recognition over a longer interval. Whether this increases harmful activation depends on whether a corresponding T-cell population survives selection, the presenting cell's activation state and the presence of regulatory control. In another setting, efficient presentation during thymic development might improve deletion of a harmful specificity. It would therefore be simplistic to predict risk solely from stronger peptide binding measured in isolation.

Association studies also require statistical caution. Several neighboring alleles may be inherited together because recombination has not separated them frequently in a population. A variant associated with disease may mark another causal variant in the same chromosomal region. An association can also differ across populations because frequencies and combinations of alleles differ. Mechanistic evidence might come from comparing peptide binding, antigen presentation or activation using cells that differ in the candidate factor while other conditions are controlled. The discovery of an association does not establish that a particular HLA molecule is the only cause of an autoimmune response.

Molecular mimicry is a mechanism through which a response against a foreign antigen can cross-react with a host structure. The term does not require an entire microbial protein to be identical to a human protein. An antibody recognizes a particular three-dimensional arrangement of chemical features, and a T-cell receptor recognizes a surface formed by peptide and presenting molecule. Different sequences can sometimes generate sufficiently similar recognition surfaces. Conversely, sequence similarity alone does not demonstrate cross-reactivity if the relevant structures are processed differently, inaccessible or presented by incompatible MHC molecules.

A biologically plausible mimicry model begins with microbial infection and an appropriate activation context. A lymphocyte responding to the microbe proliferates. Some descendants may recognize a host structure sufficiently well to respond under suitable conditions. Local inflammation can increase presentation of self material or alter the threshold for activation. If regulatory mechanisms fail to restrain the cross-reactive population and the self target is accessible, tissue damage may follow. The original infection can end while the activated immune response persists, because the target belonging to the host remains available. Each step in this chain requires separate evidence; demonstrating only cross-binding is not enough to establish disease causation.

The classical association between group A streptococcal infection and rheumatic fever is often discussed in relation to molecular mimicry. The important principle is that an immune response generated in one antigenic context can react with components of host tissues. The full disease includes multiple cellular and antibody-dependent mechanisms and is not reducible to a single cross-reactive antibody. An exam question may use a simplified model in which a microbial epitope and a cardiac self epitope share recognition features. Such a model teaches the logic of cross-reactivity without implying that every individual infected by the organism develops autoimmune disease.

Environmental events can promote self-directed responses without molecular mimicry. Inflammation may produce increased expression of costimulatory molecules and cytokines, and damaged tissues may release self antigens. This creates conditions in which autoreactive cells that were previously restrained receive stronger activating signals. Such bystander activation differs from mimicry because the stimulus promoting activation need not resemble the self antigen structurally. The two mechanisms can operate together: a cross-reactive cell may become especially dangerous in an inflammatory environment that lowers the threshold for a productive response.

Tissue damage can also broaden an existing immune response. When cells are injured, previously less accessible molecular structures can become available for uptake and presentation. Lymphocytes recognizing additional epitopes may then be recruited into the response. This phenomenon, called epitope spreading, is conceptually different from molecular mimicry. Mimicry involves cross-recognition by a receptor; spreading involves development or expansion of responses to additional epitopes as the antigenic environment changes. A response initially focused on one self structure can become harder to contain if secondary injury repeatedly supplies new targets.

The persistence of a self target introduces a feedback problem. During a successful response to a transient foreign antigen, clearance reduces the stimulus maintaining effector activity. During autoimmunity, a tissue may continue producing the target protein, or normal turnover may repeatedly generate relevant antigenic fragments. Injury can increase antigen availability further. At the same time, activated innate cells can provide signals that reinforce adaptive responses. This creates a potential positive feedback loop. It does not mean that all autoimmune disease is progressive without interruption; inhibitory signals, repair and changes in antigen availability can still produce periods of reduced activity.

Loss of a tissue barrier is another route by which circumstances change without a mutation in the antigen receptor. An antibody that previously had little access to an antigen may reach it after vascular permeability increases. T cells can enter inflamed tissue more efficiently when chemokines and adhesion molecules support recruitment. The same concentration of circulating autoreactive lymphocytes can thus produce different outcomes before and after an inflammatory change. Blood measurements alone may poorly represent what is happening at the tissue interface.

Several rare genetic disorders provide especially clear evidence that individual tolerance mechanisms can be critical. AIRE deficiency illustrates failure of thymic representation of self antigens. Defects affecting FOXP3 can impair regulatory T-cell function and produce severe immune dysregulation. These examples are instructive because the primary abnormality lies in regulation rather than the existence of an unusual foreign pathogen. They should not be treated as evidence that most autoimmune diseases result from mutations in one of these two genes. Common susceptibility is usually more complex, and the rare examples are used here to establish causal biological principles.

Differences between individuals in susceptibility can be studied through family data, population comparisons and controlled animal models. An increased frequency among relatives can reflect shared inherited factors, shared environment or both. Concordance below one hundred percent among genetically identical individuals demonstrates that genotype alone does not fully determine the measured outcome, but it does not identify the missing influence. A controlled experiment altering one immune regulatory gene can provide stronger evidence about the role of that gene in a specific model, although the findings cannot automatically be generalized to every human autoimmune disorder.

The appropriate conceptual comparison is therefore between necessary, sufficient and contributing conditions. A particular HLA allele may contribute to risk without being necessary or sufficient for disease. A particular autoreactive clone may be sufficient to produce injury in an appropriately prepared experimental recipient without being the sole initiating factor in natural disease. A cytokine may be necessary for a specific phase of inflammation without determining the original antigenic specificity. Precision about these terms is especially valuable when evaluating hypothetical gene deletions or rescue experiments.

9. Organ-specific autoimmune injury: tissue destruction and receptor dysfunction

An autoimmune response is described as organ-specific when its major functional consequences are concentrated in a particular organ or tissue. This can occur because the target antigen is preferentially expressed in that organ, because the critical effector gains access there, or because that tissue is especially dependent on the targeted function. The term does not mean that all participating immune cells remain in the organ. Lymphocytes may be activated in draining lymph nodes, antibodies may circulate throughout the body and cytokines may affect multiple locations. Classification refers to the dominant distribution of pathological effects.

Type 1 diabetes mellitus provides an example of immune-mediated loss of a specialized cell population. Pancreatic beta cells produce insulin, which helps regulate the distribution and utilization of metabolic fuels. A sufficient loss of functional beta-cell mass impairs the ability to maintain appropriate blood glucose concentrations. Immune cells directed against beta-cell-associated antigens can participate in inflammation within pancreatic islets. CD8 T cells can recognize relevant peptides presented by MHC class I on beta cells, while CD4 T cells, macrophages and other components can contribute to the local inflammatory environment.

The physiological consequence follows from the relationship between cell number, secretory function and demand. A pancreas does not necessarily lose glucose-regulatory capacity in exact proportion to the earliest loss of beta cells, because remaining cells may retain or adjust their secretory activity. When effective insulin production becomes insufficient for physiological demand, the metabolic consequences become more prominent. A molecular observation such as peptide-specific T-cell activation is therefore not identical to the organ-level endpoint. A complete explanation must connect the immune process to loss of function in the targeted cell population.

Autoantibodies against several beta-cell-associated antigens can appear in type 1 diabetes. Their detection may provide information about an autoimmune process, but the presence of autoantibodies does not demonstrate that the major beta-cell destruction is caused by antibody binding. The contribution of cytotoxic T cells and cellular inflammation makes type 1 diabetes a useful model of predominantly cellular autoimmune injury. An experimental question describing a high antibody concentration and extensive CD8 infiltration should not be answered simply by equating the first measured molecule with the principal effector. Evidence of necessity or sufficiency is needed.

The distinction between biomarker and mediator can be tested conceptually. A biomarker is a measurable feature associated with a biological condition. A causal mediator participates directly in producing the outcome. If removal of an antibody population leaves beta-cell killing unchanged in a carefully controlled model, the antibody population may serve mainly as a marker under those conditions. If blockade of cognate peptide–MHC recognition substantially reduces killing while antibody binding is unchanged, that observation would support a T-cell-dependent mechanism. Neither hypothetical outcome excludes contributions from other components at earlier stages of disease.

An antibody can also produce organ-specific disease without primarily destroying the target cell population. Graves disease illustrates stimulation of an endocrine receptor by autoantibody. The thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor is normally engaged by a hormone whose secretion is controlled by feedback from circulating thyroid hormones. When an antibody activates the receptor, the source of stimulation is partly independent of pituitary control. The feedback loop may reduce TSH concentration, yet receptor activity can remain high because the stimulating antibody persists. This explains why increasing production of a downstream hormone does not necessarily turn off the causal stimulus.

Hashimoto thyroiditis provides a contrasting pattern within the same organ. Here tissue inflammation and loss of functional thyroid tissue can reduce the gland's ability to synthesize thyroid hormones. Antibodies against thyroid-associated proteins can accompany the process, and cellular immunity contributes to tissue injury. The two examples demonstrate that the name of the affected organ does not identify the mechanism or the direction of physiological dysfunction. One autoimmune response may stimulate an existing receptor; another may reduce the number or effectiveness of cells responsible for hormone production.

At a neuromuscular junction, organ-specific dysfunction can arise from reduced efficiency of signaling rather than endocrine disturbance. Autoantibodies against acetylcholine receptors can impair transmission through effects on receptor availability, receptor function and complement-dependent changes to the postsynaptic structure. The electrical impulse in the motor neuron and the release of transmitter may remain relatively intact while the muscle response becomes less reliable. The student can integrate this example with the prior cellular communication subunit by tracing the defect to the receiving side of the signaling interface.

These cases demonstrate three different routes to functional failure: destruction of specialized cells, pathological stimulation of a receptor and impairment of receptor-dependent communication. The routes can be distinguished experimentally by measuring target-cell viability, abundance of receptor, signaling activity and production of the relevant physiological output. A receptor-binding antibody should not be assumed to be antagonistic without a functional test, because antigen binding can stabilize different conformational or signaling states. A tissue with substantial antibody deposition should not automatically be assumed to have lost every antigen-bearing cell.

A final consideration concerns regeneration. If a tissue can replace injured cells efficiently and regulation is restored, functional recovery may be possible. If a specialized population has limited regenerative capacity or a persistent immune response targets newly formed cells, the functional deficit can persist. This relationship is one reason why a short episode of immune activity and a prolonged autoimmune process can have different biological consequences even if they initially recognize the same antigen. The chapter concerns these general tissue principles rather than treatment decisions.

10. Systemic autoimmunity and the example of lupus

The term systemic autoimmunity describes a pattern in which immune injury can involve several organs rather than being predominantly restricted to one tissue. One reason for such distribution is the widespread occurrence of the antigenic material. DNA, RNA and associated proteins are present in many nucleated cells. Their presence in a normal cell does not mean that they are ordinarily available for recognition by circulating antibodies. Nuclear organization, cellular membranes and efficient clearance of dying cells help control the extracellular availability of these materials. When disposal is altered, however, molecular structures normally located within cells can persist in environments where immune components encounter them.

Systemic lupus erythematosus is an instructive example because it connects the disposal of cellular material, adaptive recognition, innate nucleic-acid sensing, complement and tissue injury. The disease is heterogeneous; no single molecular pathway explains every manifestation in every individual. A central immunological pattern involves autoantibodies directed against nuclear or nucleoprotein antigens. Antibodies to double-stranded DNA are a familiar example, although lupus-associated autoantibodies can recognize many other structures. The mere presence of DNA in a cell is not the cause of injury. Changes in accessibility, recognition and immune regulation determine whether this material contributes to inflammation.

During ordinary cell turnover, phagocytes remove apoptotic cells and their fragments. Prompt uptake restricts the accumulation of intracellular components in extracellular locations and can occur in a regulatory context that does not initiate a damaging response. If the amount of dying-cell material exceeds clearance capacity, or if relevant disposal pathways are impaired, nuclear components can remain available for longer. A self-reactive B cell may encounter a structure derived from such material. Appropriate or inappropriate helper-cell signals then influence whether the B cell expands and produces antibody. This sequence illustrates why normal cellular turnover and immune regulation cannot be studied as entirely independent processes.

A nucleoprotein particle provides more than one possible stimulus. Its protein component may yield peptides for presentation to T cells, while its nucleic acid component can interact with certain innate receptors if it reaches the correct cellular compartment. This means a B cell recognizing one component of a particle can internalize other components associated with it. B-cell receptor specificity determines which particles are preferentially captured, while intracellular sensors may influence the activation response after uptake. It is not necessary for an innate receptor and a rearranged antigen receptor to recognize the same chemical epitope for them to cooperate.

Immune complexes containing nucleic acids can also enter cells through Fc receptor-dependent uptake. In an illustrative pathway, plasmacytoid dendritic cells encounter IgG-containing complexes, internalize them and expose their nucleic-acid cargo to endosomal Toll-like receptors. TLR7 recognizes particular RNA-related stimuli, whereas TLR9 recognizes suitable DNA-related motifs. The response can promote production of type I interferons, including interferon alpha. These cytokines influence multiple immune populations and can reinforce an environment favoring immune activation. Type I interferon is a cytokine classification and has no necessary connection to type I hypersensitivity, which is defined by a different mechanism.

The same complexes may participate in tissue injury through extracellular complement and inflammatory-cell activation. In a glomerulus, deposited complexes can be associated with impaired filtration. In small vessels or other susceptible tissues, local activation of inflammatory cells can alter tissue integrity. Because the antigenic material is not limited to a single specialized organ and complexes may circulate, several sites can become involved. Different tissues need not develop identical lesions, because vascular architecture, antigen availability, local clearance and cellular responses differ.

Complement plays a particularly revealing dual role. Early classical-pathway proteins participate in recognition and disposal of cellular debris and complexes. Their inherited deficiency may impair protective clearance and increase susceptibility to lupus-like autoimmunity. Once an inflammatory immune-complex lesion develops, complement activation can also generate products that amplify local injury. These statements are compatible because they refer to different functions and stages of the same system. Removing an early component can impair disposal while reducing some downstream activation; the net consequence depends on which function limits the biological outcome.

Measured complement concentration likewise requires careful interpretation. A low concentration of a circulating component may reflect consumption during activation, a reduced rate of synthesis, inherited deficiency or another change affecting its distribution. If complement fragments are abundant in tissues while intact circulating components fall during an inflammatory episode, consumption is a plausible interpretation. A single low concentration cannot by itself distinguish these possibilities. When an examination provides time-series data, evidence from synthesis, breakdown products and tissue deposition should be integrated rather than treating low complement as a universal synonym for complement deficiency.

Lupus can involve mechanisms beyond immune-complex deposition. Autoantibodies directed against fixed blood-cell antigens may contribute to reductions in particular circulating cell populations. T-cell responses and cytokines can affect inflammatory environments in several tissues. These observations reinforce the distinction between classification of a disease by its dominant distribution and classification of an individual lesion by its effector mechanism. A patient-level label such as systemic autoimmune disease does not imply that every affected cell or tissue is damaged by an identical pathway.

Compare a beta-cell-directed immune response with a response against broadly distributed nuclear material. In the first case, specialized target-cell loss produces a characteristic endocrine deficit. In the second, soluble or particulate antigenic material can be transported and deposited at several sites, with organ-specific consequences determined by tissue physiology. Both responses can involve B and T cells, and both depend on failures in the control of self-reactivity. Their differences are explained by antigen distribution, effector access and target function rather than by assuming two entirely separate categories of adaptive immunity.

Chronicity is especially important in systemic disease. A foreign soluble protein administered on one occasion may eventually be cleared, allowing immune-complex formation to decline. In contrast, self material derived from ordinary cell turnover is continually generated. An inflammatory episode can increase cellular injury and thus release additional antigenic material. A continuing response may therefore supply conditions that sustain itself. Such a loop is a plausible way of understanding persistence without assuming that the initial trigger remains present indefinitely. It also explains why a mechanism that interrupts one stage of a feedback loop may affect later stages without removing the original genetic susceptibility.

In a controlled hypothetical study, investigators compare two groups of cells exposed to complexes containing the same antibody specificity. One group receives complexes carrying nucleic acid, whereas the other receives an otherwise comparable preparation without accessible nucleic acid. If the first group produces more type I interferon and this difference is reduced by blocking relevant endosomal sensing, the result supports a contribution from the nucleic-acid cargo. Controls for uptake, cell number and viability are required. The result would not establish that every manifestation of systemic autoimmunity is interferon dependent. It would identify one pathway through which antigen recognition and innate signaling can cooperate.

11. Quantitative interpretation: binding, clearance and functional reserve

Quantitative models are most useful when they distinguish variables that are often confused. In this chapter, antigen binding, immune-cell activation, mediator concentration and tissue injury are related but separate outcomes. A numerical relationship derived for the first outcome cannot automatically be applied to the last. The purpose of a model is to make an assumption explicit and generate a prediction that can be compared with evidence.

Consider an idealized reversible interaction between a monovalent ligand L and a receptor R, producing a complex RL. The dissociation constant Kd is the equilibrium ratio of free receptor concentration times free ligand concentration to the concentration of the complex. If the total receptor concentration is the sum of free receptor and complex, substitution gives the fractional receptor occupancy as free ligand concentration divided by the sum of Kd and free ligand concentration. This result assumes independent equivalent binding sites, equilibrium and a known free ligand concentration. It is not a formula for the degree of inflammation in a tissue.

Suppose an antigen-binding interaction has a hypothetical Kd of 5 nanomolar. With a free ligand concentration of 5 nanomolar, the fraction occupied is one half. At a ligand concentration of 20 nanomolar, the fraction is four fifths, or 0.8. Increasing free ligand fourfold therefore does not increase occupancy fourfold, because receptor occupancy approaches an upper limit. The result illustrates saturable binding. In comparing two antibodies, a smaller Kd implies a stronger equilibrium association under this simple model, but their biological effects may still differ because their epitopes, isotypes or spatial organization differ.

Now imagine two model allergens that produce the same measured IgE binding-site occupancy. One carries only one accessible epitope per molecule, while the other carries several suitably spaced epitopes. The multivalent allergen may link adjacent receptor-associated IgE molecules more efficiently. Consequently, equal equilibrium occupancy can coexist with different signaling outputs. The simple occupancy formula has not failed at its stated task; it has been used to describe a different variable from the one controlling receptor aggregation. An additional model of receptor organization would be required to predict the activation response.

The balance between mediator release and removal provides a second example. Suppose an activated tissue cell population releases a hypothetical soluble mediator at a constant rate q, expressed as nanomolar per minute, and the mediator disappears at a first-order rate k times its concentration. The concentration approaches a steady value q divided by k when production and removal balance. If q is 30 nanomolar per minute and k is 0.5 per minute, the simplified steady-state value is 60 nanomolar. A reduction in release to 15 with unchanged removal predicts 30 nanomolar instead. A reduction in clearance can produce a high concentration even if secretion is unchanged. These are alternative causal explanations for similar measurements.

The half-life for first-order disappearance after secretion stops is approximately 0.693 divided by k. At k equal to 0.5 per minute, the half-life is about 1.386 minutes. After two half-lives, one quarter of the initial concentration remains. A tissue response may nevertheless persist longer, because downstream changes in endothelial junctions or smooth-muscle activity do not necessarily reverse at the instant mediator concentrations fall. A late inflammatory phase may also depend on additional molecules and recruited cells rather than persistent release of the initial granule contents.

The spatial consequences of tissue swelling can be examined with a simplified physical relationship. For steady laminar flow of an incompressible Newtonian fluid through a straight rigid cylindrical tube, the resistance is proportional to viscosity and tube length and inversely proportional to the fourth power of the radius. If length and viscosity are held constant and the radius falls from 2 millimeters to 1.5 millimeters, the predicted ratio of new resistance to old resistance is the fourth power of 2 divided by 1.5. The numerical value is approximately 3.16. The relationship demonstrates why relatively modest narrowing can have a large effect on flow. Real airway geometry and airflow are more complicated, so this estimate is an illustrative model rather than a physiological prediction for a patient.

The same principle of nonlinear functional consequences appears in tissues composed of specialized cells. A simplified beta-cell population might initially contain 100 equal functional units, each contributing one unit of secretory capacity. If 25 cells are lost and the remaining cells increase individual output from one to 1.2 units, total capacity becomes 75 times 1.2, or 90 units. The loss of a quarter of the cells has been partially compensated at the level of total secretion. If further loss leaves only 40 cells, even an output of 1.2 per cell yields 48 units. A measurement of organ function therefore cannot be converted directly into a cell count without assumptions about individual-cell performance.

A similar model can be applied to receptor-dependent signaling. If an antibody reduces the number of available receptors from 100 to 50, the maximal signal need not fall by exactly 50 percent. Some biological systems contain a receptor reserve, meaning that a near-maximal response may be obtained without activation of every available receptor. Other systems operate closer to the limit of their functional capacity. In an experiment, measuring receptor abundance and the response to increasing concentrations of an ordinary ligand can distinguish loss of receptors from a change in downstream signaling efficiency. The interpretation requires the shape of the response curve, not merely its value at one ligand concentration.

Population-level risk requires a different quantitative framework. Suppose a hypothetical susceptibility allele is found in 60 of 100 individuals with a defined autoimmune disease and in 30 of 100 unaffected controls. The odds of carrying the allele among cases are 60 divided by 40, or 1.5. The corresponding odds among controls are 30 divided by 70, or about 0.429. The ratio is 3.5. This odds ratio describes an association in the hypothetical sample. It does not mean that 60 percent of allele carriers will develop disease, because the numbers of cases and controls were selected by the study design rather than sampled according to population disease prevalence.

These examples connect molecular and population reasoning without conflating them. Occupancy is a fraction of sites, concentration is an amount per volume, a clearance constant has dimensions of reciprocal time, flow resistance relates pressure difference to flow, and an odds ratio is dimensionless. Confusion of units can expose a mistaken calculation. More importantly, a calculated value should be linked to the biological variable it actually describes. The relation between that variable and disease may require additional evidence.

12. Designing experiments that distinguish the mechanisms

An immune-mediated lesion can be investigated by asking which component supplies antigen specificity, which component executes tissue injury and which conditions make the target vulnerable. In a well-designed study these questions are tested separately. Counting infiltrating cells can identify populations associated with a lesion, but cannot by itself determine whether they initiated injury, arrived in response to it or contribute to resolution. A mechanistic investigation uses comparisons that change one relevant factor while retaining others as far as possible.

Serum-transfer and cell-transfer models illustrate the difference between a soluble and a cellular source of specificity. If serum from a sensitized donor transfers an antigen-specific response to a suitable recipient, a transferable soluble component is implicated. A second step can test whether depletion of a particular immunoglobulin removes that activity and whether restoration of the purified component rescues it. Controls with irrelevant antibody and unrelated antigen establish specificity. If the response requires viable antigen-specific T cells, cell transfer rather than serum transfer may reproduce the phenotype. These experiments have interpretive limits, including the recipient's existing immune status, antigen presentation and compatibility.

To distinguish type I sensitization from nonspecific mast-cell activation, a hypothetical investigator measures mediator release in cells exposed to a defined multivalent antigen. Antigen challenge produces 6 percent of total stored mediator release in unsensitized cells, 8 percent after loading with irrelevant IgE and 72 percent after loading with specific IgE. Blocking FcεRI-associated signaling reduces the last value to 12 percent. A separate receptor-independent activation condition produces 65 percent release despite the block. This pattern supports a receptor-associated defect rather than wholesale loss of secretory competence. The small background release is important: it prevents interpreting every detected molecule as proof of specific activation.

These data are illustrative, not reported historical measurements. The percentage denominator should be defined as the total amount of mediator measured in a comparable cell population, including the amount retained after stimulation. If a treatment reduces the initial stored mediator quantity, the percentage released can change even when the absolute extracellular concentration does not. Appropriate measurements of cell number, viability and total granule content are therefore necessary. A control that activates secretion independently of FcεRI helps locate the defect, but it does not identify a particular kinase without more selective evidence.

A different hypothetical comparison examines a fixed antigen on a target-cell surface. Binding of an intact IgG molecule and its antigen-binding fragment is first confirmed under matched conditions. Intact IgG plus Fc receptor-bearing effector cells causes 70 percent target removal; the fragment plus the same effector cells causes 10 percent removal; effector cells without specific antibody cause 8 percent removal. This pattern supports an important role for the antibody Fc region in target removal. It would not prove that complement is involved if complement was absent from all conditions. The conclusion must match the manipulated variable rather than import a familiar pathway by association.

If instead the antigen is a soluble protein, a study could distinguish aggregate formation from inflammatory consequences by tracking antigen, antibody and complement independently. A condition that preserves formation of complexes but impairs their disposal may increase tissue deposition. A condition that preserves deposition but prevents recruitment of neutrophils may reduce injury. In both cases, the number of complexes initially formed could remain unchanged. This illustrates why a single readout of antibody–antigen binding cannot distinguish type III pathology from an ordinary protective clearance response.

For a type IV response, the restriction of T-cell recognition can be exploited experimentally. A population of antigen-specific T cells is cultured conceptually with target cells expressing either the relevant presenting molecule or an alternative form unable to display the cognate peptide. If the first target population is preferentially killed, the result supports recognition dependent on the relevant peptide–MHC combination. A target cell must also remain susceptible to the downstream death pathway. A failure to kill could arise from altered antigen processing, altered MHC expression or resistance to cytotoxic effectors. Measuring peptide–MHC display and target susceptibility separately can distinguish these explanations.

Genetic perturbations can reveal different stages of tolerance. In a model lacking a thymic regulator of tissue-associated antigen expression, investigators may compare the abundance of particular autoreactive T cells leaving the thymus with their abundance in wild-type controls. A larger escaping population would support a defect at the developmental stage. If the same number of autoreactive cells leaves the thymus in two groups but expansion in peripheral tissues differs after an identical stimulus, the difference could instead involve peripheral activation or suppression. Measuring both stages avoids mistakenly identifying every autoimmune phenotype as a central selection defect.

A rescue experiment is particularly informative when its target is carefully chosen. Restoration of an absent regulatory cell population can reduce autoimmune manifestations in a model where regulatory deficiency contributed to injury. The rescued population may act at several points in the immune response, so successful rescue does not establish that all affected organs share exactly the same effector mechanism. Similarly, restoring presentation of a particular self antigen in the thymus could reduce the emergence of cells recognizing that antigen without repairing unrelated defects in innate signaling. Rescue is evidence of causal participation, not proof of universal sufficiency.

A final comparison concerns specificity versus generalized inflammation. If a regulatory mutation increases responses to many unrelated antigens, the primary defect may be a broad activation threshold rather than creation of one novel antigen specificity. If a mutation selectively changes presentation of one peptide, a narrower pattern might occur. Both can produce inflammatory tissue changes, but the expected breadth differs. A strong experimental answer should specify an observation that would distinguish the proposed alternatives, such as responses to unrelated antigens, counts of defined lymphocyte clones or changes in local tissue access.

The applications of this reasoning extend beyond classifying diseases. An engineered antibody intended to bind a receptor may require a constant region that recruits additional effector functions, or it may need to avoid those functions so that receptor binding changes signaling without damaging target cells. Studies of immunological tolerance use regulatory perturbations to determine how activation thresholds are maintained. Studies of immune complexes examine how changes in clearance affect tissue exposure. Each application draws on the same separation of binding, activation, effector mechanism and tissue response. The next chapter applies this framework to the protective and potentially damaging immune responses generated during bacterial and parasitic infections.

Worked numerical examples

1. Binding occupancy

With Kd = 5 nM and free ligand = 20 nM, θ = 20/(5+20) = 0.80. The fraction of occupied independent sites is 80%; receptor clustering is not predicted.

2. Immune complex steady state

F = 12 units/h and k = 0.6 h⁻¹ give F/k = 20 units. Halving k to 0.3 h⁻¹ gives 40 units, assuming unchanged formation and first-order removal.

3. Mediator half-life

For k = 0.5 min⁻¹, t½ = ln(2)/0.5 = 1.386 min. Two half-lives leave 25% of the initial mediator concentration if secretion stops.

If radius changes from 2 to 1.5 mm, R₂/R₁ = (2/1.5)⁴ = 3.1605. This assumes a straight rigid cylinder and laminar Newtonian flow.

75 surviving cells each producing 1.2 output units yield 90 units, or 90% of initial 100. Remaining cell function can partly compensate for loss.

At q = 30 nM/min and k = 0.5 min⁻¹, C = q/k = 60 nM. Halving q gives 30 nM if k stays constant.

With 80 units initially and a 2 h half-life, 6 h gives 80×(1/2)³ = 10 units. Disappearance from blood does not establish safe disposal.

Question 1

Which feature defines the classical type I hypersensitivity effector phase?

  1. Direct binding of IgM to red-cell antigen
  2. Deposition of soluble antigen–IgG complexes
  3. Allergen-induced clustering of IgE associated with FcεRI
  4. Recognition of peptide–MHC by a CTL
Answer and explanation

C. Allergen-induced clustering of IgE associated with FcεRI

IgE provides the antigen specificity and mast-cell receptor clustering triggers secretion. The other options describe type II, III and IV mechanisms, respectively.

Question 2

Which molecule is principally involved in inducing IgE class switching in an appropriate helper-cell context?

  1. IL-4
  2. IFN-γ
  3. C5a
  4. Perforin
Answer and explanation

A. IL-4

IL-4, with suitable B-cell activation including CD40 signaling, promotes epsilon switching. IFN-γ has a major role in type 1 cellular immunity; C5a is a complement fragment and perforin is a cytotoxic effector.

Question 3

Which property allows IgE to sensitize a mast cell before antigen exposure?

  1. Binding of its Fab region to the receptor's cytoplasmic tail
  2. Covalent attachment to MHC class II
  3. Cleavage into C3b
  4. High-affinity binding of its Fc region to FcεRI
Answer and explanation

D. High-affinity binding of its Fc region to FcεRI

FcεRI binds the Fc portion of IgE while the Fab regions remain available for antigen recognition. MHC and complement cleavage are unrelated to this receptor loading.

Question 4

Which is a preformed mast-cell granule mediator?

  1. Prostaglandin D2
  2. Histamine
  3. Leukotriene C4
  4. Newly transcribed IL-4
Answer and explanation

B. Histamine

Histamine is stored in granules and can be released rapidly. The lipid mediators are newly generated from membrane-derived precursors, and transcription requires time.

Question 5

In the classical four-type scheme, an antibody that stimulates a host hormone receptor belongs principally to which type?

  1. Type I
  2. Type II
  3. Type III
  4. Type IV
Answer and explanation

B. Type II

The receptor is a fixed cell-surface target and antibody alters its function. Cell lysis is not necessary; some extended schemes call this functional response type V.

Question 6

Which complement fragment directly acts as an important opsonin on many target surfaces?

  1. C5a
  2. C3a
  3. C9
  4. C3b
Answer and explanation

D. C3b

C3b tags targets for complement receptor recognition. C3a and C5a are inflammatory signaling fragments, while C9 participates in terminal pore formation.

Question 7

Which event best distinguishes type III from classical type II injury?

  1. Formation of complexes with initially soluble antigen, followed by deposition or local accumulation
  2. IgG recognition of an antigen fixed in a cell membrane
  3. IgE receptor cross-linking
  4. CTL recognition of a host peptide
Answer and explanation

A. Formation of complexes with initially soluble antigen, followed by deposition or local accumulation

The physical origin of the antigen–antibody complex is the key. A fixed cell or matrix target suggests type II, even if complement is involved in both.

Question 8

Which cell can provide Fc receptor-mediated clearance of IgG-coated erythrocytes?

  1. Mature erythrocyte itself
  2. Naive B cell acting through its BCR
  3. Macrophage
  4. Thymic epithelial cell
Answer and explanation

C. Macrophage

Macrophages express relevant Fc receptors and can remove coated cells. Mature red cells are not phagocytes, and the other cells serve different immunological roles.

Question 9

Which antibody class normally crosses the placenta efficiently through FcRn?

  1. IgG
  2. IgM
  3. IgE
  4. Secretory IgA
Answer and explanation

A. IgG

FcRn transports IgG. IgM generally lacks this mode of placental transfer, and neither IgE nor secretory IgA is the typical transferred class.

Question 10

Which is the defining antigen-specific component of classical delayed-type hypersensitivity?

  1. Serum IgE alone
  2. Circulating C3b alone
  3. Sensitized T lymphocytes
  4. Soluble IgG complexes alone
Answer and explanation

C. Sensitized T lymphocytes

T cells recognize peptide–MHC and organize cellular inflammation. The other listed factors can participate in different antibody-mediated patterns.

Question 11

Which cytokine is a major signal linking Th1 cells to macrophage activation?

  1. IL-4
  2. IFN-γ
  3. Erythropoietin
  4. Histamine
Answer and explanation

B. IFN-γ

IFN-γ promotes macrophage activation in relevant contexts. IL-4 is associated with type 2 immunity, erythropoietin regulates erythropoiesis and histamine is not a cytokine.

Question 12

Which target-recognition interaction is characteristic of a conventional CD8 CTL?

  1. Free intact antigen bound directly to IgE
  2. Antibody Fc attached to CD16 as its sole specificity
  3. Complement-bound polysaccharide
  4. Cognate peptide–MHC I

D. Cognate peptide–MHC I

The TCR of a conventional CD8 cell recognizes peptide–MHC I; Fc recognition through CD16 is characteristic of NK-mediated ADCC, not the TCR mechanism.

D. Receptor editing

Editing changes receptor specificity during development through further rearrangement. Switching changes the heavy-chain constant region; mutation is a distinct activated B-cell process.

Question 14

  2. Promote display of many tissue-associated self antigens during tolerance induction
  3. Stimulate IgE receptor clustering
  4. Produce antibodies against nuclear antigens

B. Promote display of many tissue-associated self antigens during tolerance induction

AIRE supports expression of many peripheral tissue antigens in thymic epithelial cells. It does not directly execute any of the three effector functions listed.

  2. TdT
  3. FOXP3
  4. IgE heavy chain
Answer and explanation

C. FOXP3

FOXP3 is a key regulatory T-cell transcription factor. C9 is complement; TdT contributes to junctional diversity; IgE is an immunoglobulin class.

Question 16

Molecular mimicry is best defined as which process?

  2. Development of a new unrelated clone solely because of cell division
  3. Absence of all MHC molecules
  4. Deposition of complement without antigen

A. Cross-recognition of foreign and self molecular structures by an immune receptor

The recognition surfaces can be structurally similar without identical full sequences. Expansion of responses to additional epitopes is epitope spreading, not necessarily mimicry.

Question 17

Which pattern most clearly illustrates systemic immune-complex autoimmunity?

  1. IgG binding a single red-cell alloantigen
  2. Nuclear antigen-containing complexes associated with inflammatory lesions in several organs
  3. A local pollen-triggered mast-cell reaction
  4. An isolated CTL response to a viral peptide
Answer and explanation

B. Nuclear antigen-containing complexes associated with inflammatory lesions in several organs

The first describes the broad distribution typical of an important mechanism in lupus. Alloimmunity, allergy and antiviral immunity do not in themselves establish systemic autoimmunity.

Question 18

Which statement about autoantibodies is correct?

  1. Their detection alone does not establish that they cause tissue damage
  2. They invariably cause complement-dependent lysis
  3. They are always IgE
  4. They are absent in all T-cell-mediated autoimmune disorders
Answer and explanation

A. Their detection alone does not establish that they cause tissue damage

Autoantibodies can be biomarkers or causal mediators, and multiple mechanisms can coexist. Their isotype and functional consequences must be independently established.

Question 19

Which process is a peripheral tolerance mechanism?

  1. Light-chain editing in an immature bone-marrow B cell
  2. Positive selection of developing thymocytes
  3. V(D)J gene rearrangement before selection
  4. Regulatory T-cell suppression of activation in a lymph node
Answer and explanation

D. Regulatory T-cell suppression of activation in a lymph node

Regulatory suppression in a peripheral site is peripheral tolerance. Editing and thymic selection act during development; receptor generation itself creates diversity.

Question 20

Which statement correctly relates hypersensitivity and autoimmunity?

  1. All hypersensitivity is autoimmune
  2. All autoimmune injury is type III
  3. Hypersensitivity classes describe injury mechanisms, whereas autoimmunity specifies self-directed recognition
  4. Immunodeficiency and autoimmunity are mutually exclusive
Answer and explanation

C. Hypersensitivity classes describe injury mechanisms, whereas autoimmunity specifies self-directed recognition

The two classifications answer different questions. Self-directed disease can use different effector pathways, and defects in regulation or clearance can coexist with reduced protective immunity.

Bypass stimulation shows that cells retain secretion competence; the defect is associated with the receptor-dependent route rather than terminal secretion alone.

  3. 3.0

B. 0.75

A. Multivalent binding can aggregate receptor-associated IgE

Receptor clustering and individual site occupancy are distinct. Ligand geometry can alter the former even when the latter is held equal.

Nucleic-acid-containing complexes enter plasmacytoid dendritic cells. An endosomal TLR inhibitor reduces interferon production without changing uptake. What is best inferred?

  1. The complexes were not internalized
  2. IgE-dependent degranulation is established
  3. Every form of lupus tissue injury is abolished
  4. Endosomal sensing contributes downstream of complex uptake
Answer and explanation

D. Endosomal sensing contributes downstream of complex uptake

Unchanged uptake localizes the effect to a downstream step; a reduction in interferon does not establish that all disease mechanisms are removed.

Question 44

A disease model has autoantibodies and T-cell infiltration. Antibody depletion does not alter beta-cell death, whereas cognate peptide–MHC blockade reduces death. Which conclusion best fits?

  1. The detected antibody is necessarily the principal killer
  2. Cell-mediated killing is supported as the dominant measured injury route
  3. The disease cannot be autoimmune
  4. The lesion must involve immune-complex deposition
Answer and explanation

B. Cell-mediated killing is supported as the dominant measured injury route

The perturbations distinguish biomarker presence from causal participation in the measured outcome. Other roles for antibodies or B cells are not excluded.

Question 47

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. All immune inflammation is autoimmune.

Correction: Autoimmunity requires self-directed adaptive recognition; inflammation can respond to microbes or direct injury. Why it is tempting: Students conflate inflammation with antigen specificity. Exam reasoning: Identify whether self antigen is the target.

2. IgE binding FcεRI automatically causes degranulation.

Correction: Receptor clustering and signaling are generally required. Why it is tempting: Priming is confused with elicitation. Exam reasoning: Compare monovalent and multivalent antigen.

3. All anaphylaxis is IgE-dependent.

Correction: Non-IgE mechanisms may also produce anaphylaxis. Why it is tempting: A clinical phenotype is mistaken for a mechanism. Exam reasoning: Look for evidence of specific IgE and receptor dependence.

4. Late-phase allergy must be type IV.

Correction: Recruitment after IgE activation can remain part of type I. Why it is tempting: Time is used as the only classification variable. Exam reasoning: Track the initiating effector.

5. Type II always causes lysis.

Correction: Fixed-target antibodies may alter receptors without killing cells. Why it is tempting: Cytotoxic terminology is taken literally. Exam reasoning: Distinguish Graves-like stimulation from cell destruction.

6. IgM always crosses the placenta.

Correction: IgG is normally transferred efficiently through FcRn. Why it is tempting: Antibody classes are treated as interchangeable. Exam reasoning: Predict fetal exposure from isotype.

7. All complement damage requires C9.

Correction: Opsonization and recruitment may occur independently of terminal pores. Why it is tempting: The cascade is remembered only as lysis. Exam reasoning: Compare C3b and C5a effects.

8. Every antibody deposit is type III.

Correction: Antibody against fixed ECM antigen can be type II. Why it is tempting: Final deposit location hides antigen origin. Exam reasoning: Determine whether antigen was initially soluble.

9. Complement deficiency always protects against lupus.

Correction: Early components help dispose of debris and complexes. Why it is tempting: Protective clearance is overlooked. Exam reasoning: Predict outcomes of impaired C1q function.

10. The largest complex is always the most pathogenic.

Correction: Clearance, localization and complex properties determine injury. Why it is tempting: Precipitation tests are overgeneralized. Exam reasoning: Compare formation and removal independently.

11. Any delayed reaction is type IV.

Correction: Antibody and complex-mediated reactions can also develop later. Why it is tempting: Timing is used instead of causal effector. Exam reasoning: Use transfer or depletion evidence.

12. All type IV reactions require macrophages.

Correction: CTLs can directly kill presenting cells. Why it is tempting: Classical Th1 models are generalized. Exam reasoning: Predict a perforin or MHC I defect.

13. Positive selection removes all self recognition.

Correction: It selects appropriate self-MHC recognition; negative selection restrains strongly self-reactive cells. Why it is tempting: Two thymic tests are confused. Exam reasoning: Identify consequences of altered self-peptide display.

14. AIRE controls every self antigen.

Correction: It controls expression of many but not all tissue-associated antigens. Why it is tempting: A key regulator is mistaken for a universal switch. Exam reasoning: Predict antigen-specific escape rather than total failure.

15. CD25 uniquely identifies Tregs.

Correction: Activated conventional T cells can express CD25 too. Why it is tempting: A useful marker is treated as exclusive. Exam reasoning: Interpret FOXP3 and functional suppression together.

16. HLA predisposition is deterministic.

Correction: Risk depends on other genes and environmental context. Why it is tempting: Association is mistaken for causation. Exam reasoning: Interpret odds ratios rather than certainty.

17. Molecular mimicry equals epitope spreading.

Correction: Mimicry is cross-recognition; spreading recruits additional specificities. Why it is tempting: Both increase self-reactive responses but through different routes. Exam reasoning: Track whether one or several clones are involved.

18. Autoantibodies always mediate injury.

Correction: They can be biomarkers, causal mediators or both. Why it is tempting: Association is treated as mechanism. Exam reasoning: Use depletion and rescue to infer causation.

19. Type I interferon is type I hypersensitivity.

Correction: The shared number refers to unrelated classification schemes. Why it is tempting: Terminology is superficially matched. Exam reasoning: Recognize the interferon and IgE pathways separately.

20. Organ-specific disease means all immune cells remain in the organ.

Correction: It describes the major distribution of functional injury. Why it is tempting: Anatomical outcome is confused with immune-cell movement. Exam reasoning: Connect systemic lymphocyte activation to local targets.

Revision and mastery

Recognition, activation, effector mechanism and tissue vulnerability are distinct steps. Type I links allergen-specific IgE to cellular mediator release. Type II focuses antibody effects on fixed structures. Type III concerns harmful handling of complexes. Type IV depends on antigen-specific T cells. Autoimmunity concerns failure to restrain self-directed responses, and may use multiple hypersensitivity mechanisms. Central tolerance shapes the repertoire; peripheral tolerance controls activation. Genetic susceptibility and inflammatory contexts alter the probability that harmful responses emerge.

  • Odds ratio = ad/bc in a two-by-two case-control table.
