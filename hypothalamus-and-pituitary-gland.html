1. Neural control becomes endocrine control

A mammal that loses water must conserve the water remaining in its circulation. It can reduce the amount lost through urine, but the kidney has no direct knowledge of the amount of water consumed earlier in the day. Cells in the brain respond to changes in the concentration of extracellular solutes and to information about the circulation. They communicate with the kidney through a circulating chemical signal. In another context the organism must adjust growth to the availability of nutrients, or regulate the adrenal cortex in response to a threat. These tasks illustrate why a central nervous structure can influence organs that are physically distant from it. The hypothalamus is a major site at which neural information is converted into endocrine instructions.

An endocrine axis is a linked series of regulatory organs. In the simplest axis a hypothalamic signal controls a pituitary hormone, the pituitary hormone controls a peripheral endocrine gland, and the peripheral hormone modifies the activity of both upstream structures. This organisation gives the organism more than one opportunity to regulate hormone production. For instance, a change in circulating thyroid hormone can alter pituitary secretion directly as well as modifying the hypothalamic drive that the pituitary receives. The arrangement is not merely a chain of commands. It is a control circuit in which the output influences its own production.

The designation of the pituitary as the master gland is useful only as a starting point. The hypothalamus controls many pituitary functions, but neither structure governs every endocrine cell. Insulin secretion responds directly to nutrients and other signals within the endocrine pancreas. Parathyroid cells detect extracellular calcium, and aldosterone secretion is strongly governed by potassium and angiotensin II. A student should not infer pituitary dependence from the fact that an organ secretes a hormone. Equally, direct feedback by a peripheral hormone does not make the hypothalamus irrelevant. An axis is coordinated by multiple inputs operating simultaneously.

The functional distinction within the pituitary is particularly important. Some pituitary cells manufacture peptide or glycoprotein hormones and release them in response to blood-borne hypothalamic factors. In contrast, certain neurons manufacture hormones in their cell bodies in the hypothalamus and release them from axon endings in the posterior pituitary. Both routes ultimately deliver hormones into the blood, yet interruption of the blood vessels of the stalk has different consequences from interruption of the axons. The anatomical distinction therefore becomes a powerful tool for interpreting experiments and disease patterns.

The term neurosecretion describes a specialised form of secretion in which a neuron manufactures a substance that can function as a hormone. The concept bridges two apparently different styles of physiological communication. A neuron can rapidly conduct an electrical impulse over a long distance without requiring the signal molecule itself to diffuse down the entire axon. An endocrine cell releases molecules that travel with blood, and the target cells may be located throughout the body. A neurosecretory neuron uses the first method to control the timing of the second. The hormone becomes a circulating representation of information initially processed by a neural circuit. The circulating hormone has an effect only if the recipient cell expresses a functional receptor and the associated cellular machinery.

A simple analogy is a central control room that sends instructions to local controllers, which in turn control machines. Unlike an artificial controller, however, the biological system has no single executive with complete knowledge of all events. Neural populations integrate partial sensory information, each hormone has its own synthesis and clearance kinetics, and target tissues have local mechanisms that adjust their responses. The analogy is useful for distinguishing regulatory levels, but it fails if it encourages the idea that the hypothalamus deliberately chooses a fixed target concentration for every hormone. Endocrine regulation emerges from interacting biological processes, many of which vary with time and with the organism's state.

The same central signal can cause different outcomes in different developmental contexts. For example, the reproductive axis is relatively quiescent during much of childhood and subsequently becomes active in puberty. The pituitary and gonads are already present before full reproductive maturity, so the mere existence of the anatomical components does not explain the adult pattern. The manner in which the hypothalamus releases GnRH, the sensitivity of target cells and the influence of feedback signals change. This is an example of a functional transition within an existing anatomical system rather than the appearance of an entirely new endocrine gland.

An endocrine signal may be permissive rather than directly responsible for the final physiological event. Adequate thyroid hormone availability supports normal growth and metabolism, but an increase in thyroid hormone by itself cannot replace the particular function of GH, IGF-1, dietary amino acids or local cartilage signals. A perturbation that reduces pituitary function can therefore affect growth by more than one route. In a broad pituitary failure model, it would be inappropriate to attribute all impaired growth to GH deficiency if the thyroid axis is also compromised. Selective loss of one hormone and simultaneous loss of several hormones should be treated as different experimental conditions.

2. Anatomy, embryological origins and the two communication routes

The hypothalamus lies in the diencephalon, bordering the third ventricle. Its neuronal populations contribute to regulation of food intake, thirst, temperature, biological rhythms, autonomic responses and reproduction. It communicates with the pituitary through the infundibulum, often called the pituitary stalk. The pituitary sits below the hypothalamus within the bony sella turcica. This location provides support but also limits the space available when tissue enlarges. The optic chiasm is nearby, so an expanding pituitary lesion can influence vision without the affected hormone acting directly on the visual system.

The adenohypophysis develops from oral ectoderm through Rathke's pouch. Its major endocrine portion is the pars distalis; the pars tuberalis extends around the stalk, and the pars intermedia is relatively inconspicuous in adult humans. The neurohypophysis develops from neural ectoderm as an outgrowth from the ventral diencephalon. It includes the pars nervosa and the infundibular region. Different embryological origins explain the striking contrast between glandular endocrine cells in the anterior lobe and axons, supporting cells and neurovascular terminals in the posterior lobe. In comparative physiology the relative development of the intermediate lobe varies among vertebrates. One should therefore avoid assuming that the adult human arrangement represents every species.

Many hypothalamic neurons involved in anterior pituitary regulation are described as parvocellular neurosecretory neurons. Their axons project to the median eminence, a neurovascular interface near the base of the hypothalamus. Unlike a typical synaptic ending that releases neurotransmitter into a narrow cleft adjacent to another neuron, these terminals discharge regulatory molecules into the extracellular space near specialised capillaries. Molecules reach the bloodstream and travel a short distance to the anterior pituitary. The same action potential and calcium-dependent exocytosis principles studied in nervous physiology help explain this release, but the destination of the message is different.

Large magnocellular neurosecretory neurons, especially in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, send long axons towards the posterior pituitary. Their peptide-containing secretory granules undergo axonal transport from the soma. When firing reaches their terminals, calcium entry promotes exocytosis and the hormones enter the general circulation. The posterior pituitary contains pituicytes, supporting glial cells that interact with the axons and terminals. It is not simply a passive cavity of stored hormone; nevertheless its major endocrine outputs are manufactured by hypothalamic neurons rather than by the pituicytes.

The supraoptic nucleus is strongly associated with vasopressin-producing magnocellular neurons, and the paraventricular nucleus contains prominent oxytocin-producing populations. Both nuclei contain neurons producing each peptide. The paraventricular nucleus also contains parvocellular populations with different projections and regulatory functions, including cells involved in CRH secretion. A nucleus is an anatomical grouping, not a guarantee that every neuron within it has the same transmitter, hormone or axonal target. In an experiment, selective manipulation of a defined cell population is therefore more informative than treating a whole nucleus as a single uniform cell.

The hypothalamus contains regions whose functional roles overlap. The suprachiasmatic nucleus receives information about environmental light through retinal pathways and participates in circadian coordination. The arcuate region contains neuronal populations involved in nutritional and endocrine regulation, including dopaminergic neurons that help regulate prolactin and neurons involved in GHRH signalling. The median eminence is not an ordinary anterior pituitary hormone-producing region. It is a specialised interface where several hypothalamic neurosecretory populations terminate near vessels. Distinguishing a neuronal cell body, its axon terminal and the receiving endocrine cell is more important than memorising an excessively detailed map of individual nuclei.

An anatomical pathway may be demonstrated by tracing a population of cells and examining the destinations of its axons. Identifying a labelled projection from hypothalamus to posterior pituitary supports direct neural connectivity. It does not alone show that those fibres contain oxytocin or vasopressin, or establish that their electrical activity causes peptide release. Combining anatomical tracing with immunolabelling for the peptide and physiological measurement of release gives much stronger evidence. Conversely, identifying a peptide in a posterior pituitary tissue extract does not establish local synthesis, because a transported peptide can accumulate at a site far from its synthesis. Localising precursor RNA to the relevant neuronal cell bodies helps resolve this distinction.

Developmental origin predicts broad tissue organisation but does not determine all physiological functions in isolation. An endocrine cell derived from oral ectoderm becomes capable of releasing hormones through a differentiation programme that includes transcriptional changes, production of peptide precursors and acquisition of secretory machinery. A neuron derived from neural ectoderm can also become endocrine in function if its terminals deliver hormone into blood. The embryological observation therefore explains the contrast between the anterior glandular lobe and posterior neural structure, while the actual mode of secretion must be established by functional evidence.

The proximity of the pituitary to the optic chiasm provides an example of a structural consequence distinct from hormonal dysregulation. An expanding mass can compress nearby neural structures and disturb pathways carrying visual information. A patient or experimental model might have signs due to a mechanical disturbance, a failure of normal endocrine tissue and an excess of a secreted hormone at the same time. In mechanistic reasoning these should be separated rather than regarded as interchangeable symptoms of hormone excess. A selective increase in hormone production without enlargement would not necessarily reproduce an anatomical compression effect.

3. Hypophyseal portal circulation

The word portal describes a vascular arrangement in which blood flows from one capillary network through connecting vessels to another capillary network before returning to the general venous circulation. At the median eminence branches of the superior hypophyseal arteries form a primary capillary plexus. Hypothalamic regulatory factors enter this plexus, and portal vessels carry the blood towards a secondary plexus in the anterior pituitary. Endocrine cells within the anterior lobe respond to the delivered factors and release their own hormones into the circulation. The important feature is the sequence of two capillary networks connected by a local vascular route.

Imagine that a small amount of a releasing hormone enters the entire circulating blood volume before arriving at the pituitary. The amount reaching a specific pituitary cell would depend on dilution, degradation, distribution and blood flow. Local delivery through portal vessels reduces the initial volume of dilution and allows substantial signalling at relatively low overall production. The blood is still carrying a chemical hormone, and the action remains neuroendocrine. Local vascular transport does not imply direct electrical transmission to the anterior pituitary.

A portal vessel does not operate like a permanent reservoir. As blood flows through, concentration changes with the amount secreted at the median eminence, flow rate and clearance from the system. If hypothalamic secretion increases briefly, a transient increase in concentration can reach the anterior pituitary. A simplified relation for a continuously supplied substance is concentration increment approximately equal to secretion rate divided by flow. Suppose, for illustration, that a factor enters a stream at 40 picograms per minute and the stream flows at 2 millilitres per minute. In the absence of pre-existing factor, local loss and recirculation, the calculated increment is 20 picograms per millilitre. Doubling the flow while keeping secretion unchanged would halve the increment. Actual endocrine tissues include exchange, pulsatility and heterogeneous vascular flow; this calculation illustrates dilution rather than giving measured human portal concentrations.

Anatomical continuity alone does not establish the chemical identity of the factor transported through the vessels. It must be combined with physiological tests. If an anterior pituitary preparation releases a hormone when exposed to a hypothalamic extract, that supports the presence of a stimulatory substance, but the extract may contain many components. Comparing purified candidates, inactive controls, concentration dependence and receptor specificity strengthens the inference. Conversely, failure of an extract to stimulate a hormone could reflect degradation of the active factor or unsuitable preparation conditions rather than absence of regulation.

Severing the stalk is a powerful conceptual experiment but an imperfect selective test. It can disrupt portal blood flow and descending axons together, damage tissue or affect blood supply. If the anterior gland survives and is separated from hypothalamic delivery, several stimulating signals are lost and their pituitary outputs generally decline. Prolactin may rise because an inhibitory input is lost. Posterior secretion can also be disrupted through loss of axonal continuity. These predictions depend on tissue viability and the nature of the injury. A result after complete stalk injury cannot be attributed exclusively to interruption of portal vessels without additional controls.

The median eminence and pituitary are specialised regions where neurovascular exchange supports endocrine signalling. Fenestrated capillaries allow effective exchange of signalling molecules at these interfaces. This does not mean that the entire hypothalamus is freely exposed to circulating macromolecules. Most central nervous tissue has different vascular barrier properties, and access varies with local organisation and the substance being considered. The endocrine interfaces solve a physiological problem: signals must move between brain tissue and circulating blood without treating every brain capillary as equivalent to a systemic endocrine vascular bed.

The superior hypophyseal arterial supply feeds the primary plexus, while the posterior pituitary also has a substantial direct arterial supply, notably from the inferior hypophyseal arteries. It is therefore inaccurate to draw the two pituitary lobes as successive hormone-processing chambers through which all blood necessarily passes. A schematic showing regulatory molecules passing through the portal vessels to the anterior lobe captures one functional route; it should not be mistaken for a complete representation of every vessel supplying the pituitary region. There are anatomical variants and additional vascular connections that are unnecessary for basic examination reasoning.

Consider a hypothetical pair of experiments in which two identical viable anterior pituitary preparations receive an equal quantity of TRH. In the first preparation the factor is delivered into a small perfusion stream immediately upstream of the tissue. In the second it is distributed throughout a much larger perfusion reservoir before being supplied. If loss by degradation and adsorption is negligible, the first route can produce a larger transient concentration at the target even though the same amount was introduced. The experimental result would support the advantage of spatially concentrated delivery but not prove that TRH is intrinsically more potent when transported in portal vessels. The molecule remains the same; its concentration time course is changed by transport geometry.

To interpret stalk interruption, consider three groups: an intact control, an animal with a selective disruption of the vascular route while posterior axons are preserved, and an animal with a disruption that also affects axons. In an idealised comparison, TSH and ACTH regulation could be altered in both experimental groups, while impairment of posterior hormone release would be more directly associated with disruption of neurosecretory axons. Real interventions may affect blood supply, inflammation or survival of tissue. These factors are potential confounders, which are variables that can change the measured response independently of the intended perturbation. An examination question that specifies a selective interruption often deliberately removes these confounders; an actual experimental conclusion requires evidence that they were controlled.

4. Hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting signals

The hypothalamic factors are named according to the anterior pituitary hormone that they regulate, except when a substance has several functions or was first named in another setting. Corticotropin-releasing hormone, or CRH, is a peptide that stimulates corticotrophs to produce and secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone. CRH acts largely through a G protein-coupled receptor and the cAMP signalling route. Vasopressin from appropriate parvocellular hypothalamic populations can potentiate ACTH release through V1b receptors on corticotrophs. This is distinct from the action of circulating vasopressin on the kidney.

Thyrotropin-releasing hormone, TRH, is a tripeptide whose action on thyrotrophs increases TSH synthesis and secretion. The receptor commonly couples to phospholipase C through Gq-family proteins. The intracellular calcium signal contributes to secretion; longer-lasting signalling influences synthesis. TRH can also stimulate prolactin secretion, demonstrating that the naming of a releasing hormone does not necessarily specify its only responsive cell type. In a living organism the dominant effects depend on receptor expression, the concentrations delivered and the other regulatory signals present.

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone, GnRH, is a decapeptide. GnRH pulses reach gonadotrophs and promote secretion of LH and FSH. Their relative production also depends on pulse pattern and inputs such as gonadal inhibin and activin-related regulation. A continuous GnRH stimulus may initially stimulate secretion but prolonged continuous exposure generally reduces gonadotroph responsiveness through receptor and downstream regulatory changes. This distinction is central to reproductive physiology: average hormone delivery is insufficient to describe the message encoded in a pulsatile input.

Growth hormone-releasing hormone, GHRH, promotes GH secretion from somatotrophs, particularly through cAMP-linked signalling. Somatostatin inhibits GH release and can also inhibit TSH secretion. In the growth axis these two hypothalamic inputs enable a pattern of secretion shaped by both excitation and inhibition. Ghrelin, produced prominently in the stomach and also linked to central regulation, can stimulate GH release via the growth hormone secretagogue receptor. Its involvement does not abolish the importance of GHRH and somatostatin.

Dopamine is a catecholamine rather than a releasing peptide. Tuberoinfundibular dopaminergic neurons normally deliver dopamine to the anterior pituitary through the portal circulation. Dopamine acting through D2 receptors restrains prolactin synthesis and release. Blocking that receptor or interrupting hypothalamic dopamine delivery tends to release the restraint, increasing prolactin. An inhibitory input is a positive physiological activity, not the absence of a signal. Loss of that input can therefore raise the output of an intact endocrine gland even while other pituitary outputs fall.

Regulation must be traced at the correct level. An increase in hypothalamic GHRH does not act principally on growth plate chondrocytes; it stimulates pituitary GH production. GH and IGF-1 subsequently influence growth. Likewise, GnRH is not the gonadal steroid hormone, TSH is not the thyroid hormone, and ACTH is not cortisol. Each label identifies a chemical signal at a particular stage of an axis. Keeping the stages separate prevents a large class of examination errors.

Hormones act through receptors, so it is helpful to translate the hypothalamic releasing-factor list into predicted intracellular events. A Gs-linked receptor activates adenylyl cyclase, which catalyses formation of cyclic AMP from ATP. Cyclic AMP can activate protein kinase A and influence both rapidly acting secretory processes and slower changes in transcription. A Gq-linked receptor can activate phospholipase C, generating inositol trisphosphate and diacylglycerol from membrane phosphoinositides. Calcium released from intracellular stores and calcium entering from outside the cell can cooperate to promote secretion. These routes overlap and interact; they should not be described as rigid pathways with no cross-talk.

For CRH-stimulated ACTH release, the corticotroph integrates cAMP-related signalling with other inputs, including vasopressin-associated calcium signalling. If a preparation receives two signals together and its response exceeds the sum of individual increments, the response is described as synergistic under the conditions tested. If it equals the sum, it is additive. To establish either relation one must calculate increments relative to the same baseline rather than simply add absolute measured concentrations. Suppose baseline ACTH release in a hypothetical culture is 10 arbitrary units, CRH raises it to 30, vasopressin raises it to 20 and both raise it to 65. The individual increments are 20 and 10, predicting an additive response of 40 including baseline. The observed 65 is greater than 40, indicating supra-additivity in that example. It would still be necessary to test more concentrations and replicate cultures before generalising.

Somatostatin illustrates the value of inhibition in a system that releases hormones in bursts. Suppressing secretion during intervals can permit a low trough between periods of stimulation. A reduced inhibitory signal may increase basal output or change pulse timing without necessarily producing a simple proportional increase in every peak. This is why the effect of modifying inhibitory inputs should be assessed from time-series data. If the measurement covers only a short interval, an apparent decrease in a stimulated peak could coexist with increased secretion across the entire observation period.

Dopamine differs chemically from the releasing peptides and is also an important neurotransmitter in other neural circuits. Its endocrine effect in the anterior pituitary depends on the identity of the dopaminergic pathway and the receptors of lactotrophs. It would be wrong to infer that every dopamine-producing neuron participates in prolactin control. The particular projection to the median eminence allows the molecule to act as a neuroendocrine inhibitory factor. The same molecule can carry information through synaptic communication in one location and through vascular delivery in another. Classification therefore depends on the route and biological function as well as molecular structure.

5. Anterior pituitary endocrine cell types and hormone targets

Anterior pituitary cells can be distinguished by the hormones they express, immunohistochemical staining and lineage-associated transcription factors. Somatotrophs produce growth hormone, lactotrophs produce prolactin, thyrotrophs produce TSH, corticotrophs produce ACTH, and gonadotrophs produce LH and FSH. A classification according to the major secreted products is more useful for endocrine reasoning than relying exclusively on staining colour. Cells can differ in hormone abundance and function according to physiological state, and the relative proportions of cell types are not constant across all animals.

GH and prolactin are related protein hormones. Their receptors are members of the cytokine receptor family and signal through associated Janus kinases and STAT transcription factors among other pathways. ACTH is a peptide generated by processing proopiomelanocortin, abbreviated POMC. Differential processing of this precursor can produce distinct peptides in different tissues. In particular, the corticotroph does not produce a steroid hormone by processing POMC. Instead ACTH travels to the adrenal cortex and stimulates steroidogenic responses through its receptor.

TSH, LH and FSH are glycoprotein hormones containing a common alpha subunit and hormone-specific beta subunits. The beta subunits are major determinants of receptor recognition, although the intact heterodimer and glycosylation contribute to biological activity. The shared alpha subunit does not mean that the three hormones have interchangeable physiological roles. TSH primarily acts on the thyroid gland, whereas LH and FSH act on different gonadal cell populations with coordinated functions.

A tropic hormone stimulates another endocrine gland. TSH, ACTH and the gonadotropins are familiar examples. GH has direct actions in several tissues and indirect actions through IGF-1. Prolactin has prominent effects on mammary epithelium. It is useful to distinguish a hormone that regulates secretion by an endocrine organ from a hormone that acts directly on a tissue's physiological function. The distinction is not absolute, because a hormone can have several target tissues and roles.

A secretory response and a synthesis response are related but different measurements. A pituitary cell contains a pool of previously synthesised hormone in granules. A short stimulatory input may cause release from that pool before substantial new protein is manufactured. Sustained activity may increase transcription, translation and replenishment of granules. In a culture experiment a change in hormone in the medium measures release, whereas a change in intracellular hormone content can reflect both synthesis and release. Measuring one alone does not prove which process was altered.

The production of a peptide hormone begins with gene expression and translation. A secreted protein generally enters the secretory pathway through the endoplasmic reticulum, undergoes appropriate processing and moves through the Golgi apparatus into secretory granules. In many endocrine cells these granules are retained until a regulated stimulus promotes exocytosis. This organisation allows a cell to prepare a stock of hormone and release it rapidly when required. A secretory granule containing TSH or GH is not equivalent to a vesicle containing thyroid hormone or cortisol; the latter peripheral hormones are produced by other tissues through different biochemical processes.

The POMC precursor is an example in which processing matters as much as transcription. A precursor polypeptide can be cleaved by tissue-dependent proteolytic machinery, yielding products with different biological activities. In corticotrophs, ACTH is a major biologically relevant product. In tissues with a different complement of processing enzymes, additional peptide products can be generated. It is therefore insufficient to detect expression of a precursor gene and assume that all cells expressing it secrete the same proportions of mature peptides. This principle applies to other prohormones as well, including the neurohypophyseal peptide precursors.

Hormone-specific beta subunits permit glycoprotein hormones to activate different receptors. A useful conceptual comparison is between a mutation that prevents production of the shared alpha subunit and a mutation that selectively alters the LH beta subunit. The first could compromise formation of multiple functional glycoprotein hormones in expressing tissues. The second would be expected to have a more specific effect on LH assembly or function, depending on the mutation. The downstream consequences can be predicted only after identifying the disrupted product. A structural component shared by several signals is not evidence that the signals have the same targets.

Endocrine cell identity can be inferred experimentally by pairing an antibody that recognises the secreted hormone with measurement of a functional response. For example, cells that contain GH and increase GH release after GHRH exposure are consistent with somatotroph identity. Detecting GHRH receptor expression strengthens the mechanistic explanation. A mixed anterior pituitary cell preparation may nevertheless include interactions between cell types, so a response in the preparation need not arise entirely from direct action on the measured cell. Comparing enriched cell populations with the mixed preparation can help distinguish direct from indirect responses. Such reasoning builds on cell signalling and tissue organisation without requiring specialist laboratory methods.

6. Feedback organisation of the thyroid, adrenal and gonadal axes

In the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis, TRH stimulates TSH production, and TSH stimulates thyroid hormone production. Thyroid hormones exert negative feedback at hypothalamic and pituitary levels. When a primary thyroid defect reduces the hormone output, an intact control system generally responds by increasing TSH. In contrast, when the pituitary fails to deliver adequate TSH, thyroid hormone falls without the appropriate rise in TSH. A measured TSH value inside a population reference interval can still be inappropriately low for a low thyroid hormone concentration. The relationship between the two measurements is more informative than whether one value lies within an isolated interval.

In the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, CRH and vasopressin contribute to ACTH regulation. ACTH stimulates adrenal glucocorticoid secretion, and circulating glucocorticoids inhibit upstream drive. The axis responds to circadian input and to stress-related neural signals. Thus a transient rise in cortisol may be adaptive rather than a sign of a permanently displaced feedback equilibrium. The time of sampling matters: morning and evening results are not necessarily interchangeable in a diurnal human. Circadian patterns differ in timing among species according to their activity cycles.

In the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, GnRH pulses stimulate LH and FSH. Gonadal steroids and inhibin participate in feedback regulation. Negative feedback predominates in many contexts, but sustained ovarian estradiol during the appropriate preovulatory state can promote positive feedback and an LH surge. Positive feedback is not a general feature of every increase in estradiol. Its manifestation depends on reproductive phase and duration of the signal. Likewise, inhibin preferentially suppresses FSH, so two pituitary outputs responding to the same hypothalamic releasing hormone can behave differently.

Feedback is best interpreted by distinguishing the regulated variable from the signal used to correct it. In the thyroid axis, increased TSH can indicate a compensatory response rather than primary pituitary overactivity. In the adrenal axis, high ACTH with low cortisol suggests that the adrenal output is failing despite stimulation. High cortisol with low ACTH is compatible with an ACTH-independent glucocorticoid excess, while high cortisol with an ACTH concentration that is not suppressed suggests a source of persistent ACTH drive. These are mechanistic patterns, not sufficient clinical diagnoses; measurement timing, drugs, binding proteins and other physiological conditions can modify them.

An axis may exhibit long-loop feedback from a peripheral product, short-loop feedback from a pituitary output and local regulation of hypothalamic neuronal activity. Not every loop is equally important in every axis. A useful examination method is to mark each causal relation with an activating or inhibitory sign and then reason through the immediate consequences of changing one component. The next step is to allow feedback to act and distinguish the new equilibrium from the initial transient. The two predictions need not have the same direction.

The word feedback is sometimes used incorrectly for any hormone that acts on a previous organ. A feedback loop must include a causal route by which a change in output affects subsequent output through the controlling system. Consider the thyroid axis: an increase in thyroid hormone reduces central stimulation, reducing the tendency for further thyroid hormone secretion. The sign of the entire loop is negative because the response opposes the initial change in output. In contrast, a local action of thyroid hormone on an unrelated tissue is not part of this particular feedback loop unless that tissue subsequently influences axis output.

It is possible for a negative-feedback system to exhibit oscillations rather than maintaining a perfectly constant output. Every step introduces a delay because secretion, distribution, receptor action and gene expression take time. After a peripheral hormone concentration rises, the upstream response may be delayed; after stimulation declines, the peripheral concentration may remain elevated until clearance reduces it. Delayed regulation and pulsatile central input help explain why normal endocrine concentrations can fluctuate. Oscillation does not automatically establish that the feedback is positive or defective. An examination graph should be read in relation to the timescale and physiological circumstances of the measurement.

The distinction between primary, secondary and tertiary dysfunction is based on the level of the axis rather than on the severity of the effect. Primary endocrine dysfunction originates at the peripheral gland, secondary dysfunction at the pituitary and tertiary dysfunction at the hypothalamic level. Secondary and tertiary disorders are often grouped as central dysfunction because both may lead to insufficient pituitary stimulation. In simplified models, an intact pituitary can respond to a hypothalamic releasing factor when its normal drive is missing, whereas severely damaged pituitary cells cannot. In actual systems prolonged loss of stimulation may change cellular responsiveness, and responses to dynamic testing may overlap. A single stimulation result is not a universal localisation rule.

Negative feedback can be studied with a replacement intervention. If a peripheral gland is absent but central feedback pathways remain intact, introducing its hormone can suppress excessive upstream stimulation. If upstream cells fail to sense the replacement hormone because their receptor or signalling pathway is defective, suppression may not occur as expected. A measured failure of suppression is therefore evidence about the intactness of the loop but must be interpreted together with exposure to the replacement, the time course of the response and the specificity of the perturbation. It is not automatically evidence that the peripheral gland is producing too much hormone.

For the reproductive axis, inhibin offers a particularly clear way to examine selective feedback. Because gonadotrophs produce both LH and FSH, a student might predict that any disturbance in gonadal feedback changes both equally. Inhibin acts preferentially on FSH production. A loss of inhibin-producing function can increase FSH even if other signals maintaining LH regulation are relatively preserved. This differential response shows why the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis is more accurately represented by several connected pathways than by a single arrow from GnRH to one undifferentiated gonadotropin.

7. Growth hormone, IGF-1 and the regulation of growth

Growth is a cumulative process involving increases in cell number, cell size, tissue mass and skeletal dimensions. GH supports growth but is not the only signal involved. Nutrition, thyroid hormones, insulin, gonadal steroids, developmental state and local growth factors influence how strongly tissue responds. A rapidly growing animal therefore cannot be understood by measuring circulating GH in isolation. Its growth plates must remain capable of longitudinal growth, substrates must be available, and the intracellular machinery of responsive cells must function.

Somatotrophs secrete GH in pulses under the influence of GHRH, somatostatin, ghrelin and feedback. GH binds to its receptor, activating receptor-associated JAK2 and downstream signalling including STAT5. One consequence is increased production of insulin-like growth factor 1, IGF-1, in the liver and other tissues. Liver-derived IGF-1 circulates as an endocrine factor; locally produced IGF-1 also participates in tissue growth. The IGF-1 receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase, unlike the cytokine-family GH receptor. The use of two distinct signalling systems helps explain why a defect in GH signalling can reduce IGF-1 output even if the IGF-1 receptor remains intact.

IGF-1 promotes growth-related activity of growth plate chondrocytes and other cells, whereas GH also has direct tissue effects. The growth plate contains zones of chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation followed by replacement of cartilage with bone. Longitudinal growth is possible while functional growth plates persist. Excess GH before epiphyseal closure can produce excessive linear growth, known as gigantism. After closure, excess GH cannot reopen the plates to produce the same form of long-bone lengthening; acromegaly instead involves changes such as enlargement of acral structures, soft tissue growth and skeletal remodelling. Age at exposure changes the anatomical result even when the endocrine disturbance is similar.

GH supports protein accretion in an appropriate nutritional context and promotes lipolysis. It can oppose certain actions of insulin on glucose metabolism. During fasting the GH concentration may increase while IGF-1 decreases, reflecting nutritional modulation of GH responsiveness and substrate availability. This pattern helps preserve fuel use and does not imply accelerated somatic growth. Conversely, low IGF-1 in a nutritionally compromised animal is not proof that the pituitary cannot release GH. Feedback and peripheral responsiveness must both be considered.

IGF-1 restrains GH secretion through feedback at the pituitary and hypothalamus. In GH receptor resistance, diminished IGF-1 generation removes part of this feedback while growth remains impaired. The expected pattern, in a simplified severe receptor-defect example, is relatively high GH with low IGF-1. In a primary deficiency of GH production, low GH drive leads to low IGF-1. Replacement of GH would be expected to restore signalling more effectively in the latter model than in a complete GH receptor-loss model. This comparison is a causal distinction between missing ligand and loss of responsiveness, not a treatment recommendation.

A single random GH concentration can be misleading because it may occur between pulses. IGF-1 has a different time course, influenced by binding proteins and nutritional status. Suppose two animals have identical mean GH concentrations over a sampling period but one secretes discrete peaks separated by troughs while the other has a constant concentration. Downstream tissues may respond differently due to receptor and intracellular adaptation. A time-integrated concentration can be useful for describing total exposure but cannot reconstruct the pattern of exposure. This principle is widely relevant to neuroendocrine signalling.

The term insulin-like growth factor reflects similarities to insulin in molecular structure and some signalling properties, not identity with insulin. IGF-1 promotes cellular responses through its own receptor, although the insulin and IGF receptor families share related structural features. GH does not act simply by mimicking insulin at the insulin receptor. Its cytokine receptor signalling initiates distinct transcriptional and metabolic responses. In a growth experiment it is possible to bypass a GH-dependent synthesis step by providing a downstream mediator, but only the mediator-dependent portion of the response can be restored in this manner.

The liver is an important source of circulating IGF-1, but the growth plate and other tissues can produce IGF-related signals locally. It is therefore an oversimplification to equate all biologically relevant IGF-1 with the circulating concentration. Local production can contribute to tissue-specific effects, and circulating IGF-1 can be affected by binding proteins. When a tissue-specific receptor defect affects only one organ, the concentration of circulating mediators may be relatively normal while the affected tissue responds poorly. In contrast, a widespread defect in GH responsiveness can reduce hepatic IGF-1 production and affect many tissues. The distribution of the defect changes the phenotype.

Growth velocity measures change in a dimension per unit time rather than attained size alone. Consider two hypothetical juveniles that both measure 100 centimetres at the beginning of observation. After six months, animal A has grown to 104 centimetres and animal B to 101 centimetres. Their annualised velocities are 8 and 2 centimetres per year respectively, assuming the six-month rate is representative. The calculation describes a difference in current growth, not its cause. Differences in developmental stage, nutritional intake and skeletal maturity could alter velocity independently of GH secretion. Measuring GH-related signals and controlling for age and physiological context would be needed to attribute the difference to a particular endocrine defect.

In growth physiology, a low GH concentration in a single sample should not be interpreted identically to low responses over repeated or appropriately controlled observations. A brief trough is expected in a pulsatile system. Conversely, persistent high output that fails to fall appropriately under a suppressive physiological condition suggests a different regulatory defect from a normal pulse. This is a general feature of dynamic endocrine testing: the observed response to a defined perturbation can be more informative than a static measurement. The actual clinical protocols and diagnostic cutoffs vary and are outside the scope of this chapter.

A classical approach to distinguishing hormone production from tissue responsiveness is to compare a deficient animal with a resistant animal. The deficient animal lacks sufficient functional GH, while the resistant animal produces GH but cannot transmit its signal through the relevant receptor. If both receive identical bioactive GH and adequate nutrition, the deficient animal should show a stronger rise in GH-dependent mediators than the completely resistant one. Measuring receptor activation and IGF-1 synthesis before and after exposure would help identify where the response fails. This hypothetical experimental logic explains why hormone concentration and phenotype need not move in the same direction.

8. Prolactin and the logic of tonic inhibition

Prolactin is secreted by lactotrophs and supports the secretory activity of mammary epithelial cells in the appropriate hormonal environment. Its receptor activates cytokine-receptor-associated signalling, prominently JAK2 and STAT5 in mammary cells. Prolactin contributes to milk synthesis, while oxytocin causes contraction of myoepithelial cells to move milk already present within the mammary gland. In a question about lactation, the terms synthesis and ejection must therefore not be used interchangeably.

Prolactin is particularly informative because its baseline regulation differs from the typical releasing-hormone model. Dopamine from hypothalamic neurons continually restrains the lactotroph population. Reduced dopaminergic input during suckling contributes to the prolactin response. In an isolated viable pituitary preparation, removal of hypothalamic input can allow prolactin release rather than suppress it. Adding dopamine should restore inhibition if receptors and downstream signalling remain functional. The response provides a means to distinguish a tonic inhibitory mechanism from a tonic stimulatory mechanism.

Prolactin can itself influence hypothalamic dopamine neurons, creating a short feedback circuit that limits its own secretion. Pregnancy and lactation modify the balance of regulatory inputs. Estrogens influence lactotroph biology, and several signals besides dopamine can modify prolactin release. Describing dopamine as the principal tonic inhibitor does not imply that every prolactin fluctuation is caused solely by changes in dopamine concentration.

Excess prolactin can inhibit reproductive axis activity through effects on hypothalamic circuits controlling GnRH. Consequently a disturbance in one anterior pituitary output may alter other axes without requiring simultaneous destruction of all pituitary cell types. A stalk lesion can cause elevated prolactin through impaired dopamine delivery; a prolactin-secreting tumour can also elevate prolactin. The shared consequence does not establish a shared cause. To distinguish these mechanisms experimentally, one would need additional anatomical and functional evidence.

The physiological roles of prolactin differ across vertebrates. Mammalian lactation provides the familiar example, but comparative endocrinology also identifies roles in osmoregulation, parental behaviour and other functions in particular species. One should not infer from a mammalian example that the hormone evolved exclusively to support milk production or that every vertebrate prolactin response involves a mammary gland. The general principle is that a hormone can be conserved while its targets, expression patterns and biological roles vary.

A useful analysis of tonic inhibition compares a receptor agonist with a receptor antagonist. In an intact lactotroph, stimulation of functional D2 receptors tends to reduce prolactin secretion. Blocking endogenous dopamine action tends to relieve restraint. In a preparation that has already been completely isolated from dopamine, adding an antagonist may produce little additional effect if there is no endogenous inhibitory signal left to block. Adding dopamine to that preparation should reduce secretion if the receptor is functional. This distinction shows why an intervention can have different effects in an intact animal and in an isolated cell preparation.

The amount of circulating prolactin is not a direct quantitative measure of milk output under all physiological conditions. Secretory capacity of the gland, other endocrine signals, removal of milk and local regulation also influence lactation. This is similar to GH physiology, where growth depends on developmental and nutritional conditions as well as circulating hormone. A physiological hormone response may be necessary for an outcome without being sufficient to determine its magnitude in every circumstance. A scientifically useful experiment measures both the hormone and the target-tissue response rather than assuming that one is an exact proxy for the other.

9. Posterior pituitary synthesis, axonal transport and vasopressin

Vasopressin, called antidiuretic hormone or ADH, and oxytocin are closely related nonapeptides. They are synthesised as parts of larger precursor molecules in magnocellular neurons. The precursor is processed in the secretory pathway, and the peptide is transported in granules along axons together with its associated neurophysin. The vasopressin precursor also yields copeptin. When action potentials arrive at terminals in the posterior pituitary, depolarisation opens voltage-dependent calcium channels and promotes release by exocytosis. The site of peptide synthesis is thus spatially separated from the principal site of release into blood.

An increase in effective extracellular osmolality tends to draw water out of cells. Central osmosensitive pathways respond to such changes and increase the drive for vasopressin release and thirst. Volume and pressure information from the circulation also contributes, especially when effective circulating volume is markedly reduced. The animal must sometimes conserve water even when osmolality alone would favour lower vasopressin output. Integration of these inputs gives physiological context to the hormone response.

Vasopressin binds V2 receptors on the basolateral surface of collecting duct principal cells. These Gs-coupled receptors increase cAMP and activate downstream signalling that promotes insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels into the apical membrane. Water can then move through the cell in response to the osmotic gradient established within the renal medulla, exiting through basolateral water channels. Increased permeability does not itself provide the osmotic driving force. If the medullary gradient is impaired, even abundant aquaporin channels cannot produce normal concentration of urine. The renal mechanisms are studied in greater detail in subunit 7.F.

Vasopressin also acts through V1a receptors in vascular smooth muscle, which can promote contraction, especially at higher circulating concentrations. The V1b receptor participates in ACTH regulation in the anterior pituitary. The three receptor effects show why a hormone should not be assigned a single intracellular pathway independently of its target cell. V2 receptor activation involves cAMP, while V1-family signalling is typically linked to phospholipase C and intracellular calcium. Receptor subtype explains how one circulating molecule can influence water transport, vascular tone and endocrine secretion in different cells.

Consider a simplified example of low vasopressin secretion. Collecting duct water permeability falls, less water is reabsorbed, and urine becomes abundant and dilute when the kidney is otherwise capable of normal filtration and solute handling. Thirst may increase and compensate for water loss if drinking is possible. A similar urine pattern can result from renal resistance to vasopressin despite adequate secretion. The former mechanism is central diabetes insipidus, also termed arginine vasopressin deficiency; the latter is nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, or vasopressin resistance. Both differ from diabetes mellitus, in which hyperglycaemia can cause osmotic diuresis through glucose remaining in the tubular fluid.

The separation between secretion and response is evident in a controlled physiological comparison. An appropriate V2 agonist should increase collecting duct water permeability in a model lacking endogenous vasopressin but having intact receptors and downstream components. The same intervention would have little effect in a model with complete V2 receptor loss. Partial defects, medullary gradient disruption and other causes of impaired concentration complicate real observations. The simplified contrast nevertheless provides a general principle: replacing a signal cannot compensate fully for failure of its receptor or of an indispensable downstream mechanism.

Osmolality is the concentration of osmotically active particles expressed relative to the mass of solvent, commonly in milliosmoles per kilogram of water. Osmolarity is expressed per volume of solution. The terms are numerically close in dilute biological fluids but are not definitionally identical. Effective osmolality, or tonicity, additionally considers whether solutes can cross cell membranes rapidly enough to equilibrate. A solute that crosses a cell membrane freely may increase measured osmolality without causing the same sustained cell-volume response as an effectively impermeant solute. For regulation of cell volume, the distribution of solutes and the membrane's permeability are essential.

A controlled rise in extracellular sodium concentration, for example, can increase the tendency for water to leave susceptible osmosensitive cells. The neural response is relayed through circuits that influence magnocellular activity and thirst. The hormone response is ultimately connected to an organ-level outcome: reduced urinary water loss tends to restore the water-to-solute ratio. The feedback is negative because the consequence of the response opposes the change that stimulated it. Drinking water can also influence central regulation through anticipatory signals before complete equilibration of the newly ingested water with all body compartments.

Volume regulation introduces a second dimension. An animal may experience reduced circulating volume without an increase in osmolality, as when isotonic fluid is lost. Cardiovascular sensory pathways and associated neurohumoral signals may promote water conservation despite the absence of an osmotic stimulus. Conversely, ingestion of water can lower plasma osmolality and reduce vasopressin secretion. These responses do not contradict the osmoregulatory model. They reveal that the system integrates several physiological variables and that the relative contribution of each can change with the magnitude of the challenge.

The relation between urine concentration and total water loss depends on both urine volume and solute excretion. If a hypothetical animal excretes 600 milliosmoles of solute per day at a urine osmolality of 300 milliosmoles per kilogram, approximately 2 kilograms of urine water are required. At 600 milliosmoles per kilogram the corresponding amount is about 1 kilogram, assuming the same solute load and treating urine as dilute enough for the simplified estimate. Concentration has increased while water loss has fallen. The solute load must be held constant for this direct comparison. Changing both solute excretion and vasopressin can produce different outcomes.

Water reabsorption requires a path through the epithelial cell as well as an appropriate extracellular environment. In principal cells the apical membrane faces tubular fluid, whereas the basolateral membrane faces the interstitial side. Vasopressin stimulates regulated delivery of AQP2 to the apical membrane. Basolateral aquaporins permit water exit towards the interstitium. A mutation that impairs apical AQP2 trafficking could therefore cause renal resistance to an otherwise normal circulating signal. A mutation affecting a different component of the renal concentrating mechanism might also yield dilute urine, even though the V2 receptor and AQP2 trafficking are intact. Identical gross outcomes do not guarantee identical molecular defects.

10. Oxytocin, neuroendocrine reflexes and feedback

Oxytocin is released from hypothalamic magnocellular neuron terminals in the posterior pituitary. It acts on Gq-linked receptors in uterine smooth muscle and mammary myoepithelial cells. In the mammary gland, myoepithelial contraction helps propel milk towards the ducts. The action is mechanical displacement of milk, not synthesis of its proteins, lipids or carbohydrate. A defect in oxytocin release may therefore impair milk ejection despite preservation of prolactin-dependent synthesis.

Suckling activates sensory afferents that transmit information to central circuits, leading to coordinated oxytocin neuronal activity and pulsatile hormone release. The response connects an external mechanical stimulus to an endocrine effect. It differs from a conventional autonomic efferent pathway in which the final transmitter is released directly at an organ's nerve ending. Here the final neurosecretory terminals release hormone into blood, enabling many responsive myoepithelial cells to receive the signal.

During labour, mechanical stimulation of the cervix and related reproductive tissues can contribute to oxytocin secretion and stronger uterine contractions. Stronger contractions can enhance mechanical stimulation, creating a reinforcing loop. The loop terminates when the initiating mechanical circumstances change, notably after delivery. Human parturition is multifactorial and cannot be reduced to oxytocin alone initiating labour; prostaglandins, uterine changes and maternal–fetal signals contribute. Positive feedback is a useful explanation for amplification of a process, not proof that a single hormone determines its beginning or timing.

Oxytocin and vasopressin differ in amino-acid sequence and receptor preferences despite structural similarity. At high concentrations, related peptides can show cross-reactivity. In an experiment testing the specificity of a receptor, one should use appropriate concentration ranges and selective antagonists or receptor-deficient cells. Observation of an effect at a high experimental concentration does not establish that it normally occurs at physiological concentrations.

The distinction between a neuroendocrine reflex and a conventional endocrine feedback axis is particularly clear during suckling. In the milk-ejection reflex, an external mechanical stimulus initiates sensory neural activity, which leads to neurohormone release and contraction of mammary myoepithelial cells. The gland does not have to secrete another endocrine hormone to relay the signal. In a classical thyroid axis, hypothalamic control instead acts through an endocrine cell in the anterior pituitary and another endocrine cell in the thyroid gland. Both involve neural and endocrine integration, but the number and nature of intervening steps differ.

The timing of neuroendocrine release can be critical for its biological effect. Oxytocin release during milk ejection occurs in bursts associated with coordinated magnocellular neuronal activity. A measurement of mean hormone concentration across a long interval may obscure the temporal association between a secretory burst and a contraction. Simultaneous recording of a physiological response and hormone levels can establish temporal correspondence, but temporal correspondence alone does not prove causation. Selective interruption of receptor signalling, while preserving other relevant functions, provides stronger evidence that the hormone mediates the response.

Positive feedback requires a terminating condition to prevent continued amplification. In the context of labour, the relationship between mechanical stimulation and uterine contraction is altered by delivery; in the preovulatory reproductive axis, the sustained estradiol conditions supporting positive feedback do not persist indefinitely. Students should not generalise positive feedback into a permanent runaway process. Biological positive feedback commonly operates within a particular state and helps complete a time-limited transition. The molecular and tissue context sets boundaries on its duration.

The same mechanical stimulus can recruit more than one endocrine response. Suckling influences oxytocin-related ejection and prolactin-related synthesis. The existence of a common sensory trigger does not imply identical central circuits, hormone kinetics or target cells. A selective experiment that reduces oxytocin action might impair immediate ejection while leaving prolactin responses relatively preserved. A selective impairment of prolactin-dependent mammary activity would instead be expected to affect synthesis and maintenance over a different timescale. The interpretation depends on exactly which response was measured and when.

11. Pulses, clearance and experimental interpretation

An endocrine measurement is a concentration, not a direct measurement of secretion rate. Concentration depends on the amount of hormone entering a compartment, the volume of distribution, degradation, binding and clearance. A simplified one-compartment model with a constant effective distribution volume V and first-order elimination constant k describes concentration C by dC/dt = S/V − kC, where S is secretion in mass per unit time, V is volume, C is mass per volume and k has units of reciprocal time. With constant secretion, the steady-state concentration is S/(Vk). Doubling secretion doubles steady-state concentration in this model only if distribution volume and elimination remain unchanged.

After a brief input has ended, the same model predicts C(t) = C(0)e^(−kt). The half-life equals ln(2)/k. In an illustrative calculation, a hormone concentration of 80 units per litre falls to 40 units per litre in ten minutes when additional input and baseline concentration are negligible. Another ten minutes gives 20 units per litre, and a further ten minutes gives 10 units per litre. The implied k is about 0.0693 per minute. These values are not measurements of a specific pituitary hormone. The simple model can fail if secretion continues or clearance becomes concentration dependent.

Sampling interval influences the apparent number and magnitude of pulses. Imagine secretory peaks lasting only a few minutes but samples collected once per hour. A sample may fall between peaks and suggest little hormone release. Increasing sampling frequency improves the chance of detecting fluctuations, though measured concentration is also smoothed by distribution and clearance. Comparing two experimental groups requires comparable sampling schedules, assay properties and physiological states. A single measurement cannot establish that pulsatility is absent.

Gland ablation and replacement experiments historically helped distinguish pituitary and target-gland functions. In a conceptual thyroid-axis experiment, removal of the thyroid lowers thyroid hormone and releases negative feedback on upstream structures. Providing thyroid hormone without restoring the physical gland should suppress excessive pituitary drive if the feedback pathways remain functional. Removal of the pituitary instead lowers stimulation of the thyroid and cannot be corrected at the level of pituitary TSH output merely by providing TRH. A TSH response to TRH requires surviving responsive thyrotrophs. The direction of the result depends on where the pathway is interrupted.

In vitro experiments can further separate central and peripheral effects. Isolated anterior pituitary cells allow researchers to vary one releasing factor while avoiding changes in peripheral endocrine glands. A vehicle control establishes baseline secretion; a receptor antagonist can test whether the response depends on the predicted receptor; a viability assay can distinguish reduced secretion from nonspecific cellular injury. However, an isolated preparation lacks intact portal delivery, normal neural inputs and peripheral feedback. Conclusions about a direct cellular response do not automatically establish the complete contribution of that response in an intact animal.

A useful strategy for endocrine questions is to begin with the known perturbation rather than the name of a disorder. Draw the normal connections, identify the site at which synthesis or responsiveness changes, predict the immediate downstream effects, then follow feedback towards the upstream structures. Finally consider timing and what was actually measured. If the perturbation could affect several levels, describe competing predictions and identify a measurement or comparison that would distinguish them.

Radioimmunoassay historically made it possible to measure very small quantities of circulating hormones by exploiting antibody binding and competition between labelled and unlabelled hormone. Increasing unlabelled hormone competes with a fixed amount of labelled hormone for limited binding sites. A standard curve relating signal to known concentrations permits inference of the concentration in an unknown sample. Modern immunometric assays use different detection configurations, but all hormone measurements require attention to specificity, calibration and interference. An immunoreactive concentration does not automatically equal biological activity if the sample contains variants or fragments recognised by the antibody.

A hormone bioassay asks a different question: does the sample cause a characteristic biological response under the conditions tested? A preparation could have measurable immunoreactive GH yet reduced capacity to stimulate a GH-dependent response because the recognised protein is biologically abnormal. Conversely, an assay that does not recognise a particular molecular form well could underestimate an active signal. Comparison of immunological measurement with functional response is especially useful when the observed phenotype does not match the measured concentration. The principle is general and does not require knowledge of specialised assay troubleshooting.

A standard curve must be interpreted within its calibrated range. Suppose a hypothetical assay produces a signal proportional to hormone concentration between 5 and 50 arbitrary units but approaches saturation above that range. A very high sample producing a saturated signal cannot be assigned a precise concentration by extending the linear relationship beyond 50. Diluting the sample into the calibrated range and accounting for the dilution factor can improve quantification if the assay behaves appropriately. A fivefold dilution measured at 18 units would correspond to 90 units in the original sample under that assumption. The numbers are illustrative and not diagnostic limits.

A useful distinction in data interpretation is between absolute and relative changes. If ACTH release falls from 40 to 20 units in one preparation and from 100 to 60 units in another, the absolute decreases are 20 and 40 units but the relative decreases are 50 percent and 40 percent. Whether an absolute or relative comparison is appropriate depends on the biological question and baseline differences. The larger absolute change does not necessarily mean that the signalling mechanism is more sensitive to inhibition. Normalising results can clarify some comparisons while hiding physiologically meaningful differences in basal output.

The study of feedback also benefits from time-matched control groups. If one group receives an intervention in the early morning and another is examined in the afternoon, differences in cortisol may reflect circadian timing rather than the intervention itself. If sampling requires handling, the handling could influence stress-related endocrine responses. A well-designed comparison keeps timing and sampling conditions comparable. This is a practical application of the broader principle that the measured hormone is an integrated physiological outcome, not an isolated variable disconnected from the animal's environment.

Original practice questions

1. Part B: Where are the major hormones released by the posterior pituitary synthesised?

Answer: Magnocellular hypothalamic neurons.

The posterior lobe contains neurosecretory axon endings, not the cell bodies responsible for peptide synthesis.

2. Part B: Which anterior pituitary cell produces ACTH?

Answer: Corticotroph.

Corticotrophs process POMC to generate ACTH, which stimulates adrenal steroid production.

3. Part B: Which hypothalamic factor principally inhibits prolactin?

Answer: Dopamine.

Portal dopamine activates lactotroph D2 receptors and imposes tonic restraint.

4. Part B: Which three pituitary hormones share a common glycoprotein alpha subunit?

Answer: TSH, LH and FSH.

Their distinct beta subunits contribute to specificity.

5. Part B: What are the two capillary beds connected by portal vessels?

Answer: Median eminence primary plexus and anterior pituitary secondary plexus.

The route delivers hypothalamic factors locally.

6. Part B: What signal promotes GH release while somatostatin restrains it?

Answer: GHRH.

GHRH stimulates somatotrophs through cAMP-linked signalling.

7. Part B: Which receptor family mediates GH action?

Answer: Cytokine receptor family.

GH receptors signal via associated kinases, not intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase activity.

8. Part B: Which receptor mediates the collecting duct action of vasopressin?

Answer: V2.

V2 activates Gs and increases cAMP in principal cells.

9. Part B: Which hormone causes milk ejection?

Answer: Oxytocin.

It contracts myoepithelial cells; prolactin supports synthesis.

10. Part B: Why is the paraventricular nucleus not equivalent to an oxytocin-only nucleus?

Answer: It contains multiple neuronal populations.

Both magnocellular peptide types occur there and parvocellular populations have other roles.

11. Part C: An animal has low thyroid hormone and elevated TSH. Which site is most consistent with the simple feedback model?

Answer: Primary thyroid failure.

Reduced output despite increased pituitary stimulation suggests peripheral dysfunction and loss of negative feedback.

12. Part C: Viable anterior pituitary cells are separated from all hypothalamic signals. Which hormone may rise rather than fall?

Answer: Prolactin.

Loss of dopaminergic inhibition increases secretion, unlike the loss of most releasing inputs.

13. Part C: An animal has low IGF-1 despite elevated GH. A GH receptor deletion is found. Explain the result.

Answer: GH resistance reduces IGF-1 and weakens feedback.

Increasing the ligand cannot restore signalling through an absent receptor.

14. Part C: A collecting duct model has absent endogenous AVP but intact V2 receptors. Compare its response to a V2 agonist with a complete receptor knockout.

Answer: The deficient model responds; the knockout does not.

The agonist bypasses deficient secretion but requires the receptor.

15. Part C: Portal secretion is 60 pg/min and blood flow is 3 mL/min. What is the concentration increment in a no-loss mixing model?

Answer: 20 pg/mL.

Divide secretion rate by flow; this is a hypothetical dilution calculation.

16. Part C: A hormone drops from 100 to 25 units/L over 20 minutes after secretion stops. What is its half-life in a first-order model?

Answer: 10 minutes.

Two successive halvings reduce 100 to 25; each occupies ten minutes.

17. Part C: A pulse-dependent hormone is delivered continuously at the same average rate. Can its effect be assumed identical?

Answer: No.

Receptor responsiveness, desensitisation and time-dependent downstream signalling can differ; continuous GnRH provides an example.

18. Part C: A GH excess begins after growth plate closure. Predict the contrast with onset before closure.

Answer: Acromegalic changes instead of marked longitudinal growth.

Closed growth plates limit lengthening; soft tissue and bone remodelling can continue.

19. Part C: A mammary gland produces milk but fails to eject it during stimulation. What endocrine mechanism should be investigated?

Answer: Oxytocin release or myoepithelial responsiveness.

The failure concerns displacement of preformed milk; prolactin-dependent synthesis can remain intact.

20. Part C: A receptor blocker reduces ACTH secretion in cultured corticotrophs but also markedly reduces viability. Is receptor-specific inhibition established?

Answer: No.

Nonspecific cellular injury could reduce secretion; a viability-matched control and receptor-specific evidence are needed.

21. Part B: Name the embryological origin of the adenohypophysis.

Answer: Oral ectoderm, via Rathke's pouch.

The neural ectodermal outgrowth instead forms the neurohypophyseal structures.

22. Part B: Which hormone is generated by processing POMC in corticotrophs?

Answer: ACTH.

Processing of a precursor can produce different mature products in different tissues.

23. Part B: Which hypothalamic hormone can stimulate both TSH and prolactin release?

Answer: TRH.

Thyrotrophs and lactotrophs can respond, although tonic dopamine dominates ordinary prolactin regulation.

24. Part B: Which hypothalamic factor inhibits GH and can also inhibit TSH?

Answer: Somatostatin.

Somatostatin acts as a regulatory inhibitory input, not a GH releasing factor.

25. Part B: What is the principal target of TSH?

Answer: Thyroid follicular cells.

TSH stimulates thyroid hormone production; it is not itself T3 or T4.

26. Part B: What type of receptor is the IGF-1 receptor?

Answer: Receptor tyrosine kinase.

GH instead uses a cytokine-family receptor and associated kinases.

27. Part B: What are pituicytes?

Answer: Supporting glial cells of the neurohypophysis.

They interact with neurosecretory axons but are not the major source of circulating vasopressin.

28. Part B: Which factor preferentially inhibits FSH among the major gonadal feedback signals?

Answer: Inhibin.

Its effects help differentiate FSH from LH regulation despite their shared hypothalamic input.

29. Part B: Which vascular receptor subtype mediates a prominent vasoconstrictor action of vasopressin?

Answer: V1a.

V1a signalling differs from the collecting duct V2–cAMP pathway.

30. Part B: What is the characteristic difference between a releasing hormone and a tropic pituitary hormone?

Answer: Their primary targets occupy different regulatory levels.

A hypothalamic releasing factor controls pituitary cells, while a tropic pituitary hormone stimulates a peripheral endocrine gland.

31. Part C: In a hypothetical culture, basal ACTH is 10 units; CRH yields 30, AVP 20 and both 65. Is the combined response supra-additive?

Answer: Yes.

The additive prediction is 10 + (30 − 10) + (20 − 10) = 40; 65 exceeds 40.

32. Part C: A labelled peptide is found in posterior pituitary terminals, but precursor mRNA is detected in hypothalamic cell bodies. What mechanism fits?

Answer: Synthesis in the soma and axonal transport of secretory material.

Peptide localisation at the terminal alone would not prove local biosynthesis.

33. Part C: A hormone has first-order clearance constant 0.1/min and secretion rate 20 units/min into 10 L. Calculate steady concentration.

Answer: 20 units/L.

S/(Vk) = 20/(10 × 0.1) = 20; the model assumes constant secretion, volume and clearance.

34. Part C: A primary adrenal defect lowers cortisol but ACTH is also low. Does the idealised isolated primary failure model explain both?

Answer: No.

Loss of cortisol feedback should raise ACTH if upstream control is intact; additional central suppression or other factors must be considered.

35. Part C: A selective beta-subunit defect impairs LH assembly while TSH remains functional. Is loss of a shared alpha subunit the best explanation?

Answer: No.

The selective effect fits the LH-specific component rather than loss of the subunit shared by multiple glycoprotein hormones.

36. Part C: A hormone assay saturates above 50 units. A fivefold dilution reads 18 units in range. Estimate the initial concentration.

Answer: 90 units.

Multiply by five, assuming validated dilution behaviour; do not extrapolate the saturated response.

37. Part C: An animal loses isotonic fluid. Plasma osmolality is unchanged, yet vasopressin rises. Explain.

Answer: Volume-related signals can stimulate vasopressin.

The hypothalamus integrates cardiovascular and osmotic inputs rather than responding to one variable alone.

38. Part C: A collecting duct expresses normal AQP2 but its medullary interstitial gradient is lost. Will vasopressin necessarily normalise urine concentration?

Answer: No.

Increased permeability permits water movement but cannot replace the osmotic driving force.

39. Part C: Two animals have identical GH means, but one has sharp peaks and the other sustained levels. What additional data are needed to compare signalling?

Answer: Temporal GH profiles and target response measurements.

Pulses and constant exposure can produce different receptor adaptation and downstream effects.

40. Part C: An isolated lactotroph preparation has no endogenous dopamine input. A D2 antagonist produces no further increase in prolactin. Does this disprove tonic inhibition in vivo?

Answer: No.

The inhibitory ligand may already be absent in the preparation; adding dopamine and checking the response would test receptor competence.

41. SET: Assertion: The posterior pituitary releases hormones produced in the hypothalamus. Reason: Magnocellular neurons extend axons to neurovascular terminals in the posterior pituitary.

Answer: Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.

The axons link the site of peptide synthesis to the site of systemic release.

42. SET: Assertion: In normal feedback, low thyroid hormone can increase TSH. Reason: Thyroid hormone normally inhibits upstream central stimulation.

Answer: Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.

Loss of a negative feedback signal promotes compensatory pituitary drive.

43. SET: Assertion: Prolactin excess may reduce reproductive function. Reason: Prolactin is the major stimulus for LH synthesis.

Answer: Assertion true; reason false.

Excess prolactin can suppress reproductive-axis signalling rather than serving as a principal LH releasing factor.

44. SET: Assertion: Oxytocin participates in positive feedback during labour. Reason: Oxytocin is the only determinant of the initiation of human labour.

Answer: Assertion true; reason false.

Contraction and mechanical stimulation can reinforce one another, but parturition is multifactorial.

45. SET: Assertion: GH receptor resistance can produce low IGF-1. Reason: GH-dependent signalling supports IGF-1 production.

Answer: Both true and the reason explains the assertion.

A receptor defect impairs signalling even when GH is available.

46. SET: A hypothetical animal excretes 600 mOsm of solute daily. Estimate urine water at 600 mOsm/kg.

Answer: Approximately 1 kg/day.

Solute load divided by urine osmolality gives urine water mass under the simplified assumptions.

47. SET: Which distinguishes the posterior pituitary from the anterior pituitary: the presence of secretory granules or the cellular origin of the released hormones?

Answer: The cellular origin of the released hormones.

Both systems use secretory material, but anterior endocrine cells synthesise their hormones locally and posterior peptide release depends on hypothalamic neurons.

48. SET: Why is a vehicle control needed in a hormone receptor experiment?

Answer: To establish the effect of the delivery medium and baseline conditions.

The observed experimental response should be attributable to the intended factor rather than the vehicle.

49. SET: Name one reason a measured hormone concentration may change without an equivalent change in secretion.

Answer: Altered clearance or distribution volume.

Concentration is the balance of input, distribution and loss, not secretion alone.

50. SET: What endocrine mechanism explains an increase of prolactin following impaired portal dopamine delivery?

Answer: Disinhibition.

Reduced D2-mediated restraint allows viable lactotrophs to increase output.

Mastery summary

The anterior and posterior pituitary communicate with the hypothalamus by distinct routes. Portal blood delivers regulatory factors to anterior pituitary cells, while hypothalamic neurosecretory axons carry vasopressin and oxytocin to the posterior lobe. The thyroid, adrenal and gonadal axes are coordinated through tropic signals and feedback. GH has direct effects and IGF-1-mediated actions. Prolactin is unusual because tonic dopaminergic inhibition dominates its ordinary regulation. Vasopressin increases collecting duct water permeability; oxytocin promotes myoepithelial and uterine contraction. Interpret measured hormone concentrations in relation to their timing, clearance and the response of the target organ.
