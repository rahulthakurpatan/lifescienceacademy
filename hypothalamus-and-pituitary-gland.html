1. Neural control becomes endocrine control

A mammal that loses water must conserve the water remaining in its circulation. It can reduce the amount lost through urine, but the kidney has no direct knowledge of the amount of water consumed earlier in the day. Cells in the brain respond to changes in the concentration of extracellular solutes and to information about the circulation. They communicate with the kidney through a circulating chemical signal. In another context the organism must adjust growth to the availability of nutrients, or regulate the adrenal cortex in response to a threat. These tasks illustrate why a central nervous structure can influence organs that are physically distant from it. The hypothalamus is a major site at which neural information is converted into endocrine instructions.

An endocrine axis is a linked series of regulatory organs. In the simplest axis a hypothalamic signal controls a pituitary hormone, the pituitary hormone controls a peripheral endocrine gland, and the peripheral hormone modifies the activity of both upstream structures. This organisation gives the organism more than one opportunity to regulate hormone production. For instance, a change in circulating thyroid hormone can alter pituitary secretion directly as well as modifying the hypothalamic drive that the pituitary receives. The arrangement is not merely a chain of commands. It is a control circuit in which the output influences its own production.

The designation of the pituitary as the master gland is useful only as a starting point. The hypothalamus controls many pituitary functions, but neither structure governs every endocrine cell. Insulin secretion responds directly to nutrients and other signals within the endocrine pancreas. Parathyroid cells detect extracellular calcium, and aldosterone secretion is strongly governed by potassium and angiotensin II. A student should not infer pituitary dependence from the fact that an organ secretes a hormone. Equally, direct feedback by a peripheral hormone does not make the hypothalamus irrelevant. An axis is coordinated by multiple inputs operating simultaneously.

The functional distinction within the pituitary is particularly important. Some pituitary cells manufacture peptide or glycoprotein hormones and release them in response to blood-borne hypothalamic factors. In contrast, certain neurons manufacture hormones in their cell bodies in the hypothalamus and release them from axon endings in the posterior pituitary. Both routes ultimately deliver hormones into the blood, yet interruption of the blood vessels of the stalk has different consequences from interruption of the axons. The anatomical distinction therefore becomes a powerful tool for interpreting experiments and disease patterns.

2. Anatomy, embryological origins and the two communication routes

The hypothalamus lies in the diencephalon, bordering the third ventricle. Its neuronal populations contribute to regulation of food intake, thirst, temperature, biological rhythms, autonomic responses and reproduction. It communicates with the pituitary through the infundibulum, often called the pituitary stalk. The pituitary sits below the hypothalamus within the bony sella turcica. This location provides support but also limits the space available when tissue enlarges. The optic chiasm is nearby, so an expanding pituitary lesion can influence vision without the affected hormone acting directly on the visual system.

The adenohypophysis develops from oral ectoderm through Rathke's pouch. Its major endocrine portion is the pars distalis; the pars tuberalis extends around the stalk, and the pars intermedia is relatively inconspicuous in adult humans. The neurohypophysis develops from neural ectoderm as an outgrowth from the ventral diencephalon. It includes the pars nervosa and the infundibular region. Different embryological origins explain the striking contrast between glandular endocrine cells in the anterior lobe and axons, supporting cells and neurovascular terminals in the posterior lobe. In comparative physiology the relative development of the intermediate lobe varies among vertebrates. One should therefore avoid assuming that the adult human arrangement represents every species.

Many hypothalamic neurons involved in anterior pituitary regulation are described as parvocellular neurosecretory neurons. Their axons project to the median eminence, a neurovascular interface near the base of the hypothalamus. Unlike a typical synaptic ending that releases neurotransmitter into a narrow cleft adjacent to another neuron, these terminals discharge regulatory molecules into the extracellular space near specialised capillaries. Molecules reach the bloodstream and travel a short distance to the anterior pituitary. The same action potential and calcium-dependent exocytosis principles studied in nervous physiology help explain this release, but the destination of the message is different.

Large magnocellular neurosecretory neurons, especially in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, send long axons towards the posterior pituitary. Their peptide-containing secretory granules undergo axonal transport from the soma. When firing reaches their terminals, calcium entry promotes exocytosis and the hormones enter the general circulation. The posterior pituitary contains pituicytes, supporting glial cells that interact with the axons and terminals. It is not simply a passive cavity of stored hormone; nevertheless its major endocrine outputs are manufactured by hypothalamic neurons rather than by the pituicytes.

The supraoptic nucleus is strongly associated with vasopressin-producing magnocellular neurons, and the paraventricular nucleus contains prominent oxytocin-producing populations. Both nuclei contain neurons producing each peptide. The paraventricular nucleus also contains parvocellular populations with different projections and regulatory functions, including cells involved in CRH secretion. A nucleus is an anatomical grouping, not a guarantee that every neuron within it has the same transmitter, hormone or axonal target. In an experiment, selective manipulation of a defined cell population is therefore more informative than treating a whole nucleus as a single uniform cell.

3. Hypophyseal portal circulation

The word portal describes a vascular arrangement in which blood flows from one capillary network through connecting vessels to another capillary network before returning to the general venous circulation. At the median eminence branches of the superior hypophyseal arteries form a primary capillary plexus. Hypothalamic regulatory factors enter this plexus, and portal vessels carry the blood towards a secondary plexus in the anterior pituitary. Endocrine cells within the anterior lobe respond to the delivered factors and release their own hormones into the circulation. The important feature is the sequence of two capillary networks connected by a local vascular route.

Imagine that a small amount of a releasing hormone enters the entire circulating blood volume before arriving at the pituitary. The amount reaching a specific pituitary cell would depend on dilution, degradation, distribution and blood flow. Local delivery through portal vessels reduces the initial volume of dilution and allows substantial signalling at relatively low overall production. The blood is still carrying a chemical hormone, and the action remains neuroendocrine. Local vascular transport does not imply direct electrical transmission to the anterior pituitary.

A portal vessel does not operate like a permanent reservoir. As blood flows through, concentration changes with the amount secreted at the median eminence, flow rate and clearance from the system. If hypothalamic secretion increases briefly, a transient increase in concentration can reach the anterior pituitary. A simplified relation for a continuously supplied substance is concentration increment approximately equal to secretion rate divided by flow. Suppose, for illustration, that a factor enters a stream at 40 picograms per minute and the stream flows at 2 millilitres per minute. In the absence of pre-existing factor, local loss and recirculation, the calculated increment is 20 picograms per millilitre. Doubling the flow while keeping secretion unchanged would halve the increment. Actual endocrine tissues include exchange, pulsatility and heterogeneous vascular flow; this calculation illustrates dilution rather than giving measured human portal concentrations.

Anatomical continuity alone does not establish the chemical identity of the factor transported through the vessels. It must be combined with physiological tests. If an anterior pituitary preparation releases a hormone when exposed to a hypothalamic extract, that supports the presence of a stimulatory substance, but the extract may contain many components. Comparing purified candidates, inactive controls, concentration dependence and receptor specificity strengthens the inference. Conversely, failure of an extract to stimulate a hormone could reflect degradation of the active factor or unsuitable preparation conditions rather than absence of regulation.

Severing the stalk is a powerful conceptual experiment but an imperfect selective test. It can disrupt portal blood flow and descending axons together, damage tissue or affect blood supply. If the anterior gland survives and is separated from hypothalamic delivery, several stimulating signals are lost and their pituitary outputs generally decline. Prolactin may rise because an inhibitory input is lost. Posterior secretion can also be disrupted through loss of axonal continuity. These predictions depend on tissue viability and the nature of the injury. A result after complete stalk injury cannot be attributed exclusively to interruption of portal vessels without additional controls.

4. Hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting signals

The hypothalamic factors are named according to the anterior pituitary hormone that they regulate, except when a substance has several functions or was first named in another setting. Corticotropin-releasing hormone, or CRH, is a peptide that stimulates corticotrophs to produce and secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone. CRH acts largely through a G protein-coupled receptor and the cAMP signalling route. Vasopressin from appropriate parvocellular hypothalamic populations can potentiate ACTH release through V1b receptors on corticotrophs. This is distinct from the action of circulating vasopressin on the kidney.

Thyrotropin-releasing hormone, TRH, is a tripeptide whose action on thyrotrophs increases TSH synthesis and secretion. The receptor commonly couples to phospholipase C through Gq-family proteins. The intracellular calcium signal contributes to secretion; longer-lasting signalling influences synthesis. TRH can also stimulate prolactin secretion, demonstrating that the naming of a releasing hormone does not necessarily specify its only responsive cell type. In a living organism the dominant effects depend on receptor expression, the concentrations delivered and the other regulatory signals present.

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone, GnRH, is a decapeptide. GnRH pulses reach gonadotrophs and promote secretion of LH and FSH. Their relative production also depends on pulse pattern and inputs such as gonadal inhibin and activin-related regulation. A continuous GnRH stimulus may initially stimulate secretion but prolonged continuous exposure generally reduces gonadotroph responsiveness through receptor and downstream regulatory changes. This distinction is central to reproductive physiology: average hormone delivery is insufficient to describe the message encoded in a pulsatile input.

Growth hormone-releasing hormone, GHRH, promotes GH secretion from somatotrophs, particularly through cAMP-linked signalling. Somatostatin inhibits GH release and can also inhibit TSH secretion. In the growth axis these two hypothalamic inputs enable a pattern of secretion shaped by both excitation and inhibition. Ghrelin, produced prominently in the stomach and also linked to central regulation, can stimulate GH release via the growth hormone secretagogue receptor. Its involvement does not abolish the importance of GHRH and somatostatin.

Dopamine is a catecholamine rather than a releasing peptide. Tuberoinfundibular dopaminergic neurons normally deliver dopamine to the anterior pituitary through the portal circulation. Dopamine acting through D2 receptors restrains prolactin synthesis and release. Blocking that receptor or interrupting hypothalamic dopamine delivery tends to release the restraint, increasing prolactin. An inhibitory input is a positive physiological activity, not the absence of a signal. Loss of that input can therefore raise the output of an intact endocrine gland even while other pituitary outputs fall.

Regulation must be traced at the correct level. An increase in hypothalamic GHRH does not act principally on growth plate chondrocytes; it stimulates pituitary GH production. GH and IGF-1 subsequently influence growth. Likewise, GnRH is not the gonadal steroid hormone, TSH is not the thyroid hormone, and ACTH is not cortisol. Each label identifies a chemical signal at a particular stage of an axis. Keeping the stages separate prevents a large class of examination errors.

5. Anterior pituitary endocrine cell types and hormone targets

Anterior pituitary cells can be distinguished by the hormones they express, immunohistochemical staining and lineage-associated transcription factors. Somatotrophs produce growth hormone, lactotrophs produce prolactin, thyrotrophs produce TSH, corticotrophs produce ACTH, and gonadotrophs produce LH and FSH. A classification according to the major secreted products is more useful for endocrine reasoning than relying exclusively on staining colour. Cells can differ in hormone abundance and function according to physiological state, and the relative proportions of cell types are not constant across all animals.

GH and prolactin are related protein hormones. Their receptors are members of the cytokine receptor family and signal through associated Janus kinases and STAT transcription factors among other pathways. ACTH is a peptide generated by processing proopiomelanocortin, abbreviated POMC. Differential processing of this precursor can produce distinct peptides in different tissues. In particular, the corticotroph does not produce a steroid hormone by processing POMC. Instead ACTH travels to the adrenal cortex and stimulates steroidogenic responses through its receptor.

TSH, LH and FSH are glycoprotein hormones containing a common alpha subunit and hormone-specific beta subunits. The beta subunits are major determinants of receptor recognition, although the intact heterodimer and glycosylation contribute to biological activity. The shared alpha subunit does not mean that the three hormones have interchangeable physiological roles. TSH primarily acts on the thyroid gland, whereas LH and FSH act on different gonadal cell populations with coordinated functions.

A tropic hormone stimulates another endocrine gland. TSH, ACTH and the gonadotropins are familiar examples. GH has direct actions in several tissues and indirect actions through IGF-1. Prolactin has prominent effects on mammary epithelium. It is useful to distinguish a hormone that regulates secretion by an endocrine organ from a hormone that acts directly on a tissue's physiological function. The distinction is not absolute, because a hormone can have several target tissues and roles.

A secretory response and a synthesis response are related but different measurements. A pituitary cell contains a pool of previously synthesised hormone in granules. A short stimulatory input may cause release from that pool before substantial new protein is manufactured. Sustained activity may increase transcription, translation and replenishment of granules. In a culture experiment a change in hormone in the medium measures release, whereas a change in intracellular hormone content can reflect both synthesis and release. Measuring one alone does not prove which process was altered.

6. Feedback organisation of the thyroid, adrenal and gonadal axes

In the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis, TRH stimulates TSH production, and TSH stimulates thyroid hormone production. Thyroid hormones exert negative feedback at hypothalamic and pituitary levels. When a primary thyroid defect reduces the hormone output, an intact control system generally responds by increasing TSH. In contrast, when the pituitary fails to deliver adequate TSH, thyroid hormone falls without the appropriate rise in TSH. A measured TSH value inside a population reference interval can still be inappropriately low for a low thyroid hormone concentration. The relationship between the two measurements is more informative than whether one value lies within an isolated interval.

In the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, CRH and vasopressin contribute to ACTH regulation. ACTH stimulates adrenal glucocorticoid secretion, and circulating glucocorticoids inhibit upstream drive. The axis responds to circadian input and to stress-related neural signals. Thus a transient rise in cortisol may be adaptive rather than a sign of a permanently displaced feedback equilibrium. The time of sampling matters: morning and evening results are not necessarily interchangeable in a diurnal human. Circadian patterns differ in timing among species according to their activity cycles.

In the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, GnRH pulses stimulate LH and FSH. Gonadal steroids and inhibin participate in feedback regulation. Negative feedback predominates in many contexts, but sustained ovarian estradiol during the appropriate preovulatory state can promote positive feedback and an LH surge. Positive feedback is not a general feature of every increase in estradiol. Its manifestation depends on reproductive phase and duration of the signal. Likewise, inhibin preferentially suppresses FSH, so two pituitary outputs responding to the same hypothalamic releasing hormone can behave differently.

Feedback is best interpreted by distinguishing the regulated variable from the signal used to correct it. In the thyroid axis, increased TSH can indicate a compensatory response rather than primary pituitary overactivity. In the adrenal axis, high ACTH with low cortisol suggests that the adrenal output is failing despite stimulation. High cortisol with low ACTH is compatible with an ACTH-independent glucocorticoid excess, while high cortisol with an ACTH concentration that is not suppressed suggests a source of persistent ACTH drive. These are mechanistic patterns, not sufficient clinical diagnoses; measurement timing, drugs, binding proteins and other physiological conditions can modify them.

An axis may exhibit long-loop feedback from a peripheral product, short-loop feedback from a pituitary output and local regulation of hypothalamic neuronal activity. Not every loop is equally important in every axis. A useful examination method is to mark each causal relation with an activating or inhibitory sign and then reason through the immediate consequences of changing one component. The next step is to allow feedback to act and distinguish the new equilibrium from the initial transient. The two predictions need not have the same direction.

7. Growth hormone, IGF-1 and the regulation of growth

Growth is a cumulative process involving increases in cell number, cell size, tissue mass and skeletal dimensions. GH supports growth but is not the only signal involved. Nutrition, thyroid hormones, insulin, gonadal steroids, developmental state and local growth factors influence how strongly tissue responds. A rapidly growing animal therefore cannot be understood by measuring circulating GH in isolation. Its growth plates must remain capable of longitudinal growth, substrates must be available, and the intracellular machinery of responsive cells must function.

Somatotrophs secrete GH in pulses under the influence of GHRH, somatostatin, ghrelin and feedback. GH binds to its receptor, activating receptor-associated JAK2 and downstream signalling including STAT5. One consequence is increased production of insulin-like growth factor 1, IGF-1, in the liver and other tissues. Liver-derived IGF-1 circulates as an endocrine factor; locally produced IGF-1 also participates in tissue growth. The IGF-1 receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase, unlike the cytokine-family GH receptor. The use of two distinct signalling systems helps explain why a defect in GH signalling can reduce IGF-1 output even if the IGF-1 receptor remains intact.

IGF-1 promotes growth-related activity of growth plate chondrocytes and other cells, whereas GH also has direct tissue effects. The growth plate contains zones of chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation followed by replacement of cartilage with bone. Longitudinal growth is possible while functional growth plates persist. Excess GH before epiphyseal closure can produce excessive linear growth, known as gigantism. After closure, excess GH cannot reopen the plates to produce the same form of long-bone lengthening; acromegaly instead involves changes such as enlargement of acral structures, soft tissue growth and skeletal remodelling. Age at exposure changes the anatomical result even when the endocrine disturbance is similar.

GH supports protein accretion in an appropriate nutritional context and promotes lipolysis. It can oppose certain actions of insulin on glucose metabolism. During fasting the GH concentration may increase while IGF-1 decreases, reflecting nutritional modulation of GH responsiveness and substrate availability. This pattern helps preserve fuel use and does not imply accelerated somatic growth. Conversely, low IGF-1 in a nutritionally compromised animal is not proof that the pituitary cannot release GH. Feedback and peripheral responsiveness must both be considered.

IGF-1 restrains GH secretion through feedback at the pituitary and hypothalamus. In GH receptor resistance, diminished IGF-1 generation removes part of this feedback while growth remains impaired. The expected pattern, in a simplified severe receptor-defect example, is relatively high GH with low IGF-1. In a primary deficiency of GH production, low GH drive leads to low IGF-1. Replacement of GH would be expected to restore signalling more effectively in the latter model than in a complete GH receptor-loss model. This comparison is a causal distinction between missing ligand and loss of responsiveness, not a treatment recommendation.

A single random GH concentration can be misleading because it may occur between pulses. IGF-1 has a different time course, influenced by binding proteins and nutritional status. Suppose two animals have identical mean GH concentrations over a sampling period but one secretes discrete peaks separated by troughs while the other has a constant concentration. Downstream tissues may respond differently due to receptor and intracellular adaptation. A time-integrated concentration can be useful for describing total exposure but cannot reconstruct the pattern of exposure. This principle is widely relevant to neuroendocrine signalling.

8. Prolactin and the logic of tonic inhibition

Prolactin is secreted by lactotrophs and supports the secretory activity of mammary epithelial cells in the appropriate hormonal environment. Its receptor activates cytokine-receptor-associated signalling, prominently JAK2 and STAT5 in mammary cells. Prolactin contributes to milk synthesis, while oxytocin causes contraction of myoepithelial cells to move milk already present within the mammary gland. In a question about lactation, the terms synthesis and ejection must therefore not be used interchangeably.

Prolactin is particularly informative because its baseline regulation differs from the typical releasing-hormone model. Dopamine from hypothalamic neurons continually restrains the lactotroph population. Reduced dopaminergic input during suckling contributes to the prolactin response. In an isolated viable pituitary preparation, removal of hypothalamic input can allow prolactin release rather than suppress it. Adding dopamine should restore inhibition if receptors and downstream signalling remain functional. The response provides a means to distinguish a tonic inhibitory mechanism from a tonic stimulatory mechanism.

Prolactin can itself influence hypothalamic dopamine neurons, creating a short feedback circuit that limits its own secretion. Pregnancy and lactation modify the balance of regulatory inputs. Estrogens influence lactotroph biology, and several signals besides dopamine can modify prolactin release. Describing dopamine as the principal tonic inhibitor does not imply that every prolactin fluctuation is caused solely by changes in dopamine concentration.

Excess prolactin can inhibit reproductive axis activity through effects on hypothalamic circuits controlling GnRH. Consequently a disturbance in one anterior pituitary output may alter other axes without requiring simultaneous destruction of all pituitary cell types. A stalk lesion can cause elevated prolactin through impaired dopamine delivery; a prolactin-secreting tumour can also elevate prolactin. The shared consequence does not establish a shared cause. To distinguish these mechanisms experimentally, one would need additional anatomical and functional evidence.

9. Posterior pituitary synthesis, axonal transport and vasopressin

Vasopressin, called antidiuretic hormone or ADH, and oxytocin are closely related nonapeptides. They are synthesised as parts of larger precursor molecules in magnocellular neurons. The precursor is processed in the secretory pathway, and the peptide is transported in granules along axons together with its associated neurophysin. The vasopressin precursor also yields copeptin. When action potentials arrive at terminals in the posterior pituitary, depolarisation opens voltage-dependent calcium channels and promotes release by exocytosis. The site of peptide synthesis is thus spatially separated from the principal site of release into blood.

An increase in effective extracellular osmolality tends to draw water out of cells. Central osmosensitive pathways respond to such changes and increase the drive for vasopressin release and thirst. Volume and pressure information from the circulation also contributes, especially when effective circulating volume is markedly reduced. The animal must sometimes conserve water even when osmolality alone would favour lower vasopressin output. Integration of these inputs gives physiological context to the hormone response.

Vasopressin binds V2 receptors on the basolateral surface of collecting duct principal cells. These Gs-coupled receptors increase cAMP and activate downstream signalling that promotes insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels into the apical membrane. Water can then move through the cell in response to the osmotic gradient established within the renal medulla, exiting through basolateral water channels. Increased permeability does not itself provide the osmotic driving force. If the medullary gradient is impaired, even abundant aquaporin channels cannot produce normal concentration of urine. The renal mechanisms are studied in greater detail in subunit 7.F.

Vasopressin also acts through V1a receptors in vascular smooth muscle, which can promote contraction, especially at higher circulating concentrations. The V1b receptor participates in ACTH regulation in the anterior pituitary. The three receptor effects show why a hormone should not be assigned a single intracellular pathway independently of its target cell. V2 receptor activation involves cAMP, while V1-family signalling is typically linked to phospholipase C and intracellular calcium. Receptor subtype explains how one circulating molecule can influence water transport, vascular tone and endocrine secretion in different cells.

Consider a simplified example of low vasopressin secretion. Collecting duct water permeability falls, less water is reabsorbed, and urine becomes abundant and dilute when the kidney is otherwise capable of normal filtration and solute handling. Thirst may increase and compensate for water loss if drinking is possible. A similar urine pattern can result from renal resistance to vasopressin despite adequate secretion. The former mechanism is central diabetes insipidus, also termed arginine vasopressin deficiency; the latter is nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, or vasopressin resistance. Both differ from diabetes mellitus, in which hyperglycaemia can cause osmotic diuresis through glucose remaining in the tubular fluid.

The separation between secretion and response is evident in a controlled physiological comparison. An appropriate V2 agonist should increase collecting duct water permeability in a model lacking endogenous vasopressin but having intact receptors and downstream components. The same intervention would have little effect in a model with complete V2 receptor loss. Partial defects, medullary gradient disruption and other causes of impaired concentration complicate real observations. The simplified contrast nevertheless provides a general principle: replacing a signal cannot compensate fully for failure of its receptor or of an indispensable downstream mechanism.

10. Oxytocin, neuroendocrine reflexes and feedback

Oxytocin is released from hypothalamic magnocellular neuron terminals in the posterior pituitary. It acts on Gq-linked receptors in uterine smooth muscle and mammary myoepithelial cells. In the mammary gland, myoepithelial contraction helps propel milk towards the ducts. The action is mechanical displacement of milk, not synthesis of its proteins, lipids or carbohydrate. A defect in oxytocin release may therefore impair milk ejection despite preservation of prolactin-dependent synthesis.

Suckling activates sensory afferents that transmit information to central circuits, leading to coordinated oxytocin neuronal activity and pulsatile hormone release. The response connects an external mechanical stimulus to an endocrine effect. It differs from a conventional autonomic efferent pathway in which the final transmitter is released directly at an organ's nerve ending. Here the final neurosecretory terminals release hormone into blood, enabling many responsive myoepithelial cells to receive the signal.

During labour, mechanical stimulation of the cervix and related reproductive tissues can contribute to oxytocin secretion and stronger uterine contractions. Stronger contractions can enhance mechanical stimulation, creating a reinforcing loop. The loop terminates when the initiating mechanical circumstances change, notably after delivery. Human parturition is multifactorial and cannot be reduced to oxytocin alone initiating labour; prostaglandins, uterine changes and maternal–fetal signals contribute. Positive feedback is a useful explanation for amplification of a process, not proof that a single hormone determines its beginning or timing.

Oxytocin and vasopressin differ in amino-acid sequence and receptor preferences despite structural similarity. At high concentrations, related peptides can show cross-reactivity. In an experiment testing the specificity of a receptor, one should use appropriate concentration ranges and selective antagonists or receptor-deficient cells. Observation of an effect at a high experimental concentration does not establish that it normally occurs at physiological concentrations.

11. Pulses, clearance and experimental interpretation

An endocrine measurement is a concentration, not a direct measurement of secretion rate. Concentration depends on the amount of hormone entering a compartment, the volume of distribution, degradation, binding and clearance. A simplified one-compartment model with a constant effective distribution volume V and first-order elimination constant k describes concentration C by dC/dt = S/V − kC, where S is secretion in mass per unit time, V is volume, C is mass per volume and k has units of reciprocal time. With constant secretion, the steady-state concentration is S/(Vk). Doubling secretion doubles steady-state concentration in this model only if distribution volume and elimination remain unchanged.

After a brief input has ended, the same model predicts C(t) = C(0)e^(−kt). The half-life equals ln(2)/k. In an illustrative calculation, a hormone concentration of 80 units per litre falls to 40 units per litre in ten minutes when additional input and baseline concentration are negligible. Another ten minutes gives 20 units per litre, and a further ten minutes gives 10 units per litre. The implied k is about 0.0693 per minute. These values are not measurements of a specific pituitary hormone. The simple model can fail if secretion continues or clearance becomes concentration dependent.

Sampling interval influences the apparent number and magnitude of pulses. Imagine secretory peaks lasting only a few minutes but samples collected once per hour. A sample may fall between peaks and suggest little hormone release. Increasing sampling frequency improves the chance of detecting fluctuations, though measured concentration is also smoothed by distribution and clearance. Comparing two experimental groups requires comparable sampling schedules, assay properties and physiological states. A single measurement cannot establish that pulsatility is absent.

Gland ablation and replacement experiments historically helped distinguish pituitary and target-gland functions. In a conceptual thyroid-axis experiment, removal of the thyroid lowers thyroid hormone and releases negative feedback on upstream structures. Providing thyroid hormone without restoring the physical gland should suppress excessive pituitary drive if the feedback pathways remain functional. Removal of the pituitary instead lowers stimulation of the thyroid and cannot be corrected at the level of pituitary TSH output merely by providing TRH. A TSH response to TRH requires surviving responsive thyrotrophs. The direction of the result depends on where the pathway is interrupted.

In vitro experiments can further separate central and peripheral effects. Isolated anterior pituitary cells allow researchers to vary one releasing factor while avoiding changes in peripheral endocrine glands. A vehicle control establishes baseline secretion; a receptor antagonist can test whether the response depends on the predicted receptor; a viability assay can distinguish reduced secretion from nonspecific cellular injury. However, an isolated preparation lacks intact portal delivery, normal neural inputs and peripheral feedback. Conclusions about a direct cellular response do not automatically establish the complete contribution of that response in an intact animal.

A useful strategy for endocrine questions is to begin with the known perturbation rather than the name of a disorder. Draw the normal connections, identify the site at which synthesis or responsiveness changes, predict the immediate downstream effects, then follow feedback towards the upstream structures. Finally consider timing and what was actually measured. If the perturbation could affect several levels, describe competing predictions and identify a measurement or comparison that would distinguish them.

Original practice questions

1. Part B: Where are the major hormones released by the posterior pituitary synthesised?

Answer: Magnocellular hypothalamic neurons.

The posterior lobe contains neurosecretory axon endings, not the cell bodies responsible for peptide synthesis.

2. Part B: Which anterior pituitary cell produces ACTH?

Answer: Corticotroph.

Corticotrophs process POMC to generate ACTH, which stimulates adrenal steroid production.

3. Part B: Which hypothalamic factor principally inhibits prolactin?

Answer: Dopamine.

Portal dopamine activates lactotroph D2 receptors and imposes tonic restraint.

4. Part B: Which three pituitary hormones share a common glycoprotein alpha subunit?

Answer: TSH, LH and FSH.

Their distinct beta subunits contribute to specificity.

5. Part B: What are the two capillary beds connected by portal vessels?

Answer: Median eminence primary plexus and anterior pituitary secondary plexus.

The route delivers hypothalamic factors locally.

6. Part B: What signal promotes GH release while somatostatin restrains it?

Answer: GHRH.

GHRH stimulates somatotrophs through cAMP-linked signalling.

7. Part B: Which receptor family mediates GH action?

Answer: Cytokine receptor family.

GH receptors signal via associated kinases, not intrinsic receptor tyrosine kinase activity.

8. Part B: Which receptor mediates the collecting duct action of vasopressin?

Answer: V2.

V2 activates Gs and increases cAMP in principal cells.

9. Part B: Which hormone causes milk ejection?

Answer: Oxytocin.

It contracts myoepithelial cells; prolactin supports synthesis.

10. Part B: Why is the paraventricular nucleus not equivalent to an oxytocin-only nucleus?

Answer: It contains multiple neuronal populations.

Both magnocellular peptide types occur there and parvocellular populations have other roles.

11. Part C: An animal has low thyroid hormone and elevated TSH. Which site is most consistent with the simple feedback model?

Answer: Primary thyroid failure.

Reduced output despite increased pituitary stimulation suggests peripheral dysfunction and loss of negative feedback.

12. Part C: Viable anterior pituitary cells are separated from all hypothalamic signals. Which hormone may rise rather than fall?

Answer: Prolactin.

Loss of dopaminergic inhibition increases secretion, unlike the loss of most releasing inputs.

13. Part C: An animal has low IGF-1 despite elevated GH. A GH receptor deletion is found. Explain the result.

Answer: GH resistance reduces IGF-1 and weakens feedback.

Increasing the ligand cannot restore signalling through an absent receptor.

14. Part C: A collecting duct model has absent endogenous AVP but intact V2 receptors. Compare its response to a V2 agonist with a complete receptor knockout.

Answer: The deficient model responds; the knockout does not.

The agonist bypasses deficient secretion but requires the receptor.

15. Part C: Portal secretion is 60 pg/min and blood flow is 3 mL/min. What is the concentration increment in a no-loss mixing model?

Answer: 20 pg/mL.

Divide secretion rate by flow; this is a hypothetical dilution calculation.

16. Part C: A hormone drops from 100 to 25 units/L over 20 minutes after secretion stops. What is its half-life in a first-order model?

Answer: 10 minutes.

Two successive halvings reduce 100 to 25; each occupies ten minutes.

17. Part C: A pulse-dependent hormone is delivered continuously at the same average rate. Can its effect be assumed identical?

Answer: No.

Receptor responsiveness, desensitisation and time-dependent downstream signalling can differ; continuous GnRH provides an example.

18. Part C: A GH excess begins after growth plate closure. Predict the contrast with onset before closure.

Answer: Acromegalic changes instead of marked longitudinal growth.

Closed growth plates limit lengthening; soft tissue and bone remodelling can continue.

19. Part C: A mammary gland produces milk but fails to eject it during stimulation. What endocrine mechanism should be investigated?

Answer: Oxytocin release or myoepithelial responsiveness.

The failure concerns displacement of preformed milk; prolactin-dependent synthesis can remain intact.

20. Part C: A receptor blocker reduces ACTH secretion in cultured corticotrophs but also markedly reduces viability. Is receptor-specific inhibition established?

Answer: No.

Nonspecific cellular injury could reduce secretion; a viability-matched control and receptor-specific evidence are needed.
