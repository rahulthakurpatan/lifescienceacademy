1. The biological problem: protection without permanent inflammation

The tissues of an animal are exposed to microorganisms through food, water, air and contact with surfaces. Most of those encounters do not cause disease. Some organisms remain outside the body, some occupy surfaces without causing measurable damage, and others invade or grow within vulnerable tissues. A useful immune system must therefore do more than identify a molecule as foreign. It must interpret where that molecule appears, how much of it is available, whether tissue integrity has been compromised and whether the organism can be controlled without an expensive inflammatory response. These decisions are distributed among barriers, tissue cells, circulating cells and lymphoid organs rather than being made by one central controller.

A pathogen is an organism or infectious agent capable of causing disease in an appropriate host. Infection describes establishment of an infectious agent in a host; it is not synonymous with disease. A person may have a microbial population on an epithelial surface without tissue invasion or clinically apparent injury. Conversely, physical damage to a tissue can initiate an immune response even when microorganisms are absent. This distinction explains why a tissue response is not sufficient evidence for infection. A mechanistic explanation must distinguish the origin of a stimulus from the response that follows it.

Defense can be understood as a series of linked problems. First, prevent unwanted access to internal tissues. Second, detect the breach quickly. Third, generate local conditions that restrict the threat. Fourth, if necessary, mobilize highly selective lymphocyte populations. Finally, stop the response and restore tissue function. The stages overlap in time: a mucus layer is still functioning while tissue macrophages receive signals, and innate mechanisms remain useful after antibodies appear. The phrase first line of defense describes a functional position, not a claim that the first line switches off when subsequent mechanisms begin.

Resistance and tolerance are related but distinguishable outcomes. Resistance limits the number of microorganisms or prevents their spread. Disease tolerance limits the harm caused by a given burden, for example through maintenance of epithelial integrity. Immune tolerance, by contrast, describes mechanisms limiting responses against particular antigens, including self antigens. A student must not equate these concepts merely because their names overlap. A host may reduce injury without eliminating every microorganism, and a strong inflammatory response can worsen tissue function despite reducing microbial burden.

The immune system comprises cells, tissues and soluble molecules that coordinate these outcomes. The barriers include living cells, extracellular structures and secretions. Resident cells can detect disturbances without waiting for circulating leukocytes. Blood supplies mobile cells and soluble defense components, while lymphatic vessels connect tissue events with secondary lymphoid organs. This organization is the prerequisite for interpreting how a receptor signal at the surface of a single dendritic cell can influence the subsequent behavior of a population of lymphocytes.

2. Innate and adaptive recognition: specificity, repertoire and time

Innate immunity is mediated by inherited recognition systems and effector mechanisms that are available without an antigen-specific clonal response having developed. Adaptive immunity in jawed vertebrates includes B and T lymphocytes with diverse antigen receptors generated during development. The two descriptions refer mainly to the way recognition and response populations are organized. A receptor expressed by a macrophage does not become adaptive merely because it binds a ligand selectively; specificity and adaptive diversity are distinct properties.

The genome encodes Toll-like receptors and other pattern-recognition receptors as relatively fixed gene products. Cells expressing a particular receptor can recognize characteristic chemical or structural features shared by many organisms. Two macrophages of the same functional type may carry much the same pattern-recognition repertoire. The exact abundance of those receptors can nevertheless differ with tissue location, differentiation and prior stimulation. Germline encoding does not mean that receptor expression is invariant.

A mature conventional B cell instead displays an immunoglobulin-derived receptor whose variable region is assembled during lymphocyte development by somatic gene rearrangement. A conventional T cell similarly expresses an assembled antigen receptor, although its usual ligand is a peptide presented by a major histocompatibility complex molecule rather than a free native protein. Receptor diversity exists before a particular infection occurs. Antigen-driven clonal selection then expands appropriate populations. The initial number of cells with a useful specificity can be small, so expansion and differentiation require time.

A primary adaptive response generally takes several days to generate substantial antigen-specific effector output, while pre-existing barrier and innate responses can begin much earlier. The timing is not an absolute stopwatch. An epithelial cell may release an induced defense molecule only after transcription and translation; conversely, a previously immunized organism may contain circulating antibodies that act immediately. The mechanism and developmental origin of the response, rather than speed alone, determine its classification.

Classical adaptive memory involves persistent antigen-experienced lymphocytes and, in some settings, long-lived antibody-secreting cells. Previous encounters can also alter innate responses through changes in cellular metabolism and chromatin, a phenomenon often called trained immunity. Such altered responses need not involve rearrangement of an antigen-receptor gene or expansion of a uniquely antigen-specific clone. The classical comparison remains useful if expressed as differences in the basis of memory, not as an absolute prohibition on experience-dependent changes in innate cells.

A familiar sequence illustrates the complementarity. When bacteria pass through injured skin, epithelial and resident immune cells can recognize microbial material and initiate local defense. Antigen can simultaneously reach lymphoid tissue, where selected lymphocytes are activated under appropriate conditions. Later antibodies can bind extracellular organisms and make them easier for phagocytes to remove. The antigen specificity came from a lymphocyte, but the cell that executes uptake may be a macrophage. Assigning every complete defense process exclusively to one arm would obscure this partnership.

3. The development of innate recognition as a scientific concept

The history of immunology illustrates how a biological response can be understood at successively more precise levels. Observations of phagocytic cells established that mobile cells could surround and internalize material, while studies of serum established that soluble molecules could participate in protection. Neither observation alone explained how an immune cell selected a biologically relevant target. The mechanistic challenge was to connect selective recognition with the change in cell behavior.

Work on the fruit fly provided an important connection between development and immunity. The Toll pathway had already been associated with embryonic pattern formation. In 1996, Lemaitre and colleagues reported that components of this pathway were also important for antifungal defense in adult Drosophila. The genetic logic was particularly informative: altering components of a regulatory pathway affected the induction of an antimicrobial response and the ability of the animal to resist fungal challenge. This made a signaling pathway, rather than merely a descriptive classification of blood cells, central to the understanding of innate defense. The fly receptor responds to activated Spätzle; the example should not be simplified into a claim that all Toll-family receptors directly bind fungal surfaces.

In 1997, Medzhitov, Preston-Hurlburt and Janeway showed that a constitutively active form of a human Toll homologue could activate NF-κB-dependent outputs in human cell lines, including inflammatory and costimulatory gene expression. An important feature of this experiment is that the receptor was engineered to signal without the normal activating stimulus. It established that receptor activity was sufficient to engage downstream immune programs in that context. It did not by itself establish the natural ligand or demonstrate the full physiological response of an intact animal.

In 1998, Poltorak and colleagues connected impaired responses to bacterial lipopolysaccharide in particular mouse strains with mutations in Tlr4. The association of independently defective alleles with impaired signaling helped identify a specific host receptor pathway involved in sensing an important microbial feature. A receptor mutation may affect binding, signal transmission, protein stability or localization; the mere presence of a mutation does not establish which of those processes is defective. Subsequent molecular studies clarified the role of additional components in the recognition complex.

Taken together, these findings show how evidence from distinct approaches can converge. Comparative genetics demonstrated that a signaling family participates in defense. A gain-of-function experiment established signaling capacity in a mammalian system. Genetic defects in stimulus responsiveness tied a particular receptor to a natural microbial input. A strong examination answer preserves the different inferential strengths of these experiments. It avoids attributing the discovery of every modern receptor-ligand relationship to a single historical observation.

4. Immune cells: a division of labor

Leukocytes are nucleated white blood cells. Most originate from hematopoietic precursors, but their mature functions and tissue distributions differ. Granulocytes contain characteristic cytoplasmic granules, while monocytes and lymphocytes are conventionally grouped among agranulocytes in blood morphology. This terminology is not a statement that lymphocytes lack every intracellular granule; cytotoxic lymphocytes, for instance, carry secretory granules. Morphological and functional classifications must not be confused.

Neutrophils are major rapidly recruited phagocytes in many bacterial and fungal infections. Their segmented nuclei and abundant granules are useful recognition features on a blood smear. They can internalize material and use antimicrobial mechanisms to restrict it. A neutrophil has limited time in which to act compared with a long-lived tissue cell, so recruitment from the circulating pool is a characteristic component of many acute responses. Their abundance in a tissue may be a consequence of chemokine production rather than local differentiation from stem cells.

Monocytes circulate in the blood and can enter tissues, where some differentiate into macrophages or related populations. Many tissue-resident macrophage populations, however, have developmental origins and maintenance patterns that do not require continual replacement by circulating monocytes. Macrophages remove cellular debris, recognize disturbances, release signaling molecules and can participate in antigen presentation. A tissue macrophage may already be present at the site of injury when neutrophils have yet to arrive. This distinction between resident surveillance and recruited defense explains differences in timing without requiring different receptor principles.

Dendritic cells are specialized at connecting tissue recognition to lymphocyte activation. They can acquire antigen, alter their gene expression following appropriate activation and travel to lymphoid sites. Their capacity to stimulate naive T cells is particularly important because naive lymphocytes are generally encountered in secondary lymphoid organs rather than randomly distributed at every damaged epithelial site. The presence of an antigen-bearing cell alone is not sufficient to guarantee effective priming; appropriate costimulation and contextual signals matter.

Natural killer cells are lymphoid-lineage cells belonging to innate immunity. They detect changes in potential target cells by integrating signals from activating and inhibitory receptors rather than using the rearranged antigen receptor characteristic of conventional T cells. A reduction in inhibitory recognition of self MHC class I may contribute to target susceptibility, but loss of that signal does not guarantee killing in the absence of appropriate activating signals. Their cytotoxic execution mechanisms are developed in the following chapter.

Mast cells are usually resident in tissues, often near interfaces and blood vessels. Their granules contain mediators that can rapidly alter local conditions. Basophils are a distinct circulating granulocyte population, not simply mast cells temporarily present in blood. Both can participate in responses associated with IgE and certain inflammatory contexts. Eosinophils are prominent in several type 2 responses, including defense against some multicellular parasites and allergic inflammation. The size of a multicellular parasite makes engulfment of the whole organism impractical for a single leukocyte, creating a rationale for extracellular effector strategies.

B lymphocytes can recognize native antigen and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells after suitable activation. T lymphocytes include helper populations that coordinate immune responses and cytotoxic populations that can remove particular target cells. Regulatory T cells help constrain immune activity. The label lymphocyte therefore does not identify a single effector mechanism: NK cells, B cells and T cells share a broad morphological and developmental classification while differing profoundly in receptor organization and biological roles.

5. Primary and secondary lymphoid organs

The primary lymphoid organs provide environments for the development and maturation of lymphocytes. In mammals, bone marrow supports formation of blood-cell populations and development of B cells. The thymus provides a specialized environment for T-cell development and selection. T-cell precursors originate from hematopoietic sources before arriving in the thymus. Consequently, the statement that T cells mature in the thymus must not be interpreted as the claim that all of their precursor cells are generated there.

Selection during lymphocyte development helps generate a functional repertoire while limiting dangerous self-reactivity. The primary organs are therefore not merely factories for increasing leukocyte number. They help shape the recognition properties of the populations that subsequently circulate. Detailed receptor rearrangement, thymic selection and lymphocyte activation are assigned to later chapters; the present organizing distinction is developmental maturation versus initiation of antigen-driven responses.

Secondary lymphoid organs provide structured opportunities for mature lymphocytes to encounter antigen and accessory cells. Lymph nodes receive lymph draining from tissues, whereas the spleen monitors blood-borne material. Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues sample antigens associated with epithelial interfaces. These complementary input routes make immune surveillance more efficient than requiring each lymphocyte to travel independently through every tissue.

Lymph nodes receive material from tissue spaces through afferent lymphatic vessels. Soluble antigen can drain with fluid, while migratory antigen-presenting cells can carry tissue-derived material. Naive lymphocytes also enter nodes from the blood through specialized high endothelial venules. T and B cells occupy characteristic regions, supporting interactions between relevant cell types. Lymph eventually leaves through efferent vessels and returns to the circulation. If tissue drainage is disrupted, reduced antigen delivery to a draining node may impair priming despite preserved receptor function in the lymphocytes themselves.

The spleen does not receive lymph through a system of afferent lymphatics comparable to a lymph node. Blood delivers antigen and cells. White pulp contains organized lymphoid regions, while red pulp participates in filtering blood and removing aged or abnormal erythrocytes. The marginal region helps organize encounters between circulating antigen and immune cells. This anatomy provides a causal reason for the greater importance of splenic surveillance when microbial material enters the blood directly.

Mucosal lymphoid structures, including Peyer's patches in the intestine, function where the host is continually exposed to food antigens and microbial populations. Antigen-sampling routes allow surveillance without requiring widespread breakdown of the epithelial barrier. Sampling is not identical to invasion: a controlled transfer of material to immune cells can be compatible with intact tissue organization. A central challenge at mucosae is therefore to combine defense with restraint.

These routes explain a common experimental distinction. Antigen introduced into a peripheral tissue may first be detected in a draining lymph node; blood-borne antigen may instead be prominent in the spleen. A normal spleen cannot simply replace every anatomical function of a missing draining node. Both are secondary lymphoid organs, but the fluid they monitor and their routes of antigen access are different.

6. Cellular cooperation and the routes linking antigen to lymphocytes

The blood provides transport, but the immune cells counted in a blood sample represent only part of the immune system. Many macrophages, mast cells and lymphocytes occupy tissues or lymphoid organs. A change in circulating cell number can reflect migration between compartments, survival, release from a storage pool or production in marrow. A high circulating count therefore does not demonstrate that a particular tissue has been supplied with functional cells. This distinction is a direct application of the stock-flow reasoning developed in the preceding hematopoiesis chapter.

When lymphocytes circulate, they do not enter all sites with equal probability. The expression of adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors influences the tissues and lymphoid compartments they can enter. Specialized blood vessels in lymph nodes allow many naive lymphocytes to reach a site where antigens from peripheral tissues are delivered. The architecture creates a meeting place for two otherwise dispersed populations: rare antigen-specific lymphocytes and cells carrying relevant material from a particular tissue. Both transport and recognition must function for efficient priming.

Within a lymph node, the distribution of B cells and T cells helps organize their interactions. B cells often occupy follicles where they can encounter native antigen under appropriate conditions. T cells occupy regions enriched in dendritic cells. Some antigen arrives in soluble form and some is transported by antigen-bearing cells. These routes are not mutually exclusive, and the physical properties of the antigen influence its distribution. A lymph node should not be pictured as an undifferentiated bag in which every antigen contacts every cell simultaneously.

Dendritic cell migration connects a tissue event with the location of a lymphocyte response. Recognition of relevant stimuli can induce changes in dendritic cell behavior, including altered expression of molecules needed for communication and changes in migration. A cell that carries antigen but has not undergone appropriate activation may not efficiently initiate the same response as a suitably activated cell carrying equivalent antigen. The biological implication is that antigen capture and productive priming are distinct events. The upcoming antigen-presentation and T-cell activation chapters will explain the molecular requirements of that distinction.

A hypothetical experiment can separate these events. Suppose dendritic cells from two groups internalize equal amounts of a labeled protein. One group receives an appropriate innate recognition signal and the other does not. After a suitable interval, compare a measure of activation state and a separate measure of lymphocyte response. Equal uptake accompanied by different lymphocyte responses suggests that the amount of internalized antigen was not the only determining factor. It does not prove that every difference arises from one costimulatory molecule. Antigen processing, migration, survival and other signals remain possible contributors.

The lymphatic route is especially relevant to antigens originating outside the blood. Tissue fluid enters lymphatic capillaries and is transported through nodes before ultimately returning to the circulation. Afferent and efferent describe the direction relative to the node, not the identity of the material carried. The spleen, in contrast, is positioned to encounter material carried by blood. Its filtering role has consequences for both immune recognition and removal of abnormal erythrocytes, functions that must be distinguished in examination questions.

Primary lymphoid organs support developmental selection and survival of immature lymphocytes, whereas secondary organs coordinate encounters of mature lymphocytes with antigen. An experimental animal lacking thymic function may have severe abnormalities in conventional T-cell development even if its lymph nodes are anatomically present. An animal with defective lymphatic drainage can instead have normally developed T cells but impaired antigen delivery to a particular node. These different defects illustrate why the phrase weak adaptive immunity is insufficient as a mechanistic diagnosis.

Immune responses also occur outside conventional lymphoid organs. Effector cells can act in infected tissues, and certain lymphocyte populations reside in epithelial compartments. Organized lymphoid structures improve encounter efficiency but do not contain every event of the adaptive response. The distinction between initiation, expansion and execution provides a more accurate functional map than assigning every immune process to one organ. It is particularly important when interpreting a question that measures antigen-specific cells in a draining node but microbial clearance in a distant tissue.

7. Physical and chemical barriers

The skin provides a physical boundary whose effectiveness depends on the organization and renewal of epidermal cells. Its outer cornified layer restricts access through a combination of keratin-rich cells and extracellular lipids. Desquamation removes superficial material, while the chemical environment can limit growth of particular organisms. A cut changes more than the number of bacteria near the surface: it bypasses spatial separation and exposes underlying tissue to molecules that normally remain outside. Resident cells can then detect both microbial products and injury-associated signals.

Mucosal epithelia differ from skin because they must allow physiological exchanges such as nutrient absorption or gas movement. Intercellular junctions restrict uncontrolled passage between neighboring cells, while secreted mucus separates microorganisms from epithelial surfaces and traps material. A defective tight junction can increase paracellular access without reducing the quantity of mucus. A defect in mucus production can have a different immediate cause even if the eventual outcome is also greater microbial exposure of underlying cells.

In the respiratory tract, coordinated beating of cilia moves mucus and trapped particles toward regions from which they can be cleared. Mucus production and ciliary movement are functionally complementary rather than interchangeable. Excessively immobile mucus may trap particles initially but fail to remove them efficiently. This illustrates why a barrier can be a dynamic transport system, not merely a static wall.

Secretions provide additional chemical protection. Lysozyme hydrolyzes a bond in the peptidoglycan backbone of bacteria; accessibility of the substrate is influenced by other envelope structures. Defensins and other antimicrobial peptides can interact with microbial membranes, among other effects. Their activity depends on properties such as charge, hydrophobicity, target membrane composition and the local chemical environment. These molecules are not all interchangeable enzymes, and the term antimicrobial peptide does not specify a single molecular mechanism.

Gastric acidity restricts many ingested organisms, although acid-tolerant organisms or protected particles may survive exposure. Lower pH does not act as a universal sterilizing mechanism. Acid changes protonation states and can disrupt microbial physiology, but susceptibility differs between organisms and depends on exposure conditions. The gastrointestinal tract also contains mucus, epithelial junctions and a complex community of resident microorganisms, so its defense cannot be reduced to acid alone.

Soluble recognition and defense factors act in tissue fluid, secretions and blood. Collectins, for example, can recognize carbohydrate arrangements, and complement components can contribute to microbial restriction or opsonization. Secretory IgA also participates in mucosal protection, but its adaptive origin must be distinguished from germline-encoded innate soluble recognition factors. An anatomical location such as mucus does not determine whether a particular molecule belongs to innate or adaptive immunity.

The resident microbiota can contribute to colonization resistance by occupying ecological niches, competing for nutrients and influencing host barrier function. These effects are not equivalent to recognition of every resident organism as harmless. A normally tolerated organism may become harmful when it gains access to an inappropriate compartment. The immune significance of microbial material thus depends partly on location and host condition rather than solely on microbial identity.

8. The geography of surveillance: epithelial interfaces and tissue compartments

A barrier tissue cannot be understood from a single section of an epithelial cell. The cell has an apical surface facing a lumen or an external compartment and a basal surface facing underlying tissue. The junctional complex helps maintain this polarity by restricting uncontrolled movement of membrane components and substances between cells. Polarity provides an organizational basis for directing transporters, secretory pathways and signaling receptors to particular surfaces. It also permits a tissue to respond differently to material present on its luminal side and material reaching its internal side. This is a general principle, not a claim that every epithelial TLR is restricted to a single surface.

Imagine an epithelial monolayer grown as a conceptual model with a distinct apical and basal compartment. A tracer applied to the apical compartment normally reaches the basal compartment only slowly. If a mutation weakens intercellular junctions, the rate of passage of an extracellular tracer may increase without increasing transcellular transport through each cell. A hydrophilic tracer chosen to remain outside cells would make this distinction easier to investigate. A larger tracer may be excluded even when a smaller tracer passes, because the barrier is not necessarily a simple all-or-none partition. The resulting permeability profile can help distinguish altered junction selectivity from gross disruption of the epithelium.

The immune consequence of increased permeability depends on the contents of the exposed compartment. Movement of inert water-soluble tracer alone does not establish immune activation. If microbial products also gain access to basolateral epithelial receptors or underlying sentinel cells, the probability of activation can increase. To test whether a signaling pathway is required for the resulting response, compare cells with and without the relevant PRR under conditions in which permeability is measured independently. The presence of a functional junctional defect in both groups permits separation of the physical breach from the cellular interpretation of that breach. A receptor deletion that prevents cytokine induction does not necessarily repair the original junctional defect.

The extracellular matrix under an epithelial layer is part of the structural context through which recruited cells and soluble molecules move. It can influence retention of proteins, migration routes and tissue mechanical properties. A microorganism reaching the underlying tissue encounters a different set of nutritional conditions and defense molecules from those available in the lumen. Tissue-resident macrophages and other sentinel cells can communicate with nearby stromal and endothelial cells. The response is thus determined by a local network rather than by the epithelial cell in isolation. Earlier Academy chapters on adhesion and extracellular matrix explain how the physical relationships among these cells are established.

Skin, intestine and lung demonstrate distinct solutions to the shared problem of maintaining a boundary. Skin can support a relatively thick cornified barrier because rapid exchange of nutrients or respiratory gases across it is not its principal function. The intestine must maintain a thin absorptive interface with enormous contact with luminal contents. The alveolar surface must provide a short diffusion distance for gases; thick protective material at that surface would interfere with its main physiological task. Specialized pulmonary surfactant-associated proteins and alveolar macrophages contribute to defense in this setting, whereas ciliated clearance is especially important in conducting airways. A general theory of barriers must account for the physiological cost of making a tissue less permeable.

Epithelial renewal contributes to defense as well as structural maintenance. Damaged cells can be replaced, and normal turnover can remove organisms attached to shed cells. Excessively rapid or poorly coordinated cell loss, however, can expose underlying tissue. Cell division alone cannot maintain a functional barrier if newly formed cells fail to establish appropriate junctions and polarity. Similarly, cell death can have different consequences according to the integrity of neighboring cells and the manner in which cellular contents are handled. The relevant variable is the continuity and function of the tissue rather than simply the number of cells produced.

A mechanical clearance system can be represented as a competition between delivery, retention, growth and removal of particles. The number of particles detected at a surface at one time reflects their entry and disappearance over the preceding interval. A reduction in removal can increase the retained load even if the incoming exposure is unchanged. Conversely, increasing mucus production may not reduce retained particles if transport of that mucus is defective. These considerations explain why measuring microbial abundance at one time does not identify the failed barrier mechanism. The next step is to connect the physical function of the barrier to an independently measured recognition response.

9. Soluble protection and the molecular chemistry of antimicrobial barriers

Lysozyme illustrates how a chemical barrier can be directed against a molecular structure. Peptidoglycan contains alternating sugar derivatives connected through glycosidic bonds, with peptide crosslinks helping maintain the strength of the wall. Lysozyme catalyzes hydrolysis of the glycosidic linkage between N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetylglucosamine. Breaking these linkages weakens the polymeric network. The bacterial cell may become vulnerable to osmotic stress, but lysis depends on the overall envelope and the surrounding medium. A biochemical demonstration of substrate cleavage is not by itself proof of complete killing of intact bacteria.

The peptidoglycan of many Gram-positive bacteria is more accessible from the exterior than that of typical Gram-negative bacteria, in which an outer membrane provides an additional barrier. This distinction helps explain why sensitivity to lysozyme cannot be inferred simply from whether a bacterium contains peptidoglycan. A suitable enzyme can have a substrate present but lack physical access to it. Conversely, damage to the outer membrane can expose a previously shielded structure. Some bacteria also modify wall constituents in ways that alter susceptibility. The general reasoning resembles receptor compartmentalization: an interaction requires both chemical compatibility and encounter.

Antimicrobial peptides illustrate a different molecular strategy. Many possess positively charged residues and hydrophobic regions. Electrostatic interactions can favor association with negatively charged microbial surfaces, and hydrophobic interactions can stabilize insertion or association with lipid membranes. The resulting membrane disturbance may impair permeability, ion balance or other aspects of cell physiology. This explanation is useful for many membrane-active peptides, but not every antimicrobial peptide acts by forming the same type of pore. Some peptides also interact with intracellular targets or participate in host signaling. Structure-function relationships should be described with representative mechanisms rather than a universal model.

Electrostatic attraction between a cationic peptide and an anionic surface is affected by the ions present in solution. Dissolved ions can screen charges, reducing the range of electrostatic interactions. A peptide that is highly active in one buffer may therefore be less active in a different ionic environment. pH can alter protonation of amino acid side chains and surface groups. The local chemical conditions of a secretion help determine the biological consequences of an antimicrobial molecule. This does not imply that salt always eliminates peptide activity or that every peptide has the same optimal pH.

Selectivity is also conditional. Mammalian plasma membranes differ in lipid composition and organization from many microbial membranes, and extracellular host structures can influence peptide availability. However, a concentration that damages microbial membranes may also damage host cells at sufficiently high exposure. An experimental comparison of microbial killing and host-cell viability can reveal the relative separation of these effects under specified conditions. Selectivity is a measured property of a molecule in a biological context, not a consequence guaranteed solely by the name antimicrobial.

The iron-binding glycoprotein lactoferrin provides an example of defense through nutrient availability. Iron supports many cellular reactions, including electron transfer and the activity of numerous microbial enzymes. By binding iron, host proteins can limit the pool available for microbial growth. Certain microorganisms produce strategies for obtaining iron under limiting conditions, so the outcome is an interaction between the host restriction system and microbial acquisition systems. A fall in growth rate under iron limitation does not necessarily mean that bacteria are killed; growth restriction and direct lethality are different outcomes. Lactoferrin can also have activities beyond iron sequestration, so one assay cannot establish its entire biological role.

The soluble fraction of an innate response may contain both direct effector molecules and molecules that alter the recognition of a target by cells. Opsonization means coating material in a way that promotes its recognition and uptake by cells bearing the corresponding receptors. An opsonin need not kill the microorganism directly. An experiment measuring enhanced uptake after adding a soluble factor would support an opsonic contribution, but an independent viability measurement would be needed to determine whether the ingested organisms were killed. Complement will develop this distinction in greater mechanistic detail in Node 4.C.03.

A productive way to compare barriers is to ask what variable each one controls. A tight junction restricts the route of passage. Mucociliary transport changes retention and removal. Lysozyme alters a polymeric microbial structure. Antimicrobial peptides may perturb membrane integrity. Nutrient sequestration changes the resources available for growth. Several mechanisms can operate against the same microorganism at once. A defect in one mechanism may be partly compensated by another, but compensation is not guaranteed because their targets and anatomical locations differ.

10. Patterns and danger: what is being recognized?

A pathogen-associated molecular pattern, or PAMP, is a characteristic molecular feature associated with microorganisms and recognized by an innate sensing system. Representative examples include bacterial flagellin, lipopolysaccharide and particular forms of microbial nucleic acids. The abbreviation can be misleading if it suggests that such molecules occur exclusively in pathogenic microbes. Many commensals possess the same structural features, so microbial-associated molecular pattern is also used where pathogenicity is not implied.

A damage-associated molecular pattern, or DAMP, is an endogenous material whose exposure, release or altered state can signal cellular stress or injury. Extracellular ATP and released nuclear proteins are representative examples. The chemical identity of a molecule may be the same inside a healthy cell and outside an injured cell; a change in compartment can change its signaling significance. Not every extracellular ATP signal is mediated by a Toll-like receptor. Distinct receptor systems can participate in sensing injury, and receptor assignment must be made from evidence rather than the DAMP label alone.

A pattern-recognition receptor, or PRR, is a recognition molecule involved in detecting recurring molecular features or disturbances. These include membrane-bound TLRs, cytosolic NOD-like and RIG-I-like receptors, and carbohydrate-recognizing lectins. A cytosolic sensor can detect microbial material that would not ordinarily have access to the cytoplasm, whereas a surface receptor may sample the extracellular environment. The relevant receptor is determined by accessibility as well as molecular compatibility.

Microbial recognition can lead to more than one response. A cell may increase expression of cytokines or antimicrobial proteins, alter costimulatory molecules, or coordinate with neighboring cells. A receptor that promotes ingestion and a receptor that primarily initiates transcription need not have identical biological consequences. Some receptor systems perform overlapping functions, which is why a single gene deletion often reduces a response rather than eliminating all evidence of defense.

The distinction between PAMP and DAMP is an interpretation of stimulus origin. Both may coexist at an infected injury site. A sterile tissue injury can generate inflammatory signals through host-derived molecules, while microbial material can be detected in the absence of overt tissue damage. When assessing a hypothetical sample, the presence of a response to one class of stimulus does not establish the absence of the other. Experimental separation of the two inputs requires suitable controls.

The challenge of self versus nonself is consequently more complex than the statement that innate immunity responds to everything foreign. Many foreign molecules are tolerated, and damaged self can initiate a response. Receptor accessibility, abundance, ligand form, tissue context and regulatory mechanisms all contribute to the output. This is why a receptor-ligand table is only the beginning of mechanistic understanding.

11. A comparative map of pattern-recognition systems

Toll-like receptors are a particularly useful family for understanding innate recognition because the membrane-spanning architecture links an accessible molecular feature to cytoplasmic signaling. However, TLRs cannot sample every biologically relevant compartment. A virus replicating in the cytoplasm can generate nucleic acid structures accessible to cytoplasmic sensors even if little material reaches endosomes. A bacterium or fungal particle can expose surface carbohydrates that are recognized by lectin systems. Overlapping families allow cells to detect related disturbances by different routes.

NOD1 and NOD2 are examples of cytosolic NOD-like receptors associated with sensing fragments derived from bacterial peptidoglycan. Their typical ligand preferences differ, and their downstream responses can include inflammatory gene expression. Their location distinguishes them from surface TLRs. If a bacterial fragment is introduced into the cytoplasm, a cytosolic receptor may become relevant even when a surface receptor is absent. This principle should not be generalized to every member of the NOD-like receptor family, because some participate in different cellular sensing and regulatory processes. The family name indicates structural relationships, not identical ligands.

RIG-I-like receptors illustrate cytoplasmic detection of viral RNA-associated features. RIG-I and MDA5 differ in aspects of their RNA recognition preferences. Their activation can contribute to antiviral gene expression through the adaptor MAVS. This pathway provides a useful conceptual contrast with an endosomal TLR3 response: both can be associated with double-stranded RNA sensing and interferon induction, yet the receptors, compartments and downstream adaptor organization are different. A single interferon measurement therefore cannot reveal whether the initiating nucleic acid sensor was endosomal or cytosolic.

The cGAS-STING system provides an analogous comparison for DNA-associated sensing. Cytosolic DNA can activate the enzyme cGAS, leading to synthesis of a cyclic nucleotide signal that activates STING and promotes downstream responses. This is distinct from TLR9-mediated endosomal DNA sensing. Host DNA can also reach inappropriate compartments during cellular stress. For the present chapter, the important concept is that abnormal molecular location can activate a pathway even when the molecular constituent is not foreign. The detailed molecular regulation of this system is beyond the approved TLR-centered scope.

C-type lectin receptors recognize particular carbohydrate structures or related ligands, and some initiate signaling while others contribute to uptake or adhesion. Dectin-1 is a representative example associated with recognition of fungal beta-glucans. It helps explain why absence of a particular TLR does not remove all capacity to detect fungal material. Soluble lectins, by contrast, need not be membrane-spanning receptors; their recognition functions can be coupled to other defense mechanisms. The term lectin describes a recognition property, not an exclusive signaling architecture.

The word pattern should not be interpreted as a repeated visual shape alone. A receptor can be selective for a chemical modification, a defined molecular arrangement, a polymer length or a molecular end. Such features can convey information about the biochemical process that produced the ligand. The discriminatory power of a nucleic acid receptor depends partly on those properties and partly on the compartment where the ligand appears. A receptor can recognize a repeated molecular feature without being able to determine the exact species of organism from which it originated.

A receptor combination can carry more information than a single activated receptor. For example, the coincidence of signals reflecting bacterial envelope material and intracellular exposure may influence the behavior of a cell differently from exposure to one material confined to the extracellular environment. This is a conceptual principle of integration, not an assertion that every two-receptor combination creates a synergistic response. Some combinations may be additive, antagonistic or limited by a shared downstream component. Experimentally, synergy must be demonstrated relative to a defined expectation rather than inferred from a larger combined response.

These systems illustrate the evolutionary benefit of partial redundancy. A microbial structure may be modified, concealed or released only under particular conditions. Several recognition mechanisms broaden the range of situations in which a threat can be noticed. Redundancy does not mean that all receptors are interchangeable. A knockout of one receptor can produce a large defect in a particular ligand assay while leaving responses to unrelated stimuli intact. The relative contribution of each sensor is a question about the biological context and readout, not a fixed property of the receptor family as a whole.

12. Toll-like receptor architecture and compartment-specific sensing

Toll-like receptors are type I transmembrane proteins with extracellular or luminal ligand-recognition regions rich in leucine-rich repeats, a membrane-spanning segment and a cytosolic Toll/interleukin-1 receptor domain, usually abbreviated TIR. The recognition region and the signaling region are physically connected but perform different tasks. Ligand-induced association or rearrangement of receptors brings their cytosolic domains into a configuration that supports adaptor recruitment. A mutation disrupting the TIR domain may therefore prevent signaling without abolishing ligand association.

Several mammalian TLRs sample the cell surface. TLR2 can form heterodimers with TLR1 or TLR6, which helps recognize different classes of microbial lipoproteins. TLR5 recognizes flagellin. TLR4 participates in sensing bacterial lipopolysaccharide through a recognition complex involving MD-2 and accessory delivery factors such as CD14. The lipid A portion of LPS is central to typical TLR4 stimulation, although variations in microbial LPS structure can affect the resulting response.

TLR3, TLR7, TLR8 and TLR9 are characteristically associated with endosomal recognition. TLR3 senses double-stranded RNA; TLR7 and TLR8 respond to appropriate RNA-derived ligands; TLR9 recognizes particular DNA features including suitable CpG-containing sequences. These descriptions are representative mammalian relationships, not universal statements about every species, cell type or molecular preparation. Endosomal localization gives the receptors access to nucleic acids liberated from internalized material.

Receptor localization contributes to discrimination because nucleic acids are present in both host and microorganisms. Healthy nuclear DNA is ordinarily separated from endosomal nucleic-acid receptors. Internalized self nucleic acids can become stimulatory in abnormal contexts, and endosomal processing and receptor regulation are additional constraints. A receptor recognizing a molecular pattern is not an infallible detector of microbial identity.

A useful prediction concerns receptor trafficking. Suppose a cell contains a normal amount of an endosomal TLR but a mutation prevents the receptor from reaching its normal compartment. Adding an extracellular ligand that ordinarily enters through endocytosis may no longer activate the receptor efficiently. Measuring total protein abundance would miss this defect. Imaging localization, measuring ligand uptake and assessing downstream responsiveness could distinguish trafficking failure from loss of receptor expression.

Human and mouse TLR repertoires are not identical. Human TLR10 and mouse Tlr10, for example, should not be treated as equally functional genes, and some mouse TLRs do not have a corresponding functional human receptor. An experimental result in a mouse receptor system must be interpreted within that species before inferring a human counterpart. The appropriate learning objective is receptor family logic and representative examples rather than memorizing a supposedly universal list.

13. Receptor structure, accessory proteins and ligand delivery

A common error is to imagine that a Toll-like receptor is a self-contained molecular switch that recognizes a soluble ligand in the same way regardless of the surrounding molecules. The typical TLR architecture contains a leucine-rich repeat ectodomain, a transmembrane region and an intracellular TIR domain. Recognition and signaling are coupled through the orientation and association of receptor complexes. This arrangement can require accessory proteins for ligand presentation or complex formation. A defect in one accessory protein can therefore resemble receptor deficiency in a functional assay even when the receptor remains present.

TLR4 illustrates this principle particularly clearly. Lipopolysaccharide is an amphipathic molecule associated with the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Its lipid A portion is hydrophobic and can occur in aggregated forms rather than behaving like a freely dissolved small hydrophilic ligand. In mammalian recognition, LPS-binding protein and CD14 can facilitate transfer of LPS to a receptor complex involving MD-2 and TLR4. MD-2 provides an important binding environment for lipid A. The complete functional recognition process should not be reduced to a statement that a single isolated receptor molecule binds all forms of LPS equally.

Receptor dimerization creates an arrangement in which intracellular domains are positioned for productive signaling. In a typical activated TLR4-MD-2 complex, ligand binding and interactions among receptor complexes help stabilize the activated organization. A mutation in the ligand-associated region could alter the concentration needed for activation; a mutation affecting receptor-receptor association could impair activation even if ligand association remains possible. A mutation in the intracellular region could leave both extracellular processes relatively intact but prevent downstream responses. Comparing these defects requires separate measurements of receptor abundance, ligand association and signaling.

TLR2 heterodimerization provides a different example of receptor cooperation. Association with TLR1 or TLR6 influences recognition of microbial lipoprotein structures with different acylation characteristics. The presence of TLR2 alone does not justify predicting equal responses to every lipopeptide. A cell's receptor repertoire includes the relevant partner proteins and their expression levels. A selective reduction in one response can reflect a defect in a partner receptor rather than loss of all TLR2-dependent recognition. This is why genotype-to-phenotype reasoning should identify the relevant complex before inferring the affected function.

TLR5-associated recognition of flagellin illustrates the distinction between recognizing an isolated microbial structure and recognizing an intact organism. Flagellin is a protein constituent of bacterial flagella, but its accessibility can differ when assembled into a filament, released or presented in a different biological compartment. Some bacteria do not have accessible or stimulatory flagellin. A receptor assay using a defined ligand and an assay using an intact organism are therefore not equivalent. A bacterium may stimulate several recognition pathways simultaneously even when one of its individual components is a well-characterized TLR ligand.

Endosomal receptors provide further opportunities for control through intracellular trafficking. Nucleic acid-containing material can be internalized, transported into endosomal compartments and exposed to enzymes and chemical conditions that influence its availability. Some endosomal TLRs undergo processing that contributes to their functional activation. A cell can consequently have normal expression of a receptor gene while failing to produce the correct pool of functional receptor in the appropriate compartment. Total RNA abundance, total protein abundance and surface or endosomal protein localization answer related but distinct experimental questions.

UNC93B1 is a representative trafficking-associated protein important for the proper localization and function of several nucleic acid-sensing TLRs. A defect affecting this shared machinery can impair multiple endosomal receptor pathways without requiring a mutation in each individual receptor gene. This provides a useful examination pattern: defects in a common upstream requirement produce correlated losses, while a receptor-specific mutation tends to affect a narrower set of inputs. The exact phenotype still depends on which receptors and cell types are examined. A shared trafficking component is not identical to a signaling adaptor because its primary role concerns access and localization.

Subcellular localization also changes the meaning of an inhibitor experiment. If an intervention impairs endosomal acidification, a reduction in response to an internalized nucleic acid could reflect changes in ligand processing, receptor processing or other endosomal functions. It would be too strong to conclude that the intervention directly binds and blocks a specific TLR. Independent evidence from receptor-specific mutations or rescue experiments is needed for that claim. The correct interpretation relates the perturbation to a required cellular compartment while acknowledging alternative mechanisms.

14. From recognition to signaling: MyD88 and TRIF

A receptor does not generate an inflammatory transcriptional program by binding DNA directly in the nucleus. Its cytosolic TIR domain recruits adaptor proteins that organize signaling reactions. An adaptor contributes binding surfaces and spatial arrangement, allowing appropriate downstream enzymes and regulators to assemble. This is one way receptor engagement becomes coupled to a change in gene expression.

MyD88 is a central adaptor for most mammalian TLRs. In a common signaling sequence, recruitment of MyD88 promotes assembly of IRAK-family proteins, which contribute to activation of pathways leading to transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP-1. The consequence can include production of inflammatory cytokines and other proteins supporting defense. The response depends on the cell type and available regulatory factors; the same receptor need not produce an identical set of transcripts in every tissue.

TLR3 is the major exception to the general MyD88 rule: it signals through TRIF. TLR4 can engage both MyD88-associated and TRIF-associated signaling. At the plasma membrane, TLR4 commonly uses TIRAP to help recruit MyD88; after receptor internalization, TRAM helps connect TLR4 to TRIF. TRIF can promote activation of IRF3 through downstream kinase activity and support type I interferon expression, alongside contributions to inflammatory gene expression. The pathway is not simply an exclusive choice between inflammation and interferon, because the outputs can overlap.

TLR7 and TLR9 use MyD88, yet in certain cells, especially plasmacytoid dendritic cells, their signals can produce substantial type I interferon responses through IRF7. It would therefore be incorrect to infer that every interferon response proves TRIF involvement. Similarly, NF-κB activation is not unique to MyD88-mediated TLR pathways. A transcriptional readout is evidence of a downstream output, not automatically a unique molecular identifier of the pathway that produced it.

Consider a cell with a loss of MyD88 function. The response to a typical TLR2 agonist is expected to be strongly impaired. A TLR3-associated TRIF response may remain possible if the receptor and other components are intact. An LPS response can be altered rather than abolished in every measured dimension because TLR4 has more than one adaptor route. To make a precise prediction, the experiment must specify which output is measured, at what time, and in which cell type.

Feedback regulation limits inappropriate or prolonged signaling. Cells can alter receptor availability, induce inhibitory signaling proteins and change transcriptional responsiveness following stimulation. Such regulation matters at mucosal barriers, where exposure to microbial molecules can be frequent. An inhibitory mechanism that preserves epithelial homeostasis is not necessarily evidence of failed immunity; it can prevent a response whose cost would exceed its benefit under particular conditions.

15. The logic of signal amplification, duration and negative regulation

A cell needs to convert an extracellular recognition event into changes that are large enough to affect tissue defense. Binding of a microbial ligand to a receptor is a molecular event, whereas secretion of cytokines requires the coordinated participation of many molecules and can affect neighboring cells. The connection is a chain of biochemical interactions rather than a direct one-to-one correspondence between ligand molecules and secreted cytokine molecules. Several stages can amplify an initial signal, and each stage may also offer a point at which regulation limits the output. This general organization explains why measurements of receptor binding, transcription factor activity and cytokine secretion can yield different apparent sensitivities to the same ligand.

The TIR domain of an activated TLR provides a platform for recruitment of compatible adaptor domains. In the MyD88 pathway, protein association supports assembly of a signaling complex involving IRAK-family proteins. Downstream interactions involving TRAF6 and other regulators can contribute to activation of kinase pathways. A familiar outcome is activation of the IKK complex and removal of inhibitory constraints on NF-κB activity. NF-κB can then influence transcription of suitable target genes. This sequence provides a connection to the earlier study of phosphorylation, protein-protein interactions and signal-regulated transcription. It is not necessary to memorize every molecular component of a specialized signaling complex to understand the direction of causality.

Regulated ubiquitin-dependent interactions in such pathways should not be confused with the idea that every ubiquitinated protein is immediately destroyed by the proteasome. Certain ubiquitin modifications support the assembly of signaling complexes, while others can contribute to protein turnover. The consequences depend on the substrate, the modification and the cellular machinery that recognizes it. This distinction is relevant because an inhibitor affecting protein modification may change signaling before any detectable reduction in total receptor abundance. Conversely, loss of a protein that normally removes or interprets a modification may alter the duration of the signal rather than its initial onset.

The pathway leading to NF-κB activity provides an accessible example of a regulatory gate. In many resting cells, inhibitory proteins constrain the nuclear activity of NF-κB. Activation of the appropriate upstream signaling machinery can promote removal of this constraint. The resulting movement or activity of transcription factors changes transcription only in a suitable gene-regulatory context. A cell with active nuclear NF-κB need not express every potential NF-κB-responsive gene, because chromatin state, additional transcription factors and other regulators can differ between cells. It follows that a transcription factor localization assay can establish activation of one stage without proving that a particular cytokine will be secreted.

TRIF-associated signaling also demonstrates how one receptor family can generate distinct response classes. Through appropriate downstream molecules, TRIF contributes to the activation of IRF3, which can promote transcription of type I interferon genes. Interferons secreted by an activated cell can act on other cells through interferon receptors, thereby initiating a second signaling process that differs from the original TLR signal. A neighboring cell may therefore acquire an antiviral state without having encountered the same initial microbial ligand. This is a concrete example of the difference between direct recognition and a secondary cytokine-mediated response.

The secondary signal must be distinguished from the upstream receptor experimentally. If conditioned medium from ligand-stimulated cells induces an antiviral response in other cells, several soluble components could be responsible. Blocking an interferon receptor on the recipient cells while preserving stimulation of the donor cells helps test whether an interferon-dependent relay is required. If the recipient response disappears under the block, the data support that relay in the assay. They do not prove that every antiviral effect of the donor cells requires the same molecule. The direct effect of any ligand carried over in the medium should also be considered.

Negative feedback can make a response transient even when the initiating ligand remains available. Transcription of inhibitory regulators, changes in receptor trafficking and changes in stability of signaling components can reduce the ability of a cell to respond to continued stimulation. Newly synthesized IκB proteins provide a familiar example of feedback constraining NF-κB activity, and regulators such as A20 can influence signaling through ubiquitin-associated processes. These are representative ways to limit an immune signal, not a complete catalog. Their common biological significance is that a successful response requires termination as well as activation.

At a barrier surface, prolonged production of inflammatory mediators can compromise the tissue that initially prevented invasion. Feedback therefore protects the integrity of the defense system itself. In a short assay, a cell lacking an inhibitory regulator may appear more responsive than a normal cell. In a longer experiment or an intact organism, the same defect may be associated with persistent signaling and greater tissue injury. A stronger output should not automatically be interpreted as better defense. A useful evaluation must distinguish signaling magnitude, pathogen restriction and preservation of tissue function.

Desensitization is an operational description of reduced responsiveness following a prior exposure. Repeated exposure to LPS can produce a state often called endotoxin tolerance, in which selected subsequent responses are attenuated. It is not identical to deletion of TLR4. A previously exposed cell can retain a receptor and some functions while exhibiting altered transcriptional or signaling responsiveness. Nor is attenuation necessarily equivalent across every gene. A reduced second response to one stimulus may coexist with other retained or altered responses. Interpreting the effect requires knowledge of the first exposure, interval, cell type and measured endpoint.

The converse phenomenon of heightened responsiveness following some prior innate stimuli also illustrates the importance of cellular state. Long-lasting changes in metabolic activity and chromatin regulation can influence subsequent responses of innate cells or their progenitors. Trained immunity is not defined by the same molecular process as adaptive clonal memory. In an examination comparison, the critical distinction is between an altered state of cells expressing inherited receptor systems and persistent populations with somatically generated antigen receptor specificity. The existence of one type of memory does not remove the biological significance of the other.

16. Quantitative models: interpreting barriers and receptor responses without overclaiming

The rate of passage across a simple barrier can be represented by a proportionality relationship when the concentration gradient remains approximately constant. Define J as the amount transported per unit area per unit time, P as an effective permeability coefficient and C1 minus C2 as the concentration difference between the two compartments. In a simplified passive transport description, J equals P times the concentration difference. If concentration is measured in moles per cubic metre and P in metres per second, the product has units of moles per square metre per second. This is not a universal model for living epithelia, because active transport, binding, geometry and time-dependent barrier properties can alter the relationship.

Consider a teaching comparison in which two monolayers have the same area and experience the same tracer concentration difference. The effective permeability of one is three times the other. Under the simple assumptions of the model, its tracer flux is three times greater. If the concentration difference decreases during the measurement, the flux will not remain constant even though the permeability coefficient is unchanged. The model separates a property of the barrier from the driving difference in concentration. A claim that a receptor mutant changed epithelial permeability would require a transport measurement rather than an inference from cytokine concentration alone.

A first-order clearance model can help interpret a dynamic surface barrier. Let N be the number of retained particles, I the constant rate of incoming particles and k the fractional clearance rate per unit time. If removal is proportional to the retained number and no growth occurs, the rate of change is I minus kN. At steady state the rate of change is zero, so N equals I divided by k. The units of I are particles per unit time and k has reciprocal-time units, giving particles as the result. This model is an illustrative approximation to physical clearance rather than an empirical description of all airway mucus dynamics.

Suppose I is 120 particles per minute and k is 0.4 per minute. The steady retained number is 300 particles. If k declines to 0.1 per minute while I remains unchanged, the predicted number rises to 1200. The fourfold rise follows from the inverse relationship with the clearance constant, not from a fourfold increase in environmental exposure. When particles can reproduce, the simple model fails because growth provides an additional source term. When a clearance mechanism saturates, the assumption of proportional removal can also become inappropriate. Students should therefore identify assumptions before generalizing the arithmetic to a living tissue.

Ligand binding can be represented by an equilibrium occupancy model in an idealized one-site system. Write R plus L reversibly forming RL, where R is unoccupied receptor, L is free ligand and RL is occupied receptor. The dissociation constant is the product of free receptor and free ligand concentrations divided by the concentration of complex. If total receptor concentration is the sum of free and occupied receptor, algebra gives the occupied fraction as free ligand concentration divided by the sum of dissociation constant and free ligand concentration. Both concentrations must use compatible units.

For a hypothetical receptor with a dissociation constant of 10 nanomolar, a free ligand concentration of 10 nanomolar yields one-half occupancy. At 30 nanomolar the predicted fraction is 30 divided by 40, or 0.75. At 90 nanomolar it is 90 divided by 100, or 0.90. The diminishing increments reflect saturation. This equation is not an accurate complete binding model for every TLR complex, since accessory-protein transfer, receptor dimerization, ligand aggregation, trafficking and nonequilibrium signaling can violate its assumptions. Its pedagogical purpose is to explain why response cannot rise proportionally with ligand without limit.

A signaling measurement can saturate before all receptors are occupied because downstream reactions amplify input or because another component becomes limiting. Conversely, a high proportion of occupied receptors might produce little output if the receptors are defective in adaptor recruitment. An apparent half-maximal response concentration measured from cytokine secretion is commonly described by a functional EC50, whereas a dissociation constant refers to binding equilibrium under specified assumptions. They need not be numerically identical. Comparing these quantities without checking their definitions is a recurring error in receptor-based questions.

An experimental interaction between two stimuli also requires a stated reference. Imagine that an untreated sample produces 5 arbitrary response units, stimulus A produces 25, stimulus B produces 35 and the combined condition produces 65. After subtracting the background, the individual induced outputs are 20 and 30 and the combined induced output is 60. The combined output exceeds the arithmetic sum of individual induced outputs by 10 units. This supports a more-than-additive outcome relative to that particular baseline-adjusted arithmetic model, but it does not establish the molecular mechanism or prove significance without replication and variability estimates. Different response scales can require different interaction models.

These equations help interpret data but do not substitute for biological explanation. A quantitative result becomes meaningful when it can be connected to a specific stage: passage through a barrier, availability of ligand, receptor occupancy, signal propagation or output. When several stages are collapsed into one measurement, more than one causal explanation is possible. The most defensible answer is often the one that identifies the limitation of the available measurement rather than attributing every change to the first receptor named in the question.

17. Evolutionary conservation and the limits of cross-species generalization

All living organisms need ways to detect or survive environmental disturbances, but the term immune system has different scope in different biological contexts. Plants and many invertebrates have effective defense systems without the conventional rearranged immunoglobulin and T-cell receptor repertoires of jawed vertebrates. The study of these systems demonstrates that recognition of characteristic molecular features and regulation of defensive gene expression can provide substantial protection independently of classical adaptive lymphocytes. It also prevents the mistaken inference that innate immunity is merely a temporary, incomplete version of vertebrate adaptive immunity.

The Drosophila Toll example is valuable precisely because the similarity between species is not complete. Toll in the fly and mammalian Toll-like receptors share features of their signaling architecture. However, in the fly antifungal response, extracellular recognition and proteolytic events can lead to activation of the host ligand Spätzle, which activates Toll. Mammalian TLRs commonly participate more directly in recognition complexes for particular microbial molecules. This comparison reveals how a conserved signaling principle can be connected to different upstream mechanisms. Students should learn what is homologous and what has diverged rather than merely equating proteins with similar names.

A related lesson comes from plant recognition systems. Plant cells can recognize characteristic microbial molecules through surface receptor systems and initiate defensive responses. The flagellin-derived peptide flg22 and the Arabidopsis receptor FLS2 provide a familiar conceptual example. FLS2 is a plant receptor kinase rather than a mammalian TLR5 molecule. Both systems can be associated with sensing material originating from bacterial flagellin, but that similarity of stimulus does not establish identical protein architecture or downstream signaling mechanisms. The detailed plant signaling and pathogen interactions belong elsewhere in the Academy syllabus; here the comparison clarifies the evolutionary usefulness of molecular pattern detection.

Jawed vertebrates evolved classical B- and T-cell antigen receptor systems in addition to inherited pattern-recognition systems. A diverse repertoire offers the advantage of recognizing an enormous variety of possible molecular determinants without encoding a separate complete receptor gene for each one. However, creating such diversity also creates the potential for self-reactivity and necessitates developmental selection and regulation. Clonal expansion costs time, and the location of rare responsive lymphocytes becomes important. Inherited broad recognition and clonally selective recognition thus solve related but different biological problems.

The contrast between these strategies also explains why the evolution of adaptive immunity did not make innate recognition unnecessary. A broad sensing mechanism can detect a microbial class rapidly, support initial restriction and supply signals indicating that selected lymphocytes should respond. Adaptive effectors can then provide more selective recognition and recruit innate execution mechanisms. An antibody directed against a particular surface structure can promote engagement by a phagocyte whose receptor repertoire does not itself identify that antigen with the same fine specificity. The two layers are integrated at the level of biological output.

Species differences also occur within mammals. Results obtained in laboratory mice are not automatically identical to responses in humans. Receptor repertoires can differ, and comparable cell types may vary in their expression of particular recognition molecules. In addition to genetics, developmental stage and tissue environment can alter responsiveness. A report that a ligand stimulates a mouse cell population does not justify asserting that the same ligand has an identical effect on a human population with the same broad morphological label. Cross-species comparisons require separate evidence.

A pathogen population can also evolve under immune selection. Altering, concealing or limiting exposure of a recognized molecular feature can affect the ability of a host receptor to detect it. However, microbial components often perform essential physiological functions, and changing them may carry a cost. This creates a tradeoff between maintenance of microbial function and reduced visibility to host recognition. The same reasoning helps explain why broadly conserved structural features can be useful targets of inherited sensing. It does not imply that such structures are literally immutable or that all microbes bearing one feature stimulate a receptor equally.

Another example of adaptation concerns physical barriers. Organisms with different modes of life possess surfaces suited to their physiological needs and environmental exposures. A terrestrial animal requires defenses compatible with preventing water loss, whereas a respiratory surface must support gas exchange. Plant walls and surface cuticles provide protective functions, but their architecture cannot be equated with an animal epithelium. The unifying principle is control of access and recognition of abnormal conditions, not the existence of an identical barrier in every lineage.

Evolutionary comparison is most valuable when it guides a testable distinction. If two receptors respond to material from a similar microbial source, a comparison of their domain architecture and signaling partners can reveal whether the relationship reflects conserved ancestry, convergent biological function or a mixture of both. If a pathway component is required in multiple species, that does not guarantee that every ligand or downstream output is shared. This disciplined approach supports the syllabus goal of understanding conserved principles without treating examples from one model organism as universal facts.

18. Mechanistic failures: locating the defective stage

Immune failure can arise before a recognition receptor has ever encountered its ligand. A disruption of epithelial continuity increases access of material to internal compartments. A defect in a secreted antimicrobial factor can permit greater survival at a surface. Impaired lymphatic transport can reduce delivery of antigen to a lymph node. A receptor or adaptor mutation can alter the interpretation of an encountered stimulus. These defects may all influence susceptibility to infection, but they are mechanistically distinct. In an examination problem, the first task is to identify which stage is actually supported by the observations.

A barrier defect and a recognition defect can even produce opposite initial signaling patterns. If more microbial material reaches sentinel cells with normal receptors, increased inflammatory signaling may be observed. If recognition is defective, signaling in response to a defined ligand may instead be reduced despite increased microbial burden. These expectations are conditional: severe injury, cell loss or secondary infection may change the later response. Thus, a tissue with elevated cytokine concentrations cannot be assumed to possess a stronger functional barrier, and a tissue with reduced cytokine concentrations cannot automatically be considered less exposed to microorganisms.

Suppose two groups of experimental epithelial cultures differ in their tracer permeability. Group A has a high permeability but responds normally to a defined microbial ligand delivered directly to a responsive surface. Group B has normal tracer permeability but fails to respond to the same directly delivered ligand. The first pattern is consistent with a structural or transport abnormality, whereas the second points toward recognition or downstream response machinery. The experiment has deliberately bypassed some differences in ligand access. Additional viability and independent stimulus controls would be needed before assigning the defect to a particular receptor gene.

A receptor loss-of-function mutation may remove a single route of detection while leaving several other routes intact. For example, loss of an appropriate TLR4 response does not logically eliminate recognition of bacterial lipoproteins by TLR2-containing complexes. If whole bacteria are used instead of a purified stimulus, residual activation could arise from recognition of several other components. This is why a clean receptor-specific phenotype is often clearer with a well-characterized ligand than with a complex preparation. The complexity of an intact microorganism is biologically realistic, but it complicates assignment of a response to one receptor.

An adaptor defect can produce a broader phenotype than a mutation affecting a single receptor. In a simplified mammalian system lacking MyD88, responses through several TLRs can be impaired, while a suitable TRIF-dependent TLR3 pathway may remain functional. However, a broad defect in an adaptor does not imply that every innate receptor depends on it. Cytosolic sensors and other pathways can use different signal-transduction machinery. The correct prediction is determined by mapping each test stimulus through its expected route and then identifying common components.

A defect in a trafficking-associated factor creates a third pattern. Several receptors can be produced normally but fail to reach the compartment in which their ligands are normally encountered. Such a phenotype may resemble a defect in a shared adaptor if only final transcription is measured. Protein localization and responses to stimuli that bypass the affected receptor compartment provide additional discrimination. A protein that remains trapped in an incorrect compartment may be detected by an assay of total protein content, so a normal total amount cannot establish functional availability at the correct location.

Gain-of-function changes are equally informative. A receptor or downstream component that becomes active without its usual input can produce signaling under conditions that would normally be nonstimulating. The original human Toll gain-of-function experiment illustrates how this principle can test signaling capacity. In a hypothetical disease context, inappropriate constitutive activity can contribute to unnecessary inflammatory signaling. It would not demonstrate that the receptor has gained the ability to bind a particular new microbial ligand. Ligand-independent activation and altered ligand specificity are different molecular changes.

A further distinction concerns responsiveness of the immune cell itself and responsiveness of the cells that receive its products. A macrophage can recognize an appropriate stimulus and secrete a cytokine normally, yet an altered cytokine receptor in a neighboring cell may prevent that neighbor from responding. A tissue-level defect in protection can therefore occur with intact initial innate recognition. Conversely, normal responsiveness to an externally supplied cytokine does not establish that the macrophage is capable of producing that cytokine following receptor stimulation. Addition of a downstream signal is useful because it bypasses upstream steps.

Immune organs introduce analogous distinctions at a larger scale. A developmental failure of T cells affects the available repertoire before antigen-driven activation. A defect in entry of mature lymphocytes into a lymph node affects the chance that the relevant clone will encounter antigen. A defect in antigen-bearing cell migration affects delivery of material or activation context. All can reduce the magnitude of a response measured in that node. The location of the defect can be inferred only when the upstream populations and transport processes have been examined independently.

The consequence of a defect depends on the organism and exposure. A cell population that is essential for early defense against one class of microbial challenge may contribute less prominently to a different challenge. Multiple pathways can compensate for some defects under some conditions. This contextual dependence explains why the terms essential and redundant require a specified endpoint. A receptor can be essential for a defined ligand-induced response in a controlled assay without being absolutely required for every protective response in an intact animal. Conversely, an apparently modest defect in a laboratory assay may matter strongly in a tissue that depends on that receptor.

19. Experimental and quantitative reasoning

Suppose a researcher wants to determine whether a failure to respond to a microbial preparation is caused by loss of TLR4 or by a general defect in the cell's capacity to transcribe inflammatory genes. Comparing receptor-deficient and wild-type cells exposed only to the preparation cannot resolve this question completely. A stimulus that activates an independent receptor pathway provides a useful control. If the deficient cells respond to the independent stimulus, at least part of the common downstream machinery remains functional. A matched untreated group establishes the baseline, while comparable viability and cell numbers help exclude the possibility that the response difference simply reflects fewer living cells.

A second approach is functional rescue. Reintroducing a functional version of the candidate receptor into otherwise deficient cells can strengthen a causal inference when restoration of the receptor also restores stimulus-dependent output. An empty-vector control is important because the process of introducing genetic material may itself activate sensing mechanisms. Rescue must also be interpreted with care if receptor expression greatly exceeds the physiological level. Excess receptor could change the sensitivity of the system without reproducing the original state.

A useful experiment distinguishes receptor binding from receptor signaling. A ligand-binding assay might show similar association of a microbial component with wild-type and signaling-defective cells, while a transcriptional assay reveals a marked difference. Such a pattern is compatible with a defect in signal transduction rather than loss of initial recognition. It does not by itself identify the defective adaptor. Additional perturbation or direct measurement of adaptor recruitment would be necessary to identify the affected molecular step.

Quantitative response data often need background subtraction. In a hypothetical cytokine assay, untreated wild-type cells release 10 pg/mL and stimulated cells release 110 pg/mL. Untreated mutant cells release 8 pg/mL and stimulated cells release 28 pg/mL. The induced increments are therefore 100 and 20 pg/mL. The mutant has 20 percent of the wild-type induced output, corresponding to an 80 percent reduction. Comparing only the stimulated concentrations gives 28 divided by 110, approximately 25 percent, which answers a different question because it includes basal production.

A reduction in induced cytokine concentration does not establish a corresponding reduction in receptor occupancy. The final output depends on ligand access, receptor association, signaling amplification, transcription, translation, secretion and turnover. A concentration-response curve can help distinguish a change in maximal output from a shift in the concentration required for a given response, but a mechanistic conclusion requires additional evidence. An apparent half-maximal concentration measured for a cytokine response is not automatically the equilibrium dissociation constant of the receptor-ligand complex.

The timing of sampling matters because two adaptor branches can contribute differently to early and later outputs. A mutation that leaves a late response intact may still eliminate an important early signal. Measurements at several time points distinguish delay from complete loss or reduced maximum. When a question gives only one time point, the strongest justified inference concerns the observed output at that time; it should not be extended to the entire signaling history.

20. Experimental evidence: necessity, sufficiency and the interpretation of controls

A central task of experimental immunology is to establish that a particular molecular component contributes causally to a response. The word contributes is deliberately chosen. Detecting receptor expression in a responsive cell population establishes an association but does not demonstrate that the receptor is required. Receptor expression might accompany differentiation of the cells while another receptor actually recognizes the tested stimulus. Conversely, a component present at low abundance may be functionally important even when an assay cannot easily detect it. Correlation between expression and response is an appropriate starting point, not an endpoint.

Loss-of-function evidence tests necessity under specified conditions. If deletion of a receptor prevents an otherwise reproducible response to a defined stimulus, the result supports the conclusion that the receptor is required in that system. However, a gene deletion can also alter cell development, survival or the expression of other genes. A matched control population and functional rescue improve causal interpretation. A single failure observed in a receptor-deficient animal does not necessarily prove that the deleted receptor directly binds the test molecule. It might be required for trafficking, regulation or another process involved in the response.

Sufficiency addresses a different question. If introduction of a receptor into an otherwise nonresponsive system produces stimulus-dependent responsiveness, the result supports a role for the receptor in conferring the response within that cellular context. The recipient cell may already provide necessary accessory proteins and downstream machinery. It would be unjustified to infer that the receptor acts completely alone. If introduction fails to produce responsiveness, the result does not automatically refute receptor function, because the recipient system may lack a required partner or may localize the introduced protein incorrectly.

Direct physical recognition is yet another claim. Evidence of association between a purified ligand and receptor or receptor complex helps support it. A cellular response following exposure to a ligand does not alone establish direct binding because intermediate molecules may mediate recognition. The fly Toll pathway provides a clear example of why the distinction matters: the microbial challenge initiates upstream events that activate a host ligand of Toll. If a genetic requirement for Toll were interpreted as proof of direct binding of fungal material by Toll, the conclusion would exceed the evidence.

A receptor-pathway diagram predicts relationships among perturbations. Consider two components arranged in sequence, with receptor R activating adaptor A and adaptor A contributing to output O. A constitutively active downstream component might restore some output in cells lacking receptor R if the intervening machinery is otherwise functional. Activating receptor R would not restore a strictly adaptor-dependent output if adaptor A were absent. Such reasoning is a simple form of pathway ordering. Biological networks may contain branching and feedback, so an observed partial rescue can reflect the existence of additional pathways rather than an incorrect placement of every component.

A common assay for transcriptional activity uses a reporter in which a regulatory DNA sequence controls expression of a measurable product. Reporter activity may provide a useful proxy for activation of a transcriptional pathway, but it is not the same measurement as concentration of an endogenous secreted cytokine. The reporter may contain fewer regulatory elements than the natural gene, and the stability of the reporter product can influence the apparent timing. Independent measurement of endogenous transcripts or proteins can help evaluate whether the reporter represents the physiological output of interest.

Background signals and measurement limits deserve explicit attention. A value near zero may reflect a response below the detection threshold, rather than absolute absence of every molecule. A large signal can exceed the useful range of an assay and appear artificially similar across several strong conditions. Measurements should be interpreted within the range in which the assay reports the relevant quantity reliably. In a hypothetical problem, a saturated response at two ligand concentrations cannot establish that receptor occupancy is the same at those concentrations. The limitation may lie in the measurement or in a downstream step.

Replicates allow variation to be distinguished from a consistent treatment-associated effect. Biological replicates reflect independent biological units, while repeated measurements of the same preparation mainly address technical variability. Multiple wells from a single culture do not necessarily represent the same independent information as cultures from different animals or preparations. An examination dataset with only one value per group can support arithmetic and qualitative reasoning, but it does not provide enough information to establish statistical significance. The appropriate conclusion should be limited to the pattern presented.

In innate recognition studies, the identity of a stimulus is particularly important because biological preparations can contain multiple potentially active constituents. A response attributed to a protein could instead be influenced by contaminating microbial material. Suitable purity controls, independently prepared material and comparison of receptor-deficient cells can strengthen the interpretation. A receptor-specific response to a contaminant can be genuine biologically but wrongly assigned to the intended molecule. This is a reminder that the identity of the active input is itself an experimental question.

A systematic examination strategy starts with the question being tested, not the name of a famous receptor. Identify the independent variable, the measured output and the relevant control. Ask which stage the perturbation could affect and whether the assay distinguishes that stage from alternatives. If a stimulus is delivered to a different compartment, consider whether receptor accessibility changes. If a cytokine is measured after several hours, consider intermediate gene expression and secretion. If a genotype affects multiple inputs, look for a shared component. This process produces a stronger explanation than memorization of isolated associations.

21. Physiological integration and applications of innate recognition

A simple skin wound provides a useful integrative example of immune system organization. The injury interrupts a barrier and can release endogenous material from damaged cells. If microbial products reach the exposed tissue, they provide additional potential recognition inputs. Resident cells can generate signals that influence surrounding tissue and recruit circulating leukocytes. Some antigen and antigen-bearing cells can reach a draining lymph node. These stages differ in spatial scale, from a molecular interaction to the coordination of several organs. The full inflammatory and cellular effector mechanisms are developed in the next chapter; the present focus is on why the initial breach and recognition are distinguishable events.

An inhaled particle encounters a different architecture. In conducting airways, mucus and ciliary movement can restrict its persistence. In more distal regions, other epithelial and soluble defenses and resident macrophages become important. Whether an inhaled particle stimulates a PRR depends on its composition and the cells that gain access to it. An inert particle may be cleared mechanically, whereas a microbial particle may also supply recognition ligands. A measurement of particle deposition cannot, by itself, establish the intensity of immune recognition. Physical delivery and molecular stimulation are separate variables.

The intestine demonstrates why maximal continuous activation would be an unsuitable default. Microbial constituents are abundant in the lumen, yet the epithelium must support digestion and nutrient absorption. Spatial separation, mucus, chemical defenses, controlled antigen sampling and regulated receptor responses contribute to coexistence with resident communities. Perturbation of one of these systems can change the exposure of underlying tissues without requiring a new microbial species to appear. When interpreting an inflammatory intestinal phenotype, the relative contribution of barrier damage, microbial composition and altered responsiveness should be considered.

A virus provides a complementary recognition problem because viral replication can generate molecular structures within cells. Surface or endosomal recognition can detect material that enters appropriate compartments, while cytosolic sensors can respond to replication-associated nucleic acids. The resulting type I interferon signals can influence surrounding cells. In a conceptual experiment, a cell unable to detect a particular viral RNA-associated input might still respond to externally supplied interferon if its interferon receptor system remains intact. This distinguishes production of an antiviral signal from reception of that signal by target cells.

The principle that innate signals influence adaptive responses has an important application in vaccination. Some vaccine formulations use adjuvants that promote appropriate innate activation and thereby support the response to antigen. The antigen supplies determinants for recognition by adaptive lymphocytes; the adjuvant can help provide an immunostimulatory context. These roles need not be performed by separate molecular entities in every vaccine, but distinguishing them explains why antigen binding alone may not generate a strong adaptive response. Detailed vaccine classes and protective memory are assigned to a later chapter.

Innate receptor ligands can also serve as conceptual tools in research. A defined stimulus can test whether a particular recognition route is functional, while comparison with another stimulus can probe pathway specificity. The purpose of such an experiment is not simply to obtain a high cytokine concentration. It is to isolate a stage of recognition and determine how the system responds when that stage is perturbed. The most useful experimental design separates receptor abundance, stimulus accessibility, downstream signaling and the final measured output wherever possible.

Research on epithelial integrity similarly benefits from combining physical and immune readouts. A tracer assay can assess permeability; structural examination can assess junctional organization; a response assay can assess signaling under defined exposure conditions. No single measurement establishes every aspect of barrier function. If treatment reduces a cytokine output but does not restore physical integrity, describing the treatment as repair of the barrier would be unwarranted. The conclusion should instead specify that the measured response changed while the physical defect remained.

The immune system also illustrates the general principles of multicellular communication studied throughout Unit 4. A receptor converts information about a molecular input into intracellular activity. Secreted cytokines carry information to other cells. Adhesion molecules and chemokine-dependent movement help bring cells into appropriate physical relationships. Lymphatic and blood flow distribute material among tissues and organs. The final defense response emerges from cooperation across these levels, making immunology an application of cell signaling, membrane organization, gene regulation and physiology rather than an isolated collection of names.

A useful synthesis is to distinguish a sequence of five questions whenever a biological disturbance is presented. Has access to a protected compartment changed? Which molecular features are available and where? Which receptors and accessory molecules can detect them? Which downstream pathways and cell states determine the output? Which tissue or lymphoid populations receive the consequences of that output? These questions provide a mechanistic framework for approaching unfamiliar examples. Later chapters will add the effector mechanisms and antigen-specific recognition processes that complete this framework, while preserving the initial distinction between sensing a disturbance and executing a response.

Cytokine response

Subtract baseline: 110−10=100 pg/mL; 28−8=20 pg/mL. Residual output = 20/100 × 100 = 20%; reduction = 80%. The assay does not measure receptor occupancy.

Clearance

N = I/k. With I = 120 particles/min, k = 0.4/min gives 300 particles; k = 0.1/min gives 1200 particles. This assumes constant input, first-order removal and no growth.

Ideal binding

θ = L/(Kd+L). At L = 10, 30 and 90 nM with Kd = 10 nM, θ = 0.50, 0.75 and 0.90. The model is not a measured TLR binding curve.

Permeability

At fixed area and concentration difference, tripling effective P triples flux J = PΔC. With J = 4 pmol/cm²/min, area 2 cm² and time 5 min, transported amount = 40 pmol, assuming constant flux.

Combined response

Subtract a baseline of 5 from 25, 35 and 65: induced values 20, 30 and 60. Excess over simple additive expectation = 60−20−30=10 units. Replicate variability is required for statistical inference.

Neutrophils possess phagocytic and antimicrobial machinery and are rapidly recruited in many acute bacterial responses. Basophils are granulocytes but are not the principal recruited phagocytes.

  3. They are mature neutrophils.

D — They are lymphoid-lineage innate effector cells.

NK cells integrate activating and inhibitory signals through inherited receptors. Conventional TCR and immunoglobulin expression characterize T and B cells respectively.

  2. Blood
  3. Airway mucus directly
  4. Gastric lumen directly

  4. Spleen

  2. Assertion true, reason false.
  3. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and explanation

C — Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Recognition by the ectodomain or receptor complex and adaptor recruitment by the TIR domain are separable functions. A mutation affecting TIR-dependent signaling need not disrupt an intact ligand-binding surface; the result depends on the mutation.

46. Assertion: A serum cytokine measurement alone does not prove which TLR was activated. Reason: Multiple receptors and downstream pathways can converge on related outputs.

  1. Both are true but unrelated.
  2. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.
  3. Assertion true, reason false.
  4. Assertion false, reason true.
Answer and explanation

B — Both are true and the reason explains the assertion.

NF-κB and interferon-associated responses can arise through several inputs. Assigning the initiating receptor requires additional perturbation or localization evidence.

47. Match the immune site with its characteristic function: I. thymus; II. spleen; III. lymph node; IV. bone marrow.

  1. I blood surveillance; II T-cell maturation; III hematopoiesis; IV tissue lymph surveillance
  2. I hematopoiesis only; II tissue lymph; III T-cell maturation; IV blood surveillance
  3. I mucociliary clearance; II antibody rearrangement only; III gastric defense; IV filtering lymph
  4. I T-cell maturation; II blood surveillance; III tissue lymph surveillance; IV hematopoiesis
Answer and explanation

D — I T-cell maturation; II blood surveillance; III tissue lymph surveillance; IV hematopoiesis

Primary organs support development; secondary organs organize antigen encounters. Spleen and node differ by the fluid they sample.

48. Choose the correct set of statements. I. TLR3 is TRIF dependent. II. TLR4 can use two adaptor routes. III. TLR9 is characteristically endosomal. IV. All TLRs use only MyD88.

  1. I, II and III
  2. II and IV only
  3. I and IV only
  4. All four
Answer and explanation

A — I, II and III

The first three are representative mammalian signaling relationships. IV is false because TLR3 is a major TRIF-only exception and TLR4 uses both routes.

49. Refer to the occupancy graph. For free ligand at 90 nM and Kd 10 nM, what is the ideal unoccupied fraction?

  2. 0.90
  3. 0.50
  4. 0.25
Answer and explanation

A — 0.10

Occupied fraction is 90/100 = 0.90. Unoccupied fraction is 1−0.90 = 0.10.

50. In the two-route TLR4 diagram, TRIF-associated signaling is lost while surface MyD88 signaling is preserved. Which prediction is strongest?

Answer and explanation

51. A 2 cm² monolayer has a steady tracer flux of 4 pmol/cm²/min. Under unchanged conditions how much passes in 5 min?

  1. 8 pmol
  2. 40 pmol
  3. 10 pmol
  4. 20 pmol
Answer and explanation

B — 40 pmol

Multiply flux by area and time: 4×2×5=40 pmol. The calculation assumes constant flux and does not describe an active transport change.

52. Order the conceptual steps for an ordinary TLR-dependent transcriptional response.

  1. Transcriptional output → receptor gene rearrangement → ligand access
  2. Adaptor recruitment → bacterial replication → receptor maturation in thymus
  3. Ligand access → antibody class switching → TIR deletion
  4. Ligand access → receptor activation → adaptor recruitment → transcriptional output
Answer and explanation

D — Ligand access → receptor activation → adaptor recruitment → transcriptional output

Access must precede recognition in this simplified sequence, and the activated receptor recruits intracellular partners that regulate transcription. Feedback and branches can modify the sequence without changing its basic causal order.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Innate means nonselective.

PRRs bind particular molecular features; their repertoire is inherited rather than clonally rearranged. Distinguish receptor specificity from the origin of receptor diversity; a ligand-discrimination experiment tests the former.

2. Every PAMP belongs exclusively to a pathogen.

Many commensals also carry microbial patterns; context and location affect the response. The name PAMP is easily confused with a marker unique to pathogens; compare exposure to the same pattern on commensals and invasive organisms.

3. Every DAMP is sensed by a TLR.

Extracellular ATP can signal through purinergic receptors; diverse injury-sensing pathways exist. The category DAMP describes the signal's origin, not the receptor family; match extracellular ATP to purinergic signaling.

4. All lymphocytes are adaptive.

NK cells are innate lymphoid-lineage cells. Morphological lineage is not identical to immune strategy; classify NK, B and T cells separately.

5. All macrophages derive continuously from circulating monocytes.

Many resident populations have distinct developmental origins and local maintenance. Blood recruitment is one source of macrophages, not a universal developmental rule; interpret lineage-tracing evidence by tissue.

6. Basophils are simply circulating mast cells.

They are distinct populations despite overlapping mediator functions. Shared histamine release does not establish cellular identity; compare tissue residence, development and function.

7. The spleen filters afferent lymph.

It samples blood; lymph nodes receive tissue lymph. Trace a labeled blood-borne antigen and a tracer injected into a peripheral tissue to different organs.

8. T cells originate as mature cells in thymus.

Hematopoietic precursors enter thymus for development and selection. Distinguish the origin of a precursor from the site of its maturation; predict the effect of thymic dysfunction.

9. Barrier failure equals receptor failure.

Permeability and responsiveness are separate measurable stages. A tracer-permeability assay and a controlled ligand challenge measure different stages of defense.

10. Mucus production guarantees clearance.

Ciliary movement and other transport mechanisms are also necessary. Distinguish trapping from transport; a ciliary defect may impair removal despite preserved secretion.

11. All TLRs signal exclusively via MyD88.

TLR3 uses TRIF; TLR4 can use both. Use TLR3 as a TRIF-dependent comparison and TLR4 as a dual-route case when interpreting adaptor knockouts.

12. Interferon output uniquely indicates TRIF.

MyD88-dependent TLR7/9 responses and cytosolic sensors can also induce type I interferon. Separate signaling output from route identity; compare a TLR7 response with a TLR3 response.

13. TLR4 alone recognizes all LPS forms equally.

MD-2, ligand delivery, molecular structure and species context influence activation. Ligand chemistry and receptor-complex assembly are easily conflated; compare MD-2 loss with altered LPS structure.

14. Endosomal receptors never encounter self material.

Abnormal self nucleic-acid access can contribute to activation. Compartmentalization reduces inappropriate recognition but is not absolute; reason about failed nucleic-acid clearance.

15. Total protein abundance proves correct localization.

Trafficking defects can preserve amount but impair compartment-specific availability. An immunoblot measures total abundance, whereas imaging can reveal a localization defect.

16. Functional EC50 always equals binding Kd.

Downstream amplification and complex binding can separate functional sensitivity from equilibrium affinity. A response curve measures a biological output, whereas a binding experiment measures occupancy; compare their assumptions.

17. A knockout proves direct ligand binding.

Necessity in a particular assay does not distinguish direct binding from a required accessory or signaling role. Loss of an output demonstrates dependence under the tested conditions; distinguish a direct-binding assay from a signaling rescue.

18. A higher cytokine signal always means better protection.

Inappropriate or prolonged responses can damage tissue; restriction of infection and tissue integrity must be assessed separately. A protective response must be assessed against both pathogen burden and tissue injury, rather than cytokine concentration alone.

Revision and mastery

Defense starts with controlled access, then compartment-dependent recognition, signaling, and communication with other cells and lymphoid organs. Innate and adaptive systems cooperate; they differ in receptor organization and characteristic response dynamics.

Essential relationships: J = PΔC; N = I/k for the simplified steady-state model; θ = L/(Kd+L) for ideal independent binding; induced response = stimulated value − baseline.
