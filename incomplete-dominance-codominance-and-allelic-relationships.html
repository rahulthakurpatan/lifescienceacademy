1. Inheritance and expression are different processes

A plant breeder who crosses two true-breeding plants does not directly cross their flower colours. The breeder brings together reproductive cells, each carrying one version of the genes at the loci under study. Colour appears later, after the fertilized cell divides and its descendants activate particular biochemical pathways. This distinction between what is transmitted and what is expressed is fundamental to interpreting genetics. It explains why parents with very different appearances can produce an intermediate offspring without physically mixing their hereditary information.

In the simplest model, a diploid individual has two homologous chromosomes bearing corresponding copies of a locus. Call two variants of the locus R and W. An RR plant has two copies of R; an RW plant has one of each; a WW plant has two copies of W. During normal meiosis, the homologues separate, and each haploid gamete receives one allele at this locus. The probability of an R gamete from an RW parent is one half, provided there is no transmission distortion. The visible appearance of the parent does not change that prediction. The probability is a property of chromosome transmission, not the strength of pigmentation.

Dominance is a relationship between alleles for a specified phenotype in a heterozygote. Complete dominance means that the heterozygote and one of the homozygotes fall into the same phenotypic category under the stated observations. Incomplete dominance means that the heterozygote occupies an intermediate position for the trait under consideration. Codominance means that the two distinguishable allele-associated features can be detected together. None of these definitions implies that the alleles are unequal in their probability of transmission. A dominant allele is not automatically more ancient, more common, more adaptive or more actively transcribed.

It is also important to name the level at which a phenotype is defined. The presence of a particular RNA sequence, the abundance of an enzyme, a metabolite concentration and the appearance of a whole organism are different observable variables. A heterozygote may resemble one homozygote for a gross morphological characteristic while showing an intermediate concentration of its gene product. Both descriptions can be correct, provided the observations and definitions are made explicit. Genetic labels should follow the data rather than replace an explanation of the data.

Consider a hypothetical enzyme involved in making a coloured molecule. If one active allele produces enough enzyme to meet the demand for that molecule, two active alleles need not make the tissue visibly darker. A heterozygote with one inactive allele could therefore resemble the active homozygote. If the same pathway operates in a range where product concentration is sensitive to enzyme quantity, a heterozygote might instead have less pigment. The genetic segregation is identical in the two models; only the relationship between biochemical activity and visible colour changes. This is why it is misleading to treat complete dominance as a universal physical property of alleles.

For a single autosomal locus, an RW by RW cross generates the four ordered gamete combinations RR, RW, WR and WW, each with probability one quarter. RW and WR describe the same ordinary unordered genotype, so their probabilities add. The resulting genotype ratio is 1:2:1. Complete dominance collapses two genotype classes into one visible group, producing a 3:1 phenotype ratio if R is dominant. Incomplete dominance and codominance can leave all three classes distinguishable, producing a 1:2:1 phenotype ratio. The ratio alone cannot tell which kind of distinguishable phenotype is being measured.

These models assume comparable survival from fertilization to scoring, an ordinary diploid locus, unbiased meiosis and reliable observation. If one genotype dies before observation, the distribution among living offspring can differ from the distribution at fertilization. If environmental conditions prevent a pigment from developing, phenotypic classification can fail to recover the underlying genetic ratio. Before attributing unexpected proportions to a new inheritance pattern, ask whether the observations were made at the same developmental stage and under comparable conditions.

The distinction between a locus and a phenotype

In a genetic experiment, a locus is specified by its position or identity in the genome, whereas a phenotype is specified by an observation procedure. The same stretch of chromosome can be studied by sequencing its DNA, recording the mobility of the protein it produces, or observing a visible characteristic. These measurements are not interchangeable. Suppose two plants carry alternative DNA sequences that each produce an enzyme with a slightly different amino acid sequence. The enzymes can be separable by a suitable biochemical method even if both catalyse the same reaction at the same rate. In this situation the biochemical assay distinguishes two allelic products, but a simple flower colour classification may not distinguish the plants. Neither experiment is wrong. Each addresses a different question.

The term wild type is a description used relative to a reference strain or convention; it does not supply a prediction about dominance. A new mutation may reduce function, abolish function, increase an existing activity or change the nature of the activity. Even a mutation that abolishes the function of a single gene copy does not necessarily abolish the function of the cell. Its effect depends on the contribution of the second copy and the organisation of the processes that use the gene product. Conversely, a mutant product may interfere with a complex containing ordinary subunits. In that case one functioning copy may be unable to restore normal function. This explains why a genetic cross alone cannot establish whether a particular allele is a loss-of-function or gain-of-function allele.

Genotype notation should preserve this conceptual independence. In a system where both alleles have conspicuous effects, writing one with a capital letter and one with the corresponding lower-case letter can inadvertently suggest an established hierarchy. Superscripts such as Cᴿ and Cᵂ are convenient because they identify variants of the same locus without making a claim about which visible phenotype is dominant. In ordinary ABO exercises, Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i are retained by convention. Their notation is not evidence of mechanism; the observed heterozygote phenotypes establish the relationship among the common variants. When several loci are introduced later, using a separate symbol for each locus is essential for distinguishing allelic interactions from interactions between genes.

A related distinction concerns the parental origin of alleles. In the standard autosomal model, CᴿCᵂ and CᵂCᴿ have the same phenotype because the order in which the alleles are written does not specify an independent biological effect. Researchers can test this expectation by performing reciprocal crosses, interchanging which parent supplies the pollen and which supplies the ovule. Equal reciprocal outcomes support an ordinary model without a strong parent-of-origin effect under the tested conditions. Different outcomes would justify examining maternal effects, cytoplasmic transmission, imprinting or a difference in the experimental environments. None of these explanations should be chosen solely because the reciprocals differ; each predicts additional observations that can be tested.

The distinction between genotype and phenotype becomes especially useful when the term carrier is encountered. A recessive mutant allele may be present in an individual whose outward phenotype resembles that of the reference homozygote. The individual is not genetically identical to that homozygote. An allele-specific assay may distinguish them, and they can produce different distributions of offspring. In an incomplete-dominance system the heterozygous phenotype can be visible rather than concealed, but the genotype is still inferred subject to the accuracy of the phenotype map. The strength of a genetic conclusion depends on the correspondence between the proposed molecular state and the measurement, not on a conventional label such as carrier.

2. Incomplete dominance: an intermediate phenotype

Snapdragon flower colour provides a classical example in which an intermediate phenotype makes heterozygotes recognizable. In the standard teaching cross, a homozygous red line is crossed with a homozygous white line, and the offspring are pink. Let Cᴿ and Cᵂ represent the alternative alleles in this model. The parents each produce only one allelic class of gametes. Every fertilized offspring therefore receives the Cᴿ allele from one parent and the Cᵂ allele from the other. The uniformity of the pink first generation follows from its genetic uniformity, not from a permanent dilution of red hereditary material.

The second generation is the decisive comparison with a blending model. If the first generation is self-fertilized, its gametes still contain either Cᴿ or Cᵂ. A quarter of the offspring are expected to receive two Cᴿ copies, half to receive one of each, and a quarter to receive two Cᵂ copies. Red and white reappear even though neither parent in this second cross is red or white. A model in which hereditary colour permanently blended into an intermediate substance would have difficulty explaining that recovery of the original extremes. Particulate inheritance combined with an intermediate phenotype predicts it directly.

Pink does not have to be exactly midway between red and white on every possible quantitative scale. For instance, pigment mass per gram of tissue, optical absorbance, reflected light and a human observer's judgement of hue do not necessarily have linear relationships. The defining feature is the intermediate expression of the chosen character. A statement that the heterozygote has precisely half the pigment of a red homozygote requires experimental measurement; it cannot be deduced from the term incomplete dominance alone.

A useful hypothetical pathway starts with a colourless precursor and ends in a coloured product. A fully functional allele contributes active enzyme, whereas a second allele produces little or no activity. If the supply of the substrate and the lifetime of the pigment are comparable among plants, the total functional enzyme can influence the rate of product formation. A heterozygote may produce enough pigment for a lighter colour while the functional homozygote accumulates a darker shade. Real flower pigmentation can involve multiple enzymes, transcriptional regulation, transport, vacuolar conditions and tissue development. The one-enzyme picture explains a possible mechanism rather than identifying the precise molecular lesion of every red-white snapdragon cross.

A controlled backcross distinguishes the possible offspring of the heterozygote. Crossing pink with red gives half red and half pink in the simple model, because the red parent supplies Cᴿ to every offspring. Crossing pink with white gives half pink and half white. Neither backcross can produce the opposite homozygote without the corresponding allele being present in both gametes. If an unusual offspring appears, the first scientific response is to investigate parent identity, pollen contamination, scoring and the genetic model rather than declare that alleles have transformed into each other.

An intermediate phenotype can also be produced by two different homozygous lines at separate loci, by environmental variation or by a quantitative trait controlled by many genes. Thus the appearance of a pink first generation alone is not sufficient evidence for one-locus incomplete dominance. Segregation in later generations and the results of planned crosses supply stronger evidence. If molecular genotypes can be measured, their association with colour classes can be tested directly. A reproducible correspondence between the three genotype classes and three colour classes provides evidence for the proposed genotype-to-phenotype mapping, although it does not by itself reveal which biochemical reaction causes the colour.

To interpret a hypothetical sample of 160 second-generation plants, begin with the expected counts 40 red, 80 pink and 40 white. Suppose the observations are 38, 86 and 36. The discrepancies are minus two, plus six and minus four. Their contributions to chi-square are 4/40, 36/80 and 16/40 respectively, giving 0.95. With three prespecified categories and no estimated parameters, there are two degrees of freedom. The statistic is below the conventional 5 percent critical value of about 5.99, so this sample does not justify rejecting the 1:2:1 model. It does not prove the mechanism, nor rule out models that happen to make similar predictions.

Cross design can distinguish expression from segregation distortion. Imagine that molecular genotyping confirms 40 RR, 80 RW and 40 WW plants, but several RW plants have been classified as red. The allele segregation data remain consistent with the model. Instead, the assumption that phenotype identifies genotype without error has failed. A researcher could investigate light intensity, age, tissue location and the reproducibility of the colour assay. In contrast, a departure from the molecular genotype ratio would direct attention toward transmission, fertilization, survival or sampling. These two diagnostic routes answer different biological questions.

From a coloured petal to a measurable pathway

The red, pink and white flower example is particularly powerful because it permits a student to follow inheritance at several levels. A petal is not a homogeneous solution of dye: it consists of organised cells, including epidermal cells with vacuoles containing pigments. Anthocyanins are a major class of plant pigments, and their apparent colours can depend on chemical structure, concentration, vacuolar conditions and interactions with other cellular components. A pink petal therefore need not contain exactly one half of the pigment mass of a red petal. The appearance is the optical outcome of absorption and reflection in a structured tissue. A genetic interpretation requires comparisons of equivalent tissues at equivalent developmental stages.

An experiment designed to examine the mechanism could begin by comparing genetically uniform red, white and pink plants under matched growth conditions. A visible scoring procedure might record reflectance from a defined area of each petal; a second procedure could quantify an extracted pigment relative to tissue mass. If both measurements rank the three genotypes in the same order, that supports a connection between pigment abundance and visible colour. Nevertheless, a pigment correlation cannot identify which reaction is impaired. Measuring the abundance and activity of a candidate pathway enzyme may help determine whether the difference occurs at the level of enzyme production, catalytic efficiency or substrate availability.

Suppose a controlled teaching dataset gives relative pigment amounts of 90, 48 and 6 units for the red homozygote, heterozygote and white homozygote. The midpoint of the homozygotes is 48 units, so the heterozygote is additive on that particular scale. Now suppose a visual scoring system rates the same flowers at 8, 6 and 0. The midpoint of visual scores is 4, and the heterozygote is closer to red. This is not evidence that meiosis changes according to the measurement system. It shows that the scale of observation affects the apparent magnitude of dominance. If the visual categories are only red, pink and white, there are three phenotypic classes, but their labels provide no justification for treating intervals between classes as equal.

The reappearance of both parental colour classes in the second generation is evidence against irreversible blending. An irreversible mixing model would have to explain why gametes produced by intermediate plants recover the discrete parental hereditary states. Under the chromosome model the recovery is expected, because the two homologues remain distinct through the life of the plant until segregation produces gametes. A single generation of intermediate offspring is not sufficient evidence to distinguish the two models. The second generation and backcrosses supply independent predictions. Genotyping the offspring adds another line of evidence by linking the reappearing colour classes to the underlying allele combinations.

A student may ask whether a white flower is biologically identical to a plant that never synthesises any pigment. It need not be. A particular flower phenotype could reflect an interruption in one branch of pigment synthesis while unrelated pigments and cellular functions are retained. Two white lines obtained by different genetic mutations can even produce coloured offspring if their defects occur in separate genes and the offspring receives a working copy of each. That possibility motivates complementation analysis in a later chapter. For the present single-locus model, the critical point is that identical visible categories cannot establish that two plants carry identical alleles. Both the origin of the lines and their transmission patterns matter.

Developmental timing is another potential source of misleading classifications. Flowers that are pale when newly opened may deepen in colour later. If red homozygotes are scored when mature but heterozygotes are scored before maturity, a difference attributed to incomplete dominance could instead be a difference in developmental stage. Researchers therefore record the time of observation, use comparable growing conditions and where possible score the same individuals repeatedly. A stable genotype-specific ordering under those controls provides stronger evidence for incomplete dominance than a single unstandardised observation.

3. Codominance and the resolution of phenotypes

An intermediate phenotype is not the only alternative to complete dominance. Sometimes two alleles encode distinguishable molecular products, and a heterozygote displays both. In such a case, the question is not how dark or light the resulting trait is, but whether independent observations identify each of the two features. The human MN blood group offers a useful contrast to a flower-colour scale. Allelic variation in glycophorin A gives rise to distinguishable M and N antigens on red cells. A heterozygous individual expresses both antigen specificities, which can be detected using appropriate reagents.

An antibody recognizes a particular molecular feature, called an epitope. In a simplified classroom interpretation of blood group tests, an anti-M reagent reports the M antigen and an anti-N reagent reports the N antigen. An MM red-cell sample is expected to react with anti-M but not anti-N; an NN sample has the opposite result; an MN sample reacts with both. The two positive results in the heterozygote are not equivalent to an average of one positive and one negative result. Instead, they form a distinct pattern of observations.

For an MN by MN cross, each parent produces M and N gametes in equal proportions under the usual assumptions. One quarter of the offspring receive M from both parents, half receive one of each, and a quarter receive N from both. The phenotypes are therefore expected in a 1 M to 2 MN to 1 N ratio. This is numerically identical to the red, pink and white ratio for the simple snapdragon model. The explanation differs: a single graded feature distinguishes the flowers, while two antigen specificities identify the MN heterozygote.

To compare these forms of expression, imagine two analytical instruments. One measures a single continuous variable, such as the concentration of a pigment. A second detects two differently coloured labels, each specific to a different antigen. A heterozygote might give an intermediate reading on the first instrument or two positive readings on the second. The geneticist must decide which observations are biologically relevant and how the categories were defined. Calling both instruments' results incomplete dominance merely because the heterozygote is different from either homozygote would conceal the distinction between an intermediate amount and coexpression of distinct products.

Codominant markers are especially useful in experimental genetics because they can distinguish the two homozygotes from heterozygotes. A marker assay that detects both alleles may reveal a heterozygote even when an associated visible trait shows complete dominance. In contrast, a dominant presence-or-absence marker may fail to distinguish an individual carrying one copy from an individual carrying two. This affects the inference that can be made from crosses: a codominant assay often allows genotype frequencies to be counted directly, whereas a dominant marker can require additional information. Detailed marker-based mapping belongs to a later subunit; here the key idea is that resolving genotype classes improves genetic inference.

The presence of both products need not imply that their quantities are identical. Different alleles can have different expression levels, enzyme activities, turnover rates or detection efficiencies. Codominance requires detection of both allele-associated characteristics in the heterozygote, not necessarily equal molecular concentrations. Conversely, detecting transcripts from both alleles does not imply that the organism displays codominance for every phenotype. A downstream pathway might be saturated or one product might have no appreciable effect on a particular phenotype. A good examination answer should specify which variable is observed before assigning a dominance category.

Two products, one cell: resolving the evidence

A codominance experiment requires a method that can discriminate two different molecular features rather than merely report their summed abundance. Suppose protein variants M and N migrate to different positions during electrophoresis. A homozygote carrying M yields a band at one position and a homozygote carrying N yields a band at another position. The heterozygote yields both positions if both polypeptide variants are present and the assay can resolve them. A lane containing two bands therefore carries information that would disappear if only the total amount of protein were recorded. The measurement provides a concrete way of understanding what it means for both allelic products to be detectable.

The two-band pattern does not on its own prove that the proteins are encoded by alleles of one locus. Two different genes could also encode proteins with different mobilities. To support allelism, the investigator can study inheritance of the patterns in related individuals. If a cross between individuals displaying only M and only N yields offspring displaying both, and a heterozygote cross produces the two single-band classes and the double-band class in the predicted proportions, the single-locus hypothesis gains support. Sequence information identifying alternative variants at one chromosomal position strengthens that interpretation. The assay answers a question about expression; the segregation experiment answers a question about inheritance.

For multimeric proteins, a further subtlety is possible. A protein composed of two subunits may form homodimers containing two M subunits, homodimers containing two N subunits, and mixed dimers containing one of each. If assembly is random and M and N subunits are equally abundant, the probability of an M/M dimer is one quarter, the probability of a mixed dimer is one half, and that of an N/N dimer is one quarter. A method capable of separating those complexes could produce three molecular species in a single heterozygote. This is a hypothetical model, not a claim that every codominant protein gives three electrophoretic bands. Its importance is that molecular assembly can alter the number and relative abundance of detectable products without altering the number of alleles inherited.

The assumptions for this three-species prediction are strong. If the subunits differ in stability, the two types may not be equally abundant. If homodimers form preferentially, the mixed complex will be rarer than predicted by random combination. If the variants have indistinguishable electrophoretic mobility, three actual species may appear as fewer bands. Therefore the reasoning process begins with an explicit model of protein production, assembly and detection. It does not begin by applying a memorised band ratio. The same principle appears in many molecular genetics questions in which a genotype can be inferred only after the behaviour of the measurement method has been described.

In the MN blood group, the conventional M and N antigen specificities reflect variants of glycophorin A on red cells. A heterozygote can display both distinguishable antigenic features. This differs from the ABO example in which allelic variation in a glycosyltransferase affects the structure of surface carbohydrates. Both illustrate codominance but the relationship between gene and detected phenotype is different: in one case a surface glycoprotein bears variant epitopes, in the other enzyme specificity determines carbohydrate modification. This comparison discourages the mistaken idea that every blood-group antigen is a protein directly encoded by the corresponding allele.

A negative detection result must always be interpreted in relation to the assay. A reagent may fail to recognise a variant epitope, or the abundance of a product may fall below the detection threshold. A heterozygote that appears to possess only one product by an insensitive test may possess both when a more specific method is used. Reliable evidence includes known positive and negative controls and a procedure that does not produce the same apparent signal from unrelated molecules. Codominance is a conclusion about detectable expression, not a claim that biological measurements are error-free.

4. A quantitative view of dominance and gene dosage

Many genetic phenotypes arise through a chain of events: allelic sequence influences gene expression or protein function, the protein influences a cellular process, and the cellular process contributes to the observed organismal trait. Variation at the first step need not be proportional to variation at the last. The same amount of functional enzyme can have very different biological consequences depending on the concentration of its substrate, the activities of other enzymes and the threshold used to classify the phenotype.

A simple quantitative description assigns a trait value P to each genotype. Let P(RR) = 100, P(WW) = 0 and P(RW) = 50 in arbitrary measurement units. The midpoint m is the average of the two homozygote values, here 50. The half-difference a is (100 − 0)/2 = 50. The deviation d of the heterozygote from the midpoint is P(RW) − m = 0. A zero deviation indicates an additive relationship on this specific measurement scale. When P(RW) = 75 instead, d = 25 and d/a = 0.5. The heterozygote is still intermediate, but it is shifted towards RR.

If P(RW) = 100, the deviation becomes 50 and d/a = 1. The heterozygote matches RR for this variable. If P(RW) = 0, the ratio becomes −1, indicating that the heterozygote matches WW. A heterozygote value above 100 would lie outside the range of the two homozygotes, which can be described as overdominance for the measured trait. It does not automatically imply superior reproductive fitness; fitness has to be measured separately. The sign of a depends on how the homozygotes are ordered, so the genotype labels and the trait scale should accompany numerical results.

A second example illustrates why dominance can change between observation conditions. Suppose the functional activity in three genotypes is 100, 50 and 0 units, and a developmental response switches on when at least 30 units of activity are available. Both the 100-unit and 50-unit genotypes show the response, although their biochemical activities differ. Now consider a second environment in which demand is greater and the response requires 70 units. Only the 100-unit genotype satisfies the threshold. The changed environment has altered the phenotype mapping without changing the inherited alleles. These illustrative numbers are not experimental values for a particular gene.

In standard Mendelian crosses a functional allele is often sufficient to produce a normal phenotype. Such a locus is described as haplosufficient for that phenotype under the tested conditions. When a single functional copy cannot support the usual phenotype, the locus may be haploinsufficient. This distinction should not be reduced to an arbitrary rule that every inactive allele must be recessive. A decrease in gene dosage can be phenotypically visible. Other mechanisms, including altered activity and interference between protein products, can also produce dominant effects. The full classification of mutant alleles is reserved for 8.B.04.

Gene dosage alone does not determine whether two structurally different products can coexist. In the case of enzymes that act on related substrates but transfer different chemical groups, each allele may produce a functional enzyme with its own specificity. Detecting the distinct reaction products can demonstrate codominance even if their total quantities are limited by substrate availability. A continuous scalar trait model such as d/a is useful for graded pigmentation but may be inappropriate for two independent antigen identities. No single number captures all possible relationships among genotypes and phenotypes.

Saturation, thresholds and dosage-sensitive traits

Consider a hypothetical steady-state model of pigment production in which the abundance of a functional enzyme is proportional to the number of active gene copies. Let E denote relative enzyme abundance and let P denote the resulting pigment value. Suppose P = 120E/(1 + E), where E is dimensionless relative to a chosen reference. This expression is a phenomenological saturation curve rather than a universal equation for pigment pathways. Its purpose is to model the observation that increases in enzyme amount can have diminishing effects when another process limits output. At E = 0, P = 0; at E = 1, P = 60; at E = 2, P = 80. The heterozygote thus has three quarters of the homozygous active phenotype even though it has only half the active enzyme abundance.

If the observer classifies all petals with P at least 50 as visibly coloured, the heterozygote and active homozygote belong to the same class. If the observer instead distinguishes values below 70 as light and values of 70 or more as dark, those same genotypes become distinguishable. The numerical data have not changed. The category boundary has changed. This demonstrates how an apparent change in dominance can originate in the scoring procedure rather than any molecular alteration. A continuous response also means that class labels can conceal substantial variation within a category, especially near a threshold.

A different model of dosage sensitivity involves a protein for which one active copy cannot meet a physiological requirement. In the simplest hypothetical example, two copies supply 100 units of activity, one supplies 50, and zero copies supply none. If the minimum required activity for a phenotype is 70 units, both the heterozygote and inactive homozygote fall below that requirement. A loss-of-function variant therefore behaves as dominant for the categorical abnormal phenotype. This is the logic of haploinsufficiency: the dose supplied by one functioning copy is inadequate. It is not a contradiction of the observation that many loss-of-function mutations behave recessively. Both results can follow from loss of function, because different biological systems have different requirements.

A dominant-negative model predicts a different mechanism. Imagine a functional dimer whose activity requires two intact subunits, and a mutant allele that produces a subunit able to join the complex but unable to perform its function. If the normal and mutant subunits are equally abundant and associate randomly, only one quarter of dimers contain two ordinary subunits. The remaining complexes contain at least one mutant subunit and are inactive under the assumptions. Compare this with a null allele that supplies no competing subunit. The null and dominant-negative mutations can both lower function, but the mutant polypeptide in the second case actively interferes with the ordinary product. Testing their effects in otherwise comparable genotypes may therefore distinguish two mechanistic explanations for superficially similar inheritance.

The quantitative dominance coefficient also depends on how the two homozygous endpoints are labelled. With P(RR) = 80, P(WW) = 20 and P(RW) = 65, the midpoint is 50 and the half-difference is 30. The heterozygote deviation is 15, giving d/a = 0.5 when R is the high-value allele. Reversing the allele labels reverses the sign of a under an explicitly signed convention; the biological ordering is unchanged. When the homozygote values are equal, the denominator becomes zero and this particular ratio is undefined. The absence of a meaningful quantitative coefficient does not prove that the alleles have no molecular differences. They could still be distinguished by qualitative assays.

Dominance for fitness is another trait-specific relationship. A heterozygote with an intermediate pigment amount might have the same survival as one homozygote or higher survival than both in a particular habitat. Fitness is not simply a synonym for the amount of gene product. The environmental and ecological consequences of a phenotype determine its effect on reproduction. In examination problems, use a fitness value only if it is specified, or if the question provides evidence that the particular measured trait determines fitness under defined conditions. Avoid inferring evolutionary success merely from the direction of dominance.

5. The ABO system: three alleles and four common phenotypes

The ABO blood group system connects Mendelian predictions with the chemistry of cell surfaces. Historical observations that mixtures of some human blood samples agglutinated, while other mixtures did not, helped establish the existence of reproducible blood group differences. The later identification of the molecular basis of ABO explained those differences using gene products and carbohydrate structures. In the simplified inheritance model, the locus has three common allelic classes, written Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i.

A diploid individual receives two allele copies, so six unordered genotypes are possible in this three-allele model. IᴬIᴬ and Iᴬi give the A phenotype; IᴮIᴮ and Iᴮi give B; IᴬIᴮ gives AB; ii gives O. This is an example of both multiple alleles and codominance. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant with each other for A and B antigen expression, while either ordinarily produces its recognizable antigen in the presence of i. It is therefore incorrect to write a single linear dominance order that places Iᴬ above Iᴮ or the reverse.

The molecular distinction concerns enzymes known as glycosyltransferases, which transfer sugars to suitable acceptor molecules. A and B antigens are carbohydrate structures, not the allele DNA or the transferase proteins themselves. A functional A transferase attaches an N-acetylgalactosamine residue to the appropriate H precursor. A functional B transferase attaches a galactose residue instead. The common O allele class lacks effective A or B transferase activity. Consequently, ordinary O red cells do not carry the characteristic A or B antigen modifications, but they retain the H precursor.

In the IᴬIᴮ heterozygote, both active transferase specificities contribute to antigen formation. The products are made on different suitable acceptor chains. A red cell bearing both A and B antigen types does not require that a single terminal sugar site carry both alternative additions simultaneously. The molecular phenotype is defined by the presence of populations of A-bearing and B-bearing glycan structures. Distinguishing molecules, cells and the whole blood sample prevents a common misinterpretation of the meaning of codominance.

In 1990 Yamamoto and colleagues reported molecular work connecting ABO allelic differences to transferase specificity and inactivity. This work gave a sequence-based explanation for a system that had initially been distinguished by reactions between red cells and serum. The historical progression illustrates that a genetic model can successfully predict inheritance before the exact molecular mechanism has been established. Once a proposed biochemical mechanism is available, sequence comparison, enzyme-specific activity and antigen detection provide complementary evidence. Association between a variant and a phenotype alone is weaker than evidence linking the encoded product to the expected reaction.

A simple forward-typing model compares red-cell reactions with anti-A and anti-B reagents. A positive anti-A reaction indicates the presence of an A antigen under the assumptions of the test. A positive anti-B reaction similarly indicates B antigen. Both positive reactions indicate the ordinary AB pattern, and neither positive reaction corresponds to the ordinary O pattern in the simplified model. In real laboratory interpretation, weak variants and other complications require additional checks; classroom inheritance predictions must not be substituted for clinical blood typing or transfusion decisions.

The absence of an A or B antigen is not identical to an inability to carry an ABO allele. An upstream enzyme encoded by FUT1 participates in forming the H precursor on red cells. If that precursor is absent because of an appropriate recessive H-deficient genotype, functional ABO transferases cannot make the corresponding conventional A or B antigen on those cells. This is the central teaching point of the Bombay phenotype. It involves a second locus that affects expression of the ABO locus. It is not simply another allele of ABO. The detailed two-locus inheritance and modified ratios are treated in the next chapter on epistasis.

The three-allele model is a deliberate simplification. Many molecular allelic variants exist, including variants associated with different degrees of antigen expression. The terms Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i are useful classes for solving ordinary inheritance exercises, not a complete catalogue of ABO sequences. The student must therefore recognize when a question explicitly assumes the standard model and when it supplies unusual serological or molecular evidence requiring a broader interpretation.

How genetic variation becomes a membrane carbohydrate

Red cells illustrate why an allele and its final phenotypic marker should not be confused. Mature human erythrocytes lack a nucleus, but their surface glycoconjugates were established through the differentiation of nucleated precursor cells. The presence of an antigen on a mature cell does not mean that the mature red cell is actively transcribing its ABO locus. The allele specifies an enzyme during the relevant stage of cell development, and the enzyme modifies appropriate carbohydrate acceptors. The surface marker remains measurable after the developmental machinery that produced it is no longer present in the mature cell.

The H precursor on red cells contains a terminal fucose installed through the activity of FUT1. For the common A and B forms of the ABO system, the relevant donor substrates are activated sugars, UDP-N-acetylgalactosamine and UDP-galactose respectively. A glycosyltransferase recognises an appropriate carbohydrate acceptor and transfers the selected sugar from the donor. The identity of the terminal modification influences recognition by antibodies. Students often memorise the words A and B without recognising that the letters designate distinguishable chemical structures whose presence is a consequence of enzyme activity. Connecting the carbohydrate chemistry with the sequence of the inherited gene makes the inheritance pattern intelligible.

The molecular evidence for ABO specificity is particularly instructive because the proteins responsible for producing A and B determinants are related rather than unrelated. Yamamoto and colleagues examined allelic sequence variation and linked it to the activity of the transferases. Their 1990 study identified differences between common A and B forms and an inactivating deletion in a studied O allele. The discovery supplies a molecular explanation for why a heterozygote carrying functional A and B alleles can produce both types of antigen and why common inactive O forms do not produce either modification. Not all molecular O alleles share the same DNA lesion, so the classical three-allele notation groups together diverse variants by the common phenotypic outcome.

A hypothetical experiment can distinguish the absence of functional enzyme from the absence of its substrate. Consider cells carrying a functional A allele that do not display A antigen. If the relevant precursor is present but A transferase is inactive, supplying functional transferase in a suitable model system could restore A modification. If the precursor is absent, transferase alone would not restore the phenotype. The experimental prediction follows from the order of the biochemical steps. Restoring the precursor in the latter situation could permit the existing A transferase to act. This is a conceptual comparison of substrate dependence, not a clinical or laboratory protocol. It previews the later study of gene interaction without redefining H deficiency as an ABO allele.

The common group O phenotype is not a chemically empty red-cell surface. In ordinary H-positive individuals with inactive ABO transferase variants, H structures remain available because they have not been converted into A or B determinants. This explains why an antibody directed specifically against H can distinguish ordinary O cells from cells with classical Bombay H deficiency. The distinction is essential in reasoning problems: two samples may both fail to react with anti-A and anti-B while differing fundamentally at the precursor stage. A simple phenotype label may therefore combine different molecular causes if the experimental tests do not discriminate them.

The antibody observations that historically made ABO classification possible precede knowledge of DNA sequence and glycosyltransferases. Landsteiner's early investigations compared reactions of sera with red cells from different individuals. Consistent incompatibility patterns showed that blood samples were not immunologically interchangeable. Modern molecular genetics explains the reproducible patterns in terms of inherited antigen differences. The historical progression from observable agglutination to biochemical structures and gene variants is a useful illustration of scientific explanation: molecular mechanisms account for experimental observations, rather than merely supplying a new vocabulary for them.

6. Predicting ABO inheritance and reasoning from offspring

Consider a group A parent with genotype Iᴬi and a group B parent with genotype Iᴮi. Write the possible gametes before constructing any prediction. The first parent produces Iᴬ and i gametes, each with probability one half. The second produces Iᴮ and i gametes, also equally. Combining the possible gametes yields IᴬIᴮ, Iᴬi, Iᴮi and ii. Each has probability one quarter. Under the standard H-competent model, the expected phenotype probabilities for AB, A, B and O are therefore equal.

If both parents are group A, a group O offspring is possible when both parents carry i. For the cross Iᴬi by Iᴬi, the genotype ratio is one IᴬIᴬ to two Iᴬi to one ii. The phenotype probabilities are three quarters A and one quarter O. This example shows why dominance conceals genotype information: an A phenotype alone does not distinguish IᴬIᴬ from Iᴬi, but a standard-model O offspring is informative because ii requires an i contribution from each parent.

An AB parent crossed with an ordinary O parent generates only Iᴬi and Iᴮi offspring in the standard model. The AB parent cannot supply i, so an ordinary O genotype cannot result. Nor can the O parent supply Iᴬ or Iᴮ, so AB offspring are not predicted. The possible classes are A and B in equal proportions. This kind of exclusion follows directly from the available gametes. It should not be turned into a claim of proof about biological parentage, because a single blood-group phenotype has limited discriminatory power and the simple model omits rare variants and other sources of discrepancies.

When both parents are AB, each produces Iᴬ and Iᴮ gametes. The offspring genotype probabilities are one quarter IᴬIᴬ, one half IᴬIᴮ and one quarter IᴮIᴮ. Their phenotype ratio is one A to two AB to one B. An ordinary O offspring is excluded by the model. Notice how the same 1:2:1 structure arises from this two-allele subset of the three-allele population system. A family does not need to transmit all alleles known in the wider population.

Suppose an Iᴬi by Iᴮi cross produces three children, and the exercise asks for the probability that exactly one is group O. In the standard model the O probability for each independent birth is one quarter. There are three possible positions of the O child in the ordered sequence. Each ordering has probability (1/4)(3/4)², so their sum is 27/64, or approximately 42.19 percent. The probability that at least one is group O is one minus the probability that all three are non-O: 1 − (3/4)³ = 37/64. Exactly one and at least one are distinct events. A student who multiplies the probabilities without counting the allowed birth orders will undercount the first event.

Conditional probability is equally important. For an Iᴬi by Iᴬi cross, restrict attention to offspring known to have phenotype A. Among the original four equally likely gamete combinations, three are A and two of those three are heterozygotes. Thus the conditional probability of an Iᴬi genotype given phenotype A is two thirds. Dividing by four instead of three would answer a different question, namely the unconditional probability of an Iᴬi offspring. The conditioning step discards outcomes incompatible with the supplied observation.

These probabilities describe uncertainty across independent conceptions given the parental genotypes and assumptions. They do not require each small family to contain exactly the expected proportion of children. Four offspring from an Iᴬi by Iᴮi cross need not exhibit all four blood groups. Indeed, the probability that all four groups appear exactly once is 4! × (1/4)⁴ = 24/256 = 3/32. Most four-child families under this model do not display all four classes. Expected proportions are useful as long-run summaries, not instructions that individual families must follow.

Complete phenotype-pair reasoning without hidden genotype assumptions

A phenotype cross is often an incomplete statement of the parental genotypes. In a standard ABO question, an A individual can carry IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi; a B individual can carry IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi. The two parents therefore define up to four possible genotype pairings. If both are homozygous, all their children have the AB genotype. If the A parent is homozygous and the B parent heterozygous, half the children are predicted to be AB and half A. If the A parent is heterozygous and the B parent homozygous, half are AB and half B. Only the pairing in which both parents carry i permits all four ordinary groups. The phenotype pair alone does not justify assigning a probability to each parental genotype pairing.

This logic can be reversed. A group B child born to a group A parent and a group B parent must have received i from the group A parent under the standard assumptions. The group A parent is consequently heterozygous. The B parent could carry either IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi. If the same two parents also have a group A child, the group B parent must have supplied i to that child. The observations together identify both parents as heterozygous within the simple model. Such an inference uses the origin of particular alleles, rather than a vague assertion that recessive phenotypes can skip generations.

A different family contains one AB parent and one A parent. If the A parent is IᴬIᴬ, the possible children are IᴬIᴬ and IᴬIᴮ, each with probability one half. If the A parent is Iᴬi, the four gametic combinations are IᴬIᴬ, Iᴬi, IᴬIᴮ and Iᴮi. Their phenotype probabilities are one half A, one quarter AB and one quarter B. A B child distinguishes the second parental genotype from the first, whereas an A child does not. The absence of a B child in a small family does not establish homozygosity because the probability of not observing B in n births is (3/4)ⁿ when the A parent is heterozygous.

It is helpful to distinguish a genotype that is impossible from a genotype that has not yet been observed. For the AB by A cross, ordinary group O children are impossible under either allowed A genotype because the AB parent cannot provide i. For the heterozygous pairing, a group B child is possible but may not appear in a given family. The two statements are fundamentally different: one is a zero probability prediction under a specified model, while the other is an observation with positive probability. This distinction is used throughout genetic analysis, including linkage mapping, pedigree interpretation and tests of expected ratios.

Consider an illustrative problem in which an A parent and an O parent produce four group A children. If the A parent is homozygous, the probability of this outcome is 1. If the A parent is heterozygous, it is (1/2)⁴ = 1/16. Thus the observations are sixteen times as likely under the homozygous model as under the heterozygous model. To turn this likelihood ratio into a posterior probability of the parental genotype, one must specify prior probabilities. If the two genotypes were equally plausible before observing the children, Bayes' rule would give posterior probabilities 16/17 and 1/17. Without that prior assumption, the likelihood ratio is informative but the posterior is not uniquely determined.

Pedigree questions may contain phenotype records from different generations. Rather than assigning genotypes to all individuals simultaneously, begin with the individuals whose phenotypes identify their ordinary genotypes uniquely, such as O and AB. Propagate the constraints on possible transmitted alleles to their parents and children. Then consider the ambiguous A and B genotypes. The resulting method is more reliable than guessing a complete pedigree and checking it afterwards. It also exposes inconsistencies in the assumptions or reported phenotype data. ABO information can provide exclusionary clues in the classical model, but phenotype matching does not establish individual identity or biological parentage.

7. Multiple alleles, dominance series and environmental context

A multiple-allele locus is one at which more than two alternative allelic forms exist in a population. In an ordinary diploid individual, two homologous chromosome copies still provide only two allele positions. In a population, however, new mutations and inherited variation can generate many sequence forms at that locus. The existence of numerous alleles does not contradict meiosis; each gamete still receives one copy at the ordinary single-copy locus.

For k distinguishable alleles, the number of possible unordered diploid genotypes can be counted systematically. There are k homozygotes, because each allele can be paired with itself. A heterozygote consists of two different alleles chosen without regard to order, so there are k(k − 1)/2 heterozygotes. Adding gives k(k + 1)/2 genotypes. For three alleles the result is six, as in the simplified ABO model. For four alleles the result is ten. These counts do not imply that there are equally many phenotypes, because several genotype classes can share a phenotype. Nor do they imply equal genotype frequencies.

A classical teaching example of a dominance series is coat colour at the rabbit C locus. In a simplified four-allele scheme the full-colour allele C dominates the chinchilla form cᶜʰ, which dominates the Himalayan form cʰ, which in turn dominates the albino allele c. The actual locus has additional variation, so this hierarchy is a simplified exercise model. For a C/cʰ by cᶜʰ/c cross, four genotype classes have equal probabilities: C/cᶜʰ, C/c, cʰ/cᶜʰ and cʰ/c. Applying the stated hierarchy gives half full colour, one quarter chinchilla and one quarter Himalayan. No albino offspring can occur because only one parent carries the albino allele.

The Himalayan pattern is informative because an allele's biochemical effect can depend on temperature. Pigment formation associated with the temperature-sensitive variant is greater in cooler regions of the body, producing darker extremities than the warmer trunk. Genetic background and local temperature act at different levels of explanation: the allele contributes a temperature-sensitive biochemical capacity, while the environment determines where the conditions favour its activity. Cooler body regions have not acquired a different inherited allele just because they are darker.

Dominance series can be linear for a specific phenotype and set of conditions, but the ABO example warns against treating a linear hierarchy as universal. A relationship should be established for each informative allele pair. If the alleles yield distinct products, the heterozygote can express both features; if they alter the amount of a shared product, the heterozygote may be intermediate or resemble one of the homozygotes. Thus the number of alleles at a locus and the relationship among them answer different questions. A three-allele locus can have both complete dominance and codominance, while a two-allele locus can show incomplete dominance.

Reading allelic series as pairwise experiments

A dominance series is an experimentally determined set of relationships, not a list of allele names arranged by the amount of DNA they contain. Consider three hypothetical alleles P, Q and R. To establish a complete dominance series for a particular phenotype, the three heterozygotes PQ, PR and QR must be characterised along with their corresponding homozygotes. If PQ resembles PP, PR resembles PP and QR resembles QQ, the results support P > Q > R under those conditions. Knowing only that P dominates Q and Q dominates R may suggest this ordering, but it does not directly establish the phenotype of PR. The missing heterozygote is an independent observation that can test whether the proposed series is valid.

The possibility of mixed relationships can be demonstrated by a four-allele model. Suppose A and B encode products that can be distinguished and are codominant; C produces a partial response when paired with an inactive allele D. A may dominate C for a visible category while B and C may show a distinct phenotype when measured by another method. Such a set of relationships cannot be accurately expressed as a single linear ordering. In a real genetic investigation the phenotype and measurement method must be fixed before drawing a dominance diagram. When the phenotype changes, the diagram may need to be redrawn.

Rabbit coat colour illustrates a commonly taught series at a pigment-related locus, including full-colour, chinchilla, Himalayan and albino variants. The conventional sequence is useful under a specified genetic background and standard observation conditions. It should not be taken as a complete description of every rabbit colour phenotype, because other loci also contribute to coat appearance. In particular, temperature-sensitive pigment formation in Himalayan animals means that the distribution of pigment across the body is influenced by local tissue temperature. A description such as white body with dark extremities contains spatial information, not merely an intermediate numerical colour value.

To reason about temperature sensitivity, distinguish the genotype of a hair follicle cell from the activity of the pigment-producing pathway in that cell. Hair follicles at the extremities may develop under cooler conditions than follicles in the warmer trunk. A temperature-sensitive form of an enzyme can retain greater function under the cooler conditions, permitting more pigment to form. The DNA sequence at the locus need not differ between the two body regions. The visible regional pattern follows from differential expression or activity in different local environments. If hair growth takes place under altered temperatures, the pigment deposited in newly growing hair can differ from that deposited previously. Existing hair does not simply change its inherited allele.

This principle allows a useful experiment to be designed. Compare individuals of the same genotype growing under different, biologically appropriate temperatures and compare different genotypes under the same temperatures. Record a defined measure of pigment deposition during matched periods of hair growth. A change only in the temperature-sensitive genotype supports a genotype-by-environment interaction. If every genotype changes by the same amount, the effect may be a general environmental response instead. This distinction is important because environmental influence alone does not establish that the environment changes the relative phenotypes of different genotypes.

Multiple alleles also increase the possibilities for genotype identification. With five distinguishable alleles, five homozygotes and ten heterozygotes are possible, giving fifteen unordered diploid genotypes. If those alleles form a strict complete-dominance hierarchy, the number of observable phenotypic classes could be only five. If every heterozygote has a unique distinguishable phenotype and every homozygote is distinct, all fifteen classes could be observable. Real systems may fall between these extremes. Consequently the number of alleles is not recoverable from the number of phenotypic classes alone unless the mapping between genotypes and phenotypes is already established.

8. Evaluating genetic models using observations

A rigorous genetic investigation separates the model's predictions from the observations used to test them. For a flower-colour experiment, one may begin with parents whose phenotypes remain stable over several generations of controlled self-fertilization. Reciprocal crosses help test whether the order of the parental phenotypes matters. If the first-generation individuals are uniformly intermediate, a researcher can obtain a second generation and compare counts with the predicted distribution. Parallel observations at standardized developmental stages reduce the risk that variation in flower age will masquerade as genetic variation.

A controlled comparison can go further by scoring a molecular marker or allele-specific sequence independently of flower colour. The resulting observations can be cross-tabulated: does every RR individual show the red phenotype, every RW individual show pink and every WW individual show white under the tested conditions? Exceptions can be examined as biological observations rather than discarded merely because they disagree with a model. A correlation between genotype and phenotype does not automatically identify the relevant enzyme; direct product measurements or functional evidence would be needed to strengthen that mechanistic interpretation.

For a hypothetical ABO family dataset, the simplest approach is to translate phenotype labels into sets of allowable genotypes and then apply the gamete rules. A type A individual can be IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi; a type B individual can be IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi. If an O child occurs, both parents must carry i under ordinary assumptions. That observation resolves the genotype ambiguity of the two parents in the standard model. Without an O child, their genotypes may remain ambiguous, because absence of a possible offspring class in a small sample is weak evidence against that class.

An experimental antigen assay can distinguish coexpression from an intermediate concentration if it is specific to each antigen. The relevant controls include known antigen-positive and antigen-negative samples for each reagent. A dual-positive result supports coexpression only if the two tests are sufficiently specific. A reagent that responds to both products cannot reliably distinguish the products. These observations illustrate a general logic of biological measurement: what an assay measures must be established before its result can be interpreted as evidence for a genetic model.

A final distinction concerns the location of the interacting variants. Dominance describes the relationship between alternative alleles at one locus. Epistasis describes the influence of variation at one locus on phenotypic expression at a different locus. In the ABO example, Iᴬ and Iᴮ constitute a within-locus relationship, whereas the requirement for an H precursor introduces a between-locus dependency. Both can be true in the same individual. The next chapter uses such biochemical dependencies to derive modified dihybrid ratios and to infer the order of steps in genetic pathways.

Expected counts, residuals and what a test cannot prove

When offspring are classified into red, pink and white categories, expected counts are computed by multiplying each predicted probability by the total number of observations. The expected numbers do not need to be integers, because they represent averages under repeated sampling. For instance, a sample of 102 offspring under a 1:2:1 model has expected counts 25.5, 51 and 25.5. If the observations are 30, 46 and 26, the contributions to Pearson's chi-square statistic are approximately 0.794, 0.490 and 0.010, respectively. The total is approximately 1.294. The first class makes the largest contribution, despite the second class having a slightly larger absolute difference, because the deviations are scaled by the expected counts.

The statistic measures disagreement between the observations and the model; it does not indicate the biological cause of the disagreement. The test assumes independent observations and a specified categorical probability distribution. For three classes with fixed probabilities and no fitted parameters, the degrees of freedom equal two. At the conventional five percent significance level, the corresponding critical value is about 5.99. A statistic below that value does not prove the model. Other models may give similar expected proportions, and a sample of this size may not detect modest differences. Equally, a large statistic does not by itself establish segregation distortion, selection or a new genetic locus. Further experiments are required to discriminate possible causes.

One way to investigate unexpected ratios is to score both genotype and phenotype. Imagine a cross that yields genotype counts of 50 RR, 100 RW and 50 WW, but the visible categories contain 75 red, 75 pink and 50 white. The genotype counts match the simple expected ratio exactly. If all 50 RR plants are red and all 50 WW plants are white, the additional 25 red plants must be heterozygotes. The data therefore suggest variability or misclassification in phenotype expression rather than a departure from the expected genotype distribution. A cross-tabulation of genotype and phenotype conveys information that neither set of marginal totals provides independently.

Now consider the reverse situation. Among surviving offspring there are 25 RR, 100 RW and 50 WW, with every genotype assigned its expected phenotype. The correspondence between genotype and phenotype is perfect, but the genotype counts deviate from the expected proportions. One possible cause is reduced survival of RR individuals. Alternatively, the gametes carrying R might be transmitted at a reduced rate in one or both parents. Genotyping embryos at an earlier stage and comparing the genotype distribution with that of surviving plants can help distinguish transmission differences from post-fertilization loss. The developmental stage at which the counts were recorded is therefore part of the experimental evidence.

A careful analysis of inheritance must also identify the relevant denominator. A ratio among fertilized zygotes is not necessarily the same as a ratio among germinated seedlings, mature plants or individuals selected for a visible phenotype. If one begins with a predicted 1:2:1 distribution and later conditions on survival or on a category of interest, probabilities must be renormalised. In a simple illustrative example, if all RR and RW survive but half of WW survive, relative survivor frequencies are 1/4, 1/2 and 1/8 before normalisation. Their sum is 7/8. Dividing by this sum gives 2/7, 4/7 and 1/7. These are not the original zygote probabilities. No novel dominance relation is needed to explain the altered observed ratio.

Finally, experiments on different families or plant lines should be compared before pooling observations. A pooled distribution can fit a familiar ratio even when the individual families are heterogeneous. If some families produce an intermediate heterozygote phenotype while other families do not, pooling their red, pink and white counts may obscure background-genotype effects. When a question supplies several experimental groups, check each group's predictions and conditions before interpreting totals. The experimental design determines which statistical comparisons are meaningful.

9. Alternative explanations, experimental controls and model selection

The value of a genetic model lies in its capacity to make predictions that differ from those of a competing model. A red-white cross can produce pink offspring under several different biological explanations. The first generation by itself is therefore an observation, not an identification of a mechanism. Under a one-locus model, a self-cross of genetically identical first-generation heterozygotes produces only three genotypes in a predictable distribution. Under some two-locus models, recombination can generate additional genotype combinations, potentially producing phenotypes not represented by the three original categories. A carefully designed second-generation experiment offers a way to distinguish these explanations, although it may not always distinguish them completely.

Suppose a plant biologist compares two hypothetical explanations for an intermediate flower colour. The first proposes that the same pigment accumulates in lower concentration in a heterozygous flower. The second proposes that both parents make distinct pigments, and that the heterozygote produces both. A photograph may be insufficient to discriminate these models because different pigment mixtures can reflect a similar spectrum. A measurement capable of distinguishing the chemical compounds can test whether the pink flower contains only a reduced amount of the red pigment or contains two distinguishable pigment types. The phenotype observed by the eye could be intermediate even if the biochemical observations indicate coexpression of distinguishable products.

Consider an illustrative data table in which a laboratory measures an enzyme activity and a pigment amount in two homozygotes and their heterozygote. The functional homozygote has 100 activity units and 90 pigment units. The heterozygote has 50 activity units and 85 pigment units. The inactive homozygote has zero activity units and no detectable pigment. A large reduction in measured enzyme activity has a small effect on pigment amount over the upper part of the range. One reasonable explanation is that the step catalysed by the enzyme has excess capacity when activity exceeds a certain level. The data do not prove that interpretation, since changes in degradation, other pathway activities or measurement uncertainty could contribute. A more extensive series of genotypes or experimental perturbations could test the predicted relationship between activity and pigment.

The design of such a comparison requires a common basis for the measurements. Comparing activity in a fresh leaf extract from one plant with activity in a mature petal extract from another would confound genotype with tissue identity. Pigment content per unit tissue mass may vary because of changes in water content rather than the number of pigment molecules per cell. Equal growth conditions, comparable developmental stages, independent biological samples and a clearly defined normalization procedure help the researcher identify the genetic component of the observed difference. A single measurement for each genotype cannot establish the natural variability among individuals in a population.

Even when the genotype classes are known exactly, the measured trait values can overlap. Imagine that red homozygotes have pigment measurements distributed around 100 units, pink heterozygotes around 50 and white homozygotes around 0. If measurement variability is small, the categories can be recovered reliably from the measurements. If variability is large, a measurement of 75 may be compatible with either the first or second group. Classification based on a rigid threshold then introduces errors into the observed phenotype ratio. Such errors should not be silently treated as segregation distortion. The biological hypothesis concerns transmission of the alleles; the phenotypic test concerns the accuracy with which genotypes are inferred from measurements.

A conditional probability model makes the distinction explicit. Let the three offspring genotypes have prior probabilities one quarter, one half and one quarter. Suppose an illustrative scoring system classifies 90 percent of RR plants as red, 10 percent of RW plants as red, and no WW plants as red. The total expected red fraction is (1/4)(0.90) + (1/2)(0.10) = 0.275. Among plants scored as red, the expected proportion of RR is 0.225/0.275 = 9/11. The red category therefore does not consist entirely of RR. The calculation also shows why the majority genotype among red plants cannot be obtained simply from the fraction of RR plants that are scored correctly; the prior genotype probabilities matter.

In an examination, the first question to ask about a deviation from a theoretical ratio is whether the theoretical ratio refers to genotypes or phenotypes. Suppose 200 fertilized individuals are predicted to have 50 RR, 100 RW and 50 WW. If half the WW individuals fail to survive and the other two genotypes survive completely, the expected surviving counts become 50, 100 and 25. The normalized ratio is 2:4:1, not 1:2:1. The example does not require an altered mechanism of segregation. It demonstrates how selection between fertilization and observation changes the observed distribution. To distinguish unequal survival from biased gamete production, observations of earlier developmental stages can be compared with observations of survivors.

Different sampling units can also influence confidence in a genetic result. Offspring from different plants grown in the same environment may be more comparable than offspring sampled from unrelated field populations, but seeds on one plant may share environmental and developmental conditions. If the research question concerns the generality of a phenotype mapping across genetic backgrounds, independent parental lines become important. The logical objective is not to collect the largest possible number of observations without regard to their origin; it is to collect observations that bear on the competing hypotheses. Larger samples reduce random sampling variation, while better controls reduce alternative explanations.

The same principles apply to blood-group evidence. A positive reaction in a single antigen test can suggest a phenotype, but the validity of that conclusion depends on test specificity, controls and sample identity. Molecular information can answer a related but distinct question about the alleles at a locus. A discrepancy between the two observations is a reason to investigate the relationship between genetic variation and antigen expression. It does not justify automatically privileging one result without examining the quality of both. This is particularly important when a question includes an unusual phenotype that is inconsistent with the simplest three-allele model.

An informative Part C problem may provide two hypothetical data sets rather than ask for an inheritance ratio directly. For example, a cross might produce a 1:2:1 genotype ratio under both normal and low-temperature conditions, but the colour distributions differ between the conditions. That observation supports an environmental effect on the genotype-to-phenotype relationship under the stated model. It is not evidence for a different number of alleles at the locus. A second experiment measuring pigment within each genotype under each environment would help establish the expression pattern. This reasoning links Mendelian prediction with experimental biology without abandoning the distinction between evidence and interpretation.

Constructing competing mechanistic predictions

Suppose two proposed models explain intermediate colour in a heterozygote. Model A predicts a quantitative reduction in a normal pigment due to reduced enzyme abundance; model B predicts that the heterozygote synthesises two distinct pigments that produce an intermediate visual colour when observed together. Both models can generate identical red, intermediate and pale categories in the second generation. The segregation ratio cannot distinguish them. A chemical separation of pigments gives a distinct prediction: model A predicts variation mainly in the amount of one pigment species, while model B predicts two separable species in the heterozygote under suitable detection conditions. This is an example of designing an observation that distinguishes hypotheses which otherwise explain the same data.

The chemical assay itself requires validation. A failure to detect a second pigment does not establish that it is absent if the separation method cannot distinguish it from the first pigment or if the amount is below the detection limit. Samples from the two homozygotes provide useful reference profiles. An artificial mixture of their extracts can demonstrate whether the procedure resolves the expected species in the same sample. If the artificial mixture produces two distinct signals but the heterozygote produces only one, the absence of the second signal becomes more informative. If the mixture cannot be resolved, the experiment does not discriminate the proposed models.

The same strategy applies to allele-specific RNA detection. If both RNA variants are present in a heterozygote but only one protein product is detectable, the difference may occur at translation, protein stability or detection rather than transcription. If only one RNA is detected, possibilities include unequal transcription, RNA degradation or failure of the assay to detect the alternative sequence. Measuring one step in a pathway cannot establish the state of every downstream step. Evidence at multiple levels can narrow the alternatives. In an examination, follow the chain from DNA to RNA to protein to phenotype and identify the earliest step at which the proposed models make different predictions.

A useful hypothetical rescue experiment compares a heterozygote carrying a null allele with a heterozygote carrying a dominant-negative allele. Suppose increasing the supply of ordinary protein restores the first heterozygote's activity almost to the homozygous reference level but restores the second much less efficiently. This observation is compatible with the second mutant protein competing in complex assembly, although it does not by itself prove that mechanism. Direct evidence for mixed complexes and their reduced activity would strengthen the inference. Alternative explanations include altered localisation of the introduced product or different expression levels between the experimental groups. Appropriate controls help isolate the explanatory variable.

Another approach measures the genotype-to-phenotype relationship over a range of conditions rather than at a single point. A heterozygote may appear indistinguishable from a homozygote at high substrate concentration if both pathways are saturated with respect to the measured output, but the two may diverge under more demanding conditions. Conversely, an activity difference could become less visible when the product is not required. The direction depends on the specific rate-limiting process, so a student should not assume that raising substrate always reveals or hides dominance. Plot the response predicted by the stated mechanism and then compare it with the experimental results.

For a temperature-sensitive allele, changing temperature may alter the relative response of genotypes. A useful graph places temperature on the horizontal axis and measured activity on the vertical axis, with separate curves for each genotype. Parallel declines indicate similar sensitivity across genotypes; a much steeper decline for one genotype supports differential sensitivity. Crossing curves show that the rank ordering of phenotypes changes. This could produce different dominance classifications at different temperatures. The curves do not describe transmission of alleles through meiosis; they describe how the same genotypes respond to changing conditions. A correct explanation states which variable is altered, which is controlled and which is measured.

10. Genotype inference from phenotype classes

A genetic cross can be understood as a probability model with two stages. The first stage assigns probabilities to offspring genotypes from the possible parental gametes. The second stage assigns a phenotype to each genotype under specified conditions. If the second stage is deterministic and all genotypes have distinct phenotypes, the phenotype distribution is numerically identical to the genotype distribution. If several genotypes share a phenotype, their probabilities must be added. When an experiment introduces uncertainty in classification or variable expression, the second stage instead assigns conditional probabilities.

This approach prevents confusion in multiple-allele problems. The first step is to list the alleles available from each parent. Consider a hypothetical locus with alleles A, B, C and D, ordered for a particular visible trait as A greater than B greater than C greater than D. Parents AC and BD produce AB, AD, BC and CD offspring with equal probabilities. The phenotypes are A, A, B and C, giving a 2:1:1 distribution. Although there are four alleles among the parents and four distinct offspring genotypes, only three phenotype classes appear. The number of genotypes in a population and the number of genotypes accessible from a particular mating must not be confused.

Now imagine a different four-allele locus at which all four products can be recognized independently. The same AC by BD cross still yields AB, AD, BC and CD, each with probability one quarter. However, all four offspring phenotypes are distinguishable because each displays a different pair of products. The change in dominance relationship alters the phenotypic outcome while leaving the gamete probabilities unchanged. A comparison of the two cases reveals why students should not memorize the expected ratios in isolation: a ratio follows from both the transmission model and the expression model.

In the ABO model, phenotype sets offer a practical way to organize inverse problems. A person of phenotype A can belong to either of two conventional genotypes. A person of phenotype AB belongs to one conventional genotype. If both parents are A, four combinations of possible parental genotypes must be considered before using any evidence from their offspring. An O offspring excludes all but the heterozygote-by-heterozygote combination under the simplified assumptions. If no O offspring is present, the alternative parental combinations have not all been excluded. Inference from an observed absence is usually probabilistic rather than absolute.

For example, assume two heterozygous A parents and six independent births. The probability of seeing no O offspring is (3/4)⁶ = 729/4096, approximately 17.8 percent. Even when the parental genotypes permit an O child, about eighteen out of a hundred hypothetical six-child sets would contain only A children. Thus a family with no O child cannot establish that at least one parent is homozygous. A family with an O child, on the other hand, supplies direct evidence for the presence of i in both parents under the ordinary model. This difference arises because an impossible outcome has probability zero under a hypothesis, while an unobserved but possible outcome has a nonzero probability.

When a problem provides prior genotype probabilities, family data may be used to update them. Suppose that two type A parents have an equal prior probability of being IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi, independently. There are four parental genotype combinations with equal prior probability. Three of the combinations predict that all offspring are A, while the double-heterozygote combination predicts A with probability three quarters at each birth. If two A children are observed, the relative weights of the four parental models are 1, 1, 1 and 9/16. Normalizing their sum gives a posterior probability of 9/57, or 3/19, for both parents being heterozygotes. The numerical conclusion is valid only under the explicitly supplied prior assumptions; these are illustrative and are not population estimates.

In that example the observation of A offspring decreased the probability assigned to the double-heterozygote model from one quarter to 3/19. It did not eliminate the model. This demonstrates the difference between updating the relative plausibility of hypotheses and ruling out a hypothesis logically. In genetic examination problems it is important to identify whether a question asks what is possible, what is expected most frequently, or what is most probable given additional observations. The same cross can support different answers to these three questions.

A testcross can reveal genotypes efficiently in the classical complete-dominance model. An individual of dominant phenotype is crossed with a recessive homozygote, which supplies only the recessive allele. If the tested parent is heterozygous, the offspring are predicted to segregate into two phenotype categories in equal proportions. If it is homozygous dominant, all offspring are expected to show the dominant phenotype. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote may already be recognizable from its intermediate appearance, so a testcross is not necessary merely to identify the genotype in the idealized model. It can still supply evidence that the intermediate individual transmits the two alleles separately.

A cross in which an allele-specific assay distinguishes all genotype classes can yield more information than one in which phenotypes collapse them. For example, if an allele-specific marker distinguishes RR, RW and WW, one can measure genotype ratios even where RR and RW share a visible phenotype. Comparing the marker genotype with the phenotype reveals whether the expected mapping holds across the experimental conditions. The interpretation should account for whether the marker detects the causal allele itself or a nearby associated variant. Detailed linkage analysis and marker mapping are reserved for the later nodes in Unit 8.

Joint, conditional and inverse probabilities in allelic problems

The most reliable way to solve complex allelic problems is to construct a probability distribution over the hidden genotype classes before collapsing them into observable phenotype classes. For example, suppose a locus has three alleles X, Y and Z and an X/Y parent is crossed with a Y/Z parent. The four equiprobable genotypes are XY, XZ, YY and YZ. Under a strict X > Y > Z hierarchy, the first two have phenotype X and the latter two phenotype Y. Under an alternative model in which X and Y are codominant and both dominate Z, the XY genotype has its own phenotype, XZ has phenotype X, and YY and YZ have phenotype Y. Identical parental gametes produce different visible distributions because the second stage of the probability model differs.

The conditional question might ask for the probability that an offspring with phenotype Y carries Z. In the strict hierarchy example, YY and YZ are equally likely among the Y offspring, so the answer is one half. The unconditional probability of YZ is one quarter. The difference comes from restricting the sample space to those individuals that have phenotype Y. In genetic selection problems, this restriction often happens implicitly: a researcher chooses one plant from a set of individuals of a particular colour. The denominator is the number or probability of plants in the selected category, not all offspring produced by the original cross.

An extension involves selecting parents from an F₂ generation. Suppose an ordinary complete-dominance cross gives AA, Aa and aa in proportions one quarter, one half and one quarter. Among the dominant phenotype offspring, AA has probability one third and Aa probability two thirds. Two independently selected dominant phenotype individuals are mated. The probability that both are heterozygous is (2/3)² = 4/9. Conditional on that pairing, the probability of an aa child is one quarter. Multiplying gives an unconditional probability of 1/9 for a recessive child from such a randomly chosen pair. Under an incomplete-dominance model in which heterozygotes are directly recognisable, selecting only the intermediate phenotype would instead guarantee heterozygous parents and give a recessive-endpoint probability of one quarter.

Now consider a codominant genetic marker used to select a heterozygous parent. The marker identifies the two alleles at its own locus. If the trait of interest is controlled by a different locus, the marker genotype does not automatically identify the trait genotype. The relationship depends on linkage, the phase of marker and trait alleles, and recombination between them. If the marker is the causal variant itself, such uncertainty is absent under a simple direct assay model. If it is merely associated with the causal variant, recombinant individuals can have unexpected combinations. This is why codominance is valuable for genetic mapping but cannot replace evidence about the marker's relationship with the biological trait.

A final inference problem illustrates the danger of interpreting phenotype probabilities as population frequencies. In a cross between known Iᴬi and Iᴮi parents, the four ordinary blood groups each have probability one quarter. It does not follow that all four groups have equal frequencies in the population from which the parents came. The family probabilities depend on the two known parental genotypes; population frequencies depend on allele frequencies and population structure. If an exam supplies frequencies and assumes random mating, those frequencies can be used to estimate genotype proportions and inform prior probabilities for an ambiguous parent. Without such assumptions the family cross alone does not yield those priors.

The discipline of writing down both the genotype distribution and the phenotype rule is transferable. In gene interaction problems, the distribution may involve two loci and the phenotype rule may merge several multilocus classes. In penetrance problems, a genotype may lead to a particular phenotype with a probability between zero and one rather than a deterministic mapping. In mapping problems, the gamete distribution itself may differ from independent assortment. By learning to separate these stages in simple allelic systems, the student gains a framework for identifying exactly which part of a more complex model must change when a new experimental observation is introduced.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Intermediate phenotype means hereditary material blends. The alleles retain their identities and segregate separately; recovery of both homozygotes in F₂ tests this.
  2. Incomplete dominance always means exactly half the product. Intermediate refers to the chosen phenotype scale; product abundance and colour may have a nonlinear relationship.
  3. Codominance means an average of the two parental phenotypes. Coexpression of distinguishable products is the key observation, not a midpoint.
  4. A 1:2:1 phenotype ratio alone identifies codominance. Incomplete dominance also produces three distinguishable classes under standard conditions; inspect the heterozygote.
  5. A dominant allele is more frequent. Dominance is an expression relationship; frequency depends on population processes.
  6. A dominant allele is transmitted more often. Ordinary heterozygotes transmit each allele with probability one half regardless of dominance.
  7. Multiple alleles means a diploid individual carries three copies. Three or more variants can occur in the population while ordinary diploid individuals carry two.
  8. Iᴬ dominates Iᴮ or vice versa. The ordinary AB phenotype displays both antigen identities; both alleles dominate i for their respective antigen features.
  9. A and B antigens are the transferase proteins. They are glycan structures generated by the enzymes; distinguish catalyst and product.
  10. O red cells have no surface antigens. Ordinary O red cells retain H and many unrelated blood group antigens.
  11. Bombay phenotype is a fourth standard ABO allele. H deficiency involves a different locus; absence of the precursor prevents ordinary ABO antigen expression.
  12. AB × O produces AB children. The ordinary O parent supplies only i; the children are A or B in the simple model.
  13. A group A child from Iᴬi × Iᴬi has a one-half chance of being heterozygous. Among A offspring, two of three equally likely outcomes are heterozygous; conditional probability is 2/3.
  14. A four-child A-heterozygote × B-heterozygote family must contain all four groups. The expected frequencies do not dictate an individual family; all four exactly once has probability 3/32.
  15. A blood-group match proves parentage. The ordinary model has limited exclusion power and exceptions; phenotype matching is not identification.

Original examination practice

1. Which event causes the 1:2:1 genotype ratio in a heterozygote self-cross?

Answer: Equal segregation and random union of gametes.

Explanation: Dominance alters the phenotype map, not the basic gamete probabilities.

2. What does an MN heterozygote express in the ordinary blood group model?

Answer: Both M and N antigen specificities.

Explanation: The two detectable antigen features demonstrate codominance rather than an intermediate pigment amount.

3. How many unordered diploid genotypes are possible at a four-allele locus?

Answer: Ten.

Explanation: Four homozygotes plus six distinct heterozygotes give 4(5)/2 = 10.

4. Which sugar is attached by an ordinary A transferase?

Answer: N-acetylgalactosamine.

Explanation: The B transferase instead adds galactose to the H precursor.

5. Which genotype is expected for an ordinary group O individual?

Answer: ii.

Explanation: This answer assumes the standard three-allele model and H antigen production.

6. A heterozygote has 75 units; the two homozygotes have 100 and 0. Find d/a.

Answer: 0.5.

Explanation: The midpoint and half-difference are 50. The deviation is 25, so 25/50 = 0.5.

7. A and B heterozygous parents have three children. What is the probability that at least one is O?

Answer: 37/64.

Explanation: Use the complement: 1 − (3/4)³.

8. Two Iᴬi parents have an A child. What is the probability of heterozygosity?

Answer: 2/3.

Explanation: Two of the three equiprobable A-producing gamete combinations are Iᴬi.

9. An RW self-cross yields 38 red, 86 pink and 36 white. Calculate chi-square for 1:2:1.

Answer: 0.95.

Explanation: Expected values 40, 80 and 40 give 0.10 + 0.45 + 0.40.

10. A sample carries functional Iᴬ but lacks the H precursor. Why might the A antigen be absent?

Answer: The A transferase lacks its required acceptor.

Explanation: A second locus changes ABO expression; this is epistasis, not a novel Iᴬ dominance relationship.

Revision and mastery

Separate allele transmission from phenotype expression. Start every cross with parental genotypes and gamete probabilities. Map genotypes to phenotypes only after constructing the offspring distribution. Distinguish intermediate trait values from coexpression of distinct products, and check whether assumptions about environmental conditions and other loci are justified.
