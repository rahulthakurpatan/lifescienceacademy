1. Inheritance and expression are different processes

A plant breeder who crosses two true-breeding plants does not directly cross their flower colours. The breeder brings together reproductive cells, each carrying one version of the genes at the loci under study. Colour appears later, after the fertilized cell divides and its descendants activate particular biochemical pathways. This distinction between what is transmitted and what is expressed is fundamental to interpreting genetics. It explains why parents with very different appearances can produce an intermediate offspring without physically mixing their hereditary information.

In the simplest model, a diploid individual has two homologous chromosomes bearing corresponding copies of a locus. Call two variants of the locus R and W. An RR plant has two copies of R; an RW plant has one of each; a WW plant has two copies of W. During normal meiosis, the homologues separate, and each haploid gamete receives one allele at this locus. The probability of an R gamete from an RW parent is one half, provided there is no transmission distortion. The visible appearance of the parent does not change that prediction. The probability is a property of chromosome transmission, not the strength of pigmentation.

Dominance is a relationship between alleles for a specified phenotype in a heterozygote. Complete dominance means that the heterozygote and one of the homozygotes fall into the same phenotypic category under the stated observations. Incomplete dominance means that the heterozygote occupies an intermediate position for the trait under consideration. Codominance means that the two distinguishable allele-associated features can be detected together. None of these definitions implies that the alleles are unequal in their probability of transmission. A dominant allele is not automatically more ancient, more common, more adaptive or more actively transcribed.

It is also important to name the level at which a phenotype is defined. The presence of a particular RNA sequence, the abundance of an enzyme, a metabolite concentration and the appearance of a whole organism are different observable variables. A heterozygote may resemble one homozygote for a gross morphological characteristic while showing an intermediate concentration of its gene product. Both descriptions can be correct, provided the observations and definitions are made explicit. Genetic labels should follow the data rather than replace an explanation of the data.

Consider a hypothetical enzyme involved in making a coloured molecule. If one active allele produces enough enzyme to meet the demand for that molecule, two active alleles need not make the tissue visibly darker. A heterozygote with one inactive allele could therefore resemble the active homozygote. If the same pathway operates in a range where product concentration is sensitive to enzyme quantity, a heterozygote might instead have less pigment. The genetic segregation is identical in the two models; only the relationship between biochemical activity and visible colour changes. This is why it is misleading to treat complete dominance as a universal physical property of alleles.

For a single autosomal locus, an RW by RW cross generates the four ordered gamete combinations RR, RW, WR and WW, each with probability one quarter. RW and WR describe the same ordinary unordered genotype, so their probabilities add. The resulting genotype ratio is 1:2:1. Complete dominance collapses two genotype classes into one visible group, producing a 3:1 phenotype ratio if R is dominant. Incomplete dominance and codominance can leave all three classes distinguishable, producing a 1:2:1 phenotype ratio. The ratio alone cannot tell which kind of distinguishable phenotype is being measured.

These models assume comparable survival from fertilization to scoring, an ordinary diploid locus, unbiased meiosis and reliable observation. If one genotype dies before observation, the distribution among living offspring can differ from the distribution at fertilization. If environmental conditions prevent a pigment from developing, phenotypic classification can fail to recover the underlying genetic ratio. Before attributing unexpected proportions to a new inheritance pattern, ask whether the observations were made at the same developmental stage and under comparable conditions.

2. Incomplete dominance: an intermediate phenotype

Snapdragon flower colour provides a classical example in which an intermediate phenotype makes heterozygotes recognizable. In the standard teaching cross, a homozygous red line is crossed with a homozygous white line, and the offspring are pink. Let Cᴿ and Cᵂ represent the alternative alleles in this model. The parents each produce only one allelic class of gametes. Every fertilized offspring therefore receives the Cᴿ allele from one parent and the Cᵂ allele from the other. The uniformity of the pink first generation follows from its genetic uniformity, not from a permanent dilution of red hereditary material.

The second generation is the decisive comparison with a blending model. If the first generation is self-fertilized, its gametes still contain either Cᴿ or Cᵂ. A quarter of the offspring are expected to receive two Cᴿ copies, half to receive one of each, and a quarter to receive two Cᵂ copies. Red and white reappear even though neither parent in this second cross is red or white. A model in which hereditary colour permanently blended into an intermediate substance would have difficulty explaining that recovery of the original extremes. Particulate inheritance combined with an intermediate phenotype predicts it directly.

Pink does not have to be exactly midway between red and white on every possible quantitative scale. For instance, pigment mass per gram of tissue, optical absorbance, reflected light and a human observer's judgement of hue do not necessarily have linear relationships. The defining feature is the intermediate expression of the chosen character. A statement that the heterozygote has precisely half the pigment of a red homozygote requires experimental measurement; it cannot be deduced from the term incomplete dominance alone.

A useful hypothetical pathway starts with a colourless precursor and ends in a coloured product. A fully functional allele contributes active enzyme, whereas a second allele produces little or no activity. If the supply of the substrate and the lifetime of the pigment are comparable among plants, the total functional enzyme can influence the rate of product formation. A heterozygote may produce enough pigment for a lighter colour while the functional homozygote accumulates a darker shade. Real flower pigmentation can involve multiple enzymes, transcriptional regulation, transport, vacuolar conditions and tissue development. The one-enzyme picture explains a possible mechanism rather than identifying the precise molecular lesion of every red-white snapdragon cross.

A controlled backcross distinguishes the possible offspring of the heterozygote. Crossing pink with red gives half red and half pink in the simple model, because the red parent supplies Cᴿ to every offspring. Crossing pink with white gives half pink and half white. Neither backcross can produce the opposite homozygote without the corresponding allele being present in both gametes. If an unusual offspring appears, the first scientific response is to investigate parent identity, pollen contamination, scoring and the genetic model rather than declare that alleles have transformed into each other.

An intermediate phenotype can also be produced by two different homozygous lines at separate loci, by environmental variation or by a quantitative trait controlled by many genes. Thus the appearance of a pink first generation alone is not sufficient evidence for one-locus incomplete dominance. Segregation in later generations and the results of planned crosses supply stronger evidence. If molecular genotypes can be measured, their association with colour classes can be tested directly. A reproducible correspondence between the three genotype classes and three colour classes provides evidence for the proposed genotype-to-phenotype mapping, although it does not by itself reveal which biochemical reaction causes the colour.

To interpret a hypothetical sample of 160 second-generation plants, begin with the expected counts 40 red, 80 pink and 40 white. Suppose the observations are 38, 86 and 36. The discrepancies are minus two, plus six and minus four. Their contributions to chi-square are 4/40, 36/80 and 16/40 respectively, giving 0.95. With three prespecified categories and no estimated parameters, there are two degrees of freedom. The statistic is below the conventional 5 percent critical value of about 5.99, so this sample does not justify rejecting the 1:2:1 model. It does not prove the mechanism, nor rule out models that happen to make similar predictions.

Cross design can distinguish expression from segregation distortion. Imagine that molecular genotyping confirms 40 RR, 80 RW and 40 WW plants, but several RW plants have been classified as red. The allele segregation data remain consistent with the model. Instead, the assumption that phenotype identifies genotype without error has failed. A researcher could investigate light intensity, age, tissue location and the reproducibility of the colour assay. In contrast, a departure from the molecular genotype ratio would direct attention toward transmission, fertilization, survival or sampling. These two diagnostic routes answer different biological questions.

3. Codominance and the resolution of phenotypes

An intermediate phenotype is not the only alternative to complete dominance. Sometimes two alleles encode distinguishable molecular products, and a heterozygote displays both. In such a case, the question is not how dark or light the resulting trait is, but whether independent observations identify each of the two features. The human MN blood group offers a useful contrast to a flower-colour scale. Allelic variation in glycophorin A gives rise to distinguishable M and N antigens on red cells. A heterozygous individual expresses both antigen specificities, which can be detected using appropriate reagents.

An antibody recognizes a particular molecular feature, called an epitope. In a simplified classroom interpretation of blood group tests, an anti-M reagent reports the M antigen and an anti-N reagent reports the N antigen. An MM red-cell sample is expected to react with anti-M but not anti-N; an NN sample has the opposite result; an MN sample reacts with both. The two positive results in the heterozygote are not equivalent to an average of one positive and one negative result. Instead, they form a distinct pattern of observations.

For an MN by MN cross, each parent produces M and N gametes in equal proportions under the usual assumptions. One quarter of the offspring receive M from both parents, half receive one of each, and a quarter receive N from both. The phenotypes are therefore expected in a 1 M to 2 MN to 1 N ratio. This is numerically identical to the red, pink and white ratio for the simple snapdragon model. The explanation differs: a single graded feature distinguishes the flowers, while two antigen specificities identify the MN heterozygote.

To compare these forms of expression, imagine two analytical instruments. One measures a single continuous variable, such as the concentration of a pigment. A second detects two differently coloured labels, each specific to a different antigen. A heterozygote might give an intermediate reading on the first instrument or two positive readings on the second. The geneticist must decide which observations are biologically relevant and how the categories were defined. Calling both instruments' results incomplete dominance merely because the heterozygote is different from either homozygote would conceal the distinction between an intermediate amount and coexpression of distinct products.

Codominant markers are especially useful in experimental genetics because they can distinguish the two homozygotes from heterozygotes. A marker assay that detects both alleles may reveal a heterozygote even when an associated visible trait shows complete dominance. In contrast, a dominant presence-or-absence marker may fail to distinguish an individual carrying one copy from an individual carrying two. This affects the inference that can be made from crosses: a codominant assay often allows genotype frequencies to be counted directly, whereas a dominant marker can require additional information. Detailed marker-based mapping belongs to a later subunit; here the key idea is that resolving genotype classes improves genetic inference.

The presence of both products need not imply that their quantities are identical. Different alleles can have different expression levels, enzyme activities, turnover rates or detection efficiencies. Codominance requires detection of both allele-associated characteristics in the heterozygote, not necessarily equal molecular concentrations. Conversely, detecting transcripts from both alleles does not imply that the organism displays codominance for every phenotype. A downstream pathway might be saturated or one product might have no appreciable effect on a particular phenotype. A good examination answer should specify which variable is observed before assigning a dominance category.

4. A quantitative view of dominance and gene dosage

Many genetic phenotypes arise through a chain of events: allelic sequence influences gene expression or protein function, the protein influences a cellular process, and the cellular process contributes to the observed organismal trait. Variation at the first step need not be proportional to variation at the last. The same amount of functional enzyme can have very different biological consequences depending on the concentration of its substrate, the activities of other enzymes and the threshold used to classify the phenotype.

A simple quantitative description assigns a trait value P to each genotype. Let P(RR) = 100, P(WW) = 0 and P(RW) = 50 in arbitrary measurement units. The midpoint m is the average of the two homozygote values, here 50. The half-difference a is (100 − 0)/2 = 50. The deviation d of the heterozygote from the midpoint is P(RW) − m = 0. A zero deviation indicates an additive relationship on this specific measurement scale. When P(RW) = 75 instead, d = 25 and d/a = 0.5. The heterozygote is still intermediate, but it is shifted towards RR.

If P(RW) = 100, the deviation becomes 50 and d/a = 1. The heterozygote matches RR for this variable. If P(RW) = 0, the ratio becomes −1, indicating that the heterozygote matches WW. A heterozygote value above 100 would lie outside the range of the two homozygotes, which can be described as overdominance for the measured trait. It does not automatically imply superior reproductive fitness; fitness has to be measured separately. The sign of a depends on how the homozygotes are ordered, so the genotype labels and the trait scale should accompany numerical results.

A second example illustrates why dominance can change between observation conditions. Suppose the functional activity in three genotypes is 100, 50 and 0 units, and a developmental response switches on when at least 30 units of activity are available. Both the 100-unit and 50-unit genotypes show the response, although their biochemical activities differ. Now consider a second environment in which demand is greater and the response requires 70 units. Only the 100-unit genotype satisfies the threshold. The changed environment has altered the phenotype mapping without changing the inherited alleles. These illustrative numbers are not experimental values for a particular gene.

In standard Mendelian crosses a functional allele is often sufficient to produce a normal phenotype. Such a locus is described as haplosufficient for that phenotype under the tested conditions. When a single functional copy cannot support the usual phenotype, the locus may be haploinsufficient. This distinction should not be reduced to an arbitrary rule that every inactive allele must be recessive. A decrease in gene dosage can be phenotypically visible. Other mechanisms, including altered activity and interference between protein products, can also produce dominant effects. The full classification of mutant alleles is reserved for 8.B.04.

Gene dosage alone does not determine whether two structurally different products can coexist. In the case of enzymes that act on related substrates but transfer different chemical groups, each allele may produce a functional enzyme with its own specificity. Detecting the distinct reaction products can demonstrate codominance even if their total quantities are limited by substrate availability. A continuous scalar trait model such as d/a is useful for graded pigmentation but may be inappropriate for two independent antigen identities. No single number captures all possible relationships among genotypes and phenotypes.

5. The ABO system: three alleles and four common phenotypes

The ABO blood group system connects Mendelian predictions with the chemistry of cell surfaces. Historical observations that mixtures of some human blood samples agglutinated, while other mixtures did not, helped establish the existence of reproducible blood group differences. The later identification of the molecular basis of ABO explained those differences using gene products and carbohydrate structures. In the simplified inheritance model, the locus has three common allelic classes, written Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i.

A diploid individual receives two allele copies, so six unordered genotypes are possible in this three-allele model. IᴬIᴬ and Iᴬi give the A phenotype; IᴮIᴮ and Iᴮi give B; IᴬIᴮ gives AB; ii gives O. This is an example of both multiple alleles and codominance. Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant with each other for A and B antigen expression, while either ordinarily produces its recognizable antigen in the presence of i. It is therefore incorrect to write a single linear dominance order that places Iᴬ above Iᴮ or the reverse.

The molecular distinction concerns enzymes known as glycosyltransferases, which transfer sugars to suitable acceptor molecules. A and B antigens are carbohydrate structures, not the allele DNA or the transferase proteins themselves. A functional A transferase attaches an N-acetylgalactosamine residue to the appropriate H precursor. A functional B transferase attaches a galactose residue instead. The common O allele class lacks effective A or B transferase activity. Consequently, ordinary O red cells do not carry the characteristic A or B antigen modifications, but they retain the H precursor.

In the IᴬIᴮ heterozygote, both active transferase specificities contribute to antigen formation. The products are made on different suitable acceptor chains. A red cell bearing both A and B antigen types does not require that a single terminal sugar site carry both alternative additions simultaneously. The molecular phenotype is defined by the presence of populations of A-bearing and B-bearing glycan structures. Distinguishing molecules, cells and the whole blood sample prevents a common misinterpretation of the meaning of codominance.

In 1990 Yamamoto and colleagues reported molecular work connecting ABO allelic differences to transferase specificity and inactivity. This work gave a sequence-based explanation for a system that had initially been distinguished by reactions between red cells and serum. The historical progression illustrates that a genetic model can successfully predict inheritance before the exact molecular mechanism has been established. Once a proposed biochemical mechanism is available, sequence comparison, enzyme-specific activity and antigen detection provide complementary evidence. Association between a variant and a phenotype alone is weaker than evidence linking the encoded product to the expected reaction.

A simple forward-typing model compares red-cell reactions with anti-A and anti-B reagents. A positive anti-A reaction indicates the presence of an A antigen under the assumptions of the test. A positive anti-B reaction similarly indicates B antigen. Both positive reactions indicate the ordinary AB pattern, and neither positive reaction corresponds to the ordinary O pattern in the simplified model. In real laboratory interpretation, weak variants and other complications require additional checks; classroom inheritance predictions must not be substituted for clinical blood typing or transfusion decisions.

The absence of an A or B antigen is not identical to an inability to carry an ABO allele. An upstream enzyme encoded by FUT1 participates in forming the H precursor on red cells. If that precursor is absent because of an appropriate recessive H-deficient genotype, functional ABO transferases cannot make the corresponding conventional A or B antigen on those cells. This is the central teaching point of the Bombay phenotype. It involves a second locus that affects expression of the ABO locus. It is not simply another allele of ABO. The detailed two-locus inheritance and modified ratios are treated in the next chapter on epistasis.

The three-allele model is a deliberate simplification. Many molecular allelic variants exist, including variants associated with different degrees of antigen expression. The terms Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i are useful classes for solving ordinary inheritance exercises, not a complete catalogue of ABO sequences. The student must therefore recognize when a question explicitly assumes the standard model and when it supplies unusual serological or molecular evidence requiring a broader interpretation.

6. Predicting ABO inheritance and reasoning from offspring

Consider a group A parent with genotype Iᴬi and a group B parent with genotype Iᴮi. Write the possible gametes before constructing any prediction. The first parent produces Iᴬ and i gametes, each with probability one half. The second produces Iᴮ and i gametes, also equally. Combining the possible gametes yields IᴬIᴮ, Iᴬi, Iᴮi and ii. Each has probability one quarter. Under the standard H-competent model, the expected phenotype probabilities for AB, A, B and O are therefore equal.

If both parents are group A, a group O offspring is possible when both parents carry i. For the cross Iᴬi by Iᴬi, the genotype ratio is one IᴬIᴬ to two Iᴬi to one ii. The phenotype probabilities are three quarters A and one quarter O. This example shows why dominance conceals genotype information: an A phenotype alone does not distinguish IᴬIᴬ from Iᴬi, but a standard-model O offspring is informative because ii requires an i contribution from each parent.

An AB parent crossed with an ordinary O parent generates only Iᴬi and Iᴮi offspring in the standard model. The AB parent cannot supply i, so an ordinary O genotype cannot result. Nor can the O parent supply Iᴬ or Iᴮ, so AB offspring are not predicted. The possible classes are A and B in equal proportions. This kind of exclusion follows directly from the available gametes. It should not be turned into a claim of proof about biological parentage, because a single blood-group phenotype has limited discriminatory power and the simple model omits rare variants and other sources of discrepancies.

When both parents are AB, each produces Iᴬ and Iᴮ gametes. The offspring genotype probabilities are one quarter IᴬIᴬ, one half IᴬIᴮ and one quarter IᴮIᴮ. Their phenotype ratio is one A to two AB to one B. An ordinary O offspring is excluded by the model. Notice how the same 1:2:1 structure arises from this two-allele subset of the three-allele population system. A family does not need to transmit all alleles known in the wider population.

Suppose an Iᴬi by Iᴮi cross produces three children, and the exercise asks for the probability that exactly one is group O. In the standard model the O probability for each independent birth is one quarter. There are three possible positions of the O child in the ordered sequence. Each ordering has probability (1/4)(3/4)², so their sum is 27/64, or approximately 42.19 percent. The probability that at least one is group O is one minus the probability that all three are non-O: 1 − (3/4)³ = 37/64. Exactly one and at least one are distinct events. A student who multiplies the probabilities without counting the allowed birth orders will undercount the first event.

Conditional probability is equally important. For an Iᴬi by Iᴬi cross, restrict attention to offspring known to have phenotype A. Among the original four equally likely gamete combinations, three are A and two of those three are heterozygotes. Thus the conditional probability of an Iᴬi genotype given phenotype A is two thirds. Dividing by four instead of three would answer a different question, namely the unconditional probability of an Iᴬi offspring. The conditioning step discards outcomes incompatible with the supplied observation.

These probabilities describe uncertainty across independent conceptions given the parental genotypes and assumptions. They do not require each small family to contain exactly the expected proportion of children. Four offspring from an Iᴬi by Iᴮi cross need not exhibit all four blood groups. Indeed, the probability that all four groups appear exactly once is 4! × (1/4)⁴ = 24/256 = 3/32. Most four-child families under this model do not display all four classes. Expected proportions are useful as long-run summaries, not instructions that individual families must follow.

7. Multiple alleles, dominance series and environmental context

A multiple-allele locus is one at which more than two alternative allelic forms exist in a population. In an ordinary diploid individual, two homologous chromosome copies still provide only two allele positions. In a population, however, new mutations and inherited variation can generate many sequence forms at that locus. The existence of numerous alleles does not contradict meiosis; each gamete still receives one copy at the ordinary single-copy locus.

For k distinguishable alleles, the number of possible unordered diploid genotypes can be counted systematically. There are k homozygotes, because each allele can be paired with itself. A heterozygote consists of two different alleles chosen without regard to order, so there are k(k − 1)/2 heterozygotes. Adding gives k(k + 1)/2 genotypes. For three alleles the result is six, as in the simplified ABO model. For four alleles the result is ten. These counts do not imply that there are equally many phenotypes, because several genotype classes can share a phenotype. Nor do they imply equal genotype frequencies.

A classical teaching example of a dominance series is coat colour at the rabbit C locus. In a simplified four-allele scheme the full-colour allele C dominates the chinchilla form cᶜʰ, which dominates the Himalayan form cʰ, which in turn dominates the albino allele c. The actual locus has additional variation, so this hierarchy is a simplified exercise model. For a C/cʰ by cᶜʰ/c cross, four genotype classes have equal probabilities: C/cᶜʰ, C/c, cʰ/cᶜʰ and cʰ/c. Applying the stated hierarchy gives half full colour, one quarter chinchilla and one quarter Himalayan. No albino offspring can occur because only one parent carries the albino allele.

The Himalayan pattern is informative because an allele's biochemical effect can depend on temperature. Pigment formation associated with the temperature-sensitive variant is greater in cooler regions of the body, producing darker extremities than the warmer trunk. Genetic background and local temperature act at different levels of explanation: the allele contributes a temperature-sensitive biochemical capacity, while the environment determines where the conditions favour its activity. Cooler body regions have not acquired a different inherited allele just because they are darker.

Dominance series can be linear for a specific phenotype and set of conditions, but the ABO example warns against treating a linear hierarchy as universal. A relationship should be established for each informative allele pair. If the alleles yield distinct products, the heterozygote can express both features; if they alter the amount of a shared product, the heterozygote may be intermediate or resemble one of the homozygotes. Thus the number of alleles at a locus and the relationship among them answer different questions. A three-allele locus can have both complete dominance and codominance, while a two-allele locus can show incomplete dominance.

8. Evaluating genetic models using observations

A rigorous genetic investigation separates the model's predictions from the observations used to test them. For a flower-colour experiment, one may begin with parents whose phenotypes remain stable over several generations of controlled self-fertilization. Reciprocal crosses help test whether the order of the parental phenotypes matters. If the first-generation individuals are uniformly intermediate, a researcher can obtain a second generation and compare counts with the predicted distribution. Parallel observations at standardized developmental stages reduce the risk that variation in flower age will masquerade as genetic variation.

A controlled comparison can go further by scoring a molecular marker or allele-specific sequence independently of flower colour. The resulting observations can be cross-tabulated: does every RR individual show the red phenotype, every RW individual show pink and every WW individual show white under the tested conditions? Exceptions can be examined as biological observations rather than discarded merely because they disagree with a model. A correlation between genotype and phenotype does not automatically identify the relevant enzyme; direct product measurements or functional evidence would be needed to strengthen that mechanistic interpretation.

For a hypothetical ABO family dataset, the simplest approach is to translate phenotype labels into sets of allowable genotypes and then apply the gamete rules. A type A individual can be IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi; a type B individual can be IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi. If an O child occurs, both parents must carry i under ordinary assumptions. That observation resolves the genotype ambiguity of the two parents in the standard model. Without an O child, their genotypes may remain ambiguous, because absence of a possible offspring class in a small sample is weak evidence against that class.

An experimental antigen assay can distinguish coexpression from an intermediate concentration if it is specific to each antigen. The relevant controls include known antigen-positive and antigen-negative samples for each reagent. A dual-positive result supports coexpression only if the two tests are sufficiently specific. A reagent that responds to both products cannot reliably distinguish the products. These observations illustrate a general logic of biological measurement: what an assay measures must be established before its result can be interpreted as evidence for a genetic model.

A final distinction concerns the location of the interacting variants. Dominance describes the relationship between alternative alleles at one locus. Epistasis describes the influence of variation at one locus on phenotypic expression at a different locus. In the ABO example, Iᴬ and Iᴮ constitute a within-locus relationship, whereas the requirement for an H precursor introduces a between-locus dependency. Both can be true in the same individual. The next chapter uses such biochemical dependencies to derive modified dihybrid ratios and to infer the order of steps in genetic pathways.

Original examination practice

1. Which event causes the 1:2:1 genotype ratio in a heterozygote self-cross?

Answer: Equal segregation and random union of gametes.

Explanation: Dominance alters the phenotype map, not the basic gamete probabilities.

2. What does an MN heterozygote express in the ordinary blood group model?

Answer: Both M and N antigen specificities.

Explanation: The two detectable antigen features demonstrate codominance rather than an intermediate pigment amount.

3. How many unordered diploid genotypes are possible at a four-allele locus?

Answer: Ten.

Explanation: Four homozygotes plus six distinct heterozygotes give 4(5)/2 = 10.

4. Which sugar is attached by an ordinary A transferase?

Answer: N-acetylgalactosamine.

Explanation: The B transferase instead adds galactose to the H precursor.

5. Which genotype is expected for an ordinary group O individual?

Answer: ii.

Explanation: This answer assumes the standard three-allele model and H antigen production.

6. A heterozygote has 75 units; the two homozygotes have 100 and 0. Find d/a.

Answer: 0.5.

Explanation: The midpoint and half-difference are 50. The deviation is 25, so 25/50 = 0.5.

7. A and B heterozygous parents have three children. What is the probability that at least one is O?

Answer: 37/64.

Explanation: Use the complement: 1 − (3/4)³.

8. Two Iᴬi parents have an A child. What is the probability of heterozygosity?

Answer: 2/3.

Explanation: Two of the three equiprobable A-producing gamete combinations are Iᴬi.

9. An RW self-cross yields 38 red, 86 pink and 36 white. Calculate chi-square for 1:2:1.

Answer: 0.95.

Explanation: Expected values 40, 80 and 40 give 0.10 + 0.45 + 0.40.

10. A sample carries functional Iᴬ but lacks the H precursor. Why might the A antigen be absent?

Answer: The A transferase lacks its required acceptor.

Explanation: A second locus changes ABO expression; this is epistasis, not a novel Iᴬ dominance relationship.
