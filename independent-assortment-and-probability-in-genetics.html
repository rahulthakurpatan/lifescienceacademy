1. From one inherited character to two

A genetic cross is an experiment in transmission, not simply a calculation of offspring colours. The experimenter begins with parents whose inherited differences can be followed and asks which combinations of those differences reappear in the descendants. A single character gives a first opportunity to distinguish two competing explanations: either parental hereditary differences become permanently blended, or the inherited alternatives remain distinct and can be separated in a later generation. Segregation explains the latter outcome. The next problem arises when the same parents differ in two characters at once. Does the inheritance of one character dictate the inheritance of the other, or can their hereditary determinants be transmitted separately? Independent assortment is the answer to this second question under a specified set of biological conditions.

Imagine a diploid plant that inherited a round-seed allele from one parent and a wrinkled-seed allele from the other. We write these alleles as R and r. The plant also has a locus influencing cotyledon colour, at which we write the yellow and green alternatives as Y and y. The genotype RrYy says that the plant is heterozygous at both loci. It says nothing by itself about whether the two loci lie on the same chromosome or different chromosomes. The genotype also does not tell us whether all four allele combinations occur equally frequently in the gametes. Those are separate claims about the mechanism of inheritance. To make predictions, we must specify both the alleles present and their transmission probabilities.

A locus is a position or defined genetic region, whereas an allele is a particular inherited version at that locus. A diploid individual generally has two copies of an autosomal locus. During normal meiosis, a haploid gamete receives one of the two alternatives. Fertilization joins one gamete from each parent and restores the diploid state. This alternating reduction and restoration provides the basis for Mendelian ratios. The gamete of a double heterozygote is represented by two letters, such as RY, because it carries one allele from each locus. Writing Rr as a normal haploid gamete confuses the diploid parental genotype with the product of meiosis. Similarly, a gamete labelled RR cannot be treated as an ordinary product of a heterozygote at one locus.

Dominance is a relationship between alleles in the expression of a phenotype. If Rr and RR both produce round seeds under the conditions of observation, R is described as completely dominant to r for this character. This does not imply that R is transmitted more frequently, that it always gives a physiological advantage, or that it is more common in a population. In the simplest cross, R and r each occur in half of the gametes of Rr. The distinction between expression and transmission becomes particularly important when we study two loci. Independent assortment describes transmission, whereas the familiar phenotype ratios additionally depend on how each offspring genotype is expressed.

Consider the cross RRYY × rryy. With the stated homozygous parents, every gamete from the first parent carries RY, and every gamete from the second carries ry. Their F1 offspring are therefore RrYy. Under complete dominance the F1 seeds are round and yellow. This result alone cannot demonstrate independent assortment: even if the loci are tightly linked, all F1 individuals would have the same alleles and display the same dominant characters. A genuine test must observe the products of meiosis in the F1 generation, either indirectly through a self-cross or more directly through a cross with a suitable tester.

The F1 may be crossed with another F1 or, in a self-fertilizing plant, allowed to self-fertilize under controlled conditions. The resulting F2 population can exhibit four phenotypic classes: round yellow, round green, wrinkled yellow and wrinkled green. The combinations round green and wrinkled yellow differ from the two original parental phenotypes. Their occurrence shows that parental characters need not be inherited as inseparable packages. Their mere presence, however, is not sufficient evidence of statistical independence. A linked pair of loci can also produce novel combinations by crossing over. The frequencies, experimental design and alternative explanations must therefore be considered together.

Mendel's experiments offered a way to separate the question of inheritance from subjective impressions about resemblance. He selected traits with distinguishable categories, used lines with stable inherited characteristics, controlled pollination, and recorded progeny counts. His published 1866 account includes crosses involving seed shape and colour. In a modern chromosome-based explanation, the hereditary factors followed in these experiments correspond to alleles at loci, and the separation of alternative factors corresponds to transmission through gametes. Mendel did not have the contemporary molecular or cytological framework available to him; the value of the historical result is that quantitative patterns made a mechanistic model testable before the physical identity of genetic information was known.

The F2 seed-shape and seed-colour observations reported by Mendel provide a useful historical example: 315 round yellow, 108 round green, 101 wrinkled yellow and 32 wrinkled green seeds. These are observed counts, not a set of exact theoretical proportions. When placed in the order just given, the four classes can be compared to the 9:3:3:1 prediction. They total 556. A model predicting exactly 312.75 round yellow seeds does not imply that a fraction of a seed exists. It implies a mean count over many comparable experiments. The noninteger expected count is meaningful because probability is a property of a model, while an actual experiment produces integers.

The important progression is from observation to a quantitative model, and then from the model to biological interpretation. It is possible to observe an approximately Mendelian ratio without identifying the precise chromosomes or biochemical pathways involved. Conversely, a well-supported chromosome model does not guarantee that the next group of sixteen offspring contains exactly nine individuals of one phenotype. A probability is a prediction about a distribution of outcomes, not a timetable governing which individual must appear next. To understand genetics at the analytical level, we must keep the distinction between a biological mechanism, a mathematical prediction and a finite observation throughout the chapter.

A useful vocabulary for the remainder of the discussion is marginal probability, joint probability and conditional probability. A marginal probability concerns one event while ignoring the outcome at another locus. A joint probability concerns their simultaneous occurrence. A conditional probability asks about an event among a selected subset. These three questions can all be asked of the same table of offspring. The statement of independence concerns how the joint and conditional probabilities relate to the marginal probabilities. It is stronger than observing a correct marginal ratio at each locus separately.

2. Chromosomal mechanism of independent assortment

The mechanism becomes visible when the two loci are located on separate homologous chromosome pairs. Suppose one pair carries R and r and a different pair carries Y and y. Before meiosis, DNA replication produces sister chromatids, so each chromosome is represented by two chromatids. Homologous chromosomes then associate during prophase I and become organized into bivalents. The homologues are not the same as sister chromatids: a homologous pair comprises chromosomes inherited from the two parents, whereas sisters arise by replication of a single chromosome. Even if sisters have exchanged segments during recombination, the distinction between the homologues and sister chromatids remains essential for understanding segregation.

At metaphase I each bivalent is positioned on the spindle so that the two homologues are directed toward opposite poles. For a pair carrying R and r, either R-bearing homologue can face a particular pole. For the pair carrying Y and y, either Y-bearing homologue can face that same pole. Under the conventional model the direction selected by the second pair does not depend on the direction selected by the first. Two relative orientations can therefore be distinguished: the R and Y homologues face the same pole, or the R and y homologues face the same pole. Reversing both poles changes the absolute orientation but not the resulting pair of complementary allele combinations. In an ensemble of cells with random orientations, both relative arrangements are equally frequent.

During anaphase I the homologues separate. In a simplified diagram with no exchange involving the marker loci, one relative orientation puts R and Y together in one daughter cell and r and y together in the other. The other orientation produces R with y and r with Y. Meiosis II then separates sister chromatids, producing haploid products. If all four products were recovered in the simplified first orientation, there would be two RY and two ry products; the second orientation would produce two Ry and two rY. It is incorrect to demand that each individual meiotic division yield exactly one of each of the four possible gamete types. Equal frequencies are a population-level expectation across many meiotic divisions. Recombination between the centromere and marker can complicate individual tetrad patterns, so the illustration is explicitly a model of assortment without relevant exchanges.

Imagine selecting one gamete at random from the entire population of products. Its R or r allele depends on segregation of one pair; the Y or y allele depends on segregation of another. With fair segregation, each allele occurs with probability one-half. With independent relative orientation, the joint probability of R and Y is one-half multiplied by one-half, or one-quarter. The same argument gives one-quarter for Ry, rY and ry. The four classes exhaust the possible haploid genotypes and the four probabilities sum to one. This provides a biological derivation of the familiar four gametes from a dihybrid rather than an unexplained rule to memorize.

There is a subtle difference between random chromosome orientation and random fertilization. The first process concerns the segregation of alleles into gametes within a parent. The second concerns the association of gametes from different parents. Even if a parent produces RY, Ry, rY and ry in equal proportions, the predicted F2 distribution requires that the fertilizing gametes combine according to the assumed probabilities. If fertilization is biased toward one gamete combination, the genotype frequencies of zygotes may no longer equal the products of the gametic probabilities. If differential survival operates after fertilization, counts from adult progeny can depart from the zygote predictions. A ratio observed in surviving offspring therefore reflects the combined processes of gamete production, fertilization, development and scoring.

For n independently orienting homologous pairs, each pair can contribute one of two parental homologues to a particular haploid product. Ignoring crossing over, the number of possible parental-chromosome sets is 2 to the power n. This is a count of possible sets across many gametes, not the number of chromosomes in a gamete and not the number of different products of a single meiotic division. A diploid organism with three homologous pairs has eight possible sets, but each normal haploid product contains three chromosomes. The combinations become increasingly numerous as the haploid chromosome number increases. Crossing over adds further diversity by exchanging homologous segments, producing chromosomes that are not simply intact maternal or paternal homologues.

The same counting logic can be applied to heterozygous loci when they assort independently. A genotype AaBbCc with the three loci independently assorting produces eight possible allele combinations. A genotype AABbcc, however, has only one heterozygous locus: B versus b. It can produce ABc and Abc, not eight different allelic gametes. Homozygous loci contribute their fixed allele to every normal gamete. Therefore the appropriate exponent for counting distinct gametic genotypes is the number of heterozygous, independently assorting loci, not the total number of loci listed in the genotype.

The underlying mechanism also explains why segregation is not synonymous with independent assortment. Segregation is separation of alternative alleles at a single locus into different gametes. Independence is a relationship between segregation at different loci. Two tightly linked loci may each segregate normally, yet the joint transmission of their alleles is strongly associated. For example, a double heterozygote carrying AB on one homologue and ab on the other, in the absence of crossing over between the loci, produces AB and ab gametes in equal proportions. At the A locus the probability of A is one-half; at B the probability of B is one-half. However, the probability of AB is one-half rather than one-quarter. The separate marginal probabilities are identical to those of an independently assorting double heterozygote.

This linked example gives a powerful diagnostic. If observing A in a gamete changes the probability of observing B, then the two events are not independent in that gamete distribution. In the complete coupling example, observing A identifies AB, so the conditional probability of B given A is one. Observing a identifies ab, so the conditional probability of B given a is zero. In an independently assorting double heterozygote, both conditional probabilities are one-half. This statistical distinction arises from the physical organization of the chromosomes. Later chapters will explain how crossing over changes the joint gamete frequencies and how this information is used in genetic mapping.

Chromosomes on different pairs generally assort independently under regular meiosis, but statistical independence between marker loci does not uniquely prove that they occupy different chromosomes. Distant loci on the same chromosome can approach an observed recombination fraction of one-half, making their two-locus transmission patterns indistinguishable from independence by this criterion. Conversely, a deviation from the independence prediction does not uniquely establish physical linkage: segregation distortion, selection among gametes or offspring, and nonindependent sampling can also change measured frequencies. A genetic model is a causal explanation supported by multiple observations, not an automatic conclusion from a single ratio.

The independent assortment model assumes that the events used in calculations have been defined correctly. Alleles A and a in the same haploid gamete are mutually exclusive under normal segregation; they are not independent. A and B at separate independently assorting loci can coexist. The physical location of the alternatives determines which statements are biologically possible before we apply any mathematical rule.

3. Probability rules for genetic events

A probability is a number between zero and one assigned to an event. In a genetic cross, an event could be the production of an Ab gamete, the formation of an AaBb zygote, or the observation of a recessive phenotype among offspring. The probability depends on the sample space, which is the collection of outcomes under consideration. A sample space of gametes is not the same as a sample space of zygotes. A sample space of all zygotes is not necessarily the same as a sample space of viable adults. Failure to specify the sample space is the origin of many apparently contradictory answers in genetics.

For an Aa parent undergoing ordinary meiosis, the outcomes at the A locus are A and a with probabilities one-half each. These outcomes are exhaustive and mutually exclusive. If a gamete carries A, it does not also carry a, given the assumed haploid state. Thus the probability of A or a equals one. By contrast, in a double heterozygote the events “carries A” and “carries B” are neither mutually exclusive nor exhaustive. Some gametes carry both, some carry only one, and some carry neither. Simply adding the marginal probabilities would count the AB gametes twice.

The general multiplication rule states that P(A and B) equals P(A) multiplied by P(B given A). Here the letters A and B denote events, which may or may not be identical to allele labels. If the occurrence of A does not alter the probability of B, then P(B given A) equals P(B), and the expression reduces to the ordinary product rule P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). It is this reduced form that is commonly used for independent loci. The general form remains valid when the events are dependent, provided the conditional probability is defined. The reduced form must not be applied automatically merely because an examination question uses the word “and”.

For example, an AaBb individual with independent assortment produces an Ab gamete with probability P(A) × P(b) = (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4. If the same loci are linked in coupling phase, AB/ab, with recombination fraction 0.20 and equal reciprocal recombinant classes, the probability of Ab is 0.10. Both marginal probabilities still equal one-half, but P(b given A) = 0.10/0.50 = 0.20. The general multiplication rule now gives (1/2)(0.20) = 0.10, agreeing with the gamete frequency. This comparison establishes exactly which assumption was needed to obtain one-quarter in the independent case.

The addition rule concerns a union of events. For two events E and F, P(E or F) = P(E) + P(F) − P(E and F), because the overlap would otherwise be counted twice. When the events cannot occur together, the overlap is zero and the rule simplifies to addition. The probability that an Aa × Aa offspring is homozygous is the sum of P(AA) and P(aa), namely one-quarter plus one-quarter or one-half. An individual cannot be AA and aa at the same locus, so no correction is needed. On the other hand, the probability that an AaBb self-cross offspring is aa or bb is not one-half. The aa and bb classes overlap at aabb. The correct probability is 1/4 + 1/4 − 1/16 = 7/16.

In ordinary language, “or” can be inclusive or exclusive. The inclusive event contains outcomes satisfying either condition or both. The exclusive event contains outcomes satisfying exactly one condition. If a question asks for offspring with exactly one recessive phenotype in AaBb × AaBb, the relevant events are aaB_ and A_bb. They are disjoint because the first is dominant at B while the second is recessive there. Each has probability (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16. Their sum is 6/16. If the question instead asks for at least one recessive phenotype, aabb must also be counted, giving 7/16. A single phrase changes the set of offspring counted.

A complement consists of every outcome in the sample space that is not in the specified event. The event “at least one recessive phenotype” has complement “no recessive phenotypes”. In the independent dihybrid self-cross, the latter requires a dominant phenotype at both loci and has probability 9/16. Subtracting from one gives 7/16 for the original event. This method is especially valuable when there are many loci or offspring. Rather than enumerating every possible combination containing one or more recessive outcomes, calculate the simpler event that none occurs.

Conditional probability is defined by dividing the joint probability by the probability of the conditioning event: P(E given F) = P(E and F)/P(F), when P(F) is greater than zero. Its denominator is the probability of entering the selected subset. In Aa × Aa, the offspring genotypes AA, Aa and aa have probabilities one-quarter, one-half and one-quarter. Among individuals displaying a dominant phenotype under complete dominance, aa is absent. The probability of heterozygosity in this selected population equals (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3. This result is not the probability of a heterozygote among all offspring, which remains one-half. The distinction is a change in the denominator, not a change in the mechanism of meiosis.

Consider a selected A_B_ offspring from AaBb × AaBb. If the loci assort independently and the phenotype at each locus is determined independently, the probability of Aa among A_ is 2/3 and that of Bb among B_ is 2/3. The probability of AaBb among the double dominant subset is therefore 4/9. One can obtain the same answer from the unselected sample: the probability of AaBb is one-quarter, while that of A_B_ is 9/16; dividing gives (1/4)/(9/16) = 4/9. Agreement between these two methods is a useful check that the correct sample space has been selected.

Independence does not follow from knowing two equal marginal probabilities. Imagine four equally numerous gamete classes AB, Ab, aB and ab in one model and only AB and ab, each with one-half probability, in another. Both models have P(A) = P(B) = 1/2. However, their joint probabilities differ. Conversely, mutually exclusive nonzero-probability events cannot be independent because their joint probability is zero while the product of their probabilities is positive. When deciding which formula applies, identify the events and inspect whether they can occur together and whether one changes the chance of the other.

A final distinction concerns the probabilities of offspring from a cross and successive offspring from the same parents. Once parental genotypes and a constant reproductive model are specified, separate fertilizations are commonly treated as independent trials. Under this model, the genotype of a first offspring does not change the probability of a later offspring. But if parental genotype is unknown, the first offspring may provide information that changes our estimate of the parents' genotypes and hence the predicted probability for later offspring. Conditional on the actual parental genotypes the births may be independent; after averaging over uncertainty in those genotypes, the observations can become statistically dependent. Genetics problems often exploit this distinction when they ask about the probability of a recessive offspring after information about siblings has been supplied.

4. The dihybrid cross and the 9:3:3:1 model

We can now derive the classical dihybrid phenotype ratio without appealing to memorization. Assume two diploid parents, each heterozygous at two autosomal loci, represented as RrYy. Assume normal segregation, independent assortment, random union of gametes, equal viability, complete dominance at each locus and no interaction between the loci that changes the phenotype categories. These assumptions specify a model. The predicted result is not a universal law for any two visible traits. A change in even one assumption can change the observed ratio.

At the R locus, the cross Rr × Rr generates RR, Rr and rr with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. At the Y locus, the equivalent cross generates YY, Yy and yy with the same probabilities. Because the loci assort independently in the parents and the union of gametes is random, the two zygotic locus genotypes are independently distributed. The joint probability of RRYY is therefore (1/4)(1/4) = 1/16. The joint probability of RrYy is (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4. Each of the remaining seven genotype classes can be obtained in the same manner. There are nine distinct two-locus genotype classes because each locus has three possible diploid genotypes and 3 × 3 = 9.

A Punnett square makes the gametic origin of these classes explicit. Write RY, Ry, rY and ry along one edge for the first parent and the same four gametes along the other edge for the second. Each cell represents one ordered union of two gametes. Since both gamete distributions are uniform and independent under random fertilization, each of the sixteen cells has probability 1/16. The cells do not represent sixteen different genotypes. For example, RY × ry, Ry × rY, rY × Ry and ry × RY all produce RrYy. They are distinct routes to the same offspring genotype and their probabilities are added.

The notation R_ denotes a phenotype-compatible genotype with at least one R allele; it includes RR and Rr. The underscore is not an unknown physical allele or an additional allele type. In a self-cross, P(R_) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4. Similarly, P(Y_) = 3/4. The probability of round yellow offspring is P(R_Y_) = (3/4)(3/4) = 9/16. Round green offspring have R_yy with probability (3/4)(1/4) = 3/16. Wrinkled yellow offspring have rrY_ with probability (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16. Finally, wrinkled green offspring have rryy with probability (1/4)(1/4) = 1/16. The four mutually exclusive and exhaustive classes total sixteen sixteenths. Their relative weights give 9:3:3:1.

The phenotype ratio hides the greater richness of the genotype distribution. Within the round yellow class, RRYY occurs with probability 1/16, RRYy with 2/16, RrYY with 2/16 and RrYy with 4/16. If a round yellow F2 plant is selected at random, the probability that it is homozygous dominant at both loci is 1/9, not 1/16. The probability that it is heterozygous at both loci is 4/9. The probability that it is homozygous at exactly one of the two loci and heterozygous at the other is 4/9. These probabilities characterize the genotype composition of a visually selected class, not the original F2 distribution.

When the parental cross is RRyy × rrYY rather than RRYY × rryy, the F1 genotype is still RrYy. Under independent assortment, changing the original combinations does not alter the F2 phenotype prediction. This is because the separate allele transmissions do not retain information about the parental pairing. If the loci were linked, the arrangement of alleles on the homologous chromosomes would matter: RY/ry is a different phase from Ry/rY. Thus a useful experimental comparison involves reciprocal parental combinations or molecular evidence about the marker arrangement. Equal dihybrid ratios under these assumptions need not identify the chromosomal position of the markers.

The familiar ratio can also be collapsed by examining one trait at a time. Round offspring include the round yellow and round green classes; their combined probability is 9/16 + 3/16 = 12/16 = 3/4. Wrinkled offspring total 4/16 = 1/4. The same marginal 3:1 ratio appears for yellow versus green. However, this collapse loses information about the association between the traits. A linked model with normal segregation can retain both marginal 3:1 phenotype ratios while failing to produce the expected joint distribution. A study recording only the totals for each trait separately cannot distinguish all alternative two-locus models.

The difference between the theoretical probability and the observed count is essential in experimental interpretation. For N = 160 offspring, the expected counts under the model are 90, 30, 30 and 10. For N = 100 offspring the expectations are 56.25, 18.75, 18.75 and 6.25. We do not round these values before using a goodness-of-fit statistic: rounding introduces avoidable changes in expected counts and may destroy the requirement that their sum equals N. The experiment will yield integer counts, and deviations can be examined relative to the sampling variation predicted by the model.

A dihybrid cross also need not mean self-crossing identical heterozygotes. In RrYy × Rryy, the R locus has the ordinary 3:1 phenotype probabilities, whereas the Y locus has a testcross-like 1:1 distribution. Their independent products give round yellow and round green each with probability 3/8, and wrinkled yellow and wrinkled green each with probability 1/8. The ratio is 3:3:1:1 in that order. An examination problem with two heterozygous loci in one parent is not enough to justify 9:3:3:1; the genotype of the other parent must be inspected. Likewise, when one parent is homozygous dominant at a locus, all offspring express the dominant phenotype at that locus under complete dominance, although the offspring genotypes can still differ.

The derivation makes clear why genotype ratios, phenotype ratios, and gamete ratios are distinct. The gametic probabilities describe parental meiotic products; the genotype probabilities describe combinations created at fertilization; the phenotype probabilities describe the expression of those combinations. A correct solution states which of these distributions is required, derives the relevant probability, and only then converts it into a ratio or a count.

5. Testcrosses and the direct measurement of gamete classes

A dihybrid self-cross combines gametes from two genetically variable parents. Although it is a powerful illustration of independent assortment, the offspring do not directly reveal the precise gamete received from either parent. For example, an AaBb offspring could be produced by several ordered pairs of gametes. A more direct experimental design is available when a suitable homozygous recessive tester can be crossed with a double heterozygote. The tester contributes only ab gametes under ordinary segregation, so each offspring receives a known recessive allele at both loci from that parent. Any dominant allele in the offspring must have come from the heterozygous parent. This converts each informative offspring into a record of a particular transmitted gametic genotype.

In the cross AaBb × aabb, the first parent can produce AB, Ab, aB and ab. If these four classes occur with equal probability, the tester's constant ab gamete yields AaBb, Aabb, aaBb and aabb in equal frequencies. Under complete dominance at each locus, these four genotypes produce four distinguishable phenotypes. The phenotype ratio is 1:1:1:1, in contrast to the 9:3:3:1 distribution from the self-cross. The reason for the contrast is that the testcross replaces one variable gamete population with a constant genotype. In the self-cross there are sixteen equally likely ordered gamete unions; in the testcross there are four.

The tester does not force independent assortment in the heterozygote. It provides a way of observing gamete transmission. If the heterozygote produces AB gametes more often than Ab, the AaBb testcross offspring should correspondingly outnumber Aabb, provided gamete success and offspring survival do not vary by class. Thus the expected ratio is an experimental prediction, not a consequence of using the word testcross. A testcross can reveal a departure from independence because the tester permits those departures to remain visible.

Suppose a teaching experiment involving 240 testcross offspring yields 62 AaBb, 58 Aabb, 61 aaBb and 59 aabb. Equal frequencies predict 60 individuals in each class. The values are not identical to 60, but the differences are small and balanced. The marginal frequency of transmission of A is (62 + 58)/240 = 0.50. The marginal frequency of B is (62 + 61)/240 = 0.5125. The joint frequency of AB is 62/240, about 0.2583. These observed proportions are reasonably close to the model values of one-half and one-quarter. A goodness-of-fit test formalizes the comparison by weighting deviations relative to expected counts.

Now consider a different illustrative experiment using the same number of offspring: 96 AaBb, 24 Aabb, 24 aaBb and 96 aabb. Each of the two alleles at either locus is still transmitted to 120 of the 240 progeny. If we reduced the data to the two monohybrid marginal distributions, both would exhibit exactly 1:1 segregation. Yet the joint distribution is very different from the independent prediction. AB and ab occur four times as frequently as either Ab or aB. A linked model in coupling phase, with a recombination fraction of 0.20, gives expected proportions 0.40, 0.10, 0.10 and 0.40. The observation is compatible with this explanation, but the design must also control alternative causes, including differential survival.

We should distinguish the phase of the heterozygote from its written unphased genotype. The notation AaBb gives the alleles present at two loci but not their arrangement on homologues. When AB and ab are the two parental haplotypes, the arrangement is called coupling or cis phase. When Ab and aB are the parental haplotypes, the arrangement is repulsion or trans phase. In the latter case, the abundant testcross classes at a recombination fraction of 0.20 would be Aabb and aaBb instead of AaBb and aabb. Both arrangements have the same unphased AaBb genotype. At independent assortment the distributions converge to the same four equal frequencies, so phase no longer changes this two-locus probability prediction.

A tester can also be useful when the unknown parent is selected for its double dominant phenotype rather than known to be AaBb. There are four possible genotypes compatible with A_B_: AABB, AABb, AaBB and AaBb. With aabb as the other parent, an AABB individual produces only AaBb progeny. An AABb parent produces AaBb and Aabb in equal proportions. An AaBB parent produces AaBb and aaBb in equal proportions. An independently assorting AaBb parent produces all four classes with equal probability. This set of possibilities connects the testcross to genotype inference. The observation of an aa offspring proves that the dominant-phenotype parent supplied a, assuming parentage and ordinary transmission; the observation of a bb offspring similarly establishes transmission of b.

Absence of a class in a small sample is not a logical proof that a parent cannot produce it. Suppose eight offspring of a testcross are all double dominant. If the tested parent is actually AaBb with independent assortment, each offspring has probability one-quarter of being AaBb and the chance of eight such offspring is (1/4)^8 = 1/65,536. This is small but nonzero. If the tested parent is AABb, the corresponding probability is (1/2)^8 = 1/256, because every offspring is dominant at A but only half are dominant at B. If the tested parent is AABB, the probability is one under the simple model. Data of this kind provide evidence of varying strength for alternative genotypes. They do not turn probability into certainty when the sample is finite.

A testcross is a kind of backcross if the tester belongs to the parental generation of the hybrid, but the terms do not mean the same thing. A backcross involves mating a hybrid with an original parental genotype or a genetically comparable parental line. A testcross is designed to infer genotype or gamete transmission by crossing with an informative tester, commonly homozygous recessive. Backcrossing AaBb to AABB produces only dominant phenotypes under complete dominance, making the phenotypes less useful for distinguishing gametes. The same cross can become informative if molecular genotypes of the offspring are measured. The underlying concept of an informative cross is therefore more general than the visible phenotype-based classical design.

In interpreting a real cross, check whether the phenotype of a testcross offspring truly reveals the allele at the locus of interest. Incomplete penetrance, masking by other genes, an environmental effect, or misclassification can break the simple correspondence. Direct genotyping can sometimes resolve this ambiguity. For a controlled genetic experiment the question is not merely whether four categories exist, but whether each observed category is a valid indicator of a defined inherited combination. This principle prepares the way for later topics on gene interaction and linkage mapping.

6. Polyhybrid crosses and the forked-line method

The Punnett square is useful for connecting gametes to offspring genotypes, but its size grows rapidly with the number of independently assorting heterozygous loci. If each parent is heterozygous at three such loci, AaBbCc, it produces eight possible gamete genotypes. A self-cross has an 8 × 8 array of ordered gamete unions, containing 64 cells. For four heterozygous loci the array becomes 16 × 16, or 256 cells. As the number increases, manually enumerating each combination becomes both slow and susceptible to mistakes. The product rule offers an alternative that preserves the biological logic of segregation while avoiding unnecessary enumeration.

The forked-line method is a visual representation of the product rule. At the first locus, split the outcomes into the genotype or phenotype classes needed for the question. From each branch, draw the possible outcomes at the second locus, with their probabilities. Continue until every locus has been considered. The probability of any terminal path is the product of the probabilities along it, provided the loci are independently distributed in the offspring. If several terminal paths satisfy the question, add their probabilities only after ensuring that the paths are disjoint. The method is not a different genetic principle; it is an organized way to apply the same principle repeatedly.

For an AaBbCc × AaBbCc cross with complete dominance, consider the probability of A_B_cc. At the A locus, A_ occurs with probability 3/4. At B, B_ also occurs with probability 3/4. At C, cc occurs with probability 1/4. Their product equals 9/64. If the question instead requires the exact genotype AaBBcc, the corresponding factors are 1/2, 1/4 and 1/4, giving 1/32. The phenotype expression A_ includes two genotype outcomes, while Aa specifies only one. Confusing these events at even one locus can double or triple the numerical answer.

The forked-line representation helps to derive all eight phenotype classes of the trihybrid self-cross. With A_, B_ and C_ treated as dominant outcomes, the all-dominant class occurs with probability 27/64. The three classes with two dominant characters and a specified recessive character each occur with probability 9/64. The three classes with one specified dominant character and two recessive characters each occur with probability 3/64. The all-recessive class occurs with probability 1/64. The ratio, when grouped in this order, is 27:9:9:9:3:3:3:1. Its weights sum to 64. The counts of classes with identical weights arise from which loci are assigned a dominant or recessive outcome, not from the sequence in which they are written.

A question may request “exactly two dominant phenotypes” rather than a particular arrangement such as A_B_cc. There are three disjoint qualifying classes: A_B_cc, A_bbC_ and aaB_C_. Each has probability 9/64, so the combined probability equals 27/64. Notice that this equals the probability of the all-dominant class in this particular cross. That numerical equality does not mean the two events are the same. One event has three genotype-pattern categories, whereas the other has one broad phenotype category. It is often valuable to list the biological outcomes before simplifying fractions, especially when two different interpretations accidentally yield the same number.

The number of possible distinct offspring genotypes in an independent self-cross with n heterozygous loci is 3^n, because each locus has the three genotypes AA, Aa and aa, with analogous alternatives at other loci. The number of phenotype classes under complete dominance and no interaction is 2^n, because each locus has only two distinguishable outcomes. The number of gamete genotypes from each parent is also 2^n, but for a different reason: each heterozygous locus contributes one of its two alleles to a gamete. The number of ordered gamete unions is 4^n. These four quantities answer distinct questions. For n = 3 they are 27 genotype classes, 8 phenotype classes, 8 gamete genotypes per parent and 64 ordered gamete unions.

The rules change if the parental genotypes are not uniform heterozygotes. Consider the cross AaBBccDd × aaBbCcDD with four independently assorting loci. At A, the probabilities are Aa and aa, each one-half. At B, BB and Bb each occur with one-half probability, but all offspring have B_. At C, Cc and cc occur equally. At D, DD and Dd each occur equally and all are D_. There are two possible genotypes at each of four loci, giving 16 distinct joint genotype classes. There are only four phenotype classes because variation in dominant versus recessive expression occurs only at A and C. The probability of AaBbccDd is (1/2)^4 = 1/16. The probability of A_B_ccD_ is (1/2)(1)(1/2)(1) = 1/4. This example shows why one should construct a locus-by-locus table before invoking general counting formulas.

When one parent is homozygous at one or more loci, the number of gamete classes can differ between the parents. In the cross AABbCc × AaBbcc, the first parent varies at B and C and produces four gamete genotypes, whereas the second varies at A and B and also produces four. At A there are two possible zygote genotypes, AA and Aa; at B there are three; at C there are two. The cross therefore has twelve possible zygote genotypes although there are sixteen ordered gamete unions. The difference arises because some gamete pairs create the same zygote genotype, particularly at B. The probability of the genotype AaBbCc is (1/2)(1/2)(1/2) = 1/8. It occupies two of the sixteen ordered cells. Counting all possible distinct genotypes and treating them as equally probable would produce 1/12, an incorrect result.

Sometimes a question asks whether a new breeding line can be recovered from an F2 population. Suppose three desired dominant alleles A, B and C are initially carried by one true-breeding parent and the corresponding recessive alternatives by another. The F1 is AaBbCc. If the objective is to select an individual homozygous dominant at all three loci, the probability in an independently assorting F2 is (1/4)^3 = 1/64. The probability of a visually all-dominant individual is 27/64, much larger. A randomly selected all-dominant individual has probability (1/64)/(27/64) = 1/27 of being homozygous at all three loci. The fact that a phenotype is desirable does not imply that it will breed true. Progeny testing or genotyping is needed to determine which selected individuals possess the intended homozygous genotype.

If the target is a mixture of homozygous dominant and homozygous recessive genotypes, the same approach applies. In a self-cross of AaBbCcDd the probability of AAbbCCdd is (1/4)^4 = 1/256. The probability that an individual is homozygous at every locus, regardless of which allele is present, is instead (1/2)^4 = 1/16. There are 2^4 = 16 different fully homozygous genotype classes, each with probability 1/256, giving the same total. The distinction between a particular class and a collection of equivalent classes is crucial in calculating the number of offspring required to locate a desired individual.

For n loci each with independent dominant-versus-recessive outcomes of the same probabilities, we may group classes by how many loci display a given phenotype. The number of different ways to choose k loci from n is the combination coefficient written C(n,k). It is n! divided by k!(n−k)!, where factorial means the product of positive integers up to the given number and 0! is defined as one. In a self-cross of a tetrahybrid with complete dominance, the probability of exactly two recessive phenotypes is C(4,2)(1/4)^2(3/4)^2 = 6 × 9/256 = 54/256. The coefficient counts the six possible choices of the two recessive loci. It does not count how many genetically distinct heterozygous individuals exist within each phenotype class.

This grouping can be used only when the specified categories are equally probable across loci. If A has a recessive-phenotype probability of one-half because it is a testcross-like locus, B has one-quarter because both parents are heterozygous, and C has zero because a parent is homozygous dominant, then the probability of exactly one recessive phenotype cannot be calculated from a binomial expression using a single common probability. Instead enumerate the disjoint possibilities: aaB_C_ with probability (1/2)(3/4)(1) = 3/8 and A_bbC_ with probability (1/2)(1/4)(1) = 1/8. The total is 1/2. A possible-looking algebraic formula is not a substitute for checking the probabilities established by the actual cross.

With three or more loci it is also important to distinguish joint independence from pairwise independence. A set of variables is jointly independent if the probability of every combination factors into the corresponding marginal probabilities. Pairwise independence merely says that each pair satisfies the analogous relationship. For a simple mathematical example, four gamete classes ABC, Abc, aBc and abC each assigned probability one-quarter have equal allele marginals and pairwise equal combinations, yet the probability of ABC is one-quarter rather than one-eighth. This is an illustrative probability distribution, not a claim that such a distribution is generated by normal independent orientation of three bivalents. It shows why the assumption of independence among all loci must be stated when multiplying three or more factors.

In a genetic experiment with many markers, genotype classes can become too numerous to estimate each frequency precisely. A large sample is required if the aim is to observe rare combinations. The analytical value of the forked-line method is that it allows a predicted probability to be calculated without needing every class to appear in the actual experiment. The experimenter can then focus the sampling plan on the most informative categories rather than assuming that all possible outcomes will be equally represented.

7. Repeated offspring, binomial probability and sampling

The probability of an outcome in one offspring does not immediately tell us how many times that outcome will occur among several offspring. If a cross has probability one-quarter of producing aa, a family of four offspring could contain zero, one, two, three or four aa individuals. The expected number is one, but the actual count need not equal one. The distinction between a single-trial probability and the distribution of counts is the foundation of the binomial model. This model applies when we count the number of occurrences of a defined event among a fixed number of independent trials, all with the same probability of that event.

Let p denote the probability that an offspring has the target genotype or phenotype, and let q = 1 − p denote the probability that it does not. The event labelled success is simply the event being counted; the word does not imply that the phenotype is medically, evolutionarily or agriculturally desirable. A binomial trial has two mutually exclusive outcomes with probabilities p and q. If several biologically different phenotypes all count as “not the target”, they can be grouped together for this particular calculation. The grouping is valid because the question asks only whether the target event occurred, not which of the other outcomes occurred.

For n independent offspring, the probability of one particular ordered sequence containing exactly k target offspring and n−k nontarget offspring equals p^k q^(n−k). Every sequence with exactly k successes has this same probability when p remains constant. There are C(n,k) possible selections of the k positions occupied by target offspring. These sequences are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities add. The resulting formula is P(X = k) = C(n,k)p^k(1−p)^(n−k), where X is the random variable counting target offspring. The combinatorial factor is not needed when the order is specified in advance. It is needed when the same total can occur in different orders.

For example, if Aa × Aa produces aa with probability one-quarter, the probability that exactly two of four offspring are aa is C(4,2)(1/4)^2(3/4)^2 = 6 × 9/256 = 54/256, or approximately 0.21094. The probability that the first two offspring are aa and the next two are not aa is only 9/256, because this is one of the six sequences. A question that specifies a sequence rather than merely a count must not be multiplied by the combination coefficient.

We can calculate the full distribution for four offspring. The probabilities for k = 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 are respectively 81/256, 108/256, 54/256, 12/256 and 1/256. Their sum is 256/256 = 1. This is a useful validation test: the distribution covers every possible value of the count. The maximum probability occurs at one target offspring in this case, but even this most likely count has probability only about 0.422. Thus a prediction of a mean count of one does not imply that every family of four will contain one aa individual.

The binomial mean is E(X) = np. To see why, define an indicator for each trial that takes value one when the target occurs and zero otherwise. The expected value of each indicator is p, so the expected sum is np. This expectation is a long-run average across comparable groups, not necessarily an integer. The variance is np(1−p) when the trials are independent. Each indicator has variance p(1−p); independence makes the covariances between different indicators zero, allowing their variances to be added. The standard deviation is the square root of the variance and has units of counts. For n = 100 offspring with p = 1/4, the expected count is 25, variance 18.75 and standard deviation approximately 4.33. Differences of a few individuals from the expected count are ordinary sampling fluctuations.

Relative fluctuations decrease as the sample becomes larger. For the observed proportion X/n, the expected value is p and the standard deviation is the square root of p(1−p)/n. At n = 16 and p = 1/4, the standard deviation of the proportion is approximately 0.1083. At n = 1600 it is approximately 0.0108. The absolute count fluctuation grows with sample size, but the proportional fluctuation shrinks. This is why a genetic ratio is usually more clearly estimated from many independent offspring than from a handful. It also explains why a small difference in relative frequencies may be statistically detectable in a large experiment, whereas a seemingly large percentage difference can be uninformative in a small one.

Another common question asks for the probability of at least one target offspring. The complement consists of zero targets. If p is constant and trials are independent, P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − (1−p)^n. In a dihybrid self-cross the probability of aabb is 1/16. Among sixteen independently sampled offspring, the probability of at least one aabb is 1 − (15/16)^16, approximately 0.644. The expected number is one, but the probability of getting none is still approximately 0.356. Even in this perfectly Mendelian model, failing to recover a double recessive individual in a sample of sixteen is not an extraordinary result.

The complement also provides an efficient way to estimate the number of offspring required for a chosen chance of finding a rare class. Suppose a plant breeder wants at least a 95 percent chance of recovering one AABBCC individual from AaBbCc self-cross progeny. The target probability is 1/64. The requirement is 1 − (63/64)^n ≥ 0.95. Rearranging gives (63/64)^n ≤ 0.05. Taking natural logarithms yields n ln(63/64) ≤ ln(0.05). Since ln(63/64) is negative, division reverses the inequality. The required n is at least ln(0.05)/ln(63/64), approximately 190.22, so the minimum whole number of offspring is 191. A check by evaluating the complement at both 190 and 191 confirms the direction of rounding. A 95 percent chance is a planning criterion, not a guarantee.

When the target probability is extremely small, the approximation (1−p)^n ≈ exp(−np) can help develop intuition. For example, the probability of no occurrences when the expected count is about three is roughly exp(−3), approximately 0.05. Hence for a rare genotype one often needs a sample with expected count near three to have around a 95 percent chance of observing at least one. This is only an approximation to the exact binomial complement; for examination calculations with given probabilities, use the exact expression or check the approximation's accuracy. Importantly, the expected count needed for a high probability of detection is larger than one.

The binomial model assumes that each trial has a common probability and is independent of the other trials. In biological experiments, siblings may share environmental conditions, a maternal effect, a developmental environment or a batch-specific source of error. Seeds from the same plant may not behave as statistically interchangeable independent observations if there is substantial plant-to-plant variation in viability or transmission. Counting hundreds of offspring from one parent does not necessarily replace independent biological replicates when the question concerns variation among parents. The simple binomial model is a starting point under the stated assumptions, and departures from those assumptions must be considered before drawing conclusions from unusually variable counts.

Sampling from a finite group without replacement is another situation in which the constant independent probability model may fail. Imagine an already produced batch containing exactly ten recessive and thirty dominant seeds. If a seed is sampled and removed, the composition of the remaining batch changes. The next probability depends on the outcome of the first draw. This differs from making independent predictions for new offspring generated by the same cross. For small sampling fractions the dependence may be weak, but in a problem about drawing many seeds from a known finite batch, conditional probabilities should be applied rather than assuming independent Bernoulli trials.

A single binomial count may also discard information. In a dihybrid cross, counting only the double recessive individuals treats all other classes as equivalent. That is suitable for a recovery calculation, but it cannot detect all ways in which the full 9:3:3:1 distribution departs from expectation. If the four classes are important separately, the relevant distribution is multinomial: a fixed number of independent offspring fall into one of several mutually exclusive categories with given probabilities. The expected count of each class is still its probability multiplied by the total sample size. The class counts are not independent because their sum is fixed. This observation leads directly to the degrees of freedom used in chi-square analysis.

8. Conditional inference and genotype selection

Genetic probability is frequently used not only to predict offspring, but also to infer an unobserved genotype from visible evidence. A dominant phenotype is a classic example of incomplete information. A plant that looks A_ might be AA or Aa. The two possible genotypes are not necessarily equally likely, because their probabilities depend on the cross that produced the plant. In an Aa self-cross, one quarter of all offspring are AA and one half are Aa, so among dominant-phenotype offspring the probabilities are one third and two thirds respectively. A dominant-phenotype individual selected from a different parental cross may have an entirely different distribution of possible genotypes.

Suppose a randomly selected double dominant F2 plant comes from AaBb × AaBb under independent assortment. The prior probabilities within this selected group are 1/9 for AABB, 2/9 for AABb, 2/9 for AaBB and 4/9 for AaBb. The probabilities reflect the multiplicities of the joint genotypes, not an assumption that the four possibilities are equally probable. If the plant is then crossed to aabb, the probability that a single offspring is double recessive depends on which genotype was selected. Only AaBb can produce ab with nonzero probability in this group. Conditional on AaBb, the probability of aabb is 1/4. Averaging over the selected group's possibilities yields (4/9)(1/4) = 1/9. The same result can be derived by asking at each locus for the probability that a randomly selected dominant F2 individual transmits a recessive allele: (2/3)(1/2) = 1/3 at each locus, giving 1/9.

There is an important distinction if several offspring are obtained from the same selected plant. It is tempting to compute the chance of two double recessive offspring as (1/9)^2. This would be appropriate if each offspring came from a newly and independently selected dominant F2 parent, under the same model. But when both offspring come from the same parent, their probabilities share the same unknown genotype. The correct probability is P(parent is AaBb) × P(two double recessive offspring given AaBb) = (4/9)(1/4)^2 = 1/36, not 1/81. After observing a double recessive offspring, the parent is known to be AaBb under the assumed model, and the predicted probability of another double recessive offspring becomes 1/4. The evidence reveals information about a stable genetic state rather than changing the parent's meiosis.

The general framework behind this inference is Bayes' rule. If a genotype hypothesis H could produce an observed outcome D, then its probability after observing D is proportional to its prior probability multiplied by the probability of D under that hypothesis. In symbols, P(H given D) = P(D given H)P(H)/P(D). The denominator sums the joint probabilities over the possible mutually exclusive hypotheses. The rule is useful when evidence is compatible with more than one genotype and does not uniquely identify the parent.

For a simple one-locus example, choose an A_ individual randomly from an Aa × Aa cross. Its prior probabilities are 1/3 for AA and 2/3 for Aa. Cross it with aa and observe three offspring, all showing A_. If the tested parent is AA, all such offspring are dominant with probability one. If it is Aa, the probability of three dominant offspring is (1/2)^3 = 1/8. The joint probabilities for the evidence and parental genotypes are therefore (1/3)(1) = 1/3 and (2/3)(1/8) = 1/12. The total probability of the evidence is 5/12. Conditional on the evidence, the probability that the parent is Aa equals (1/12)/(5/12) = 1/5. The probability that the next offspring from the same parent will be recessive is then (1/5)(1/2) = 1/10.

The observation of dominant offspring increases support for the AA hypothesis but does not logically exclude Aa. If even one recessive offspring is observed, the AA hypothesis has probability zero under the specified ordinary segregation model; the parent must be Aa. However, biological interpretation in an actual experiment must consider whether all assumptions are justified, including verified parentage, phenotype scoring and the absence of mutation or other mechanisms that could generate a misleading phenotype. A probability problem often states these assumptions implicitly, while a scientific investigation must make them explicit.

The same conditional reasoning is important when offspring are counted only after a selection stage. Suppose an illustrative dihybrid self-cross has the expected zygote proportions 9:3:3:1, but aabb offspring do not survive to the scoring stage. Among all zygotes the double dominant probability is 9/16. Among surviving offspring the denominator is 15/16, since the double recessive class has been removed. The surviving class probabilities are 9/15, 3/15 and 3/15, or 3:1:1. The absence of double recessives does not mean that ab gametes were never formed. The genotype was present at fertilization under the model but was absent from the observed stage. The distinction between a gametic defect and a postzygotic viability effect can be investigated by scoring earlier developmental stages or using genotypic markers.

If survival probabilities differ but are not zero, each original class probability must be weighted by its survival probability and then normalized. Suppose the four zygote classes have probabilities 9/16, 3/16, 3/16 and 1/16 and survival probabilities 1, 1, 1 and 1/2 respectively. The unnormalized frequencies among initial zygotes that survive are 9/16, 3/16, 3/16 and 1/32. Their sum is 31/32. Dividing each by 31/32 gives surviving frequencies 18/31, 6/31, 6/31 and 1/31, a ratio of 18:6:6:1. This example separates the genetic prediction at fertilization from the observation after development. The same mathematical procedure can be applied to differential detection or deliberate selection, provided the relevant probabilities are correctly specified.

A selected sample can produce an association between two variables even if they were independent in the original population. For example, among all F2 offspring the events aa and bb are independent under the standard model. If we select only offspring with at least one recessive phenotype, the remaining sample contains aaB_, A_bb and aabb. Their relative weights are 3, 3 and 1. Within this subset the probability of aa is 4/7 and the probability of bb is 4/7, but the joint probability is 1/7 rather than 16/49. Selection has changed the sample space and the probabilities. This is why independence must always refer to a particular population and observation stage. It is not a label permanently attached to a pair of traits regardless of how the observations were collected.

A final useful habit is to distinguish forward prediction from reverse inference. Forward prediction starts with a given genotype and asks about offspring. Reverse inference starts with observed offspring and asks about a genotype or a model. The first uses the laws of segregation and the probability rules directly. The second may require conditional probability, likelihood comparison or statistical testing. Knowing that a particular genotype can produce an observed pattern is not enough to show that it is the only compatible genotype. A strong explanation identifies alternatives and asks what additional evidence would distinguish them.

9. From Mendelian ratios to expected counts

The purpose of a goodness-of-fit analysis is to compare what a specified genetic model predicts with what a controlled experiment actually yields. Before any statistical calculation, we must identify the categories in which the data were collected. In a standard dihybrid self-cross with complete dominance, the four phenotypic classes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Every offspring included in the analysis belongs to exactly one of the four classes. If the experiment instead records molecular genotypes, the categories may be more numerous. If only one marker has been scored, the data contain fewer categories. The statistical model must match the actual observations.

A theoretical ratio such as 9:3:3:1 is a relative weight, not a list of expected counts for every experiment. To convert it into probabilities, divide each weight by the sum of all weights. For a sample containing N scored offspring, multiply each probability by N. For the four classes in the dihybrid self-cross, the expected counts are therefore 9N/16, 3N/16, 3N/16 and N/16. They necessarily sum to N, as long as the ratio accounts for all scored categories. The observed counts also sum to N because the categories form a partition of the sample. This conservation of the total plays an important role in determining the degrees of freedom of the test.

Suppose a class is theoretically absent. For example, an AABB × aabb cross under the ordinary model predicts that every offspring will be AaBb. There is no expected recessive phenotype class. If an apparently recessive offspring is observed, one cannot substitute a zero expected count into a formula that divides by expectation. Instead, the observation directly conflicts with a model that assigns that class probability zero. Before rejecting the biological mechanism itself, check the assumptions and the possibility of incorrect parentage, misclassification, mutation or another mechanism not included in the simple model. A zero-probability outcome and a rare but nonzero-probability outcome are conceptually distinct.

When predicting expected counts from a ratio, do not estimate the ratio from the very observations being tested unless that fitting step is incorporated into the statistical procedure. For example, if the null hypothesis is a specified 1:1 testcross, the expected counts are N/2 and N/2. If we instead use the observed proportions to define the expected counts, every expected value will equal its observed value and no discrepancy can be detected. Such a calculation merely restates the data. More sophisticated goodness-of-fit procedures allow some model parameters to be estimated from the observations, but the number of independent fitted parameters must be reflected in the degrees of freedom.

The difference O−E, where O denotes observed count and E denotes expected count, is called a residual. A positive residual means the observed class exceeds expectation; a negative residual means it falls short. Because observed and expected totals are equal, the residuals sum to zero. A direct sum of the residuals would therefore always be zero and cannot quantify the overall departure from the model. Squaring the residuals prevents positive and negative departures from cancelling. Weighting by the expected count allows discrepancies in classes of different expected sizes to be compared on a scale related to sampling variability.

Consider an illustrative F2 sample of 160 offspring with counts 94, 29, 28 and 9. The expected counts under independent assortment and complete dominance are 90, 30, 30 and 10. The residuals are +4, −1, −2 and −1, which sum to zero. The largest absolute discrepancy belongs to the double dominant class, but the relative importance of a difference is not determined by absolute size alone. A discrepancy of five in a class expected to contain 100 is quite different from a discrepancy of five in a class expected to contain five. The expected frequency therefore matters in the statistical weighting.

Sampling variation in a multinomial distribution can be understood by examining one class at a time. If the probability of class i is p_i and N independent trials are performed, the count of that class has a binomial marginal distribution with mean Np_i and variance Np_i(1−p_i). The counts of different classes cannot be independent, because a particular offspring contributes to one class and not the others. Their sum is fixed. Pearson's goodness-of-fit statistic uses the expected counts in its denominators and has a useful approximate chi-square distribution under its null assumptions. It is not simply a sum of independent squared binomial z scores; the shared total imposes a dependence structure. This is why the number of categories alone is not the number of independent deviations.

The distinction between an expected proportion and an expected count is crucial. If the sample has observed proportions 0.55, 0.20, 0.18 and 0.07, a comparison to 9/16, 3/16, 3/16 and 1/16 can describe the relative discrepancies. But a goodness-of-fit statistic based only on those proportions, with the sample size omitted, does not reflect the strength of the evidence. The same proportions could arise in a small experiment with modest fluctuations or in a very large experiment with highly reproducible differences. If the observed proportions are written as f_i, the statistic based on counts can equivalently be expressed as N multiplied by the sum of (f_i−p_i)^2/p_i. The factor N is indispensable.

For example, take a hypothetical testcross distribution with four equal expected probabilities and observed proportions 0.30, 0.20, 0.20 and 0.30. At N = 100, the observed counts are 30, 20, 20 and 30, and the expected counts are 25 each. The four terms of the chi-square statistic each equal one, giving a total of four. At N = 1,000 with the same proportions, the observed counts are 300, 200, 200 and 300 and expectations are 250 each. Each term now equals ten, giving a total of forty. The relative size of the genetic departure is identical, but the evidence against the exact null ratio is stronger in the larger independent sample. Statistical detectability and biological effect size are therefore different properties.

A goodness-of-fit test does not determine which biological explanation is true merely by detecting a departure. A systematic excess of particular classes can suggest a mechanistic hypothesis, but that hypothesis needs its own predictions and controls. It is helpful to retain the signed residuals alongside the total statistic. Two different patterns can have the same total squared discrepancy. One may show an excess of parental allele combinations and a shortage of recombinant classes, whereas another may show a deficiency of all offspring expressing a particular recessive character. The first pattern suggests linkage as a candidate; the second may suggest viability or scoring effects. The numerical test summarizes fit, while the residual pattern preserves information needed for biological interpretation.

10. Pearson chi-square goodness-of-fit analysis

Karl Pearson introduced the chi-square goodness-of-fit approach in 1900. In classical genetics its value is that it transforms the discrepancy between a set of observed counts and a set of model-predicted counts into a single statistic that can be compared with a known reference distribution. This permits a distinction between deviations that are quite plausible under sampling variation and deviations that would be unusual if the specified model were true. It does not require the observed counts to match their predictions exactly, and it does not assign a probability that a genetic law is true.

The first step is to state the null hypothesis. For a conventional independent dihybrid self-cross, a suitable null hypothesis is that the probabilities of the four scored phenotypic classes are 9/16, 3/16, 3/16 and 1/16. This includes more than independent assortment: it also incorporates normal segregation, the assumed parental genotypes, complete dominance, the absence of phenotype-modifying interaction and equal recovery of the classes. An alternative hypothesis for an omnibus goodness-of-fit test is simply that at least one class probability differs from the specified values. It need not identify a single alternative genetic mechanism.

Pearson's statistic is χ² = Σ (O_i−E_i)²/E_i, summed over all k categories having positive expected counts. The symbol O_i denotes the observed count in category i and E_i denotes its model-predicted expected count. Both quantities must be expressed as counts rather than percentages. The numerator is a squared count discrepancy and the denominator is a count; as a mathematical statistic evaluated on frequencies it is dimensionless. If all observations exactly match their expectations, the statistic is zero. As discrepancies grow relative to the expected counts, its value increases. It cannot be negative.

For a model with k categories whose probabilities are fully specified in advance and one fixed total N, the usual degrees of freedom are k−1. To understand the subtraction, suppose four category counts are recorded. Once the total and any three counts are known, the fourth count is determined. There are therefore only three independent count deviations. If a regular model contains m independent parameters estimated from the same data, the usual asymptotic degrees of freedom become k−1−m, subject to the assumptions of the fitting procedure. In the standard examination problem with a prespecified Mendelian ratio, m = 0. A four-class 9:3:3:1 or 1:1:1:1 goodness-of-fit test has three degrees of freedom; a two-class 3:1 test has one.

Choose a significance level α before inspecting the data if making a formal decision. A frequently used level in teaching problems is 0.05. Under the idealized null model, this means that the decision procedure is designed to reject the null about five percent of the time in repeated comparable experiments when the null is actually correct, subject to the accuracy of the reference distribution. This is a Type I error rate. It does not mean that five percent of the observed offspring must be classified as wrong, or that the chance the null is false equals 95 percent. A different α may be justified in a different experimental context.

The chi-square goodness-of-fit test uses the upper tail. Under the null, a large discrepancy statistic is evidence against the predicted distribution. At three degrees of freedom and α = 0.05, the approximate critical value is 7.815. A statistic greater than this value would be rejected at that significance level. At one degree of freedom, the corresponding critical value is approximately 3.841. The reference distribution changes with degrees of freedom because more categories generally allow more independent directions of departure from the model. Comparing a four-class statistic with a one-degree-of-freedom cutoff is an incorrect use of the table.

The p-value is the probability, assuming the null model and reference-distribution conditions hold, of obtaining a chi-square statistic at least as large as the one observed. This is a probability of a range of possible data-derived statistics, conditional on the hypothesis. It is not the probability of the hypothesis conditional on the data. If p is less than the prespecified α, we reject the null. If p is greater than or equal to α, we fail to reject it, using a consistent convention for equality. In either case the conclusion should be expressed in relation to the data, the specific model and the assumptions. Failure to reject does not prove that all loci are independently assorting or that every biological assumption holds.

We can now examine Mendel's historical two-character data. The original report records 556 seeds in four classes: round yellow 315, round green 108, wrinkled yellow 101 and wrinkled green 32. Under the specified 9:3:3:1 model, the expected counts are 312.75, 104.25, 104.25 and 34.75. The first contribution is (315−312.75)²/312.75, approximately 0.01619. The second is (108−104.25)²/104.25, approximately 0.13489. The third is (101−104.25)²/104.25, approximately 0.10132. The fourth is (32−34.75)²/34.75, approximately 0.21763. Their sum is approximately 0.47002. The nominal degrees of freedom are three and the statistic is far below the 5 percent critical value of 7.815.

If the historical observations are treated as a multinomial sample satisfying the model assumptions, the upper-tail probability for this statistic is about 0.925. There is no evidence against the specified ratio on this particular test. The calculation is a modern instructional analysis of the published counts, not a claim that Mendel performed Pearson's later statistical test. The seeds came from multiple plants, so a full modern reanalysis of the historical experimental design would also consider the sampling structure. A high goodness-of-fit p-value cannot establish how the observations were collected, or prove that the biological mechanism was uniquely determined. This distinction respects both the historical evidence and the limits of the statistical model.

For a contrasting hypothetical testcross, take observed counts 96, 24, 24 and 96 in the classes corresponding to AB, Ab, aB and ab. The equal-frequency null expects 60 in each class. Every absolute residual is 36, so each term equals 1296/60 = 21.6, and the total statistic is 86.4. At three degrees of freedom this is far above 7.815. The independent equal-frequency prediction is rejected at α = 0.05. Because the reciprocal parental combinations are enriched and reciprocal recombinant combinations depleted, a linkage model with a recombination fraction near 0.20 is a reasonable next hypothesis. However, a conclusion about physical linkage must still be supported by the cross design and the absence of other explanations.

Consider a second illustrative testcross with counts 62, 58, 61 and 59, again with an expected count of 60 in each class. The contributions are 4/60, 4/60, 1/60 and 1/60, giving χ² = 10/60 = 1/6, approximately 0.167. This is also below the critical value at three degrees of freedom. The appropriate statement is that the observed variation is not sufficient to reject the equal-frequency null at the chosen level. It would be inaccurate to say that the data establish exactly 25 percent transmission of every gamete type in the population. The observed frequencies are estimates, and their uncertainty remains even when the null is not rejected.

A common error is to calculate the statistic from the raw ratio values as if they were observed or expected counts. A ratio of 1:1:1:1 specifies equal probabilities, not an expectation of one offspring in every class. Another error is to divide by the observed count rather than expected count. A third is to round fractional expectations and thereby alter the model. The correct calculation begins with counts derived from N and prespecified probabilities. It retains precision through the sum and rounds only the reported statistic. An examination solution can be checked quickly by verifying that both totals equal N and that all contributions are nonnegative.

The chi-square reference distribution is an approximation when applied to discrete count data. Its accuracy can be poor when expected counts are small. The familiar introductory recommendation that expected counts should be at least five per class is a practical guideline rather than an absolute theorem covering every possible setting. In a 9:3:3:1 cross, the rarest class has expectation N/16. To reach five in that class requires N ≥ 80. For a sample of only 32 offspring the rarest expectation is two; a routine large-sample chi-square p-value may be unreliable. The concern is the expected count, not whether a particular observed count is below five. A class with zero observed individuals and a sufficiently large expected count can contribute legitimately to the statistic.

For small samples, an exact binomial test may be used when the problem has two classes. If the target probability is known, the binomial distribution gives probabilities of every possible count without appealing to a continuous approximation. For multiple categories an exact multinomial goodness-of-fit calculation or an appropriately designed simulation under the null can be considered. These methods evaluate the distribution of a discrepancy under the specified discrete model. A student need not learn specialist algorithms here, but should recognize why a small expected count undermines the usual chi-square approximation. Combining classes is defensible only if the resulting categories address the biological hypothesis; combining them simply to satisfy an approximation rule may remove precisely the information needed to test linkage or gene interaction.

A failure to reject can arise because the null is close to the true distribution, because the sample is too small to detect an alternative, or because a particular categorization has discarded informative variation. The probability of failing to reject a false null under a specified alternative is a Type II error; power is one minus that probability. Increasing the number of independent observations generally improves the ability to detect a fixed departure. However, if the underlying experimental design is biased, increasing sample size can provide more precise evidence of a biased distribution rather than recover the intended genetic mechanism. Quality of design and quantity of observations are complementary, not interchangeable.

11. Experimental interpretation and competing genetic models

A strong genetics experiment is designed so that the observations discriminate between plausible mechanisms. The independent assortment model predicts a specific distribution of gametes from a double heterozygote. A linked model with normal segregation can predict the same marginal allele frequencies but different joint frequencies. A viability model can generate the expected gametes yet alter the distribution of surviving offspring. A phenotype interaction model can leave the underlying genotype distribution unchanged while merging several genotypes into a different set of visible categories. Because these explanations can all affect an observed ratio, the design of the cross and the stage at which observations are made become part of the reasoning.

Start by asking what is actually measured. A phenotype scored at maturity reflects inheritance, gene expression, development and survival. A DNA marker scored in an early embryo provides more direct information about the inherited genotype, but only for individuals that are recovered at that stage. A genotype scored in gametes can separate transmission from later survival more directly, although sampling or measurement bias may still occur. In classical pea experiments, clear seed phenotypes made it possible to count large numbers of offspring without modern molecular markers. Modern genotyping can extend the same logical framework to alleles that do not have easily distinguishable visible effects.

A controlled cross should use parental lines whose genotype assumptions are supported by previous breeding observations or molecular evidence. The F1 should be verified as heterozygous at the relevant loci. The scoring scheme should be defined before the count is examined, with criteria that distinguish the phenotypes reliably. If ambiguous individuals exist, excluding them without recording their number can bias the final proportions. Blinded scoring, independent replicate crosses and explicit reporting of missing observations help distinguish a true biological departure from a measurement artefact. These are general features of experimental reasoning, not additional Mendelian laws.

Suppose an investigator records a two-marker testcross with equal marginal A:a and B:b proportions but an excess of AB and ab. A first explanatory hypothesis is physical linkage in the heterozygote's coupling phase. A second is that individuals carrying Ab or aB combinations are less likely to survive to scoring. The two hypotheses can predict the same distribution at maturity. To distinguish them, compare the allele combinations at an earlier stage with the mature distribution, or use directly informative gametic markers where possible. If the early distribution is already skewed, a transmission-related explanation is supported. If the early distribution is equal but the later distribution changes, differential recovery after fertilization becomes a stronger explanation.

Reciprocal crosses provide another useful contrast. In one cross the double heterozygote provides pollen or sperm; in the reciprocal it provides the egg-bearing contribution. Under a simple autosomal independent assortment model with identical transmission and recovery in the two directions, their expected testcross proportions agree. A difference between the two directions motivates questions about sex-specific recombination, gamete-specific selection, maternal influences or other biological asymmetries. It is not correct to infer maternal inheritance merely because two reciprocal crosses differ. The differences provide a clue; the alternative mechanisms must be tested. Detailed sex-linked and extrachromosomal inheritance are considered later in this subunit.

The relationship between goodness of fit and a test of statistical independence needs particular care. A four-class testcross can be represented as a 2 × 2 table of transmitted A versus a and B versus b. A goodness-of-fit test against four equal probabilities tests both equal segregation at each locus and the absence of association. A chi-square test of independence applied to the same two-way table tests whether the joint distribution factors into the marginal distributions estimated from the table. It does not independently require those margins to be 1:1. For a 2 × 2 table its usual degrees of freedom are (2−1)(2−1) = 1, whereas the goodness-of-fit test against four equal predetermined probabilities has three. The two tests answer different null hypotheses and need not reach identical conclusions.

For a hypothetical example, consider testcross counts AB = 42, Ab = 28, aB = 18 and ab = 12. The total is 100. The margins are A = 70, a = 30, B = 60 and b = 40. The observed joint frequencies exactly equal the products of the observed marginal proportions: 0.7 × 0.6 = 0.42, 0.7 × 0.4 = 0.28, 0.3 × 0.6 = 0.18 and 0.3 × 0.4 = 0.12. The observed data show no association in the table. Yet the observed frequencies are not those predicted by a standard Mendelian equal-gamete testcross, since the margins depart from one-half. The equal-frequency goodness-of-fit statistic is (42−25)²/25 + (28−25)²/25 + (18−25)²/25 + (12−25)²/25 = 20.64. The equality prediction is rejected at three degrees of freedom. This example demonstrates why equal segregation and independence must not be treated as the same statistical hypothesis.

In a dihybrid self-cross under complete dominance, a 2 × 2 table can also be made from the two phenotype outcomes at each locus. The expected joint frequencies follow from the fixed 3/4 and 1/4 marginal probabilities. A test of association between observed phenotype categories is not necessarily a direct test of gametic independence because the phenotypes combine information from two parental gametes and may be altered by expression or viability. The testcross is preferred in many classical linkage problems precisely because, under its assumptions, each progeny class identifies a gamete from the heterozygote. The informative nature of the measurement is part of the biological argument.

The next node examines allelic relationships in which complete dominance is not the correct expression model. A useful preview shows why such departures do not necessarily invalidate independent assortment. Suppose A has incomplete dominance, so AA, Aa and aa can all be distinguished, while B shows complete dominance. In an independently assorting AaBb self-cross the A phenotype probabilities are 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4, and the B phenotype probabilities are 3/4 and 1/4. The six joint phenotype probabilities are 3/16, 1/16, 6/16, 2/16, 3/16 and 1/16 when ordered by A genotype with dominant and recessive B within each. There are six categories rather than four, but the transmission mechanism can still be fully Mendelian. The expected ratio follows from the expression map as well as the segregation model.

Similarly, a gene interaction can produce an altered phenotype ratio even with independent segregation. If two distinct genotype classes generate the same visible phenotype because the products of two genes participate in one pathway, their probabilities must be added. A later node will analyze these modified ratios mechanistically. Here the key idea is that the sample space of genotype outcomes is not necessarily the same as the sample space of observed phenotypes. If a question supplies a genotype-to-phenotype rule, apply that rule after computing the genotype probabilities. Do not assume that any departure from 9:3:3:1 must indicate linkage. Linkage changes joint transmission probabilities; gene interaction changes the assignment of genotypes to phenotypes.

Finally, the scientific relevance of independent assortment extends beyond calculating ratios. It explains how combinations of inherited variants can be reorganized across generations and why a cross can recover combinations not present in either parental phenotype. In breeding experiments, it permits the prediction of recovery frequencies when desired alleles are transmitted independently. In genetics research, it provides a baseline model against which linked markers and distorted transmission can be detected. In examination reasoning, it teaches a general strategy: specify the biological mechanism, identify the probability distribution it predicts, calculate the expected observations, and then decide what the evidence supports. This strategy remains valid even when subsequent chapters introduce more complex expression and inheritance patterns.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Dominance means higher transmission.

Correct concept: Dominance describes expression in heterozygotes; an ordinary Aa parent passes A and a equally.

Why it is tempting: The word dominant sounds like a reproductive advantage.

Examination reasoning: A question may ask whether dominant alleles occupy more gametes. Distinguish phenotype from segregation.

2. Every meiosis of a dihybrid generates all four gametes.

Correct concept: In the no-marker-exchange illustration, each orientation produces two complementary types; equal frequencies arise across many divisions.

Why it is tempting: Punnett squares display the pooled gamete distribution.

Examination reasoning: Draw the bivalents for one cell before predicting the individual products.

3. A haploid gamete can be Aa.

Correct concept: Normal haploid products have one allele per autosomal locus.

Why it is tempting: Students copy diploid genotype symbols into gamete lists.

Examination reasoning: Count one allele at every locus in each candidate gamete.

4. Two 1:1 marginals prove independent assortment.

Correct concept: Joint gamete frequencies must factor into marginal probabilities.

Why it is tempting: Separate monohybrid tables hide the combinations.

Examination reasoning: Compare P(AB) with P(A)P(B), not just the marginals.

5. All dihybrid crosses give 9:3:3:1.

Correct concept: The ratio requires two double heterozygotes, independent loci, complete dominance and equal recovery.

Why it is tempting: The ratio is memorized without its cross.

Examination reasoning: Write both parents and derive the one-locus distributions first.

6. A testcross causes equal gametic frequencies.

Correct concept: It reveals gamete transmission if the tester and scoring are informative.

Why it is tempting: The theoretical testcross ratio is treated as a property of the tester.

Examination reasoning: Check whether linkage or biased recovery changes the offspring distribution.

7. Add probabilities whenever the word or appears.

Correct concept: Subtract overlapping events unless they are disjoint.

Why it is tempting: Ordinary language obscures inclusive versus exclusive or.

Examination reasoning: List the shared aabb class before computing a union.

8. Multiply probabilities whenever and appears.

Correct concept: Use conditional multiplication; the simple product requires independence.

Why it is tempting: The mnemonic omits its assumption.

Examination reasoning: Check linkage, selection and the specified sample space.

9. A dominant F2 is heterozygous with probability 1/2.

Correct concept: Among dominant offspring of Aa × Aa, the fraction is 2/3.

Why it is tempting: The unconditional 1/2 is carried over to a selected subset.

Examination reasoning: Divide by P(A_)=3/4.

10. Sixteen possible Punnett-square cells mean sixteen distinct genotypes.

Correct concept: The ordered unions can yield the same genotype; a dihybrid self-cross has nine.

Why it is tempting: Cells are mistaken for genotype categories.

Examination reasoning: Combine reciprocal gamete unions producing the same genotype.

11. The mean count is the count that must occur.

Correct concept: A finite sample has a distribution of possible counts.

Why it is tempting: The theoretical ratio is interpreted as a deterministic schedule.

Examination reasoning: Compute binomial probabilities or variability rather than asserting exact counts.

12. No rare offspring means the genotype is impossible.

Correct concept: A nonzero-probability class can be absent by chance.

Why it is tempting: Small samples make missing classes seem definitive.

Examination reasoning: Evaluate (1−p)^n and investigate alternative explanations.

13. A large p-value proves the null model.

Correct concept: It indicates insufficient evidence to reject under the test assumptions.

Why it is tempting: The p-value is confused with the probability that the hypothesis is true.

Examination reasoning: State the hypothesis, sample size, power limitations and conditional meaning.

14. The chi-square guideline refers to observed counts.

Correct concept: The usual small-cell concern concerns expected frequencies.

Why it is tempting: Students inspect the smallest observed number rather than the model.

Examination reasoning: Calculate every E first; consider an exact method if sparse.

15. df is always the number of categories.

Correct concept: For a fixed-ratio goodness-of-fit test with k positive categories, df=k−1; fitting parameters can reduce it further.

Why it is tempting: The fixed total constraint is overlooked.

Examination reasoning: Count independent deviations and distinguish association tests.

16. A recombination fraction of one-half proves separate chromosomes.

Correct concept: Distant markers on one chromosome can appear independent.

Why it is tempting: The inverse of the independent-chromosome prediction is assumed.

Examination reasoning: Do not infer physical location solely from a two-locus fraction.

17. A distorted phenotype ratio necessarily proves linkage.

Correct concept: Gene interaction, penetrance, recovery and scoring can also alter ratios.

Why it is tempting: Visible phenotypes are equated with gametic genotypes.

Examination reasoning: Compare informative crosses or direct genotype evidence across stages.

Original examination practice

These original questions use the stated Mendelian models and illustrative datasets; they are not reproduced previous-year questions. Unless otherwise stated, assume regular diploid meiosis, independent assortment, random fertilization, complete dominance, equal viability and independently sampled offspring.

Q1. Part B

Independent assortment is a claim about which relationship?

  1. Dominant alleles always being transmitted more often
  2. Transmission at one locus not determining transmission at another
  3. Sister chromatids separating at meiosis I
  4. All phenotypes being equally frequent
Answer and explanation

B. Transmission at one locus not determining transmission at another — Independent assortment concerns joint allele transmission across loci. Dominance affects expression, not transmission; homologues normally separate at meiosis I, and phenotype classes can have unequal probabilities.

Q2. Part B

An AaBbCc parent has independent assortment. How many different haploid gamete genotypes are possible?

  1. 3
  2. 6
  3. 8
  4. 27
Answer and explanation

C. 8 — Each of the three heterozygous loci contributes two alternative alleles, so 2³ = 8. Twenty-seven counts diploid genotype classes in a trihybrid self-cross, not gametes.

Q3. Part B

Which statement about normal gametes from AABbcc is false?

  1. ABc is possible
  2. Abc is possible
  3. Every gamete contains A and c
  4. aBc is possible
Answer and explanation

D. aBc is possible — Only A is available at the A locus and only c at C; B or b is possible. The allele a is absent, so aBc cannot be generated under the stated model.

Q4. Part B

Which meiotic event supplies the physical basis of assortment of different chromosome pairs?

  1. Independent relative orientation of bivalents at metaphase I
  2. DNA synthesis during S phase
  3. Separation of homologues in mitotic anaphase
  4. Translation of dominant alleles
Answer and explanation

A. Independent relative orientation of bivalents at metaphase I — Bivalent orientations determine the combinations of homologues that enter daughter cells at anaphase I. Replication and translation do not choose the relative orientations.

Q5. Part B

A and a are mutually exclusive in a normal haploid gamete. If each has probability 1/2, P(A or a) is:

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/2
  3. 3/4
  4. 1
Answer and explanation

D. 1 — They exhaust the possible alleles and have no overlap: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Multiplying gives an impossible joint event, not the union.

Q6. Part B

In an independent AaBb self-cross with complete dominance, P(aaB_) is:

  1. 1/16
  2. 3/16
  3. 1/4
  4. 9/16
Answer and explanation

B. 3/16 — At A, aa has probability 1/4; at B, B_ has probability 3/4. Their product is 3/16. The 9/16 class is double dominant.

Q7. Part B

In the same cross, how many distinct two-locus diploid genotype classes exist?

  1. 4
  2. 8
  3. 9
  4. 16
Answer and explanation

C. 9 — Each locus has three genotype classes, and their products give nine. Sixteen is the number of ordered gamete unions; four counts phenotypes under complete dominance.

Q8. Part B

AaBb × aabb gives which phenotype ratio if both loci assort independently and dominance is complete?

  1. 1:1:1:1
  2. 9:3:3:1
  3. 3:1
  4. 1:2:1
Answer and explanation

A. 1:1:1:1 — The tester contributes only ab, so the four equally probable gametes of the double heterozygote generate four distinguishable classes. The 9:3:3:1 ratio applies to two double heterozygotes.

Q9. Part B

Which probability rule is valid for any events E and F with P(E)>0?

  1. P(E and F) = P(E)+P(F)
  2. P(E and F) = P(E)P(F) in every case
  3. P(E and F) = P(E)P(F given E)
  4. P(E and F) = 1−P(E)
Answer and explanation

C. P(E and F) = P(E)P(F given E) — The conditional multiplication rule is general. The simpler product P(E)P(F) is valid when the events are independent.

Q10. Part B

An Aa × Aa individual is selected among dominant offspring. The chance it is Aa is:

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/2
  4. 2/3
Answer and explanation

D. 2/3 — Divide the heterozygous probability 1/2 by the probability 3/4 of being dominant. One-half is the unconditional probability, and one-third is the conditional AA probability.

Q11. Part B

For a specified 9:3:3:1 null with four positive-probability categories, degrees of freedom equal:

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

C. 3 — With a fixed total there are three independently varying counts. A 2 × 2 association test has one degree of freedom, but it is not this fully specified goodness-of-fit test.

Q12. Part B

For 96 offspring under a 9:3:3:1 null, the expected double recessive count is:

  1. 6
  2. 9
  3. 16
  4. 24
Answer and explanation

A. 6 — The rare class has probability 1/16, so 96/16 = 6. Twenty-four is a quarter of the sample and corresponds to one recessive marginal, not both.

Q13. Part B

What is the expected number of aabb offspring in 160 independent AaBb self-cross progeny?

  1. 5
  2. 10
  3. 30
  4. 90
Answer and explanation

B. 10 — Multiply 160 by 1/16. Thirty is the expected count of either specified single-recessive class; ninety is the double-dominant expectation.

Q14. Part B

For chi-square goodness of fit, which denominator is used for each squared discrepancy?

  1. Observed count
  2. Total sample size only
  3. Number of categories
  4. Expected count
Answer and explanation

D. Expected count — Each term is (O−E)²/E. The expected count is prescribed by the null distribution; dividing by the observed count yields a different statistic.

Q15. Part B

A chi-square statistic of zero means:

  1. Observed counts match expected counts exactly
  2. All possible hypotheses are true
  3. The sample size must be infinite
  4. The probability of the null is zero
Answer and explanation

A. Observed counts match expected counts exactly — Every nonnegative contribution is zero if and only if O equals E in each positive-expected category. This observation does not prove the model.

Q16. Part B

Which is a correct interpretation of a p-value of 0.20?

  1. The null has probability 0.20 of being true
  2. 20% of the offspring are incorrectly scored
  3. Under the null, a statistic at least this large has probability about 0.20
  4. There is a 20% probability of making any mistake
Answer and explanation

C. Under the null, a statistic at least this large has probability about 0.20 — The p-value is a tail probability conditional on the null, not a posterior probability of the null or a fraction of erroneous data.

Q17. Part B

Which condition is needed for an ordinary binomial model of offspring counts?

  1. Exactly one success in every group
  2. Independent trials with a constant event probability
  3. All possible phenotypes equally frequent
  4. Every observed count equals its expectation
Answer and explanation

B. Independent trials with a constant event probability — The binomial counts occurrences of a two-outcome event across a fixed number of independent trials with common p. It permits unequal success and failure probabilities and random counts.

Q18. Part B

If p = 1/4, the probability of zero successes in four independent offspring is:

  1. 1/256
  2. 54/256
  3. 108/256
  4. 81/256
Answer and explanation

D. 81/256 — All four trials must fail, so (3/4)^4 = 81/256. The other listed values are probabilities of different counts.

Q19. Part B

Which result can occur even when each of two loci individually segregates 1:1?

  1. Linkage producing unequal joint gamete classes
  2. Only a 9:3:3:1 phenotype distribution
  3. No possible recombinant offspring
  4. A failure of all meiosis
Answer and explanation

A. Linkage producing unequal joint gamete classes — Linked loci may have fair marginal segregation but correlated joint transmission. Complete linkage can eliminate recombinants in the simple model, but it is not required for unequal joint classes.

Q20. Part B

What does a recombination fraction near 0.50 show about two markers?

  1. They are necessarily on different chromosomes
  2. They must occupy the same locus
  3. Their two-locus transmission can behave as independent
  4. Their dominant alleles are always beneficial
Answer and explanation

C. Their two-locus transmission can behave as independent — A fraction near one-half is compatible with different chromosomes or distant loci on the same chromosome. It does not establish exact physical location or fitness.

Q21. Part C

In AaBb × Aabb, independent assortment and complete dominance give what probability of A_bb?

  1. 3/16
  2. 3/8
  3. 1/4
  4. 1/2
Answer and explanation

B. 3/8 — A_ has probability 3/4 from Aa × Aa; bb has probability 1/2 from Bb × bb. Multiplication gives 3/8. The 3/16 alternative assumes a self-cross at B.

From AaBBccDd × aaBbCcDD, what fraction are AaBbccDd?

  1. 1/4
  2. 1/8
  3. 1/32
  4. 1/16
Answer and explanation

D. 1/16 — Each required genotype at A, B, C and D has probability 1/2, so the joint probability is (1/2)^4 = 1/16. Check the genotype rather than simply the dominant phenotype.

Q23. Part C

Among double-dominant F2 offspring of an independent AaBb self-cross, what fraction are homozygous at exactly one locus?

  1. 4/9
  2. 1/9
  3. 1/4
  4. 2/3
Answer and explanation

A. 4/9 — AABb and AaBB each have conditional weight 2/9, so exactly one homozygous locus has probability 4/9. AABB is homozygous at both, not exactly one.

Q24. Part C

From a trihybrid self-cross, what is the probability of exactly one recessive phenotype, assuming independent loci and complete dominance?

  1. 9/64
  2. 3/64
  3. 27/64
  4. 37/64

C. 27/64 — Choose the recessive locus in three ways. Each arrangement has probability (1/4)(3/4)^2 = 9/64. The total is 27/64; 37/64 is the probability of at least one recessive phenotype.

Q25. Part C

A tetrahybrid self-cross gives what probability of exactly two homozygous loci, regardless of which allele is homozygous?

  1. 1/16
  2. 3/8
  3. 9/64
  4. 1/2
Answer and explanation

B. 3/8 — At each locus homozygosity has probability 1/2. Choose two loci out of four: 6(1/2)^4 = 6/16 = 3/8. This differs from counting recessive phenotypes, which have probability 1/4.

Q26. Part C

For AaBbCc × AaBbCc, an all-dominant offspring is selected. What is the chance it is AABBCC?

  1. 1/64
  2. 1/8

  4. 1/27

A recessive phenotype has probability 1/4 in each independent offspring. Which expression gives the probability of exactly three recessive offspring out of five?

  3. 5(1/4)^3
  4. 1−(3/4)^5

In a testcross, aabb appears with probability 1/4. How many independent offspring are needed for at least 95% chance of one aabb?

  1. 4
  2. 8
  3. 11
  4. 12

C. 11 — Solve (3/4)^n ≤ 0.05. The quotient ln(0.05)/ln(0.75) is about 10.41; round up to 11. Expected count alone does not determine recovery probability.

Q29. Part C

A randomly chosen A_ F2 plant from Aa self-cross is testcrossed to aa. Two offspring are both dominant. What is the posterior probability that the tested parent is Aa?

  1. 2/3
  2. 1/3
  3. 1/2
  4. 1/6
Answer and explanation

B. 1/3 — The evidence likelihoods are 1 for AA and 1/4 for Aa. Joint probabilities are 1/3 and (2/3)(1/4)=1/6; dividing 1/6 by the total 1/2 gives 1/3. The 1/6 option is the joint, not conditional, probability.

Q30. Part C

A randomly chosen dominant F2 from Aa × Aa is crossed with aa. What is the probability the first two offspring are both recessive?

  1. 1/9
  2. 1/4
  3. 4/9
  4. 1/6
Answer and explanation

D. 1/6 — Only an Aa parent can yield recessives. Multiply its conditional probability 2/3 by (1/2)^2 to get 1/6. Squaring the marginal one-offspring probability 1/3 ignores the shared unknown parental genotype.

Q31. Part C

A testcross produces AB = 80, Ab = 20, aB = 20, ab = 80. Under equal recovery, the simplest candidate explanation is:

  1. Coupling-phase linkage with r = 0.20
  2. Repulsion-phase linkage with r = 0.20
  3. Independent assortment with both marginal ratios 3:1
  4. No recombination
Answer and explanation

A. Coupling-phase linkage with r = 0.20 — The two abundant classes AB and ab are consistent with parental haplotypes in cis. Recombinant classes total 40 of 200 = 0.20. The marginal allele ratios are 1:1; the pattern is evidence, not proof, of linkage.

Q32. Part C

Another testcross yields AB = 25, Ab = 75, aB = 75, ab = 25. If linkage is the explanation, which phase and recombination fraction fit?

  1. cis, 0.25
  2. cis, 0.75
  3. trans, 0.25
  4. trans, 0.50
Answer and explanation

C. trans, 0.25 — Ab and aB are the enriched parental classes, indicating trans phase. AB and ab total 50/200 = 0.25. A recombination fraction of 0.75 is not an appropriate two-point value in this simple model.

Q33. Part C

An early embryo sample from a testcross has four equal genotype classes. At maturity, aabb is almost absent. Which inference is best supported?

  1. The heterozygote never made ab gametes
  2. Postzygotic recovery differs among classes
  3. Independent assortment has been disproved directly
  4. The a locus must be Y-linked
Answer and explanation

B. Postzygotic recovery differs among classes — The early sample includes the genotype, so the corresponding gametes and fertilization occurred. A later deficit supports differential survival or recovery; the data do not directly prove the precise cause.

Q34. Part C

Under a 9:3:3:1 zygote model, survival of the double recessive class is zero and all others survive. Which mature phenotype ratio is predicted?

  1. 9:3:3:1
  2. 9:3:1
  3. 4:2:1
  4. 3:1:1
Answer and explanation

D. 3:1:1 — Removing the weight-one class leaves 9:3:3 among survivors; divide all weights by three. It does not imply that the double recessive zygote was never produced.

Q35. Part C

An F2 sample has 180, 60, 50 and 30 offspring. Under 9:3:3:1, χ² is approximately:

  1. 6.67
  2. 5.00
  3. 3.33
  4. 8.33
Answer and explanation

A. 6.67 — The total is 320 and expectations are 180, 60, 60, 20. Terms are 0, 0, 100/60 and 100/20, totaling 6.67. At df=3 this is below 7.815.

Q36. Part C

A testcross has counts 40, 10, 10, 40. What is the chi-square statistic against equal frequencies?

  1. 9
  2. 18
  3. 36
  4. 90
Answer and explanation

C. 36 — Expected counts are 25. Each of four terms equals 225/25=9, totaling 36. This rejects a fully specified equal-frequency null at α=0.05, df=3.

Q37. Part C

A 9:3:3:1 F2 sample contains 48 scored offspring. Which issue should be checked before routine chi-square inference?

  1. The number of categories must be six
  2. The rarest expected count is three
  3. Expected counts must be rounded to whole numbers
  4. All observed classes must be identical
Answer and explanation

B. The rarest expected count is three — The rarest expectation is 48/16=3, below the common introductory guideline of five. An exact or simulation-based method may be preferable. The concern is the expected, not observed, counts.

Q38. Part C

The same testcross proportions 0.30, 0.20, 0.20, 0.30 are observed at N=100 and N=1000. Which comparison holds for the equal-frequency null?

  1. Same χ² because percentages agree
  2. Larger sample has smaller χ²
  3. Smaller sample must reject but larger must not
  4. χ² rises from 4 to 40
Answer and explanation

D. χ² rises from 4 to 40 — The statistic is N times the weighted squared proportional deviation. It scales tenfold here. The relative difference stays the same, while statistical evidence strengthens under independent sampling.

Q39. Part C

Testcross counts AB=42, Ab=28, aB=18, ab=12. Which statement is correct?

  1. Observed joint proportions factor into observed margins, but the 1:1:1:1 null fails
  2. All margins are 1:1
  3. The association-test statistic must equal 20.64
  4. Both tests use three degrees of freedom
Answer and explanation

A. Observed joint proportions factor into observed margins, but the 1:1:1:1 null fails — The margins are 0.7/0.3 and 0.6/0.4, whose products match the four observed proportions. The equal-frequency χ² is 20.64 with df=3; the association test uses estimated margins and df=1.

Q40. Part C

A testcross with 16 offspring produces no aabb. Assuming independent equal gamete classes, what is the probability of this outcome?

  1. 0
  2. (1/4)^16
  3. (3/4)^16
  4. 1/4
Answer and explanation

C. (3/4)^16 — Every offspring must be one of the other three classes, giving (3/4)^16 ≈ 0.0100. A rare observation can occur under a correct model; absence alone does not logically eliminate the class.

Q41. Part C

In a self-cross, A shows incomplete dominance while B shows complete dominance. If loci are independent, how many distinguishable phenotype categories and what probability of AaB_?

  1. 4, 9/16
  2. 6, 3/8
  3. 9, 1/4
  4. 6, 1/8
Answer and explanation

B. 6, 3/8 — Three A phenotypes multiplied by two B phenotypes give six. Aa occurs with probability 1/2 and B_ with 3/4, giving 3/8. Expression changes class counts without changing assortment.

Q42. Part C

Two loci are independent in all zygotes. A sample includes only aa or bb offspring (inclusive). What is P(aabb given selection) in a standard dihybrid self-cross?

  1. 1/16
  2. 1/4
  3. 4/7
  4. 1/7
Answer and explanation

D. 1/7 — The selected population has total probability 7/16; aabb contributes 1/16. Their ratio is 1/7. One-sixteenth is unconditional; four-sevenths is either recessive marginal in the selected set.

Q43. Part C

For three binary markers, only ABC, Abc, aBc and abC have probability 1/4 each. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Pairwise independence does not establish joint independence
  2. All three are jointly independent because each marginal is 1/2
  3. Each pair must be associated
  4. The probability of ABC is 1/8
Answer and explanation

A. Pairwise independence does not establish joint independence — Each pair displays all four combinations with equal probability. Yet ABC has probability 1/4 rather than the product 1/8. Joint independence is a stronger condition.

Q44. Part C

A cross yields gamete frequencies P(AB)=0.40, P(Ab)=0.10, P(aB)=0.10, P(ab)=0.40. What is P(B given A)?

  1. 0.20
  2. 0.50
  3. 0.80
  4. 0.40
Answer and explanation

C. 0.80 — P(A)=0.50. Divide P(AB)=0.40 by 0.50 to obtain 0.80, indicating association. The 0.40 alternative is the joint probability.

Q45. Part C

A four-category goodness-of-fit model estimates one independent parameter from the same counts under regular assumptions. What are the usual asymptotic degrees of freedom?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

B. 2 — Use k−1−m = 4−1−1 = 2. The simple fixed-probability four-class Mendelian test would instead use three.

Q46. SET / integrated reasoning

Assertion–reason: Assertion: A 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio requires more than independent assortment. Reason: The conventional ratio also assumes complete dominance and no phenotype-changing interaction between loci.

Answer and explanation

Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion. Independent assortment specifies transmission. The phenotype map must also convert the nine genotypes into the four predicted categories; incomplete dominance or interaction can alter that map.

Q47. SET / integrated reasoning

An illustrative F2 sample has 72, 24, 24 and 8 offspring. Calculate χ² under the conventional model and state the conclusion.

Answer and explanation

χ² = 0; df = 3; fail to reject the model at α = 0.05. N=128 gives expected counts exactly 72, 24, 24 and 8. Every term is zero. Perfect agreement of one sample does not prove the biological model.

Q48. SET / integrated reasoning

List two predictions that distinguish a transmission bias from a late viability effect in an informative testcross.

Answer and explanation

Transmission bias should be present in directly sampled gametes or early zygotes; a later viability effect should change the distribution between early and late observations. Compare genotypes at distinct stages, use verified parental genotypes and controlled scoring. Early bias may have other explanations, including fertilization bias, so the observations should be interpreted with controls.

Q49. SET / integrated reasoning

In AaBbCc × AaBbCc, calculate the chance of at least one recessive phenotype in one offspring and explain the shortcut.

Answer and explanation

37/64. No recessive phenotype has probability (3/4)^3=27/64. The complement is 1−27/64=37/64. Adding three 1/4 probabilities directly would double-count overlaps.

Q50. SET / integrated reasoning

Multiple statement: I. Independent loci may yield unequal phenotype ratios. II. Equal allele marginals prove joint independence. III. A fully specified four-class goodness-of-fit test usually has df=3. Identify the true statements.

Answer and explanation

I and III only. I is true because phenotype ratios additionally depend on expression and survival. II is false: linked loci can have 1:1 marginals. III follows from one fixed-total constraint.

Revision and mastery

Independent assortment is a joint transmission model supported by relative orientation of homologous pairs in meiosis I. Separate one-locus segregation does not guarantee independence between loci. A cross yields gamete probabilities, which produce zygote probabilities through fertilization, followed by an expression and recovery model. Derive the probabilities before applying ratio shortcuts.
