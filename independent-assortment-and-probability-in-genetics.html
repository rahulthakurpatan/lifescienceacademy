1. From one inherited character to two

A genetic cross is an experiment in transmission, not simply a calculation of offspring colours. The experimenter begins with parents whose inherited differences can be followed and asks which combinations of those differences reappear in the descendants. A single character gives a first opportunity to distinguish two competing explanations: either parental hereditary differences become permanently blended, or the inherited alternatives remain distinct and can be separated in a later generation. Segregation explains the latter outcome. The next problem arises when the same parents differ in two characters at once. Does the inheritance of one character dictate the inheritance of the other, or can their hereditary determinants be transmitted separately? Independent assortment is the answer to this second question under a specified set of biological conditions.

Imagine a diploid plant that inherited a round-seed allele from one parent and a wrinkled-seed allele from the other. We write these alleles as R and r. The plant also has a locus influencing cotyledon colour, at which we write the yellow and green alternatives as Y and y. The genotype RrYy says that the plant is heterozygous at both loci. It says nothing by itself about whether the two loci lie on the same chromosome or different chromosomes. The genotype also does not tell us whether all four allele combinations occur equally frequently in the gametes. Those are separate claims about the mechanism of inheritance. To make predictions, we must specify both the alleles present and their transmission probabilities.

A locus is a position or defined genetic region, whereas an allele is a particular inherited version at that locus. A diploid individual generally has two copies of an autosomal locus. During normal meiosis, a haploid gamete receives one of the two alternatives. Fertilization joins one gamete from each parent and restores the diploid state. This alternating reduction and restoration provides the basis for Mendelian ratios. The gamete of a double heterozygote is represented by two letters, such as RY, because it carries one allele from each locus. Writing Rr as a normal haploid gamete confuses the diploid parental genotype with the product of meiosis. Similarly, a gamete labelled RR cannot be treated as an ordinary product of a heterozygote at one locus.

Dominance is a relationship between alleles in the expression of a phenotype. If Rr and RR both produce round seeds under the conditions of observation, R is described as completely dominant to r for this character. This does not imply that R is transmitted more frequently, that it always gives a physiological advantage, or that it is more common in a population. In the simplest cross, R and r each occur in half of the gametes of Rr. The distinction between expression and transmission becomes particularly important when we study two loci. Independent assortment describes transmission, whereas the familiar phenotype ratios additionally depend on how each offspring genotype is expressed.

Consider the cross RRYY × rryy. With the stated homozygous parents, every gamete from the first parent carries RY, and every gamete from the second carries ry. Their F1 offspring are therefore RrYy. Under complete dominance the F1 seeds are round and yellow. This result alone cannot demonstrate independent assortment: even if the loci are tightly linked, all F1 individuals would have the same alleles and display the same dominant characters. A genuine test must observe the products of meiosis in the F1 generation, either indirectly through a self-cross or more directly through a cross with a suitable tester.

The F1 may be crossed with another F1 or, in a self-fertilizing plant, allowed to self-fertilize under controlled conditions. The resulting F2 population can exhibit four phenotypic classes: round yellow, round green, wrinkled yellow and wrinkled green. The combinations round green and wrinkled yellow differ from the two original parental phenotypes. Their occurrence shows that parental characters need not be inherited as inseparable packages. Their mere presence, however, is not sufficient evidence of statistical independence. A linked pair of loci can also produce novel combinations by crossing over. The frequencies, experimental design and alternative explanations must therefore be considered together.

Mendel's experiments offered a way to separate the question of inheritance from subjective impressions about resemblance. He selected traits with distinguishable categories, used lines with stable inherited characteristics, controlled pollination, and recorded progeny counts. His published 1866 account includes crosses involving seed shape and colour. In a modern chromosome-based explanation, the hereditary factors followed in these experiments correspond to alleles at loci, and the separation of alternative factors corresponds to transmission through gametes. Mendel did not have the contemporary molecular or cytological framework available to him; the value of the historical result is that quantitative patterns made a mechanistic model testable before the physical identity of genetic information was known.

The F2 seed-shape and seed-colour observations reported by Mendel provide a useful historical example: 315 round yellow, 108 round green, 101 wrinkled yellow and 32 wrinkled green seeds. These are observed counts, not a set of exact theoretical proportions. When placed in the order just given, the four classes can be compared to the 9:3:3:1 prediction. They total 556. A model predicting exactly 312.75 round yellow seeds does not imply that a fraction of a seed exists. It implies a mean count over many comparable experiments. The noninteger expected count is meaningful because probability is a property of a model, while an actual experiment produces integers.

The important progression is from observation to a quantitative model, and then from the model to biological interpretation. It is possible to observe an approximately Mendelian ratio without identifying the precise chromosomes or biochemical pathways involved. Conversely, a well-supported chromosome model does not guarantee that the next group of sixteen offspring contains exactly nine individuals of one phenotype. A probability is a prediction about a distribution of outcomes, not a timetable governing which individual must appear next. To understand genetics at the analytical level, we must keep the distinction between a biological mechanism, a mathematical prediction and a finite observation throughout the chapter.

A useful vocabulary for the remainder of the discussion is marginal probability, joint probability and conditional probability. A marginal probability concerns one event while ignoring the outcome at another locus. A joint probability concerns their simultaneous occurrence. A conditional probability asks about an event among a selected subset. These three questions can all be asked of the same table of offspring. The statement of independence concerns how the joint and conditional probabilities relate to the marginal probabilities. It is stronger than observing a correct marginal ratio at each locus separately.

2. Chromosomal mechanism of independent assortment

The mechanism becomes visible when the two loci are located on separate homologous chromosome pairs. Suppose one pair carries R and r and a different pair carries Y and y. Before meiosis, DNA replication produces sister chromatids, so each chromosome is represented by two chromatids. Homologous chromosomes then associate during prophase I and become organized into bivalents. The homologues are not the same as sister chromatids: a homologous pair comprises chromosomes inherited from the two parents, whereas sisters arise by replication of a single chromosome. Even if sisters have exchanged segments during recombination, the distinction between the homologues and sister chromatids remains essential for understanding segregation.

At metaphase I each bivalent is positioned on the spindle so that the two homologues are directed toward opposite poles. For a pair carrying R and r, either R-bearing homologue can face a particular pole. For the pair carrying Y and y, either Y-bearing homologue can face that same pole. Under the conventional model the direction selected by the second pair does not depend on the direction selected by the first. Two relative orientations can therefore be distinguished: the R and Y homologues face the same pole, or the R and y homologues face the same pole. Reversing both poles changes the absolute orientation but not the resulting pair of complementary allele combinations. In an ensemble of cells with random orientations, both relative arrangements are equally frequent.

During anaphase I the homologues separate. In a simplified diagram with no exchange involving the marker loci, one relative orientation puts R and Y together in one daughter cell and r and y together in the other. The other orientation produces R with y and r with Y. Meiosis II then separates sister chromatids, producing haploid products. If all four products were recovered in the simplified first orientation, there would be two RY and two ry products; the second orientation would produce two Ry and two rY. It is incorrect to demand that each individual meiotic division yield exactly one of each of the four possible gamete types. Equal frequencies are a population-level expectation across many meiotic divisions. Recombination between the centromere and marker can complicate individual tetrad patterns, so the illustration is explicitly a model of assortment without relevant exchanges.

Imagine selecting one gamete at random from the entire population of products. Its R or r allele depends on segregation of one pair; the Y or y allele depends on segregation of another. With fair segregation, each allele occurs with probability one-half. With independent relative orientation, the joint probability of R and Y is one-half multiplied by one-half, or one-quarter. The same argument gives one-quarter for Ry, rY and ry. The four classes exhaust the possible haploid genotypes and the four probabilities sum to one. This provides a biological derivation of the familiar four gametes from a dihybrid rather than an unexplained rule to memorize.

There is a subtle difference between random chromosome orientation and random fertilization. The first process concerns the segregation of alleles into gametes within a parent. The second concerns the association of gametes from different parents. Even if a parent produces RY, Ry, rY and ry in equal proportions, the predicted F2 distribution requires that the fertilizing gametes combine according to the assumed probabilities. If fertilization is biased toward one gamete combination, the genotype frequencies of zygotes may no longer equal the products of the gametic probabilities. If differential survival operates after fertilization, counts from adult progeny can depart from the zygote predictions. A ratio observed in surviving offspring therefore reflects the combined processes of gamete production, fertilization, development and scoring.

For n independently orienting homologous pairs, each pair can contribute one of two parental homologues to a particular haploid product. Ignoring crossing over, the number of possible parental-chromosome sets is 2 to the power n. This is a count of possible sets across many gametes, not the number of chromosomes in a gamete and not the number of different products of a single meiotic division. A diploid organism with three homologous pairs has eight possible sets, but each normal haploid product contains three chromosomes. The combinations become increasingly numerous as the haploid chromosome number increases. Crossing over adds further diversity by exchanging homologous segments, producing chromosomes that are not simply intact maternal or paternal homologues.

The same counting logic can be applied to heterozygous loci when they assort independently. A genotype AaBbCc with the three loci independently assorting produces eight possible allele combinations. A genotype AABbcc, however, has only one heterozygous locus: B versus b. It can produce ABc and Abc, not eight different allelic gametes. Homozygous loci contribute their fixed allele to every normal gamete. Therefore the appropriate exponent for counting distinct gametic genotypes is the number of heterozygous, independently assorting loci, not the total number of loci listed in the genotype.

The underlying mechanism also explains why segregation is not synonymous with independent assortment. Segregation is separation of alternative alleles at a single locus into different gametes. Independence is a relationship between segregation at different loci. Two tightly linked loci may each segregate normally, yet the joint transmission of their alleles is strongly associated. For example, a double heterozygote carrying AB on one homologue and ab on the other, in the absence of crossing over between the loci, produces AB and ab gametes in equal proportions. At the A locus the probability of A is one-half; at B the probability of B is one-half. However, the probability of AB is one-half rather than one-quarter. The separate marginal probabilities are identical to those of an independently assorting double heterozygote.

This linked example gives a powerful diagnostic. If observing A in a gamete changes the probability of observing B, then the two events are not independent in that gamete distribution. In the complete coupling example, observing A identifies AB, so the conditional probability of B given A is one. Observing a identifies ab, so the conditional probability of B given a is zero. In an independently assorting double heterozygote, both conditional probabilities are one-half. This statistical distinction arises from the physical organization of the chromosomes. Later chapters will explain how crossing over changes the joint gamete frequencies and how this information is used in genetic mapping.

Chromosomes on different pairs generally assort independently under regular meiosis, but statistical independence between marker loci does not uniquely prove that they occupy different chromosomes. Distant loci on the same chromosome can approach an observed recombination fraction of one-half, making their two-locus transmission patterns indistinguishable from independence by this criterion. Conversely, a deviation from the independence prediction does not uniquely establish physical linkage: segregation distortion, selection among gametes or offspring, and nonindependent sampling can also change measured frequencies. A genetic model is a causal explanation supported by multiple observations, not an automatic conclusion from a single ratio.

The independent assortment model assumes that the events used in calculations have been defined correctly. Alleles A and a in the same haploid gamete are mutually exclusive under normal segregation; they are not independent. A and B at separate independently assorting loci can coexist. The physical location of the alternatives determines which statements are biologically possible before we apply any mathematical rule.

3. Probability rules for genetic events

A probability is a number between zero and one assigned to an event. In a genetic cross, an event could be the production of an Ab gamete, the formation of an AaBb zygote, or the observation of a recessive phenotype among offspring. The probability depends on the sample space, which is the collection of outcomes under consideration. A sample space of gametes is not the same as a sample space of zygotes. A sample space of all zygotes is not necessarily the same as a sample space of viable adults. Failure to specify the sample space is the origin of many apparently contradictory answers in genetics.

For an Aa parent undergoing ordinary meiosis, the outcomes at the A locus are A and a with probabilities one-half each. These outcomes are exhaustive and mutually exclusive. If a gamete carries A, it does not also carry a, given the assumed haploid state. Thus the probability of A or a equals one. By contrast, in a double heterozygote the events “carries A” and “carries B” are neither mutually exclusive nor exhaustive. Some gametes carry both, some carry only one, and some carry neither. Simply adding the marginal probabilities would count the AB gametes twice.

The general multiplication rule states that P(A and B) equals P(A) multiplied by P(B given A). Here the letters A and B denote events, which may or may not be identical to allele labels. If the occurrence of A does not alter the probability of B, then P(B given A) equals P(B), and the expression reduces to the ordinary product rule P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). It is this reduced form that is commonly used for independent loci. The general form remains valid when the events are dependent, provided the conditional probability is defined. The reduced form must not be applied automatically merely because an examination question uses the word “and”.

For example, an AaBb individual with independent assortment produces an Ab gamete with probability P(A) × P(b) = (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4. If the same loci are linked in coupling phase, AB/ab, with recombination fraction 0.20 and equal reciprocal recombinant classes, the probability of Ab is 0.10. Both marginal probabilities still equal one-half, but P(b given A) = 0.10/0.50 = 0.20. The general multiplication rule now gives (1/2)(0.20) = 0.10, agreeing with the gamete frequency. This comparison establishes exactly which assumption was needed to obtain one-quarter in the independent case.

The addition rule concerns a union of events. For two events E and F, P(E or F) = P(E) + P(F) − P(E and F), because the overlap would otherwise be counted twice. When the events cannot occur together, the overlap is zero and the rule simplifies to addition. The probability that an Aa × Aa offspring is homozygous is the sum of P(AA) and P(aa), namely one-quarter plus one-quarter or one-half. An individual cannot be AA and aa at the same locus, so no correction is needed. On the other hand, the probability that an AaBb self-cross offspring is aa or bb is not one-half. The aa and bb classes overlap at aabb. The correct probability is 1/4 + 1/4 − 1/16 = 7/16.

In ordinary language, “or” can be inclusive or exclusive. The inclusive event contains outcomes satisfying either condition or both. The exclusive event contains outcomes satisfying exactly one condition. If a question asks for offspring with exactly one recessive phenotype in AaBb × AaBb, the relevant events are aaB_ and A_bb. They are disjoint because the first is dominant at B while the second is recessive there. Each has probability (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16. Their sum is 6/16. If the question instead asks for at least one recessive phenotype, aabb must also be counted, giving 7/16. A single phrase changes the set of offspring counted.

A complement consists of every outcome in the sample space that is not in the specified event. The event “at least one recessive phenotype” has complement “no recessive phenotypes”. In the independent dihybrid self-cross, the latter requires a dominant phenotype at both loci and has probability 9/16. Subtracting from one gives 7/16 for the original event. This method is especially valuable when there are many loci or offspring. Rather than enumerating every possible combination containing one or more recessive outcomes, calculate the simpler event that none occurs.

Conditional probability is defined by dividing the joint probability by the probability of the conditioning event: P(E given F) = P(E and F)/P(F), when P(F) is greater than zero. Its denominator is the probability of entering the selected subset. In Aa × Aa, the offspring genotypes AA, Aa and aa have probabilities one-quarter, one-half and one-quarter. Among individuals displaying a dominant phenotype under complete dominance, aa is absent. The probability of heterozygosity in this selected population equals (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3. This result is not the probability of a heterozygote among all offspring, which remains one-half. The distinction is a change in the denominator, not a change in the mechanism of meiosis.

Consider a selected A_B_ offspring from AaBb × AaBb. If the loci assort independently and the phenotype at each locus is determined independently, the probability of Aa among A_ is 2/3 and that of Bb among B_ is 2/3. The probability of AaBb among the double dominant subset is therefore 4/9. One can obtain the same answer from the unselected sample: the probability of AaBb is one-quarter, while that of A_B_ is 9/16; dividing gives (1/4)/(9/16) = 4/9. Agreement between these two methods is a useful check that the correct sample space has been selected.

Independence does not follow from knowing two equal marginal probabilities. Imagine four equally numerous gamete classes AB, Ab, aB and ab in one model and only AB and ab, each with one-half probability, in another. Both models have P(A) = P(B) = 1/2. However, their joint probabilities differ. Conversely, mutually exclusive nonzero-probability events cannot be independent because their joint probability is zero while the product of their probabilities is positive. When deciding which formula applies, identify the events and inspect whether they can occur together and whether one changes the chance of the other.

A final distinction concerns the probabilities of offspring from a cross and successive offspring from the same parents. Once parental genotypes and a constant reproductive model are specified, separate fertilizations are commonly treated as independent trials. Under this model, the genotype of a first offspring does not change the probability of a later offspring. But if parental genotype is unknown, the first offspring may provide information that changes our estimate of the parents' genotypes and hence the predicted probability for later offspring. Conditional on the actual parental genotypes the births may be independent; after averaging over uncertainty in those genotypes, the observations can become statistically dependent. Genetics problems often exploit this distinction when they ask about the probability of a recessive offspring after information about siblings has been supplied.

4. The dihybrid cross and the 9:3:3:1 model

We can now derive the classical dihybrid phenotype ratio without appealing to memorization. Assume two diploid parents, each heterozygous at two autosomal loci, represented as RrYy. Assume normal segregation, independent assortment, random union of gametes, equal viability, complete dominance at each locus and no interaction between the loci that changes the phenotype categories. These assumptions specify a model. The predicted result is not a universal law for any two visible traits. A change in even one assumption can change the observed ratio.

At the R locus, the cross Rr × Rr generates RR, Rr and rr with probabilities 1/4, 1/2 and 1/4. At the Y locus, the equivalent cross generates YY, Yy and yy with the same probabilities. Because the loci assort independently in the parents and the union of gametes is random, the two zygotic locus genotypes are independently distributed. The joint probability of RRYY is therefore (1/4)(1/4) = 1/16. The joint probability of RrYy is (1/2)(1/2) = 1/4. Each of the remaining seven genotype classes can be obtained in the same manner. There are nine distinct two-locus genotype classes because each locus has three possible diploid genotypes and 3 × 3 = 9.

A Punnett square makes the gametic origin of these classes explicit. Write RY, Ry, rY and ry along one edge for the first parent and the same four gametes along the other edge for the second. Each cell represents one ordered union of two gametes. Since both gamete distributions are uniform and independent under random fertilization, each of the sixteen cells has probability 1/16. The cells do not represent sixteen different genotypes. For example, RY × ry, Ry × rY, rY × Ry and ry × RY all produce RrYy. They are distinct routes to the same offspring genotype and their probabilities are added.

The notation R_ denotes a phenotype-compatible genotype with at least one R allele; it includes RR and Rr. The underscore is not an unknown physical allele or an additional allele type. In a self-cross, P(R_) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4. Similarly, P(Y_) = 3/4. The probability of round yellow offspring is P(R_Y_) = (3/4)(3/4) = 9/16. Round green offspring have R_yy with probability (3/4)(1/4) = 3/16. Wrinkled yellow offspring have rrY_ with probability (1/4)(3/4) = 3/16. Finally, wrinkled green offspring have rryy with probability (1/4)(1/4) = 1/16. The four mutually exclusive and exhaustive classes total sixteen sixteenths. Their relative weights give 9:3:3:1.

The phenotype ratio hides the greater richness of the genotype distribution. Within the round yellow class, RRYY occurs with probability 1/16, RRYy with 2/16, RrYY with 2/16 and RrYy with 4/16. If a round yellow F2 plant is selected at random, the probability that it is homozygous dominant at both loci is 1/9, not 1/16. The probability that it is heterozygous at both loci is 4/9. The probability that it is homozygous at exactly one of the two loci and heterozygous at the other is 4/9. These probabilities characterize the genotype composition of a visually selected class, not the original F2 distribution.

When the parental cross is RRyy × rrYY rather than RRYY × rryy, the F1 genotype is still RrYy. Under independent assortment, changing the original combinations does not alter the F2 phenotype prediction. This is because the separate allele transmissions do not retain information about the parental pairing. If the loci were linked, the arrangement of alleles on the homologous chromosomes would matter: RY/ry is a different phase from Ry/rY. Thus a useful experimental comparison involves reciprocal parental combinations or molecular evidence about the marker arrangement. Equal dihybrid ratios under these assumptions need not identify the chromosomal position of the markers.

The familiar ratio can also be collapsed by examining one trait at a time. Round offspring include the round yellow and round green classes; their combined probability is 9/16 + 3/16 = 12/16 = 3/4. Wrinkled offspring total 4/16 = 1/4. The same marginal 3:1 ratio appears for yellow versus green. However, this collapse loses information about the association between the traits. A linked model with normal segregation can retain both marginal 3:1 phenotype ratios while failing to produce the expected joint distribution. A study recording only the totals for each trait separately cannot distinguish all alternative two-locus models.

The difference between the theoretical probability and the observed count is essential in experimental interpretation. For N = 160 offspring, the expected counts under the model are 90, 30, 30 and 10. For N = 100 offspring the expectations are 56.25, 18.75, 18.75 and 6.25. We do not round these values before using a goodness-of-fit statistic: rounding introduces avoidable changes in expected counts and may destroy the requirement that their sum equals N. The experiment will yield integer counts, and deviations can be examined relative to the sampling variation predicted by the model.

A dihybrid cross also need not mean self-crossing identical heterozygotes. In RrYy × Rryy, the R locus has the ordinary 3:1 phenotype probabilities, whereas the Y locus has a testcross-like 1:1 distribution. Their independent products give round yellow and round green each with probability 3/8, and wrinkled yellow and wrinkled green each with probability 1/8. The ratio is 3:3:1:1 in that order. An examination problem with two heterozygous loci in one parent is not enough to justify 9:3:3:1; the genotype of the other parent must be inspected. Likewise, when one parent is homozygous dominant at a locus, all offspring express the dominant phenotype at that locus under complete dominance, although the offspring genotypes can still differ.

The derivation makes clear why genotype ratios, phenotype ratios, and gamete ratios are distinct. The gametic probabilities describe parental meiotic products; the genotype probabilities describe combinations created at fertilization; the phenotype probabilities describe the expression of those combinations. A correct solution states which of these distributions is required, derives the relevant probability, and only then converts it into a ratio or a count.
