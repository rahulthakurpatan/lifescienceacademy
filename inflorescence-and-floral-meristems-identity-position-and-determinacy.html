Inflorescence and Floral Meristems: Identity, Position and Determinacy

Understand reproductive shoot architecture, lateral floral meristem identity, LFY, AP1, CAL, TFL1, and the regulation of floral determinacy.

1. The developmental problem: one apex, several possible futures

A young Arabidopsis plant initially forms a rosette of leaves close to the soil. Later a stem elongates above the rosette and produces flowers in an ordered succession. At first sight this transformation seems to mean that the shoot tip has changed into a flower. A closer examination shows something more interesting. The tip of the reproductive shoot remains a source of new structures, while individual flowers arise as separate growing centers on its sides. Thus the plant accomplishes two apparently contradictory tasks at the same time: it maintains a continuing supply of new flowers and restricts each flower to a finite set of structures. Explaining how neighboring growing centers adopt these different futures is the central problem of floral meristem identity.

A meristem is an organized population of dividing cells that supplies new cells to a growing plant. It is not simply any collection of rapidly dividing cells. A shoot apical meristem has a persistent spatial organization, including a central population that supplies descendants and peripheral regions in which new structures arise. Its products depend on developmental stage and position. During vegetative growth, the apex produces leaf primordia and contributes to the stem. During reproductive growth, the same primary axis may instead generate floral primordia and reproductive branches. The cells do not need to acquire a different genome to achieve this change. Different developmental programs become active in different contexts, altering what the descendants make.

Organogenesis is the formation of organs from developing tissues. A leaf primordium is the earliest recognizable developing leaf, while a floral primordium is the earliest recognizable developing flower. The latter is not equivalent to a single petal primordium. It gives rise to a floral meristem, an organized developmental center that subsequently initiates the component organs of a flower. A flower is therefore a specialized reproductive shoot with a particular developmental program. Its relatively short axis and closely arranged organs distinguish it from a typical elongated vegetative shoot, but both depend on the production and organization of new cells. This relationship explains why a mutation that changes developmental identity may replace a flower with a shoot-like structure rather than simply removing the flower.

Meristem identity refers to the type of developmental program that a meristem follows. Identity is inferred from the structures generated, their position and sequence, and the molecular activities present before the structures mature. A meristem that makes leaves and axillary buds is developmentally distinct from one that makes floral organs. A meristem that repeatedly makes other meristems is also distinct from a floral meristem. Identity is not synonymous with shape: two young rounded protrusions can possess different developmental futures even if their earliest external appearance is nearly identical. For this reason genetic and molecular markers are particularly informative at early stages when the visible phenotype has not yet emerged.

Three levels of decision must be distinguished. First, a plant becomes competent to reproduce and receives signals that favor the vegetative-to-reproductive transition. Second, growing centers within the reproductive shoot acquire particular meristem identities. Third, within a correctly specified floral meristem, different positions acquire the identities of particular floral organs. These decisions are related but cannot substitute for one another. A plant may enter a reproductive phase and still fail to make normal flowers because lateral meristems retain shoot-like properties. Alternatively, a plant may produce appropriately positioned flowers that contain abnormal organs. The first defect concerns formation of a flower-producing center; the second concerns regional patterning within that center.

The distinction is especially important when interpreting a mutant that has not produced an open flower by a given day. The defect could involve the date of reproductive induction, the conversion of lateral meristems into flowers, the growth of already specified flowers, or a later process required for opening. Counting open flowers alone cannot identify the failed step. A developmental analysis instead examines the apex at successive stages, distinguishes leaves from bracts and flowers from branches, and tests which regulatory programs have been activated. This approach replaces a vague label such as failure to flower with a mechanistic account of the stage at which the normal trajectory diverged.

In a typical Arabidopsis raceme, the central reproductive meristem retains its capacity to generate new lateral structures for a substantial period. Each lateral floral meristem, by contrast, produces a limited succession of floral organs and ultimately exhausts its central stem-cell activity. The distinction between continuing and finite growth is not a distinction between living and dead cells. A flower can remain metabolically active, enlarge, undergo fertilization and subsequently form a fruit even after its floral meristem has ceased making new primordia. Developmental determinacy is defined by the limitation of organ-producing activity, not the immediate death of the structure.

This chapter uses Arabidopsis as a reference system because its relatively simple raceme makes these developmental decisions easy to distinguish genetically. Comparisons with snapdragon, Antirrhinum majus, demonstrate that related regulatory principles operate in other flowering plants, although the detailed architectures are not identical. The challenge is to understand which observations reflect general properties of meristem identity and which depend on the arrangement and developmental history of a particular species.

2. From vegetative shoot to reproductive architecture

The vegetative shoot apical meristem occupies the growing end of the main shoot. Its central region is surrounded by a peripheral zone in which new lateral organs are initiated, while internal and basal regions contribute to the growing stem. Leaves arise as local primordia rather than as a complete ring of differentiated tissue. Cells recruited into each primordium follow a developmental path different from the cells remaining in the apex. Once a leaf has formed, a new growth center can develop in its axil, the angle between the leaf and the stem. Repeated production of these units builds a branched shoot system. The continuing activity of the apex is essential to generating new units, but it does not require every derivative to retain meristem identity.

When reproductive development begins in Arabidopsis, the main shoot apex changes its output. Elongated internodes raise the reproductive shoot above the basal rosette. The lower portion of the reproductive axis can bear cauline leaves and associated secondary inflorescences, while the upper region typically bears individual flowers rather than ordinary visible subtending leaves. A cauline leaf is a leaf borne on the elongating stem, in contrast to the leaves of the basal rosette. Bolting, the conspicuous elongation of the flowering stem, is therefore a useful visible landmark, but it is not identical to the molecular transition of the apex. One can observe changes in apex identity before the elongated stem becomes obvious.

The inflorescence meristem, abbreviated IM, is the growing center responsible for generating reproductive lateral structures. In the standard raceme model it remains at the main apex. The floral meristem, abbreviated FM, is a separate lateral center that makes the organs of one flower. A secondary inflorescence meristem may itself produce further flowers. The hierarchy matters: an inflorescence is a group or arrangement of flowers and its supporting axes, whereas a flower is an individual reproductive structure within that arrangement. The words do not describe successive sizes of the same object. A small inflorescence can contain multiple young flowers, while a large solitary flower remains a single flower.

During the transition, the identity of the existing primary apex changes from vegetative shoot meristem to reproductive inflorescence meristem. New lateral meristems then acquire floral identity. In a raceme, a typical sequence is therefore vegetative shoot apex, reproductive shoot apex, and lateral floral meristems. The last step is a spatial relationship as well as a temporal one. The main apex does not normally transform into each successive lateral flower. Instead, descendants at its flanks form independent growth centers while the main center persists. If a diagram draws these events as a single chain, the arrows must be interpreted carefully: the first arrow represents a change in the state of an existing apex, whereas the second represents production and specification of additional meristems.

The individual flowers of a raceme generally arise in an acropetal succession, meaning that older flowers occur toward the base and younger floral primordia occur toward the tip. Each flower may be attached by a small stalk, the pedicel. The main supporting axis is distinct from these individual flower stalks. Observing the tip of a raceme at one instant reveals multiple developmental stages because new primordia continue to form while earlier primordia progress through floral development. This arrangement enables an investigator to compare stages within one plant, although stage must be assigned using morphology rather than assuming that every primordium at a particular distance is exactly the same age.

The word indeterminate refers to the ability of a meristem to continue producing additional units without ending in a specified terminal organ as part of its usual developmental program. It does not imply unlimited real-world growth. An Arabidopsis inflorescence eventually stops producing new flowers because of age, reproductive feedback or resource and environmental conditions. This eventual arrest does not by itself mean that the apex has adopted floral identity. A determinate inflorescence can instead terminate specifically by conversion of its apex to a terminal flower. The two outcomes may both appear as an end to elongation but reflect different developmental mechanisms. A terminal flower is identified by its organ arrangement and position on the axis, not simply by being the highest flower visible at the time.

In a cymose arrangement, a terminal flower develops at the end of a growing axis, and continued production of flowers may depend on lateral axes below it. The terminal flower may be developmentally older than adjacent lateral flowers, unlike the simple acropetal series of a raceme. These alternatives show that terminal floral fate is not intrinsically abnormal across flowering plants. It is abnormal when it appears at the primary apex of a normally indeterminate Arabidopsis raceme as a consequence of a mutation. The same developmental event can contribute to a normal architecture in one species and reveal a regulatory failure in another.

Architecture is generated by successive local decisions rather than by a complete miniature arrangement preformed in the seed. At a given point, an existing meristem may retain its identity, change to a different identity, initiate an organ, or generate a new meristem. Repeating these decisions at several levels generates the eventual pattern of flowers and branches. If a lateral primordium remains an inflorescence meristem rather than becoming floral, it can produce another generation of lateral primordia. A modest change at the level of a single identity decision can consequently have a large effect on the entire plant form. This amplification through repeated development explains why meristem identity mutants often show striking architectures rather than isolated defects.

3. Position, initiation and the establishment of floral fate

A meristem must decide not only what to make but where to make it. During vegetative shoot development, local regions of auxin accumulation at the periphery help position new leaf primordia. PIN-FORMED transport proteins contribute to directional auxin movement, and neighboring primordia influence the pattern of subsequent initiation. Related principles operate at reproductive apices, where localized primordium formation occurs on the sides of the inflorescence meristem. However, position alone cannot specify the type of organ. A peripheral primordium in a vegetative apex may become a leaf, whereas a peripheral primordium in a reproductive apex may become a flower. The signaling environment and competence of the recruited cells have changed.

It is useful to separate initiation from identity even though the events interact. Initiation involves local growth and formation of a distinct primordium. Identity involves activation of regulatory programs that determine what the primordium will subsequently generate. A mutation affecting the positioning system might leave an apex largely devoid of lateral primordia; a mutation affecting floral identity might permit numerous primordia to arise but cause them to grow as shoots. Both plants might produce fewer normal flowers, yet the former has failed to create the appropriate starting structures, while the latter has created structures with the wrong developmental fate. Comparison of young apices is essential for distinguishing the two.

The spatial difference between the central inflorescence apex and its periphery is especially striking because the cells are close enough to share systemic signals. A plant cannot rely solely on whether a general flowering signal reaches the reproductive shoot, since the signal must permit one part of the shoot to produce flowers while another retains the ability to generate them repeatedly. Local regulators provide the necessary interpretation of the general signal. In Arabidopsis, TERMINAL FLOWER1 activity supports the nonfloral identity of the inflorescence apex. LEAFY and APETALA1 activity, together with related regulators, promotes floral identity in emerging lateral meristems. These regulatory domains help maintain a difference between the central source of new structures and the individual flower-producing centers.

A domain is a region in which a gene is expressed or a molecular activity is present. A gene-expression domain need not coincide exactly with a visible anatomical boundary. A transcript may accumulate in certain cells while its protein acts in other cells, and a protein's activity can depend on partners that are distributed differently from the protein itself. Consequently a colored reporter image should not be read as a literal map of all biochemical activity. Distinguishing transcript localization, protein localization and functional consequences is a central skill in developmental biology. The correspondence of multiple kinds of evidence is more informative than any single expression image.

Early floral meristems in Arabidopsis are relatively inconspicuous outgrowths. As they develop, sepal primordia appear on the flanks, followed by additional floral organs in characteristic positions. At these early stages the structure still requires a renewing population of cells to generate successive organ primordia. It is not born as a fully determinate collection of differentiated organs. Rather, it possesses a transient ability to produce new structures that is subsequently terminated at the appropriate stage. The initial establishment of floral identity must therefore be compatible with continued growth long enough to construct a complete flower. A premature shutdown of stem-cell activity could produce a flower that lacks some organs despite having acquired floral identity.

The geometry of a flower also changes the problem faced by the meristem. During repeated shoot growth, successive lateral organs are distributed along an elongating axis. In a flower, organ primordia are organized in a compact spatial pattern, often described in terms of concentric whorls. The shift in spatial organization accompanies the acquisition of floral identity. Work in Antirrhinum demonstrates a connection between floral meristem identity genes and the change from spiral organization toward floral whorls. Yet the regulation that specifies a meristem as floral must be distinguished from the regulation that gives each whorl its particular organ identity. It is possible to initiate floral organs in approximately normal positions and still transform their identities through a homeotic mutation.

Competence adds a temporal dimension. A young tissue may contain a regulator but fail to respond fully until other molecular conditions are met. An experimental increase in a floral regulator does not necessarily generate complete flowers at every location or developmental stage. The availability of interacting transcription factors, accessibility of regulatory DNA and developmental state of the tissue may determine the outcome. Thus it is useful to describe floral regulators as causal contributors to the specification of floral identity in appropriate contexts rather than as universally sufficient instructions for every plant cell. The same reasoning applies when a reporter for a floral identity gene is active before a clearly recognizable flower has appeared.

The distinction between a potential fate and a realized fate is related to the concepts of specification and determination introduced in developmental biology. A specified tissue follows a characteristic path under a suitable environment, whereas a determined tissue retains that fate even after relevant environmental changes. Gene expression and morphology alone may indicate that a fate is being adopted, but they do not automatically prove that it is irreversible. Testing persistence after changing the inducing conditions or perturbing regulators can help assess the stability of the decision. In plant development the relevant experimental context must respect the organized growth of tissue and the fact that removing a primordium may itself disrupt its signals and resources.

4. Landmark genetic discoveries and the meaning of a mutant phenotype

A powerful way to study developmental identity is to find plants that generate one kind of structure in a place normally occupied by another. The altered structure provides a visible record of a developmental decision that has changed. In classical forward genetics, investigators first identify a reproducible mutant phenotype, then determine which genetic locus is responsible. A phenotype is the observable consequence of genotype and environment. The usefulness of mutants depends not merely on the abnormal appearance but on understanding which developmental event is affected and whether the mutated locus acts specifically in that event.

Early work on Arabidopsis identified terminal flower mutants in which the main reproductive axis ceased generating lateral flowers and ended in a flower. Shannon and Meeks-Wagner reported in 1991 that the tfl1-1 mutant showed earlier flowering together with conversion of the primary inflorescence apex toward terminal floral development. The presence of recognizable lateral flowers helped distinguish this mutation from a general incapacity to make floral organs. The phenotype provided evidence that a genetic activity was required to preserve inflorescence development after the reproductive transition. The result also warned against defining all flowering regulators exclusively in terms of the date of first flowering: the same locus can influence both timing and subsequent meristem behavior.

An independent line of work identified mutations that changed the identity of structures on the sides of reproductive shoots. Weigel and colleagues in 1992 characterized LEAFY in Arabidopsis and connected it with the earlier identified FLORICAULA gene of Antirrhinum. In strong leafy mutants, structures that should become flowers often retain features of shoots, especially at earlier positions of the inflorescence. Later structures may show varying degrees of floral differentiation, illustrating that the absence of one regulator need not abolish every parallel developmental input. The relationship between the corresponding genes in the two model plants provided evidence that important components of floral identity regulation had been conserved during evolution.

Studies of APETALA1 revealed a different combination of defects. The name refers to a conspicuous floral phenotype, including abnormal outer floral organs and reduced or missing petals in common mutant descriptions. The affected structures may also produce secondary flowers in the axils of bract-like first-whorl organs. This branching behavior is evidence that the gene participates in establishing or maintaining the identity of the flower as a reproductive unit, rather than only specifying the appearance of an individual petal. Naming a gene after a visible feature is convenient for identification but may obscure its broader functions. The appropriate way to assign a developmental role is to examine the full phenotype, including the developmental stages preceding the final organ defects.

A second closely related gene, CAULIFLOWER, proved particularly informative because its activity partly overlaps APETALA1. A mutation of CAL alone may produce relatively little obvious disturbance in an otherwise functional genetic background. When both AP1 and CAL functions are impaired, however, repeated meristem proliferation produces a cauliflower-like cluster rather than the normal immediate production of flowers. The double mutant exposes a regulatory contribution that can be masked by the activity of a related gene. The classical genetic and molecular investigation by Kempin, Savidge and Yanofsky in 1995 established the relatedness of the two genes and their overlapping contributions to the formation of floral meristems.

This is a case of partial functional redundancy, in which more than one gene contributes to the same developmental outcome. Redundancy need not mean that both genes have identical expression patterns, molecular partners or strength of activity. The fact that an ap1 mutant has a phenotype despite the presence of CAL indicates that CAL cannot substitute fully for all AP1 functions. At the same time, the much stronger meristem proliferation in the double mutant reveals that CAL provides important overlapping activity. The apparent severity of a single mutant therefore depends on the contribution of other genes in the same tissue and on the conditions under which the plant is grown.

Subsequent experiments examined the relationship between LFY and AP1 more directly. Wagner, Sablowski and Meyerowitz reported in 1999 that activation of LFY could induce AP1 transcription in appropriate developmental tissues even when synthesis of new proteins was inhibited. This result supported a direct transcriptional relationship because it reduced the need to propose an intervening newly synthesized activator. It did not mean that AP1 expression is independent of every pre-existing protein or of developmental competence. Rather, the experiment refined a broad genetic relationship into a more immediate regulatory model. The distinction between necessity, sufficiency and direct regulation will recur throughout this chapter.

Later work on interactions among meristem identity genes showed that floral and inflorescence regulatory activities are not simply independent switches. Studies in 1999 connected the spatial exclusion of TFL1 transcription from floral meristems with the activity of floral identity regulators. This suggested that the initial distinction between the center and periphery could be reinforced by regulatory interactions. More recent molecular evidence has clarified how the related proteins FT and TFL1 can influence shared target genes through the transcription factor FD. Together these findings illustrate how a developmental model grows through several kinds of evidence: morphology identifies the affected event, genetics reveals required activities, expression studies locate those activities, and molecular experiments test the proposed regulatory relationships.

5. LEAFY and the switch to a flower-producing center

LEAFY, abbreviated LFY, encodes a plant transcription factor. A transcription factor is a protein that affects the expression of genes, commonly by recognizing regulatory DNA or participating in a complex that does so. Its developmental role depends on the target genes available in the tissue where it is active. LFY is not itself a floral organ or a structural component of the petal. Its activity helps establish the gene-expression state in which a young meristem can follow the flower developmental program. Several downstream regulators then execute aspects of that program, from stabilization of floral fate to the specification of particular regions within the flower.

LFY activity increases in association with the reproductive transition and is prominent in developing floral primordia. The increase occurs in the context of other regulatory inputs that reflect developmental age, inductive signals and local position. This timing is crucial. The lateral growth center must acquire floral identity before initiating a full set of regionally patterned floral organs. A factor whose expression precedes the activation of floral organ identity genes is well placed to control this earlier developmental step. Temporal precedence is consistent with a causal role, but by itself it does not demonstrate that one gene regulates another. Loss-of-function experiments and direct tests of target activation are needed to establish the relationship more convincingly.

A strong loss-of-function lfy mutation reduces the ability of lateral meristems to adopt normal floral identity. In an early position of the reproductive shoot, a structure expected to produce floral organs may instead produce leaves or bract-like organs and additional growing centers. Later positions can retain more floral characteristics. The genotype of the plant is the same at each position, so variation along the axis shows that other factors change with developmental time or location. Increasing activity of parallel floral-promoting pathways as the plant matures can contribute to this difference. It is therefore inaccurate to state that lfy plants must invariably lack all floral organs at every node.

The developmental contrast between a normal and an altered primordium becomes clearer when the structures are compared at equal stages. A wild-type floral primordium organizes its growth around the formation of the finite floral organ set. In a strongly affected lfy primordium, continued generation of shoot-like structures can instead produce a branch. A branch and a flower have related developmental origins as products of shoot meristem activity, but their identities cause them to generate different descendants. The phenotype is a change in what the newly formed meristem does, not merely a reduction in its rate of cell division. A growth-rate defect alone would not predict the systematic replacement of flowers by branching structures.

In a complementary gain-of-function experiment, a regulator is expressed earlier, more strongly or in a different location from its usual domain. Ectopic expression means expression outside the normal spatial or temporal pattern. Classic experiments with LFY have shown that increased or ectopic activity can accelerate floral development and favor conversion of shoot meristems toward floral identity under suitable conditions. Such findings support the capacity of LFY to promote the fate rather than acting only as a passive marker. However, the interpretation must include the tissue context and any associated changes in timing. A successful conversion in a competent shoot apex does not imply that isolated root epidermal cells would execute the same developmental program simply because they express LFY.

LFY has a relationship with subsequent organ identity mechanisms as well. Its expression and regulatory action contribute to the activation of downstream floral programs, including AP1 and, with appropriate partners, regional organ identity genes. A broad floral regulator can therefore influence a gene whose expression is restricted to a particular part of the flower. Spatial restriction can be achieved by requiring an additional regulator present only in that part or by repression in the other regions. This explains how the presence of LFY throughout much of a developing floral meristem is compatible with the later emergence of several organ types rather than a uniform floral organ.

The practical distinction between a master regulator and a solitary regulator is important. LFY is sometimes called a master regulator because it is capable of strongly influencing a developmental program at an early stage and regulates multiple downstream processes. The term does not mean that all other genes are unnecessary. Partial phenotypes in lfy mutants, interactions with AP1 and CAL, and context-dependent responses to LFY expression all show that floral identity emerges from a network. A productive model retains LFY as a major determinant while allowing additional activities to modify the threshold, stability and completeness of the floral program. This interpretation provides a more reliable basis for predicting double mutants than treating the entire developmental process as a one-gene switch.
