1. From detection to defense

A bacterium crossing a damaged epithelial surface encounters a very different environment from the one in which it had been living. In a tissue, it can encounter cells capable of engulfing it, soluble molecules that bind its surface, and chemical conditions that interfere with its metabolism. Yet these protective mechanisms cannot be present at their maximum intensity everywhere and all the time. A molecule that damages a bacterial membrane might also damage a host membrane. An enzyme capable of digesting microbial proteins might digest the extracellular matrix. The biological problem is therefore not simply how to recognize an invader, but how to deploy destructive activity at an appropriate place and time, and how to withdraw it after its useful work is finished.

Recognition and effector action are distinguishable stages. A pattern recognition receptor detects a molecular feature associated with microbes or injured tissue. Its engagement can change the behavior of the cell bearing it. The resulting behavior might include ingestion, secretion of a messenger, altered adhesion, or increased synthesis of an antimicrobial substance. The receptor does not have to participate directly in the chemical reaction that destroys the microbe. For instance, a macrophage that detects bacterial products may release a cytokine which acts upon endothelial cells. The resulting vascular change can permit another cell, a neutrophil, to reach the bacterial population. Recognition by one cell thus helps organize an effector action performed by another.

The location of a threat strongly influences the appropriate defensive strategy. An extracellular bacterial cell is an object that can be surrounded by a phagocyte and enclosed within a membrane-bound organelle. A virus replicating within an epithelial cell is much less accessible to a phagocyte while that host cell remains intact. Eliminating an infected host cell can interrupt viral replication and remove the source of infectious progeny. Natural killer cells are suited to recognizing and eliminating certain abnormal host cells, whereas professional phagocytes are particularly effective against many extracellular particles. These distinctions concern the compartment occupied by the threat, not an absolute rule that one leukocyte type protects exclusively against one pathogen class.

The response also has a spatial problem. A small injury initially involves a limited number of resident sentinel cells, while a large reserve of circulating leukocytes may be available elsewhere in the body. Direct contact between resident cells and distant leukocytes is not needed to initiate recruitment. Diffusible mediators, endothelial adhesion molecules and chemotactic signals together translate a local disturbance into a route by which additional cells leave the blood. This tissue-level process is called inflammation. Inflammation includes changes in circulation, vascular permeability, cellular composition and mediator production; it is not a synonym for infection, nor does it mean simply the presence of a neutrophil.

Finally, successful local defense does not require that all information remain local. A dendritic cell can carry evidence of what it encountered into a lymphoid organ, where rare lymphocytes with suitable antigen receptors may be found. The dendritic cell's state provides contextual information that helps determine the lymphocyte response. This makes the innate response a decision-making partner of adaptive immunity rather than merely a temporary substitute for it. To understand the later chapters on antibody and T-cell responses, it is necessary first to understand how a local biological disturbance is translated into cellular recruitment, target destruction and communication with lymphocytes.

2. How cellular defense became an experimental science

Before the molecular basis of immunity was known, investigators faced a basic interpretive difficulty: collections of white blood cells were visible at sites of injury, but their presence did not itself show whether they caused disease or protected the organism. The same microscopic observation could support opposite explanations. If white cells arrived after damage, they might be reacting to injury rather than causing it. If they contained microbial material, this could reflect a defensive action or an incidental association with material already dying. Distinguishing these alternatives required examining the behavior of living cells and the fate of the particles they encountered.

Mechnikov's work on phagocytosis established an influential cellular account of defense. His observations of mobile cells in comparatively simple animals and his subsequent studies of interactions between leukocytes and microbes shifted attention from static tissue appearances to active cell behavior. In his 1908 Nobel lecture, he discussed cell movement, uptake and the fate of ingested infectious agents as connected processes. The importance of this evidence was not that all protection could be reduced to ingestion. Rather, it established that cells could themselves perform protective work and that their recruitment to an injured or infected region could have a functional explanation.

The historical contrast between cellular and humoral explanations illustrates why modern immunology requires both. A soluble factor that binds an organism might have little direct killing activity but greatly enhance recognition by a phagocyte. Conversely, a powerful phagocyte can fail to ingest an organism whose surface is poorly recognized. The soluble factor and the cell do not compete as explanations; they solve different stages of the same problem. This insight prepares the distinction between opsonization and the subsequent intracellular mechanisms of killing. It also explains why a serum-dependent improvement in ingestion does not establish that the serum itself killed the ingested particles.

A second important step was the identification of a specialized cell whose function could not be inferred from its shape alone. Steinman and Cohn reported a distinct dendritic cell population in mouse peripheral lymphoid organs in 1973. Morphological features and tissue distribution provided evidence that the cells were not simply another appearance of familiar macrophages. Later functional experiments established the extraordinary ability of dendritic cells to stimulate T lymphocytes. The distinction between identifying a cellular population and establishing its physiological role is essential. A cell with branched processes is not automatically an effective primer of naive T cells; its origin, receptor repertoire and activation state must be evaluated.

The study of natural killer cells revealed another kind of defensive logic. In 1986, Kärre and colleagues reported preferential rejection of lymphoma variants deficient in mouse major histocompatibility complex class I expression. A conventional antigen-specific T-cell model alone would not predict that reducing a molecule required for many T-cell recognition events would necessarily make a cell more vulnerable. The results supported a complementary recognition strategy in which loss of normal self-identifying molecules can remove an inhibitory influence on NK cells. Later receptor studies supplied molecular mechanisms for this behavior. The historical experiment should not be interpreted as proving that any MHC-deficient cell will be destroyed; activating input and NK-cell competence also matter.

These examples share a methodological principle. A proposed immune mechanism must explain a comparison between appropriately controlled conditions. The amount of material inside a phagocyte cannot alone establish that the material is dead. Expression of an activation marker cannot alone establish that a dendritic cell initiated a specific lymphocyte response. A decrease in surviving target cells does not establish which killer cell acted or whether the target had become intrinsically fragile. By measuring distinct steps separately and changing one relevant variable at a time, experiments convert plausible descriptions into testable explanations.

3. Professional phagocytes: division of labor

A professional phagocyte is a cell adapted to engulf particulate material and regulate its fate. Neutrophils and macrophages are major examples. Dendritic cells can also capture particles, although in many contexts their distinctive contribution is the acquisition and display of antigenic information. These cells have different lifetimes, tissue distributions and priorities. It is more informative to ask what task each cell is equipped to perform than to memorize a single list of functions that appears to assign all forms of uptake, killing and signaling equally to every leukocyte.

Neutrophils are granulocytes, so named because their cytoplasm contains prominent granules with stored proteins. A mature human neutrophil typically has a nucleus divided into several connected lobes. This appearance distinguishes it from a monocyte in a conventional stained blood preparation, although the functional properties of a cell cannot be established from nuclear shape alone. Many neutrophils are available in the circulation and marrow reserve, allowing a rapid increase in delivery to an inflamed site. Their stored antimicrobial components allow a rapid response without waiting for every effector molecule to be synthesized after recognition. This is valuable during an acute increase in microbial burden, when the time needed to expand a specialized lymphocyte population may be comparatively long.

Granules are not all identical storage compartments. Primary or azurophilic granules contain substances such as myeloperoxidase and proteases; other granule populations contain different antimicrobial proteins and membrane components. Controlled delivery of these contents to a newly formed phagosome brings chemically distinct activities into proximity with the engulfed particle. A phagocyte that released its full granule contents indiscriminately into the blood would waste resources and expose neighboring cells to injury. Regulation of granule traffic is therefore as significant for defensive specificity as the ability of the individual granule proteins to damage microbes.

Macrophages often operate within tissues before large-scale recruitment begins. Their cytoplasm and endolysosomal system support ingestion and degradation of diverse cargo, from microbial particles to dying host cells and extracellular debris. Macrophages also produce mediators that influence neighboring endothelial cells, fibroblasts and leukocytes. Depending on tissue location and environmental signals, they may participate in sustained antimicrobial defense or contribute to repair. A tissue macrophage is not merely a long-lived neutrophil: the priorities and regulatory programs of the two cells are different, even where they share an oxidase or a receptor involved in phagocytosis.

Blood monocytes can enter tissues and acquire macrophage-like or dendritic-like functions under appropriate conditions. However, the statement that every tissue macrophage comes from a circulating adult monocyte is too broad. Several tissue-resident macrophage populations are established during development and can maintain themselves locally. Inflammation can recruit additional monocyte-derived cells, with the magnitude and persistence of that contribution depending on the tissue and the stimulus. This distinction prevents confusion between a developmental lineage and a functional state observed during inflammation.

Dendritic cells encounter material in peripheral tissues and in lymphoid organs. Many resting conventional dendritic cells are efficient at acquiring antigens, including soluble substances and particles. Encountering microbial signals can alter their priorities, increasing their capacity to stimulate lymphocytes and promoting migration where appropriate. This is not a universal loss of all uptake activity, nor does every dendritic cell migrate after activation. Some resident lymphoid dendritic cells encounter antigen delivered by other routes. Their hallmark is the capacity to integrate antigen acquisition with the signals that govern the initiation and character of a T-cell response.

The differences among these cells have experimental implications. Suppose two cell types ingest equal numbers of fluorescent bacteria, but one causes a greater fall in the number of viable bacteria while the other induces stronger proliferation of antigen-specific naive T cells. The uptake measurement establishes a shared capacity to acquire particles. The viability assay and lymphocyte proliferation assay establish different downstream functions. A useful comparison would normalize cell numbers and initial bacterial exposure, account for extracellular organisms and include appropriate negative controls for T-cell activation. Without these controls, greater uptake or a larger cell population could masquerade as specialized killing or presentation.

The defensive roles of neutrophils and macrophages are not restricted to infection. For example, a tissue that experiences mechanical damage generates dying cells and fragments of extracellular material even if no microorganism enters. Macrophages and recruited phagocytes can remove such material, while signals generated by damaged cells help recruit and activate them. When dead cells are removed efficiently without spilling their contents, the tissue can proceed toward restoration. If debris continues to accumulate, danger-associated signals can persist. Thus the same general cellular capability, engulfment, supports different biological outcomes according to the identity of the cargo and the circumstances in which it is taken up.

4. Recognition, attachment and phagosome formation

Phagocytosis is a form of endocytosis adapted to uptake of relatively large particles. It differs from the internalization of individual receptor-bound molecules through small coated vesicles because a substantial area of plasma membrane must be extended around a particle. The size and geometry of a bacterium, yeast cell or cellular fragment impose mechanical constraints on the engulfing cell. The membrane must remain continuous throughout the process, while the cytoskeleton supplies the force and organization required to construct a cup around the cargo. At the end, the particle is separated from the extracellular environment by a closed host-derived membrane.

The first stable contact depends on molecular recognition. Some receptors bind structures on microbial surfaces directly. Others recognize host-derived molecules that have already attached to a target. The latter molecules are called opsonins when their presence promotes uptake. Antibody of a suitable class can provide Fc regions that are recognized by phagocyte receptors; fragments of activated complement can provide ligands for complement receptors. The detailed generation of complement opsonins belongs to the next chapter. For now, the important structural relationship is that the coating creates a bridge between a particle and a receptor on the phagocyte, so the phagocyte need not itself have a receptor for every molecular feature of the particle.

A pattern recognition receptor and a phagocytic receptor are categories based on function, not necessarily mutually exclusive families of molecules. Some receptors both recognize a microbial feature and support uptake. Others primarily transmit signals that alter transcription, secretion or responsiveness to additional stimuli. A Toll-like receptor detecting bacterial material should not be assumed to serve as the mechanical attachment receptor that directly drives engulfment. If deletion of a sensing receptor diminishes inflammatory cytokines without reducing measured particle internalization, the observation is entirely compatible with recognition and uptake being accomplished by different receptor systems.

Multiple receptor-ligand interactions can stabilize contact with a large particle. The cell must then rearrange its cortical actin, a network beneath the plasma membrane, to shape a growing phagocytic cup. Local activation of actin-regulating proteins promotes polymerization and the movement of membrane around the object. The exact architecture and signaling differ according to receptor and cargo. A simplified zipper model is useful for understanding how successive receptor engagements can support extension along a coated surface. It is not an assertion that all phagocytic mechanisms require identical sequential contacts. In particular, different complement and Fc receptor pathways can produce different patterns of membrane movement.

The availability of membrane also constrains uptake. Engulfing a large particle consumes surface area in a way that internalizing a small soluble ligand does not. Membrane rearrangement and delivery from intracellular stores can support an expanding cup. When opposite edges meet, membrane remodeling closes the compartment. A particle at the surface, a particle partially surrounded by a cup and a particle fully enclosed in a phagosome are therefore three distinct physical situations. A static micrograph may fail to distinguish them, especially when a fluorescent object lies in the projected image of the cell. Experiments designed to measure uptake must demonstrate internalization rather than simple overlap.

Actin-dependent engulfment consumes cellular energy through the cycling of cytoskeletal and regulatory systems. ATP is used in actin turnover and motor activity, among other processes, and GTP-binding regulatory proteins coordinate local changes. This does not mean that a universal number of ATP molecules is expended for each bacterium. Particle size, receptor distribution, membrane tension and the cell's prior state all influence the required work. The useful thermodynamic principle is that the cell actively organizes membrane and cytoskeleton in a non-equilibrium process rather than relying on a bacterium to diffuse spontaneously into a membrane-bound internal compartment.

Once a particle has been enclosed, its fate is still undecided. Many microbes remain viable immediately after ingestion. Others are killed rapidly, and some have evolved mechanisms to alter the intracellular compartment in which they reside. A cell that displays normal ingestion but deficient microbial killing can therefore show recurrent infectious susceptibility even though microscopic phagocytosis appears normal. This distinction leads directly to the next mechanism: the conversion of a newly formed phagosome into a compartment with appropriate chemical and enzymatic activities.

5. Phagosome maturation and non-oxidative killing

A newly closed phagosome inherits much of its membrane from the cell surface, but its function changes as it interacts with intracellular trafficking systems. In a macrophage, early phagosomes commonly acquire characteristics of early endosomal compartments before progressing toward a later state that communicates with lysosomes. Small regulatory GTPases such as Rab5 and Rab7 are associated with different stages of this progression. They help coordinate the recruitment of proteins required for appropriate membrane identity and transport. Their sequential association is a useful marker of maturation, although the organelle is not defined by a single protein and the precise sequence can vary with the cargo.

The lysosome is a membrane-bound organelle rich in degradative enzymes. Fusion of a maturing phagosome with lysosomal compartments can deliver these enzymes into the space occupied by the ingested particle. A vacuolar proton-pumping ATPase moves protons into the lumen, establishing a tendency toward acidification. An acidic environment favors the activity of many lysosomal hydrolases and can challenge microbial mechanisms that maintain intracellular pH. The proton gradient requires energy, because maintaining a difference in proton electrochemical potential across a membrane cannot occur indefinitely without active transport or another source of free energy.

The term phagolysosome describes the mature degradative compartment formed through interaction of the phagosome with lysosomes. It is not simply a second particle that enters the cell and joins the bacterium. Both compartments are parts of the cell's membrane-trafficking network, and fusion permits their luminal contents to mix while retaining a boundary with the cytoplasm. The resulting local environment concentrates enzymes and other antimicrobial agents near the cargo. A membrane that preserves this separation helps protect the host's own cytosolic proteins and other organelles from unnecessary exposure to those agents.

Neutrophil phagosomes deserve a qualification. They receive material from pre-existing granules very rapidly, and their early ionic and pH properties can differ substantially from those of macrophage phagolysosomes. In particular, intense oxidase activity can contribute to an initially alkaline neutrophil phagosome. Thus the useful textbook association between lysosomal acidification and digestion must not be turned into a claim that all professional phagocytes establish the same pH immediately after engulfment. The antimicrobial environment results from the combined effects of ion movement, enzyme delivery, oxidase activity and time. Different antimicrobial proteins have different preferred chemical conditions.

Lysozyme can cleave bonds within bacterial peptidoglycan, weakening a structural component of many bacterial cell walls. Its effectiveness depends on accessibility; the outer membrane of many Gram-negative bacteria can restrict access to underlying peptidoglycan. Other antimicrobial proteins can disturb membranes, bind essential microbial nutrients, or cleave proteins. Lactoferrin, for example, can bind iron and help restrict its availability. A microbial cell requires suitable ion balance, intact membranes, functional proteins and access to nutrients. Disturbing multiple requirements at once can produce stronger inhibition than interfering with a single requirement in isolation.

Proteases delivered by granules or lysosomes hydrolyze peptide bonds and can disrupt bacterial structures and virulence-associated proteins. Some antimicrobial peptides interact with microbial membrane surfaces and impair membrane integrity. The effects of these agents depend on the composition of the target and the chemical properties of the phagosomal lumen. It would be inaccurate to attribute every failure of microbial growth inside phagocytes to protein digestion alone. In some organisms, membrane damage precedes enzymatic degradation; in others, restriction of nutrients contributes to failure to replicate. Conversely, a particle may be killed while still retaining recognizable structures for a substantial time.

The consequences of interference with maturation can be studied by distinguishing uptake, luminal acidification, lysosomal cargo delivery and microbial viability. Imagine cells exposed to a mutation affecting a trafficking regulator. If the number of internalized bacteria is normal, but delivery of a labeled lysosomal protein is reduced and bacterial survival increases, a downstream defect is plausible. The conclusion would be stronger if restoration of the normal regulator restored both lysosomal delivery and killing. The observations would not by themselves demonstrate that acidity was the sole missing factor, because fusion also changes enzyme concentration, membrane proteins and ionic properties. Measurements of pH and enzyme activity would distinguish these possibilities.

These mechanisms also explain why phagosome maturation is an important target of microbial adaptations. An intracellular organism that delays delivery of harmful luminal contents or survives within an altered compartment can persist despite efficient capture. This is a brief mechanistic connection to later study of bacterial infections, rather than a universal property of pathogens. For the current chapter the central distinction is between the cellular decision to engulf and the subsequent establishment of an effective antimicrobial compartment. To understand killing fully, the degradative functions of that compartment must be considered alongside oxygen-dependent chemistry.

6. The respiratory burst: producing reactive oxygen species

A phagocyte does not rely exclusively on enzymes that hydrolyze biological polymers. It can also generate chemically reactive derivatives of oxygen. Oxygen in its ordinary molecular form is essential to aerobic metabolism, yet conversion into particular reactive species can create conditions incompatible with the survival of a microorganism. The challenge is to produce these substances at useful locations without exposing all host structures to the same chemical stress. In phagocytes, a regulated membrane-associated enzyme complex provides a way to direct oxygen reduction toward the compartment containing a target.

The rapid increase in oxygen consumption associated with activation of a phagocyte is often called the respiratory burst or oxidative burst. The word respiratory can be misleading if interpreted as a synonym for mitochondrial ATP production. An activated neutrophil may consume much more oxygen because a specialized oxidase uses oxygen as an electron acceptor. Although the cell's metabolic state supplies resources for this reaction, the oxidase does not pump protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane to drive ATP synthase. Its immediate function in this context is to transfer electrons and thereby generate reactive species.

The phagocyte NADPH oxidase is commonly designated NOX2 after its catalytic membrane component. A membrane-associated pair includes gp91phox, the NOX2 protein, and p22phox. Several other components are found predominantly in the cytosol of resting cells and join the active complex following suitable stimulation; these include p47phox, p67phox and p40phox, with a small GTPase of the Rac family participating in activation. The architectural principle is more important than memorizing every name: separation of necessary components reduces constitutive activity, whereas regulated assembly permits rapid initiation of electron transfer at a membrane.

NADPH is a reduced pyridine nucleotide carrying electrons that can participate in reductive reactions. The pentose phosphate pathway is an important source of NADPH in phagocytes, linking glucose metabolism to microbial killing. The oxidase transfers reducing equivalents from NADPH on the cytosolic side of the membrane to oxygen on the opposite side. When assembled on a phagosomal membrane, the system can generate superoxide within the phagosomal lumen. Assembly at the plasma membrane can instead generate reactive oxygen species outside the cell. Thus the same enzyme family can have different spatial consequences depending on the membrane at which it is active.

An idealized net equation for the oxidase reaction is NADPH + 2 O₂ → NADP⁺ + H⁺ + 2 O₂•⁻. One NADPH molecule supplies two electrons, and each oxygen molecule accepts one electron to become superoxide. This equation accounts for the electron and proton balance but does not describe all the structural intermediates in electron transfer through the enzyme. Superoxide is an oxygen-derived radical anion; its charge and unpaired electron distinguish it from molecular oxygen. The enzyme does not directly produce two molecules of hydrogen peroxide from NADPH. Hydrogen peroxide arises through further transformations of superoxide.

Superoxide can undergo dismutation, represented by 2 O₂•⁻ + 2 H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. The reaction can occur spontaneously and is accelerated by superoxide dismutase where present. Dismutation converts two radical species into a peroxide molecule and molecular oxygen. Hydrogen peroxide is not itself a free radical, but it is classified among reactive oxygen species and participates in oxidative chemistry. Distinguishing a reactive species from a radical prevents the common error of treating all oxygen-derived antimicrobial agents as chemically identical. Differences in reactivity, lifetime and membrane permeability influence which cellular components each species can affect.

The movement of electrons into a phagosomal compartment also changes electrical charge distribution across its membrane. If electron transfer continued without compensating ion movements, the resulting membrane potential would eventually oppose further electron transfer. Proton conductance through channels such as Hv1 helps support sustained oxidase activity and contributes to charge and pH regulation. Other ion fluxes also affect the compartment. This is one reason why a phagosomal pH cannot be inferred merely by looking at the proton coefficient in the net oxidase reaction: proton consumption and production in subsequent chemistry, active pumps and compensating ion movements must also be considered.

Activated neutrophils can generate intense, localized oxidant production. Macrophages also employ reactive oxygen species, but the magnitude, duration and relationship to other antimicrobial mechanisms depend on their activation state. Nitric oxide generated by inducible nitric oxide synthase can contribute to antimicrobial activity, especially in some activated macrophage systems. Reactive nitrogen chemistry can interact with oxygen-derived radicals, but the contribution of nitric oxide varies with species and context. A model derived from a strongly activated mouse macrophage should not be assumed to give the same quantitative output in every human phagocyte.

Oxidant production depends on available molecular oxygen and reducing equivalents. When oxygen supply is limited, oxygen-dependent reactions face a substrate constraint, even if all oxidase proteins are intact. This does not imply that a phagocyte in a relatively hypoxic tissue becomes wholly inactive. Glycolysis, granule proteins, nutrient restriction and other mechanisms can still contribute to defense. Conversely, the presence of oxygen in an environment does not establish that a cell is undergoing an oxidative burst; an activated oxidase and appropriate electron supply are required. In an experiment, both metabolic environment and receptor stimulation must therefore be considered when interpreting differences in oxidant production.

7. Myeloperoxidase and the integration of microbial killing mechanisms

The hydrogen peroxide generated following oxidase activation can act as a substrate for myeloperoxidase, an enzyme particularly abundant in neutrophil azurophilic granules. Myeloperoxidase contains a heme group that participates in redox catalysis. In the presence of chloride, its activity can generate hypochlorous acid, a powerful oxidant. A simplified balanced expression for the chlorination reaction is H₂O₂ + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → HOCl + H₂O. This equation connects the availability of oxidase-derived peroxide with the presence of a halide and the catalytic activity of a granule enzyme. It does not describe every possible product of myeloperoxidase chemistry.

Hypochlorous acid can react with biological molecules, including sulfur-containing groups in proteins. Such reactions can alter enzyme activity, disrupt microbial structures and interfere with the cellular processes needed for survival. Because the chemistry is reactive rather than directed toward one antigenic epitope, confinement near the microbial target is especially useful. The molecular specificity of the original recognition event and the broad chemical reactivity of the resulting effector are not contradictory. The former determines where the cell acts; the latter helps damage a range of vulnerable components once the target is in an appropriate compartment.

A deficiency of myeloperoxidase is mechanistically distinct from a deficiency of NOX2. If oxidase activity is lost, production of superoxide through that enzyme is impaired and the downstream peroxide supply from that route is reduced. If myeloperoxidase alone is deficient, upstream oxidase activity can remain intact while generation of its characteristic halogenating products is impaired. These cells may still possess other antimicrobial systems. A question asking which product would be directly affected must therefore be answered at the correct position in the reaction sequence. Loss of a downstream enzyme does not by itself remove the capacity of the upstream enzyme to transfer electrons to oxygen.

The phagosomal antimicrobial environment is not simply a container filled with a single disinfectant. Granule-derived proteases, antimicrobial peptides, ion fluxes, oxidative products and nutrient restriction can reinforce or partly compensate for each other. Different bacteria possess different defenses against oxidative and non-oxidative stress. A microbial enzyme that removes hydrogen peroxide may reduce susceptibility to some reactions, but it does not necessarily protect against membrane-active peptides or nutrient limitation. The contribution of any one host mechanism should therefore be judged in relation to the organism, cell type and experimental conditions.

One can understand the importance of integrated killing through a hypothetical factorial experiment. A normal phagocyte population is compared with cells lacking functional oxidase and with cells in which a selected granule protease is inhibited. A fourth group combines both impairments. If each single impairment modestly increases microbial survival but the combined impairment produces a much larger increase, the results suggest that the two pathways contribute complementary protective effects. They do not establish that the pathways are completely independent. Oxidase activity can change the phagosomal ionic environment and thereby affect enzyme activity. Conversely, granule delivery can alter the supply of membrane components as well as luminal proteins. A combined defect must be interpreted within this coupled system.

The relation between chemical damage and microbial survival requires attention to what an assay measures. An increase in oxidation of a fluorescent reporter may indicate exposure to reactive chemistry without proving that an organism is dead. A fall in bacterial colony counts indicates a loss of recoverable colony-forming capacity under the assay conditions but does not identify the precise molecular lesion. Detecting chlorinated microbial proteins can support exposure to myeloperoxidase-related chemistry, yet the most important damaged target may be another molecule. Converging evidence from chemical measurements, viability assays and controlled enzyme perturbations makes a stronger causal argument than any isolated indicator.

The oxidative burst must eventually stop. Continued oxidant generation after useful microbial targets have been removed would expose the phagocyte and neighboring tissue to unnecessary stress. Reversal of activation signals, disassembly or inactivation of enzyme complexes, substrate changes and the cell's eventual fate can contribute to termination. Antioxidant systems in host tissues also limit the consequences of reactive molecules that escape their intended compartments. These protective systems should not be described as completely eliminating all oxidative chemistry. A functioning defense depends on a regulated difference between useful local reactivity and damaging uncontrolled exposure.

8. Separating uptake, oxidative activity and killing in experiments

A useful examination model for the oxidative burst is chronic granulomatous disease, an inherited disorder caused by defects in the phagocyte NADPH oxidase system. The name describes a clinical condition, but the relevant lesson is cellular: phagocytes may recognize and ingest microorganisms yet have deficient oxidant generation and impaired defense against particular organisms. The disease illustrates that normal morphology and normal uptake do not guarantee effective intracellular killing. It also establishes the value of biochemical activity measurements in addition to cell counts.

The nitroblue tetrazolium test is a classical way of demonstrating a change associated with the respiratory burst. Reduction of the dye produces a colored insoluble product in cells with suitable oxidative activity under stimulation. In modern laboratory reasoning, dihydrorhodamine-based assays are often used to assess oxidant-related responses by measuring fluorescence in individual cells. The readout of either test depends on the chemistry of the indicator and the cellular conditions; it is not identical to a direct count of every molecule of superoxide generated. For example, myeloperoxidase activity can influence dihydrorhodamine oxidation, so an abnormal fluorescence pattern must be interpreted with suitable controls rather than automatically assigned to a particular gene.

A common design compares unstimulated and stimulated samples. The unstimulated sample establishes the background signal, while a known competent stimulated sample establishes that the stimulus and detection system can produce the expected response. A test population showing little fluorescence after stimulation might have a defect in oxidase function, but other explanations must initially be considered. The cells might be nonviable, the stimulus might not engage the required signaling route, or the dye might be handled differently. Measuring viability and using a suitable alternative stimulus help distinguish a receptor-proximal defect from a broader failure of the oxidative system.

Suppose a mutant phagocyte responds weakly to a microbial receptor ligand but produces a normal oxidant signal when stimulated by an agent that acts downstream of that receptor. The pattern argues against a complete inability of the oxidase to transfer electrons. Instead, an upstream activation step is implicated, although precise identification would require further evidence. If both types of stimulation fail while cell viability and NADPH supply appear adequate, a lesion in shared activation machinery or the enzyme itself becomes more plausible. This is pathway reasoning: a downstream stimulus can bypass some upstream defects, but cannot rescue the absence of an indispensable component downstream of its point of action.

To quantify antimicrobial function, the experimenter must distinguish extracellular organisms from internalized organisms. Suppose bacterial survival is measured after equal numbers have entered normal and mutant cells. If intracellular viable bacteria persist preferentially in the mutant, the result is consistent with reduced post-engulfment antimicrobial activity. If the starting number of internalized organisms differs, comparing only final colony counts may give a misleading impression. Normalizing survivors to the number initially internalized permits a more informative comparison, provided that the assay recovers organisms similarly from the two groups.

Several other measurements can separate different steps. Surface binding can be distinguished from internalization by using an appropriate method to suppress fluorescence from external particles or by examining optical sections. A pH-sensitive reporter can provide information about the luminal environment. Delivery of a lysosomal marker can help establish interaction with late endosomal or lysosomal compartments. An oxidant reporter can indicate a change in oxidative chemistry. A viable-count assay measures a biologically important downstream outcome. None of these readouts is a substitute for all the others. Their purpose is to locate where a perturbation changes the causal sequence.

Neutrophils can also act outside a sealed phagosome. Degranulation releases selected granule components into the extracellular environment, and in some circumstances neutrophils produce extracellular traps containing chromatin and associated antimicrobial proteins. These structures may help immobilize organisms and concentrate certain effectors near them. However, a trapped bacterium is not necessarily dead, and extracellular chromatin does not by itself prove that a protective trap-forming process occurred. Extracellular DNA can arise from different modes of cell injury and death. Moreover, extracellular release of proteases and other inflammatory substances can harm host tissue, illustrating the cost of effector activity outside a contained compartment.

An example of this trade-off arises when a target is too large to be engulfed completely or lies on a surface that prevents the phagocyte from closing its membrane around it. The cell may engage receptors and release substances without forming a fully enclosed phagosome, a situation often described as frustrated phagocytosis. Reactive material can then act on neighboring extracellular structures. The process illustrates why the same receptor engagement that is beneficial against a small ingestible target may be damaging in another geometric context. The biological outcome depends not only on which signaling pathway is activated, but also on whether the cell can physically contain its effector products.

9. Natural killer cells and the logic of altered-self recognition

When a virus enters a host cell, the viral proteins and nucleic acids are initially separated from the extracellular environment by the host cell membrane. Destroying extracellular virus particles is useful, but it may not eliminate a cell that is actively producing more of them. Natural killer cells, commonly abbreviated NK cells, are innate lymphocytes capable of eliminating selected abnormal host cells and releasing signaling molecules that influence other immune cells. Their cellular appearance and cytotoxic potential might suggest that they are simply a subtype of conventional cytotoxic T cell, but their mechanisms of target recognition are fundamentally different.

A conventional cytotoxic T lymphocyte recognizes an appropriate peptide associated with a major histocompatibility complex class I molecule using a clonally rearranged T-cell receptor. NK cells do not use this conventional rearranged antigen receptor. Instead, their activation depends on a repertoire of germline-encoded receptors that detect combinations of ligands on potential targets and signals in their surroundings. In humans, many NK cells express inhibitory killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors, or KIRs, with particular specificities for class I HLA molecules. Another important inhibitory receptor complex includes CD94 and NKG2A, which recognizes HLA-E. Mouse NK-cell receptor families include inhibitory Ly49 receptors; the human and mouse systems should not be treated as lists of interchangeable gene names.

Major histocompatibility complex class I molecules are displayed by most nucleated host cells. Some NK receptors respond to appropriate self class I molecules by delivering an inhibitory signal. Such recognition provides information that the target has retained particular normal features. The target can also display activating ligands, especially when stressed, infected or transformed. An activating receptor such as NKG2D can recognize certain stress-associated host molecules. The decision to kill reflects the integration of these opposing influences, rather than the binding of one receptor to a unique foreign antigen in every encounter.

The term missing self describes a situation in which reduced or absent expression of relevant self class I ligands removes an inhibitory influence. This is biologically significant because some infected or transformed cells reduce class I display, potentially avoiding recognition by cytotoxic T cells. Reduced class I can simultaneously make these cells more susceptible to appropriate NK-cell responses. It is a complementary defense strategy: an abnormal cell cannot necessarily avoid all immune surveillance simply by losing a structure that one recognition mechanism requires. However, loss of inhibition is permissive rather than an unconditional instruction to kill.

Consider three targets with comparable viability and size. The first retains appropriate self class I and has little activating ligand. The second has reduced class I and abundant activating ligand. The third also has reduced class I but presents very little activating ligand. A competent NK population may show stronger killing of the second target than the first or third. The comparison demonstrates two dimensions of recognition. If the second and third differ only in the activating ligand, their different behavior cannot be explained by missing self alone. If restoring a relevant class I ligand to the second target reduces killing, the effect supports a role for inhibition in that particular receptor-ligand combination.

An important additional idea is induced-self recognition. Stress can increase the display of host molecules that engage activating receptors even when class I is not completely lost. Such activating input may, in some situations, outweigh inhibitory signals. This explains why a target with normal total class I expression can sometimes be susceptible to NK-cell killing. Total class I is not a universal numerical measure of inhibition: the particular class I alleles, their expression, and the inhibitory receptors expressed by the responding NK cells are relevant. A target may express class I molecules that do not engage a particular NK cell's inhibitory receptors effectively.

The capacity to respond is itself regulated. During development and maturation, interactions between NK-cell inhibitory receptors and self ligands can help calibrate functional responsiveness, a process often termed NK-cell education. An NK cell lacking appropriate engagement of self-specific inhibitory receptors is not necessarily constitutively aggressive toward every normal cell. It may exhibit reduced responsiveness under some conditions. Cytokines can alter this state and support activation. At the examination level, the important consequence is that NK cells cannot be modeled simply as cells in which removal of any inhibitory receptor inevitably produces maximum activity under all conditions.

Mature erythrocytes offer a useful warning against an overgeneralized missing-self rule. They are enucleated and lack the typical class I expression pattern of nucleated cells, but they are not normally eliminated by NK cells merely for this reason. The absence of an inhibitory ligand alone does not provide the appropriate combination of activating signals, cell contacts and effector context. The example helps separate a conditional principle of target recognition from an incorrect universal rule that every class I-negative cell must be killed.

10. Cytotoxic synapse, perforin, granzymes and NK-cell coordination

Once the balance of signals favors attack, a natural killer cell must deliver its cytotoxic machinery to the appropriate target while limiting collateral injury. Contact between NK and target cell creates an organized interface often called an immune or cytotoxic synapse. Adhesion and cytoskeletal changes stabilize the contact, and the NK cell can polarize its secretory machinery toward the interface. This geometry is analogous in principle to directional secretion elsewhere in cell biology, but the cargo here includes proteins capable of initiating death in another cell. Concentrating release at the interface improves spatial control.

Cytotoxic granules contain perforin and granzymes, among other components. Perforin can associate with target membranes in a calcium-dependent manner and form membrane pores. Granzymes are proteases that must reach relevant intracellular substrates in the target to exert their characteristic cytotoxic effects. Perforin facilitates their access to the target cytosol, through mechanisms involving membrane permeabilization and, in some settings, endosomal delivery and escape. A simplified textbook diagram often shows granzymes passing through pores. This is a useful illustration of the dependence on perforin but should not be mistaken for a complete account of every physical route by which granzymes enter the target cytosol.

Granzyme B can activate or engage pathways involving caspases and mitochondrial apoptotic machinery, producing regulated cell death. The target is not simply digested externally by the NK cell. Intracellular reactions within the target contribute to its destruction. Other granzyme activities and death-receptor pathways can also participate in NK-mediated cytotoxicity. For instance, appropriately expressed Fas ligand on an effector can engage Fas on a susceptible target and initiate death-receptor signaling. The relative contribution of granule-mediated and receptor-mediated killing depends on the target and the state of the effector.

The distinction between degranulation and killing is especially important in an experimental setting. Cytotoxic granules have membrane proteins that become exposed on the NK-cell surface when granule membranes fuse with the plasma membrane. Surface CD107a is commonly used as an indicator of degranulation. If a perforin-deficient NK cell releases granule contents, it may display this indicator while exhibiting impaired granule-dependent killing. Conversely, a cell with intact perforin that fails to form a productive target contact may have little directed release. Measuring both degranulation and target-cell death helps locate defects in the sequence.

NK cells also release cytokines. Interferon gamma can influence macrophage antimicrobial activity and other aspects of immunity. Signals such as IL-12 and IL-18 from activated innate cells can promote interferon gamma production by NK cells, while IL-15 is important for their development, maintenance and responsiveness. Type I interferons can contribute to antiviral activation states. A tissue can therefore employ a cooperative network: sentinel cells detect infection, cytokines increase the readiness of NK cells, and NK-derived cytokines influence the behavior of phagocytes and antigen-presenting cells. The network does not imply that every cytokine has only one producing cell or one possible responding cell.

Another route of NK activation illustrates cooperation with adaptive immunity. Many human NK cells express CD16, an Fc receptor capable of binding the Fc portion of suitable IgG antibodies associated with a target. The antibodies confer target recognition through their antigen-binding regions while NK cells supply cytotoxic effector activity. This mechanism is called antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. It differs from missing-self recognition because a target-bound antibody provides an activating bridge. It also differs from neutralization, in which antibody binding directly interferes with a pathogen or toxin's function without requiring this cytotoxic effector cell.

Comparison with cytotoxic T lymphocytes reveals how evolution can employ related effector machinery under different recognition systems. Both cell types can polarize granules and use perforin and granzymes. A conventional cytotoxic T cell's specificity depends on its rearranged T-cell receptor and appropriate peptide–MHC recognition. An NK cell integrates germline receptor signals and cytokine context, with antibody-directed activation possible through Fc receptors. Inhibiting perforin may impair granule-dependent killing by both cells even though the upstream signals that select their targets differ. In contrast, a mutation that prevents conventional T-cell receptor rearrangement affects the adaptive recognition system without directly deleting NK-cell germline receptors.

Natural killer cells are not synonymous with natural killer T cells. NKT cells are a distinct group of T lymphocytes with antigen receptors and characteristic recognition properties; their name reflects overlapping phenotypic features rather than identity with conventional NK cells. For the current topic, the essential distinction is receptor logic. NK cells illustrate the adaptive value of recognizing abnormal cellular states quickly without waiting for the expansion of a new antigen-specific conventional T-cell clone. This rapid response buys time and can shape the inflammatory environment in which a later adaptive response develops.

11. How a local inflammatory response begins

The normal internal environment of a tissue is maintained through continuous exchange between blood, interstitial fluid and lymph. Resident cells obtain nutrients and oxygen, remove waste products and communicate with one another. The endothelium, the cell layer lining the interior of blood vessels, participates actively in controlling this exchange. Under ordinary conditions most circulating leukocytes do not leave the blood indiscriminately. They encounter many vascular surfaces during circulation, but relatively few sites provide the combination of signals necessary for substantial recruitment. An inflammatory response changes this local pattern of exchange and movement.

Consider a small sterile injury. Cells damaged by mechanical force may release intracellular molecules into the extracellular space, where their appearance can indicate loss of tissue integrity. Resident immune cells and other tissue cells can detect these damage-associated molecular patterns. If bacteria are introduced into the wound, their molecular components provide additional pathogen-associated signals. Both types of stimulus can promote inflammation, although the precise receptor systems and resulting mediator mixtures may differ. Hence microbial infection is one cause of inflammation, not its definition. The observation of redness and swelling cannot, by itself, establish that a tissue contains a pathogen.

An important property of sentinel cells is their ability to convert a molecular encounter into signals that reach other cell types. A resident macrophage may produce cytokines after receptor stimulation. A mast cell can release stored mediators such as histamine following appropriate activation. Epithelial cells and stromal cells can also contribute signaling substances. These responses need not begin at exactly the same time. Release of a preformed mediator can occur rapidly, whereas synthesis of a new protein cytokine requires transcription, translation and secretion. Lipid mediators can be synthesized rapidly from cellular lipid precursors. The early response therefore contains multiple time scales rather than a single instantaneous burst of all possible inflammatory substances.

The cellular distribution of receptors helps determine where a mediator acts. A molecule released near a blood vessel may act upon endothelial cells or vascular smooth muscle. A chemokine encountered by a passing neutrophil may modify the cell's adhesion state. Another cytokine may increase synthesis of antimicrobial proteins in a macrophage. A tissue that contains the mediator but lacks the relevant responding receptor cannot produce the same effect through that route. This distinction is particularly useful when interpreting gene-deletion questions: absence of a receptor in the target cell is mechanistically different from failure of the source cell to produce the ligand, even though both can reduce a particular downstream response.

Inflammation is a coordinated tissue response, not an additional category of antigen receptor. The same inflammatory process may be promoted by signals from innate receptors, complement fragments or an adaptive immune response. For example, activated lymphocytes can release cytokines that sustain macrophage activation or recruit other leukocytes. The current chapter concentrates on how the response is initiated by innate mechanisms. Later chapters explain how lymphocytes and antibodies exploit or sometimes dysregulate the same effector machinery.

The inflammatory response must also discriminate between a transient disturbance and a persistent one. A brief stimulus that is removed quickly can initiate a response that declines once the local threat and debris have been cleared. A sustained source of microbial or damage-associated signals can maintain mediator production and continued recruitment. Even after microbes are no longer viable, damaged tissue can supply signals that prolong the response. The persistence of inflammation is therefore not an unambiguous measure of microbial replication. Distinguishing the initiating stimulus from the mechanisms that maintain tissue injury will become important when considering resolution.

12. Cytokines, chemokines, histamine and lipid mediators

A cytokine is a signaling protein that alters the behavior of responsive cells. Cytokines can influence activation, differentiation, survival, protein synthesis and communication. Some act predominantly over relatively short distances; some can have effects throughout the organism when they reach sufficient concentrations in circulation. The same cytokine can act on several cell types, a property often termed pleiotropy. Several different cytokines can also contribute to similar outcomes, a form of functional redundancy. These properties explain why a biological response cannot always be predicted simply by counting how many named cytokines are present.

Tumor necrosis factor, commonly abbreviated TNF, and interleukin-1 are important examples of inflammatory cytokines. They can promote an activated endothelial state in which expression of adhesion molecules and production of other mediators are altered. Such changes increase the likelihood that circulating leukocytes will be captured and recruited at the affected site. This role does not mean that TNF is itself an adhesion molecule. The cytokine acts through its own receptors and changes the abundance or function of molecules involved in cell adhesion. A defect in a downstream adhesion molecule might therefore impair recruitment even when the tissue contains high concentrations of TNF.

Interleukin-6 can contribute to communication between an inflammatory site and distant organs, including the induction of acute-phase protein production in the liver. Some acute-phase proteins participate in host defense or modify inflammatory processes. This illustrates an endocrine-like aspect of immune communication: a local event can change the synthesis of proteins in a distant tissue. The liver does not need to encounter the same microorganism directly to respond to appropriate circulating signals. Nevertheless, the concentration and duration of circulating mediators matter, and a localized response does not inevitably generate a strong systemic acute-phase response.

Chemokines form a family of cytokines especially important in directing or regulating cell movement. CXCL8, also known as interleukin-8, is a representative human chemokine that promotes neutrophil recruitment through receptors including CXCR1 and CXCR2. CCL2 is commonly associated with recruitment of monocytes expressing its receptor CCR2. These are useful examples rather than absolute cell-type assignments. Receptor expression varies with the cell's state, and multiple chemokines may act in the same tissue. Moreover, not all chemotactic agents are chemokines. Bacterial formylated peptides, certain lipid mediators and complement-derived fragments can also provide directional cues.

Histamine is chemically different from a protein cytokine. It is a small amine stored in granules of mast cells and certain other cells. Upon release, it acts on responsive vascular and other cells through histamine receptors. Its local effects can include arteriolar dilation and increased permeability of particular microvascular segments. The rapidity of a histamine response is related in part to the availability of stored material, rather than a requirement to synthesize a new histamine molecule for every early response. In some inflammatory settings, histamine is a major early contributor; other stimuli generate substantial inflammation through pathways that do not depend primarily on histamine.

Prostaglandins are lipid mediators generated from arachidonic acid through cyclooxygenase-dependent pathways. PGE₂ provides a useful example because it can influence vascular function, sensitization of pain pathways and regulation of body temperature. A chemically distinct group of lipid mediators, the leukotrienes, is generated through lipoxygenase-associated pathways. Leukotriene B₄ can provide a strong signal for recruitment and activation of certain leukocytes. These examples demonstrate that an inflammatory tissue uses many molecular classes of mediator, each with its own synthesis, receptor systems and range of action.

The relationship between inflammatory mediators and temperature is particularly instructive. Local warmth can arise because more warm blood reaches a region of tissue. Fever, in contrast, involves a regulated change in central temperature control, with prostaglandin-mediated signaling contributing to this response. A patient can have local warmth without a systemic increase in body temperature. The two phenomena are related to inflammation but arise at different levels of physiological organization. An examination problem that describes red, warm skin should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of a changed hypothalamic temperature set point.

The effects of mediators are often context-dependent. PGE₂, for instance, can promote certain signs of inflammation while limiting some cellular immune activities under particular conditions. IL-10 often has regulatory effects that restrain excessive cytokine production, whereas other mediators can support activation. The biological outcome of a complex mediator mixture is not obtained simply by labeling every molecule as either good or bad. A response can contain signals that amplify useful defense and signals that constrain its duration or intensity. Their relative importance changes as the tissue moves from recognition toward clearance and repair.

A mechanistic experiment can separate the role of a mediator from the role of a responding cell. If conditioned fluid from activated macrophages increases adhesion-molecule expression on cultured endothelial cells, a soluble factor is implicated. Neutralizing a candidate cytokine in that fluid and observing a reduced effect would support its contribution. Treating endothelial cells with the purified candidate could test whether it is sufficient under the chosen conditions. Neither result alone establishes that the candidate accounts for every endothelial change in a living tissue. The conditioned fluid may contain many active substances, and vascular cells in an organism experience blood flow and signals not present in a simple culture system.

13. Vascular responses and the cardinal signs of inflammation

The vascular changes of acute inflammation help transform a small focus of tissue disturbance into an accessible site for circulating defense components. Arteriolar dilation decreases resistance to flow through a vascular region, allowing increased local blood delivery if the pressure relationships permit it. More blood passing through superficial vessels can contribute to redness and warmth. Increased flow also changes the delivery of plasma proteins and circulating cells. The magnitude of these effects depends on the anatomy of the tissue, the vascular segment involved and the prevailing systemic circulation. A local change cannot be inferred from vessel diameter alone if pressure and upstream resistance change simultaneously.

Increased vascular permeability is a different phenomenon from dilation. Permeability concerns the ability of substances to cross the vessel wall. Under appropriate mediator stimulation, endothelial junctions can become more permissive, particularly in postcapillary venules. Water and plasma proteins can then enter the surrounding tissue more readily than under resting conditions. Proteins outside vessels can influence fluid movement and participate in defense. The resulting accumulation of protein-rich inflammatory fluid is commonly described as exudation. The physical enlargement of a vessel and the passage of material through its wall are separate processes even when the same inflammatory stimulus promotes both.

Fluid movement across a microvascular wall depends on hydrostatic pressure, osmotic forces and properties of the endothelial barrier. In a simplified conceptual picture, an increased permeability to plasma proteins reduces the effectiveness of the barrier in retaining these proteins in blood. Protein leakage can alter osmotic relationships and favor accumulation of interstitial fluid. Lymphatic drainage can compensate for increased filtration up to a point. When fluid delivery exceeds removal, swelling becomes evident. Modern understanding includes the importance of the endothelial glycocalyx and subglycocalyx conditions; it would be misleading to treat a single simplified pressure equation as a complete description of inflamed microvascular fluid exchange.

The classical local signs of inflammation are redness, heat, swelling and pain; loss of function is often included as a fifth sign. Redness and heat commonly reflect local circulatory changes. Swelling reflects increased interstitial fluid and may also be influenced by cellular accumulation. Pain can arise through activation or sensitization of sensory neurons by mediators, as well as mechanical effects of swelling and tissue injury. Loss of function may result from several of these changes together. These relationships are useful explanatory associations, not a requirement that every inflamed tissue visibly display every sign.

A tissue located deep within the body may undergo significant inflammation without visible redness at the surface. A small response may generate molecular and cellular changes without noticeable pain. Conversely, an altered blood supply can make a region appear red without establishing the presence of a complete inflammatory program. Clinical signs are consequences of physiological processes; they are not molecularly specific assays. A student interpreting experimental data should connect a measured variable to the underlying process rather than infer a particular receptor or cytokine from the appearance of tissue alone.

As fluid leaves certain vessels, the local blood can become relatively concentrated and flow patterns may change. In suitable vascular regions, hydrodynamic interactions with erythrocytes help bring leukocytes nearer the vessel wall, a phenomenon termed margination. This increases opportunities for leukocyte-endothelial contact. Margination is not equivalent to firm adhesion and does not by itself carry cells through the wall. A cell near the endothelium still needs appropriate adhesive interactions and migratory signals to proceed through subsequent recruitment stages.

The location of leukocyte exit is also significant. Postcapillary venules are common sites of leukocyte extravasation in many tissues. The endothelial organization, local hemodynamics and inducible adhesion molecules make these regions well suited for recruitment. However, tissue-specific vascular beds can employ distinct patterns of leukocyte movement. Lung capillary recruitment, for example, need not reproduce every feature of the classical postcapillary venule model. The model provides a useful general sequence for NET and SET reasoning; its individual steps must be interpreted with awareness of tissue-specific differences.

The beneficial and harmful consequences of vascular leakage follow from the same physical changes. Increased access of plasma defense proteins to a small infected region can improve local protection. Excessive fluid accumulation in a confined tissue can impair function. Widespread changes in vascular tone and permeability can compromise systemic circulation. Thus an effective response is not simply the strongest possible dilation or the greatest possible leakage. It is a regulated process that improves defense at an acceptable physiological cost.

14. Leukocyte recruitment: capture, rolling, arrest and extravasation

Circulating leukocytes move in a fluid environment and experience forces generated by blood flow. For a neutrophil to leave the circulation at an inflammatory focus, it must first interact with the endothelium at that location. A static picture of two cells adhering to one another does not explain how the first contact occurs under flow. The cell must form bonds that can capture it, allow controlled movement along the surface and then support more stable arrest. Different adhesion molecules contribute to these stages, and inflammatory signals regulate their availability and activity.

Selectins are carbohydrate-binding adhesion proteins. P-selectin and E-selectin on activated endothelium can interact with appropriate carbohydrate-containing ligands on leukocytes, including properly modified PSGL-1 in relevant interactions. P-selectin may be mobilized rapidly from intracellular endothelial storage compartments following stimulation. E-selectin expression can increase after inflammatory cytokine signaling and new protein synthesis. Because the molecular requirements differ, a leukocyte with an improperly glycosylated ligand can have poor selectin-dependent adhesion even if the protein scaffold of the ligand is still detectable on its surface.

Rolling arises through the formation and rupture of bonds between the leukocyte and the endothelial surface under flow. As old contacts release, new contacts can form farther along the direction of movement. The result is a reduced velocity and repeated close encounters with the endothelial surface. Rolling is neither simple free transport nor complete arrest. This intermediate behavior increases the time available for the leukocyte to encounter signals presented at the vascular surface. Selectins are key contributors to classical rolling, although other molecules, including integrins under appropriate conditions, can contribute to rolling behavior.

Chemokines associated with the endothelial surface can engage leukocyte receptors during rolling. Many chemokine receptors are G-protein-coupled receptors. Their activation can rapidly change the conformation and organization of integrins on the leukocyte membrane through intracellular signaling, often called inside-out activation. The integrin does not need to be synthesized anew in order to become more adhesive. A receptor already present at the surface can change its ligand-binding properties. This creates a rapid transition between a cell able to roll and a cell capable of forming stronger adhesive contacts.

Leukocyte integrins such as LFA-1 and Mac-1 can interact with endothelial intercellular adhesion molecules. LFA-1 is the αLβ2 integrin and Mac-1 is the αMβ2 integrin; both contain the β2 chain CD18. LFA-1 can bind ICAM-1 and other appropriate ICAM-family ligands. Mac-1 also contributes to adhesive and migratory functions. Increased integrin affinity, together with receptor distribution and bond formation at the contact site, can enable the cell to resist detachment under flow. Endothelial expression of suitable ligands is necessary even when the leukocyte integrin is active.

The distinction between affinity and expression is crucial for interpretation. Two leukocyte samples can display similar quantities of an integrin but differ in their ability to arrest because the integrin activation state differs. Conversely, a normal inside-out activation signal cannot create stable interaction with a ligand that is absent from the opposing cell. In a conceptual flow experiment, one can compare rolling velocity, the fraction of rolling cells that arrest and the duration of arrested contacts. These measurements locate different changes in the recruitment sequence.

Firm adhesion is followed by further steps. A leukocyte can spread and crawl along the endothelial surface, seeking an appropriate location for crossing. It then passes between endothelial cells or, in some contexts, through an endothelial cell. The process of crossing is called transmigration or diapedesis. Several junction-associated adhesion proteins, including PECAM-1 in representative settings, contribute to coordination of this passage. Crossing the endothelial layer also requires movement through the associated basement membrane and surrounding tissue structures. The process is regulated rather than equivalent to a nonspecific leak of large cells through any permeable vessel.

Increased permeability to plasma proteins and increased leukocyte transmigration should therefore not be treated as interchangeable. A mediator may change endothelial junctional behavior so that fluid or proteins cross more readily without providing all the adhesion and directional signals needed for efficient neutrophil entry. A mutation in a leukocyte adhesion protein can reduce cell recruitment even when inflammatory edema remains evident. Measuring fluid leakage alone cannot establish the success of cellular recruitment, just as measuring rolling alone cannot establish that the cells reached the interstitium.

The classical sequence is particularly useful when considering leukocyte adhesion deficiencies. A severe reduction in functional β2 integrins can impair firm adhesion and tissue recruitment. Circulating neutrophils may be present in increased numbers because exit into tissues is compromised and other regulatory changes occur. Nevertheless, the infected tissue may contain too few effective neutrophils. The apparent paradox of abundant leukocytes in blood but poor local cellular defense is resolved by distinguishing cell availability from cell delivery. A blood count does not measure the number of leukocytes that have successfully reached an inflammatory focus.

A defect in selectin-ligand glycosylation can compromise an earlier stage by reducing capture and rolling. The remaining cells that do reach an activated endothelial surface might still possess functional integrins. A defect in chemokine-receptor signaling can instead weaken activation of adhesion mechanisms or movement toward particular signals, even when selectin-mediated contact is intact. These examples are not interchangeable descriptions of leukocyte adhesion deficiency. Each perturbation affects a different molecular requirement, and its consequences must be traced through the subsequent stages.

After crossing the vascular wall, the neutrophil still has to find the location where its actions are useful. It moves within a three-dimensional environment containing extracellular matrix, other cells and gradients of soluble or matrix-associated signals. Adhesive contacts used in tissues can differ from those used for arrest on the vascular endothelium. In some confined environments leukocytes can migrate with relatively little dependence on particular integrin-mediated traction mechanisms. The general principle is that entry into the tissue and directed movement through that tissue are separate tasks. Arrival at a vessel near an infection does not guarantee contact with the microbial target.

15. Chemotaxis and movement through an inflammatory tissue

Chemotaxis is the directional movement of a cell in relation to a chemical gradient. A gradient is a spatial change in concentration, not merely a high concentration of a molecule. If a neutrophil encounters identical concentrations on all sides, the ligand may activate its receptors without providing a directional cue. A gradient can provide different information at different regions of the cell surface, allowing signaling systems to establish a front and a rear. The cell can then move preferentially toward a region associated with stronger attractive input.

The leading region of a migrating leukocyte often exhibits active actin remodeling and protrusive activity, while the rear undergoes coordinated contraction and release of contacts. Intracellular signaling helps maintain polarity even though the external concentration differences over the dimensions of a single cell can be small. Receptor adaptation, feedback and interaction with the cytoskeleton contribute to the ability to respond as the cell moves through changing concentrations. A leukocyte is not simply pulled toward a source by a physical force exerted directly by dissolved chemokine molecules; it actively generates movement in response to information obtained from its surroundings.

Different signals can mark different portions of the route. Chemokines on or near an inflamed vessel can help initiate arrest and entry, while additional signals in tissue can guide cells toward local targets. Bacterial formyl peptides can provide information associated with the microbial source itself. Leukotriene B₄ or other cell-derived signals can contribute to coordination among recruited cells. Because signals overlap, removal of one chemokine need not abolish every aspect of movement. It may change the number of cells recruited, their timing, their trajectory or the relative composition of the infiltrate.

An important contrast is between chemotaxis and chemokinesis. Chemokinesis refers to an alteration in motile behavior, such as increased speed or frequency of movement, that does not necessarily involve directional bias toward a chemical gradient. A population of cells exposed to a uniform attractant concentration might move more vigorously in random directions. If a microscope records only the total distance traveled, this change might be mistaken for enhanced chemotaxis. Directional analysis requires information about cell displacement relative to the gradient or a suitable comparison of migration under gradient and uniform conditions.

An elementary quantitative measure is the chemotactic index, defined in one common tracking approach as net displacement along the gradient divided by the total path length. For a cell moving in a straight line toward the source, the ratio approaches one. A cell that wanders without net movement along the gradient has a value near zero, and one moving overall away from the source has a negative value. The measure is dimensionless because both quantities are lengths. Its usefulness lies in distinguishing directionality from speed, but the choice of gradient axis, observation interval and tracking method must be stated. Different studies can use different definitions of a migration index.

A hypothetical migration experiment might expose two groups of neutrophils to the same spatial gradient. Both travel a similar total distance, but the control cells accumulate much greater displacement toward the source. A defect in directional sensing or polarity control is then more plausible than a complete inability to move. If the second group travels almost no distance under either gradient or uniform conditions, a broader motility or viability defect becomes more plausible. To infer the role of a receptor, a comparison with a different attractant can help determine whether the defect is specific to one signaling route or shared by several responses.

An experiment using a membrane with pores between two compartments can also compare migration in response to a candidate chemoattractant. More cells appearing in the lower compartment when attractant is placed there suggests an effect on migration. However, increased random movement, altered adhesion to the membrane or improved survival could contribute to the count. A useful control places the same attractant concentration on both sides of the membrane, reducing the initial directional concentration difference. Single-cell tracking in a defined gradient can provide stronger direct evidence of directional bias. The lesson is to identify what an assay measures before assigning the result to a specific biological process.

Chemotaxis is ultimately useful because it links the position of effector cells with the source of danger. When gradients decline after clearance, the same cells no longer receive the same directional and activation signals. This can reduce continuing accumulation and help the tissue progress toward resolution. If the source persists or the tissue continually generates new signals after injury, recruitment can remain active. Understanding how a local response ends therefore requires attention to both the cessation of influx and the fate of cells that are already present.

16. Resolution, repair and the costs of inflammatory activity

Acute inflammation is often presented as a sequence in which neutrophils arrive first and monocytes follow. This is a useful approximation for many acute bacterial and sterile injuries, but the sequence is not universal. A tissue may already contain macrophages capable of responding immediately. Resident and recruited mononuclear cells can participate at overlapping times, and the composition of the infiltrate varies with the stimulus, species, tissue and duration. Viral infections, for instance, can involve substantial recruitment or activation of different lymphocyte populations. Time and cellular composition should therefore be interpreted together rather than used as a rigid diagnostic rule.

The removal of the initiating stimulus is important for ending inflammation, but resolution is not simply the exhaustion of all inflammatory substances. Chemotactic signals must diminish or change, the influx of additional cells must be reduced, and the cells already present must be removed or redirected toward appropriate functions. Macrophages can help accomplish these tasks by ingesting apoptotic cells and responding to changes in their environment. Stromal cells and lymphatic vessels also contribute to restoration of tissue organization. The end of inflammation is a regulated biological process with its own cellular and molecular events.

Many neutrophils eventually undergo apoptosis after recruitment and activation. Apoptosis generally preserves membrane integrity during early stages of cell dismantling, reducing exposure of surrounding tissue to intracellular contents. Dying cells can display phosphatidylserine on their outer plasma-membrane surface, where this phospholipid is less abundant in many healthy cells. Macrophages can detect the resulting changes directly or through bridging proteins. The ingestion of apoptotic cells is known as efferocytosis. Unlike the ingestion of a bacterium accompanied by strong microbial signals, efferocytosis often promotes programs that limit further inflammatory activity and support removal of debris.

The distinction between efferocytosis and secondary necrosis has important consequences. If apoptotic cells are not removed before losing membrane integrity, intracellular contents can enter the surrounding tissue. These molecules can contribute to new danger signals and perpetuate inflammation. A defect in apoptotic-cell clearance can thus sustain inflammation even if recruitment of cells has initially served its purpose and the original pathogen has been controlled. Removing dying leukocytes efficiently is part of tissue protection, not merely a housekeeping event that begins after immunity has ended.

Signals that promote resolution include selected lipid-derived mediators such as lipoxins and resolvins in appropriate contexts. Their functions can involve limiting further leukocyte recruitment and supporting clearance processes. Regulatory cytokines such as IL-10 can restrain production of particular inflammatory mediators. The precise mixture depends on the tissue and response. It is useful to distinguish a reduction in the production of new inflammatory signals from active mechanisms that remove existing inflammatory cells. Blocking the entry of additional neutrophils does not immediately eliminate neutrophils already present, and removal of existing cells is unlikely to restore tissue composition if strong influx continues.

The population of neutrophils in a tissue provides a simple example of a balance between input and loss. If recruitment is rapid and removal is slow, the population increases. If recruitment diminishes and removal becomes relatively important, the population decreases. The same number of cells at a single observation time could represent an actively increasing population, a transient balance, or a declining population. A time series is therefore needed to distinguish accumulation from failure of resolution. If an intervention reduces cell number at a late time, it may have reduced initial recruitment, increased removal or altered both. Observing early influx and later fate separately is necessary to identify its mechanism.

Resolution often occurs together with tissue repair. Macrophages and other cells can release signals affecting fibroblasts, endothelial cells and epithelial populations. Replacement of damaged components and restoration of barrier function reduce the likelihood that the tissue will continue producing danger signals. Repair and inflammation do not have perfectly separated boundaries, and macrophage activation states are not adequately described as two immutable cell types. The conventional M1 and M2 labels are simplified descriptions of some experimentally defined responses. Cells in an actual tissue can display overlapping properties or change their behavior over time. At examination level, distinguish inflammatory antimicrobial activation from repair-associated functions while recognizing their context dependence.

Persistent inflammatory activity can cause injury through several mechanisms already encountered. Extracellular oxidants may alter host proteins; proteases can degrade matrix components; edema may disrupt tissue function; and persistent mediator production may maintain recruitment even when the original protective benefit has declined. Conversely, premature suppression of recruitment in a tissue with actively growing microbes can weaken protection. The goal of regulation is therefore not elimination of every inflammatory reaction but matching the strength and duration of the response to the biological threat.

These principles can be used to analyze a comparison between two experimental animals. Both show similar early neutrophil recruitment after a standardized sterile stimulus. In one, cell numbers decline along with the concentration of danger-associated material. In the other, apoptotic debris persists, neutrophils remain numerous and inflammatory mediators continue to be detected. A defect in resolution is a reasonable hypothesis because the early influx was comparable. To distinguish poor efferocytosis from renewed recruitment, one could follow the fate of the initial cell population and measure subsequent influx separately. The persistence of cells alone is not sufficient to establish the underlying defect.

17. Dendritic-cell maturation connects local recognition to lymphocyte activation

Innate effector cells are capable of immediate responses without waiting for a rare antigen-specific lymphocyte to divide. Adaptive immunity adds the ability to expand cells bearing particular receptors and generate specialized responses. These capabilities create an organizational problem: a lymphocyte with the appropriate receptor may not be present at the particular microscopic site where a pathogen first appears. The organism must bring information about that encounter to a location where relevant lymphocytes circulate, while also distinguishing a potentially dangerous encounter from ordinary exposure to harmless material.

Conventional dendritic cells are especially well suited to this role. A dendritic cell in a peripheral tissue can encounter soluble or particulate antigen. Antigen is a material containing molecular features that may be recognized by immune receptors. For conventional T-cell recognition, protein antigen commonly has to be processed into peptide fragments and displayed in association with major histocompatibility complex molecules. A cell capable of performing such display is called an antigen-presenting cell. Macrophages and B cells can also present antigen, but dendritic cells have a particularly important role in initiating many responses of naive T cells.

Antigen capture and antigen presentation are distinct events. A fluorescent protein found inside a dendritic cell establishes that the cell acquired the protein, provided the assay excludes surface-associated material. It does not establish that a particular peptide from the protein is displayed at the cell surface. Furthermore, detection of a peptide–MHC complex does not show that a naive T cell recognizing it will necessarily undergo effective activation. The responding lymphocyte requires additional contextual information. This distinction between material uptake, molecular display and functional lymphocyte response prevents several common errors in the interpretation of immune experiments.

In many peripheral dendritic cells, recognition of microbial or inflammatory signals promotes a maturation program. Surface availability of peptide-presenting MHC molecules and costimulatory molecules such as CD80 and CD86 can increase. Expression of the chemokine receptor CCR7 can promote movement toward ligands including CCL19 and CCL21 in relevant lymphatic and lymphoid environments. The resulting changes coordinate the transport of antigenic information with increased ability to stimulate lymphocytes. Maturation is a functional change in state, not a guarantee that every dendritic cell adopts precisely the same phenotype or migratory behavior.

A migratory dendritic cell can enter an afferent lymphatic vessel and reach a draining lymph node. Lymph nodes provide organized environments in which dendritic cells and recirculating lymphocytes can encounter one another. Naive lymphocytes commonly enter lymph nodes from the blood through specialized high endothelial venules and explore relevant regions of the node. The antigen-bearing dendritic cell and the naive lymphocyte therefore need not start from the same compartment or travel along the same route. Lymphatic transport of antigen-bearing cells, direct drainage of soluble antigen and antigen acquisition by lymphoid-resident dendritic cells are complementary means of bringing information to the adaptive system.

Chemokines participate in this spatial organization. CCR7 on appropriately activated migratory dendritic cells helps guide their movement in response to its ligands. CCR7 also contributes to the positioning of particular lymphocyte populations in lymphoid tissues. Consequently, deleting CCR7 from all cells in an experimental organism can affect more than one aspect of the encounter between a dendritic cell and a T cell. A cell-type-specific perturbation is more informative when the research question concerns dendritic-cell migration itself. If only dendritic cells lack the receptor and lymphocyte organization remains intact, changes in the delivery of those dendritic cells can be interpreted more directly.

The initial interaction between a naive conventional T cell and an antigen-presenting dendritic cell can be described using three categories of input. The first is recognition of an appropriate peptide–MHC complex by the T-cell receptor. The second is costimulation, with interaction between CD28 on the T cell and CD80 or CD86 on the antigen-presenting cell serving as a representative example. The third category includes cytokines that help shape proliferation, survival and differentiation. The three-signal description is a useful conceptual model, not a literal count of three molecules in every responding cell. It separates antigen specificity from the context in which a response is initiated and shaped.

Costimulation is particularly significant because many host proteins and harmless environmental molecules can be acquired and displayed by antigen-presenting cells. If receptor recognition alone automatically caused a full destructive response, normal tissues would be continually endangered. The activation state of the antigen-presenting cell, additional inhibitory signals and the state of the lymphocyte help determine whether recognition is followed by a productive response, limited activation, tolerance-associated outcomes or another fate. Insufficient costimulation can favor anergy or other forms of nonproductive response in appropriate contexts, but it is not a universal prediction for every cell and antigen combination.

The maturation-associated production of cytokines links the type of danger recognized by innate cells to the properties of responding lymphocytes. For example, IL-12 produced in suitable innate responses can favor interferon-gamma-associated T-cell differentiation and support NK-cell activity. The detailed pathways of T-cell differentiation belong to later chapters. The principle established here is that the recognition of a microbial pattern provides information about the circumstances of antigen encounter, whereas the T-cell receptor provides information about peptide–MHC specificity. Two dendritic cells carrying the same antigen can generate different lymphocyte responses if their activation states and cytokine environments differ.

This separation can be tested experimentally. Suppose a dendritic cell population takes up a protein and displays an appropriate peptide–MHC complex but remains weakly costimulatory. A matched population encounters the same antigen together with a suitable innate receptor stimulus and expresses more CD80 and CD86. If antigen-specific naive T cells proliferate more strongly in response to the second population, the result is consistent with an important contribution of maturation. To test costimulation more directly, the experiment can selectively interfere with the relevant costimulatory interaction while controlling antigen display and cell viability. Otherwise, changes in peptide–MHC density or cytokine production could also account for the difference.

The concept of antigen processing includes important exceptions to overly simple distinctions between intracellular and extracellular material. Certain dendritic cells can display peptides derived from externally acquired antigen using MHC class I, a process termed cross-presentation. This allows antigen obtained from infected or transformed cells to contribute to activation of CD8 T cells without requiring the presenting dendritic cell itself to be infected. A detailed explanation of processing and loading belongs to the chapter on MHC molecules and antigen presentation. Here cross-presentation illustrates how an innate sentinel can deliver information about a threat in another cell to the appropriate adaptive recognition system.

Once specific lymphocytes have proliferated and differentiated, their products and actions can enhance innate effector mechanisms. Antibody coating can promote phagocytic uptake or antibody-dependent NK-cell cytotoxicity. Activated T cells can deliver cytokines that alter macrophage activity. These responses form feedback relationships between the innate and adaptive compartments rather than a one-way handover in which innate immunity disappears. The same phagocyte that helped control early infection can later respond to signals originating from antigen-specific lymphocytes. The first chapter established the components of immune organization; this chapter explains how their activities become connected across tissues and through time.

18. Quantitative reasoning about cellular defense

Quantitative descriptions are valuable when they clarify a specific mechanism or distinguish alternative explanations. Consider microbial killing after an initial uptake period. Let N₀ denote the number of recoverable viable intracellular organisms at the beginning of the observation interval and N(t) the number at time t. A useful normalized quantity is N(t)/N₀, the fraction that remains recoverable relative to the initial measurement. If bacteria do not replicate during the interval and recovery efficiency is comparable at both times, the complementary fraction 1 − N(t)/N₀ estimates the proportion lost from the viable population. Multiplying by one hundred expresses it as a percentage. The assumptions matter because a viable-count assay cannot separately measure replication and killing from a single endpoint.

A simple model can approximate a constant fractional rate of microbial loss. If the instantaneous rate of change of viable number is proportional to the number remaining, dN/dt = −kN, where k is a nonnegative rate constant with units of inverse time. Separating variables gives dN/N = −k dt. Integrating from the initial time to t yields ln[N(t)/N₀] = −kt, so N(t) = N₀e⁻ᵏᵗ. The fraction remaining after a time t is therefore e⁻ᵏᵗ. When N(t)/N₀ = 1/2, the half-time equals ln(2)/k. This model describes a constant hazard of loss for an initially defined population; it does not state that a particular molecular killing mechanism always follows first-order kinetics.

If microbial replication occurs at an approximately constant per-organism rate r and loss occurs at rate k, an illustrative alternative is dN/dt = (r − k)N. The observed log slope is then the difference of two rates rather than a direct estimate of killing alone. If viable number remains approximately constant, replication and loss could both be negligible or could balance each other. A flat curve does not necessarily establish that phagocytes have no antimicrobial effect. An independent measure of microbial replication or an experimental condition restricting replication can help distinguish these possibilities.

A related accounting model describes recruited cells in a tissue. Let L(t) be the number of leukocytes of a specified type at time t. Let J(t) be the rate at which they enter the counted compartment, in cells per unit time, and let R(t) be the rate at which they are removed from that compartment. The identity dL/dt = J(t) − R(t) follows from conservation of cell number if local production is negligible or accounted for. It is not necessary for the two rates to be constant. If local proliferation is appreciable, a production term must be added. Loss may include death followed by clearance, exit or other routes that remove a cell from the counted population.

Suppose influx is approximately constant at J and removal occurs with rate R = λL, where λ has units of inverse time. A steady count, if achieved under those assumptions, satisfies J = λL and hence L = J/λ. Two interventions can lower the same steady cell count by different routes. One can reduce J, perhaps through impaired recruitment; the other can increase λ, perhaps by promoting effective removal. An endpoint count cannot identify which mechanism operated. When J falls to zero and λ remains constant, the remaining population declines exponentially, with a half-time of ln(2)/λ. This is a simplified model for interpretation, not a claim that every neutrophil follows identical survival kinetics in an inflamed tissue.

Ratios used in recruitment assays also require explicit denominators. Suppose the fraction of captured leukocytes that arrest is A/C, with A the number arrested and C the number captured. The fraction of arrested cells that transmigrate is T/A, where T is the number that cross. Multiplying the two ratios gives T/C, the fraction of captured cells that ultimately cross, provided that the measurements refer to the same tracked population and time window. A defect in capture can reduce the total number of transmigrated cells even when both conditional fractions remain normal. A defect in arrest can reduce T/C while leaving the capture count unchanged. Reporting only one ratio can conceal an important upstream or downstream change.

For NK-cell killing, a simple specific-lysis calculation can be used when a measurement reports target-cell death as a percentage. If D is the measured percentage of dead target cells in the presence of effector cells and S is spontaneous death in a matched target-only group, then (D − S)/(100 − S) × 100 estimates the fraction of targets that would otherwise have survived but are lost in the effector condition. The calculation assumes that the spontaneous-death estimate is applicable to the effector-containing condition and that all percentages refer to comparable target populations. The correction differs from simply subtracting S because the denominator represents the potentially surviving target population. Other assays, such as release-based cytotoxicity assays, use their own definitions of spontaneous and maximum release.

The effector-to-target ratio in a killing experiment is the initial number of effector cells divided by the number of target cells. Increasing this ratio can increase opportunities for encounters and productive killing, but it is not a direct measurement of each effector's killing probability. Multiple effectors can contact the same target, one effector may contact several targets in succession, and target availability can become limiting. An apparent plateau in a lysis-versus-ratio plot may reflect the depletion of susceptible targets or other constraints. Comparing populations requires equal observation intervals, target identity and counting methods, rather than interpreting different effector-to-target ratios as intrinsically equivalent conditions.

19. Integrating mechanisms, comparisons and experimental predictions

The current chapter describes a coordinated vertebrate defense system, but its individual capabilities have different evolutionary histories. Phagocytosis is much older than vertebrate adaptive immunity and also serves nutritional functions in many unicellular eukaryotes. In multicellular animals, engulfment has been recruited for defense and the removal of cellular debris. Invertebrate immune cells can perform phagocytic and other protective functions without possessing the conventional vertebrate B-cell and T-cell receptor systems. This comparison shows that uptake and chemical destruction of a particle do not logically require an antigen-specific adaptive receptor.

Plant cells also recognize microbial and damage-associated signals and can deploy antimicrobial chemistry, including reactive oxygen species. However, plant cells have rigid cell walls, and plants do not organize defense through blood-borne neutrophils, mammalian NK cells or lymph-node migration of dendritic cells. A similarity in the use of a reactive molecule is not proof of identity between the cell types or defense architectures of the organisms. Plant innate defense is treated within plant physiology; the present comparison helps identify which properties of innate immunity reflect broad cellular principles and which depend on animal tissue organization.

At the level of a whole organism, distinct failures generate distinct patterns of immune dysfunction. A defect in the adhesion cascade can leave blood leukocytes abundant while reducing their arrival in tissue. A defect in phagosome maturation can permit normal uptake but impair the development of a degradative environment. Loss of functional NADPH oxidase can reduce an important route of oxidative activity without eliminating all nonoxidative mechanisms. A failure of NK-cell granule function can impair destruction of susceptible host cells without abolishing phagocytosis. A dendritic-cell migration defect can compromise adaptive priming despite relatively intact local microbial recognition. Identifying the affected step is more informative than assigning every phenotype to a general category of weak immunity.

Combinations of perturbations can reveal relationships among these steps. Imagine an inflammatory tissue containing high concentrations of neutrophil-attracting chemokines but few recruited neutrophils. Adding more chemokine might not correct a severe deficiency in firm adhesion. Conversely, a normal adhesion system cannot provide directed entry into every tissue in the absence of the appropriate signals. If isolated mutant cells arrest normally when directly provided with a downstream integrin-activating stimulus, an upstream chemokine response may be implicated. If they fail even under conditions that directly support integrin activation, a deficiency of the integrin or its ligand becomes more plausible. These predictions follow from the ordering of events rather than from memorizing disease names.

Another important distinction is between local control and organism-level protection. A mutation that reduces inflammatory swelling might preserve tissue appearance but permit microbial growth. A different mutation that increases oxidant production might reduce viable microbes yet damage nearby host cells. Judging the biological outcome requires considering both pathogen burden and host integrity. An intervention that improves one measured variable does not necessarily improve the entire response. This is why experimental studies of innate immunity often benefit from separate readouts for microbial survival, recruitment, cellular damage and tissue recovery.

The next chapter examines complement, a soluble system that intersects with the mechanisms developed here. Complement-derived opsonins can support phagocyte recognition, selected cleavage fragments can influence inflammation and recruitment, and terminal components can damage susceptible membranes. Those mechanisms should be placed alongside the cellular processes rather than confused with them. Phagocytosis encloses a target in a host-derived compartment, whereas direct complement-mediated membrane injury does not require that enclosure. Cytokines can alter the behavior of cells, while some complement fragments act through receptors to influence similar tissue-level outcomes. Recognizing the points of cooperation and the differences in mechanism provides the foundation for interpreting integrated immune responses.
