1. Why the body must redistribute fuels

A meal delivers nutrients for a limited time, but the brain, heart and other organs need energy continuously. The physiological challenge is therefore not simply to extract ATP from food. It is to distribute incoming carbon among organs, store excess fuel in suitable chemical forms, and mobilise those reserves in a controlled way when absorption stops. Exercise adds a second challenge: a contracting muscle may suddenly consume much more ATP even though the intestine has not increased nutrient delivery. A multicellular animal solves these problems through regulated exchange between tissues, not by making every cell metabolically identical.

Three categories of fuel dominate this discussion. Carbohydrate circulates principally as glucose and can be stored as glycogen. Lipid is stored mainly as triacylglycerol and circulates in several forms, including fatty acids associated with albumin and triacylglycerol carried in lipoproteins. Amino acids circulate as individual solutes and are used for protein synthesis and many other reactions. Unlike glycogen or triacylglycerol, there is no dedicated bulk storage polymer for surplus amino acids that can be mobilised without sacrificing functional tissue. Mobilisation of muscle protein therefore has physiological costs that mobilisation of stored fat does not share.

The chemical form of a reserve helps determine its purpose. Glycogen is a branched glucose polymer with numerous nonreducing ends. Enzymes can add or remove residues at many ends simultaneously, which supports rapid adjustment of glucose availability. Its hydrophilic granules are associated with water, making glycogen a comparatively bulky reserve. Triacylglycerol contains reduced hydrocarbon chains and can be stored in droplets with little associated water. Fat consequently provides a more concentrated long-term energy reserve. However, the oxidation of fatty acids depends on mitochondrial metabolism and cannot replace rapid glycolytic ATP generation in a cell without mitochondria or during a sudden increase in demand.

ATP itself is the immediate energy currency, not the long-term energy store. ATP hydrolysis drives ion pumps, biosynthetic reactions and the force-generating cycle of myosin. Cellular ATP content is kept within a relatively narrow operating range by continuous regeneration. When ATP turnover increases, ADP, inorganic phosphate and sometimes AMP increase; these substances participate in regulation of fuel oxidation. The persistence of nearly constant ATP concentration during a change in workload therefore does not show that metabolism has remained unchanged. It shows that supply has adjusted to consumption.

An organ's metabolic role can be understood by asking three questions: which substrates enter the organ, which pathways are available in its cells, and which products can leave it? The liver possesses the machinery to release free glucose into blood. Skeletal muscle contains a large glycogen reserve but lacks physiologically significant glucose-6-phosphatase activity and uses its glycogen primarily for itself. Adipose tissue releases fatty acids and glycerol during energy shortage. Erythrocytes, which have no mitochondria, convert glucose into lactate continuously. These differences establish a division of labour and explain why a fuel that is abundant in one tissue does not necessarily reach another.

Metabolic homeostasis describes regulation of internal variables such as circulating glucose despite changing input and output. It does not imply that every pathway is constant. When the intestinal supply of glucose declines, endogenous glucose production rises. When muscle glucose uptake increases during exercise, liver glucose production generally increases in parallel. Consequently the concentration measured in a blood sample may change very little while the amount of glucose flowing through the circulation per minute changes severalfold. Concentration measures the size of a pool relative to its volume, whereas flux measures the movement of material through that pool per unit time.

The simplest representation of this principle is a mass balance. Change in the quantity of circulating glucose equals the amount entering minus the amount leaving. Sources include absorption of carbohydrate from the digestive tract, hepatic glucose release and renal gluconeogenesis. Sinks include oxidation, glycogen deposition, conversion to other compounds and, in abnormal conditions, urinary loss. At a steady glucose concentration and constant distribution volume, total entry and exit must approximately match, even though individual source and sink terms may vary.

A useful distinction is that of absorptive, postabsorptive and prolonged fasting conditions. In the absorptive period, a substantial amount of recently ingested nutrient is entering the circulation; the endocrine system promotes utilization and storage. In the postabsorptive period, intestinal input has diminished and endogenous stores sustain fuel delivery. Prolonged fasting recruits additional mechanisms that reduce dependence on carbohydrate. These are physiological descriptions, not sharp transitions tied to a universal clock. Meal composition, body size, previous exercise, glycogen content and endocrine status shift their timing. Exercise is an additional demand imposed on any of these nutritional states, so the words fasting and exercise are not mutually exclusive.

This chapter follows carbon and nitrogen through the organs rather than treating each metabolic pathway as an isolated list of reactions. The same carbon atom may pass from muscle glycogen to lactate, from lactate to hepatic glucose, and then into the blood again. Alternatively, it may be oxidised by the heart before ever returning to the liver. Such alternatives are essential when interpreting data from living animals, where multiple pathways operate at once and their relative rates are continuously adjusted.

2. Organ-specific pathways and circulating substrates

The intestine is the major site where dietary carbohydrate, amino acids and lipids enter the organism. Glucose and many other small hydrophilic nutrients absorbed by intestinal epithelial cells enter the portal circulation. The liver consequently receives a nutrient-rich blood supply after feeding and can remove part of the incoming glucose before it reaches other organs. Most long-chain dietary fatty acids are re-esterified within enterocytes, incorporated into chylomicrons and delivered through lymph to the systemic circulation. The transport route of a substrate influences which organ encounters it first.

Hepatocytes express glucose transporters that permit bidirectional glucose movement according to concentration gradients. Glucokinase phosphorylates intracellular glucose to glucose-6-phosphate and has regulatory properties suited to handling an increased supply after a meal. Phosphorylation helps retain glucose carbon inside the cell and makes it accessible to glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway and glycogen synthesis. In fasting, the liver generates glucose-6-phosphate through glycogen degradation and gluconeogenesis. Glucose-6-phosphatase hydrolyses it to glucose and inorganic phosphate, allowing export of free glucose.

The liver is also responsible for synthesising and exporting lipid in very-low-density lipoproteins, processing much of the nitrogen delivered as amino acids, and producing ketone bodies when the balance of fuel oxidation changes. A particularly important point is that many hepatic pathways support other organs rather than merely providing ATP to hepatocytes. Hepatic fatty acid oxidation, for example, supplies energy that can pay for glucose synthesis. The glucose released can then be used by erythrocytes or other tissues with a greater dependence on carbohydrate.

Skeletal muscle uses blood glucose, its own glycogen, circulating fatty acids and lipid stored within muscle fibres. Its relative reliance on each fuel depends strongly on contraction intensity and duration. A resting muscle with ample oxygen and a low insulin concentration may obtain substantial energy from fatty acids. During intense contraction, glycolysis and glycogen mobilisation become especially important because ATP demand rises rapidly. Muscles can also oxidise lactate and ketone bodies when these are available. Different fibre types have different mitochondrial abundance and enzyme capacities, so the behavior of an entire muscle represents the combined activity of heterogeneous cells.

Glucose entry into muscle is regulated partly through GLUT4, a glucose transporter held in intracellular vesicles when basal demand is low. Insulin signalling and signals associated with contraction each stimulate redistribution of GLUT4 to the cell surface. The two stimuli have overlapping endpoints but distinct upstream regulatory mechanisms. This distinction explains why exercise can increase muscle glucose uptake even when circulating insulin falls. Once inside muscle, glucose is phosphorylated by hexokinase. Glucose-6-phosphate can be directed into glycogen storage or broken down for local energy production, but it cannot generally be dephosphorylated and released as free glucose to support the brain.

White adipose tissue specialises in storing triacylglycerol. After feeding, lipoprotein lipase at the capillary surface hydrolyses triacylglycerol in circulating lipoproteins, releasing fatty acids that can enter adipocytes. Intracellular esterification combines activated fatty acids with a glycerol backbone. Insulin promotes glucose uptake, which supplies carbon for glycerol-3-phosphate generation, and also inhibits intracellular lipolysis. During fasting, adipocytes hydrolyse their stored triacylglycerol, releasing fatty acids that travel through the blood largely bound to albumin. Glycerol is released into circulation and can be converted to glucose by liver and kidney.

Adipocytes generally have relatively limited capacity to phosphorylate free glycerol because glycerol kinase activity is low compared with that of the liver. This means that the glycerol backbone used for triacylglycerol synthesis depends substantially on carbohydrate-derived intermediates and, under some conditions, glyceroneogenesis. This explains why lipid storage and carbohydrate metabolism are mechanistically connected even when fatty acids are plentiful. It also explains why glycerol release from adipose tissue can be a useful indicator of lipid mobilization, although the interpretation must account for other sources and metabolic clearance.

The adult brain normally relies heavily on glucose for oxidative energy metabolism. The blood–brain barrier expresses specific transporters that support regulated glucose delivery, and neurons have a high continuous ATP requirement associated with electrical activity and ion gradients. Long-chain fatty acids do not ordinarily provide a major direct energetic contribution to the brain. During sustained fasting, however, the brain increases its use of the water-soluble ketone bodies acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate. This adaptation decreases but does not eliminate glucose demand. It thereby reduces the amount of amino acid carbon that must be mobilised from protein to support glucose synthesis.

Red blood cells differ fundamentally from neurons. Mature mammalian erythrocytes lack mitochondria and are incapable of oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid beta-oxidation or ketone body oxidation. They obtain ATP through glycolysis, converting pyruvate largely to lactate to regenerate the oxidised cofactor NAD+. The pentose phosphate pathway also supplies NADPH needed to protect cellular components against oxidative damage. Erythrocyte glucose use therefore persists during prolonged fasting, even when many other organs switch toward fatty acids or ketones.

The myocardium has a high oxidative capacity and a continuous requirement for ATP. Under ordinary aerobic conditions it can oxidise fatty acids, glucose, lactate and ketone bodies, with proportions depending on workload and circulating supply. During exercise, the increased delivery of lactate from skeletal muscle can make lactate an important cardiac fuel. This observation challenges the misconception that lactate is only a metabolic waste product. The heart's capacity to oxidise lactate also illustrates how the biochemical product released by one tissue may support the work of another.

The kidney contributes more than filtration and excretion. Renal cortical cells can perform gluconeogenesis and release glucose, whereas portions of the renal medulla are relatively glycolytic because of their oxygen environment and transport work. During prolonged fasting and some acid-base disturbances, renal glucose production becomes increasingly important. Glutamine metabolism in the kidney connects carbon metabolism with ammonium excretion and acid-base regulation. Thus a whole-organ label such as kidney can conceal major differences between its internal regions.

These organ differences establish a useful rule for examination problems: identify the relevant cell type before deciding whether a pathway is possible. The presence of a substrate in blood does not guarantee that a cell can use it. A ketone body cannot provide oxidative ATP to an erythrocyte; a muscle glycogen molecule cannot directly maintain circulating glucose; and a hepatocyte may produce ketone bodies without being able to consume them through the principal ketolytic pathway. The pathway repertoire is as important as the availability of substrate.

3. The absorptive state: allocation and storage

Following a carbohydrate-containing meal, digestive enzymes convert available dietary polysaccharides and disaccharides into absorbable monosaccharides. Glucose reaches the intestinal portal blood, and the pancreatic endocrine system detects the increased nutrient availability. In beta cells, metabolism of glucose raises the ATP-to-ADP ratio. Closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels reduces outward potassium current, depolarising the cell membrane. Voltage-sensitive calcium channels open, calcium entry promotes vesicle fusion, and insulin is secreted. Gut-derived incretin hormones can amplify nutrient-dependent insulin secretion. The rise in insulin coordinates the handling of a meal across tissues.

Insulin acts through a receptor with intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity. Receptor activation recruits intracellular signalling proteins and promotes the phosphoinositide 3-kinase–Akt pathway. In muscle and adipose cells this increases the amount of GLUT4 at the plasma membrane, thereby enhancing glucose transport under suitable concentration conditions. Akt also participates in regulation of glycogen synthesis through inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3. Insulin promotes a broader shift toward storage by regulating phosphatases, metabolic enzymes, gene expression and the delivery of substrates. Its physiological effect cannot be reduced to a single GLUT4 pathway because different organs express different glucose transport systems.

In the liver, high glucose delivery and insulin signalling favour glycogen synthesis. Glucose-6-phosphate is converted by phosphoglucomutase into glucose-1-phosphate, which reacts with UTP to generate UDP-glucose. Glycogen synthase transfers glucose from UDP-glucose to an existing glycogen chain, generating alpha-1,4 linkages. The branching enzyme transfers an oligosaccharide segment to form an alpha-1,6 linkage. The branched architecture produces many sites where synthesis or degradation can proceed. The energy required for incorporation is supplied through nucleotide metabolism; glycogen synthesis is not simply passive aggregation of glucose molecules.

Muscle responds to the meal in a complementary way. Increased transport and phosphorylation of glucose supply both ATP-producing pathways and glycogen synthesis. A muscle that has previously exercised may be particularly capable of restoring glycogen when carbohydrate becomes available. Glycogen retained in muscle is an immediately accessible local reserve for later contraction. In contrast, hepatic glycogen acts as a reserve that helps buffer systemic glucose availability. The same polymer serves different physiological functions because the enzymes, hormone responses and transport processes differ between organs.

As glycogen synthesis and immediate energy demands are satisfied, some glucose carbon can enter pathways that support fatty acid synthesis. Glycolysis supplies pyruvate, which can enter the mitochondrial matrix and be converted by pyruvate dehydrogenase to acetyl-CoA. Acetyl-CoA does not cross the inner mitochondrial membrane freely. Under appropriate conditions, its carbon is exported in citrate, which is cleaved in the cytosol by ATP citrate lyase. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase produces malonyl-CoA using ATP and bicarbonate. Fatty acid synthase uses acetyl and malonyl units with reducing power from NADPH to build fatty acid chains. This pathway converts a carbohydrate-derived carbon supply into lipid that can be esterified and stored or exported.

Malonyl-CoA also serves a regulatory role. It inhibits carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, a component of the mitochondrial transport system for long-chain fatty acyl groups. Increased malonyl-CoA availability during active fatty acid synthesis can reduce mitochondrial fatty acid entry for oxidation. In this way synthesis and oxidation are coordinated to avoid excessive simultaneous operation. The regulation is not an absolute lock: different tissues and compartments have distinct enzyme isoforms and concentrations, and substrate oxidation does not cease merely because a meal has been consumed.

Dietary fat reaches peripheral tissues through chylomicrons. Lipoprotein lipase, positioned at the luminal side of capillary endothelial cells, hydrolyses their triacylglycerol and releases fatty acids for tissue uptake. Adipocytes esterify fatty acids with glycerol-3-phosphate, storing triacylglycerol in lipid droplets. Lipoprotein remnants are eventually processed by the liver. Hepatic very-low-density lipoproteins provide a second route for delivery of triacylglycerol to peripheral tissues. These extracellular lipid transport processes must be distinguished from intracellular lipolysis: one makes lipoprotein-carried fuel accessible, whereas the other mobilises a cell's own stored lipid.

Insulin inhibits adipocyte lipolysis through signalling that reduces cyclic AMP-dependent protein kinase activity, including activation of phosphodiesterase 3B. This limits release of nonesterified fatty acids at a time when dietary nutrients are available. It also reduces the substrate delivered to hepatic ketogenesis. The suppression of fatty acid release is important because a meal contains not only glucose but often fat as well. A reduction in circulating albumin-bound fatty acids does not necessarily indicate a reduction in dietary lipid absorption: much of the newly absorbed lipid may be carried in lipoproteins instead.

Amino acids arriving after a protein-containing meal may be incorporated into newly synthesised proteins, used in synthesis of specialised nitrogen-containing molecules, or oxidised. Excess amino groups are transferred and ultimately disposed of, predominantly through hepatic urea formation. Carbon skeletons enter metabolism at several positions, depending on amino acid identity. This is a different type of storage problem from glycogen: the body cannot deposit excess amino acids in a dedicated inert polymer and later retrieve them without affecting tissue function. Protein synthesis and degradation nevertheless participate in the coordinated response to feeding and fasting.

Glucagon should be interpreted in the context of meal composition. A glucose-rich meal generally favours insulin secretion and reduces the relative hepatic influence of glucagon. A meal rich in protein may stimulate glucagon as well as insulin, helping maintain glucose output despite the insulin response. Therefore the fed state is not defined by the absolute disappearance of glucagon. The key is the combination of nutrient concentrations, hormone actions and organ-specific substrate flow. Pancreatic hormone measurements are informative, but by themselves they do not directly quantify metabolic pathway flux.

When absorption gradually declines, the rate of glucose input from the intestine decreases. Insulin secretion adjusts, the suppression of endogenous glucose production is relieved and the body begins drawing on stored fuel. The transition is smooth because metabolic regulation operates through changes in enzyme activity, transporter trafficking, substrate delivery and hormone secretion. No single clock time marks the moment at which all organs simultaneously stop storing fuel and begin mobilising it.

4. Glycogen turnover and maintenance of blood glucose

Glycogenolysis is the enzymatic mobilization of glucose residues from glycogen. Glycogen phosphorylase cleaves alpha-1,4 linkages by phosphorolysis, using inorganic phosphate and releasing glucose-1-phosphate rather than free glucose. The enzyme cannot continue indefinitely toward a branch point. When it approaches an alpha-1,6 junction, the debranching enzyme transfers a short segment to a neighboring chain and hydrolyses the remaining branch-linked residue. This latter reaction produces a small amount of free glucose. Phosphoglucomutase interconverts glucose-1-phosphate and glucose-6-phosphate, linking glycogen mobilisation to glycolysis and hepatic glucose export.

The advantage of phosphorolysis is particularly clear in contracting muscle. Free extracellular glucose requires phosphorylation by hexokinase at the cost of one ATP before it can enter glycolysis. A glycogen-derived glucosyl residue already enters the pathway as a sugar phosphate. Conversion of a residue released by phosphorylase to two lactate molecules therefore gives a net yield of three ATP, compared with two ATP from one free glucose molecule. This calculation assumes the usual glycolytic reactions and considers only ATP produced directly by substrate-level phosphorylation. It does not include the earlier energy cost of building the glycogen polymer or the energetic value of the released lactate.

Hepatic glycogen degradation is sensitive to circulating glucagon. The glucagon receptor is coupled to a stimulatory G protein. Receptor activation increases adenylate cyclase activity, elevates cyclic AMP and activates protein kinase A. Protein kinase A promotes phosphorylation and activation of phosphorylase kinase, which in turn phosphorylates glycogen phosphorylase. The cascade amplifies a hormonal signal and adjusts the rate at which glucose residues are mobilised. At the same time, phosphorylation of glycogen synthase contributes to suppression of glycogen synthesis. This reciprocal regulation reduces unnecessary simultaneous synthesis and breakdown of glycogen.

A fall in blood glucose does not need to be large for endogenous glucose production to increase. Decreased insulin action and changes in glucagon signalling can promote hepatic output as intestinal absorption falls. The liver's contribution is determined by the combined rate of glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, local glucose use and export. Even in a postabsorptive person, gluconeogenesis is not absent while glycogen is being degraded. Both pathways operate in parallel and their proportions change as fasting continues.

Muscle glycogenolysis uses related enzymes, but the upstream control is adapted to local work. A motor neuron activates contraction, intracellular calcium rises and calcium-dependent regulation of phosphorylase kinase links glycogen breakdown to excitation. Rising AMP also acts as an allosteric signal of energy demand and can promote activity of muscle glycogen phosphorylase. Adrenergic signalling can further support mobilisation. This combination allows an active muscle to increase glycolytic fuel delivery before a systemic hormonal response alone could account for the change.

Glucagon is not the principal direct activator of skeletal muscle glycogenolysis. In typical mammalian physiology, glucagon's major glucose-mobilising action is hepatic. Skeletal muscle responds to contraction-related signals and catecholamines instead. An examination question that describes an isolated muscle preparation exposed to glucagon should therefore not assume the same robust glycogen response expected from a liver preparation. The physiological effect of a hormone depends on receptor expression and the downstream regulatory network.

The liver exports free glucose using glucose-6-phosphatase, an enzyme associated with the endoplasmic reticulum system. The glucose-6-phosphate substrate must reach the catalytic compartment, and products are subsequently returned to appropriate cellular compartments for glucose release. Muscle normally lacks the equivalent glucose-export capacity. Its glycogen reserve may be large in absolute amount because total skeletal muscle mass is large, but this does not make muscle glycogen a freely interchangeable systemic glucose reservoir. Muscle can contribute glucose carbon indirectly by releasing lactate or alanine that the liver can convert to glucose.

Glycogen content is an inventory, not a direct measure of glycogenolytic flux. Suppose hepatic glycogen falls by 20 g over a particular interval. The net loss is compatible with 20 g of degradation and no synthesis, but it is also compatible with 25 g of degradation and 5 g of simultaneous synthesis. Measurements of glycogen concentration at two time points cannot resolve these opposing flows without additional evidence. Isotope labelling or controlled measurements of pathway intermediates can provide information that net pool change alone cannot.

The duration for which glycogen supports fasting metabolism varies. An adult's hepatic glycogen reserve is finite and is influenced by recent meals, exercise and previous fasting. A single statement that all glycogen vanishes after a fixed number of hours should be treated as an oversimplification. Muscle glycogen may remain substantial even when liver glycogen is depleted, especially if muscle activity was low. After a carbohydrate-rich meal, glycogen restoration also depends on uptake, insulin signalling, previous depletion and the balance between synthesis and utilization.

A prediction follows from these distinctions. If hepatic glucose-6-phosphatase function is selectively impaired, formation of glucose-6-phosphate from glycogen can continue but its release as free glucose is restricted. Glucose-6-phosphate may be redirected toward glycolysis and other pathways, and fasting glucose regulation becomes defective. A defect in muscle glycogen phosphorylase instead compromises the local use of muscle glycogen during contraction. These two disturbances affect different physiological tasks even though both concern glycogen metabolism.

5. Gluconeogenesis: making glucose when dietary input declines

The body needs a mechanism for generating glucose after dietary carbohydrate has been absorbed and glycogen reserves decline. Gluconeogenesis is the synthesis of glucose from noncarbohydrate precursors. In a mammal, the most important organs carrying out glucose-releasing gluconeogenesis are the liver and renal cortex. The pathway must be distinguished from glycogenolysis, which releases glucose residues already present in a carbohydrate polymer. Both contribute to endogenous glucose production, but only gluconeogenesis constructs glucose from precursors such as lactate, glycerol and glucogenic amino acid carbon skeletons.

A first conceptual difficulty is that gluconeogenesis is not simply glycolysis running in reverse. Many glycolytic reactions are reversible under cellular conditions, but the reactions catalysed by hexokinase or glucokinase, phosphofructokinase 1 and pyruvate kinase have large negative free-energy changes in the direction of glycolysis. Reversing the complete pathway using the same enzymes would be thermodynamically unfavourable. Gluconeogenesis bypasses these reactions using alternative enzymes and energy-consuming steps. The existence of distinct bypasses also creates important regulatory sites where the two pathways can be adjusted reciprocally.

Consider glucose synthesis starting with two molecules of pyruvate. The first bypass begins in the mitochondrial matrix. Pyruvate carboxylase combines pyruvate with bicarbonate using ATP to generate oxaloacetate. Biotin serves as the carrier of activated carbon dioxide during the reaction. Acetyl-CoA is an important allosteric activator of pyruvate carboxylase. A high supply of acetyl-CoA during fatty acid oxidation therefore favours conversion of pyruvate toward oxaloacetate instead of simply reflecting a high rate of carbohydrate oxidation.

Oxaloacetate is a four-carbon compound, whereas pyruvate contains three carbons. The additional carbon introduced by carboxylation is removed by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase, which converts oxaloacetate to phosphoenolpyruvate using GTP. Coupling carboxylation with subsequent decarboxylation provides an effective route around the strongly favourable pyruvate kinase step. The carbon added from bicarbonate is not retained as an extra carbon in the final phosphoenolpyruvate molecule. This explains how the pathway can begin with two three-carbon precursors and end with a six-carbon glucose molecule without requiring a net contribution of bicarbonate carbon.

Mitochondrial and cytosolic compartments must be considered. Oxaloacetate does not freely cross the inner mitochondrial membrane. Depending on the precursor and pathway context, its carbon may be transferred as malate or aspartate and regenerated in the cytosol, or mitochondrial phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase may generate phosphoenolpyruvate that can be transported outward. The malate route can also transfer reducing equivalents: oxidation of cytosolic malate to oxaloacetate produces cytosolic NADH. This is particularly relevant when the starting precursor is pyruvate or alanine, rather than lactate, because gluconeogenesis requires cytosolic NADH at the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase step.

From phosphoenolpyruvate, reversible glycolytic reactions generate triose phosphate intermediates. Reverse operation of phosphoglycerate kinase consumes ATP, and reverse operation of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase consumes NADH. One triose phosphate can be converted to dihydroxyacetone phosphate and condensed with another glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate molecule through aldolase to form fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase hydrolyses the phosphate ester to fructose-6-phosphate. This second bypass does not generate ATP; it uses hydrolysis to avoid the irreversible phosphofructokinase 1 reaction.

Phosphoglucose isomerase interconverts fructose-6-phosphate and glucose-6-phosphate. In the final bypass, glucose-6-phosphatase catalyses hydrolysis of glucose-6-phosphate to free glucose and inorganic phosphate. The hepatic and renal machinery for this reaction is what makes these organs capable of supplying glucose to the circulation. A cell without this capacity may use portions of the gluconeogenic pathway to generate intermediates, but it cannot necessarily release glucose in physiologically important amounts.

Synthesis of one glucose molecule from two pyruvate molecules requires four ATP, two GTP and two NADH. Two ATP are used in the pyruvate carboxylase reactions, two GTP in the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase reactions, and two ATP in the reverse phosphoglycerate kinase reactions. The two NADH are consumed during reduction of 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate. Because ATP and GTP each represent one high-energy phosphate transfer in this accounting, the requirement is often expressed as six high-energy phosphate equivalents plus reducing power. This cost illustrates why gluconeogenesis is closely coupled to energy provision from other fuels.

Lactate is a major gluconeogenic precursor. Lactate dehydrogenase converts lactate into pyruvate while reducing NAD+ to NADH. Two lactate molecules therefore provide the two cytosolic NADH needed for glucose formation, assuming appropriate redox balance and compartmentation. The resulting pyruvate enters the carboxylase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase bypass. In physiological terms, the liver can take up the end product of glycolysis in erythrocytes or working muscle and regenerate a circulating carbohydrate source. This is a recycling pathway rather than a new source of carbon if the lactate was itself formed from glucose.

Alanine supplies pyruvate through transamination rather than oxidation. Alanine aminotransferase transfers the amino group from alanine to alpha-ketoglutarate, yielding pyruvate and glutamate. Because this reaction does not directly generate NADH, gluconeogenesis from alanine has a different cytosolic redox requirement from gluconeogenesis from lactate. The nitrogen must also be handled independently of the carbon skeleton. Hepatic nitrogen metabolism therefore becomes coupled to glucose formation when alanine is a major precursor.

Glycerol reaches gluconeogenesis by a shorter route. Glycerol kinase phosphorylates glycerol to glycerol-3-phosphate using ATP. Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase can oxidise this compound to dihydroxyacetone phosphate while generating NADH. Dihydroxyacetone phosphate enters the pathway at the triose phosphate level, bypassing the pyruvate carboxylase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase steps. The theoretical phosphate expenditure for generating glucose from two free glycerol molecules by this route is two ATP at the glycerol kinase reactions, rather than six high-energy phosphate equivalents at the pyruvate bypass and reverse phosphoglycerate kinase steps. The redox products and actual cellular energy balance also depend on how the NADH produced is subsequently handled.

Not every amino acid can make net glucose. Amino acids are called glucogenic when their carbon skeleton can yield pyruvate or a citric acid cycle intermediate that supports net gluconeogenesis. Leucine and lysine are exclusively ketogenic in the standard classification. Other amino acids may provide both glucogenic and ketogenic products. The distinction becomes important in examination questions that ask whether a precursor can support net glucose synthesis, rather than merely whether some of its carbon can appear in an intermediate.

The same reasoning explains why even-chain fatty acids are not a source of net glucose in humans. Their beta-oxidation yields acetyl-CoA. The pyruvate dehydrogenase reaction is effectively irreversible in normal mammalian metabolism, so acetyl-CoA cannot simply be converted back to pyruvate. When acetyl-CoA enters the citric acid cycle, the cycle regenerates oxaloacetate while releasing carbon dioxide; it does not create a net surplus of oxaloacetate from acetyl-CoA alone. Mammals lack the glyoxylate cycle that would bypass the decarboxylation steps and permit net conversion of acetyl units to four-carbon intermediates. Odd-chain fatty acids differ because their final three-carbon propionyl-CoA fragment can enter metabolism through methylmalonyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA.

There is consequently a major difference between supplying carbon for gluconeogenesis and supplying energy for gluconeogenesis. Oxidation of an even-chain fatty acid may provide ATP, reducing equivalents and acetyl-CoA-mediated activation of pyruvate carboxylase without supplying net glucose carbon. Conversely, glycerol released from the same triacylglycerol molecule can directly contribute carbon to glucose. A statement such as fat can support blood glucose therefore needs to be interpreted carefully: its glycerol component, its odd-chain fraction if present, and its contribution to hepatic energy metabolism have different roles.

Hormonal regulation helps determine whether hepatic carbohydrate is consumed or produced. In the fasting liver, glucagon-dependent protein kinase A phosphorylation of the liver isoform of the bifunctional PFK-2/FBPase-2 enzyme favours its phosphatase activity. Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate concentration falls. Because fructose-2,6-bisphosphate activates phosphofructokinase 1 and inhibits fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, its decline reduces glycolytic drive and facilitates gluconeogenesis. This example illustrates reciprocal regulation at a point where two different enzymes catalyse opposing irreversible reactions. The effect of phosphorylation on the bifunctional enzyme is isoform-specific, so the hepatic rule should not be generalized to all tissues without checking which isoform is expressed.

Glucagon also contributes to changes in expression of enzymes supporting glucose synthesis, while insulin generally suppresses the hepatic gluconeogenic programme. These slower responses reinforce the rapid effects on enzyme activity, substrate supply and adipose lipolysis. It is possible for an enzyme concentration to remain constant while pathway flux changes substantially; for example, raising delivery of lactate and glycerol can influence substrate use without immediately changing enzyme abundance. Consequently transcriptional measurements alone cannot establish the amount of glucose produced.

Energy and redox state impose further constraints. When mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation supplies ATP and acetyl-CoA, hepatic gluconeogenesis is supported. If oxidation is severely compromised, the energy cost of glucose formation becomes difficult to meet during fasting. An excess of cytosolic NADH may also shift lactate dehydrogenase and malate dehydrogenase equilibria toward lactate and malate. This can restrict access to pyruvate and oxaloacetate even when total carbon substrates are present. The important inference is that pathway direction depends on thermodynamics and redox balance as well as hormones.

Gluconeogenesis is a continuous contributor to systemic metabolism, not a reserve pathway that turns on only after liver glycogen has reached zero. As fasting progresses, glycogen-derived glucose generally contributes a decreasing proportion while glucose formation from circulating substrates contributes a larger share. In a person with depleted hepatic glycogen, the absolute rate of endogenous glucose production may nevertheless fall if overall glucose demand declines and the brain increasingly oxidises ketones. Proportion and absolute rate are different measurements. The same pathway can account for a larger fraction of a smaller total.

6. Adipose lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation

Mobilising stored lipid is a coordinated process involving intracellular hydrolysis, transport through the blood, uptake by oxidative tissues and mitochondrial oxidation. Adipose triacylglycerol consists of three fatty acyl chains esterified to glycerol. Hydrolysis of the ester bonds occurs in a sequence rather than through a single enzyme that directly releases all components at once. Adipose triglyceride lipase catalyses the first major step, generating diacylglycerol and one fatty acid. Hormone-sensitive lipase is particularly effective at hydrolysing diacylglycerol to monoacylglycerol and another fatty acid. Monoacylglycerol lipase completes hydrolysis, releasing the final fatty acid and glycerol.

Lipid droplets are surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer and associated proteins that regulate access to stored triacylglycerol. In white adipocytes, perilipin 1 helps organize this surface and regulate mobilisation. Under catecholamine stimulation, cyclic AMP-dependent protein kinase A phosphorylates targets that include hormone-sensitive lipase and perilipin. Remodeling of the droplet surface and activation of regulatory interactions involving adipose triglyceride lipase increase access of the lipolytic machinery to its substrates. This response allows hormonal information to change the rate of release of fatty acids without requiring new enzyme synthesis.

A decrease in insulin during fasting relieves the normal suppression of lipolysis. Sympathetic stimulation and circulating catecholamines become particularly relevant during exercise. In human white adipose tissue, direct glucagon stimulation should not be assumed to be the dominant cause of lipolysis. Low insulin and adrenergic signals are central to the physiological response. Different adrenergic receptor subtypes can have stimulatory or inhibitory effects, so the net response depends on tissue location and receptor balance as well as hormone concentration.

The fatty acids released from adipocytes have limited solubility in an aqueous environment. Albumin binds and carries much of the nonesterified fatty acid pool in blood. This association is reversible and allows delivery to multiple organs. Nonesterified does not mean unbound to protein, and plasma fatty acids carried by albumin are chemically distinct from fatty acyl groups esterified within lipoprotein triacylglycerol. A laboratory measurement of total plasma triacylglycerol therefore does not directly measure the concentration of albumin-associated nonesterified fatty acids.

Not all fatty acids liberated within an adipocyte reach the blood. Some can be re-esterified to triacylglycerol when the necessary glycerol backbone and activated acyl groups are available. The gross rate of hydrolysis may therefore be larger than net fatty acid release. If glycerol and fatty acid concentrations in blood are measured, their ratio need not correspond exactly to the theoretical three-to-one stoichiometry of complete hydrolysis. Tissue uptake, re-esterification, incomplete mobilization and clearance from circulation alter the observed relationship. This is one reason tracer methods are valuable in studies of lipid metabolism.

For oxidation, a long-chain fatty acid must first be activated to fatty acyl-CoA by an acyl-CoA synthetase. ATP is converted to AMP and pyrophosphate. Hydrolysis of pyrophosphate helps drive the reaction and the overall energetic cost is conventionally counted as two high-energy phosphate equivalents. The resulting long-chain acyl-CoA cannot freely traverse the inner mitochondrial membrane. The carnitine transport system provides a mechanism for transferring the acyl group into the matrix while maintaining separate cytosolic and mitochondrial coenzyme A pools.

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 transfers the acyl group from CoA to carnitine at the outer mitochondrial membrane. The acylcarnitine is transported across the inner membrane by carnitine-acylcarnitine translocase, and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 regenerates fatty acyl-CoA in the matrix. Free carnitine can then be returned through the transport system. This arrangement gives the cell an important regulatory point before large amounts of long-chain fatty acid carbon enter beta-oxidation. Medium-chain fatty acids are less dependent on this particular transport mechanism, so an experimental effect of inhibiting CPT1 cannot be assumed identical for all fatty acid chain lengths.

Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT1. During fasting, changes in hormonal and cellular energy signals tend to reduce the fatty acid synthesis programme and relieve the inhibition of long-chain fatty acid entry. In exercising muscle, AMP-activated protein kinase can contribute to regulation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase and fuel selection. However, the control of fat oxidation is not fully explained by a single AMPK-to-malonyl-CoA pathway. Fatty acid availability, muscle fibre recruitment, oxygen delivery, mitochondrial capacity and competing carbohydrate flux all contribute.

Beta-oxidation proceeds through four recurring reactions. An acyl-CoA dehydrogenase introduces a double bond and passes reducing equivalents through electron transfer flavoprotein. Hydration adds water across the double bond. Oxidation of the hydroxyl group generates NADH and a beta-ketoacyl-CoA intermediate. Thiolase then cleaves the carbon chain using CoA, releasing an acetyl-CoA molecule and an acyl-CoA shortened by two carbon atoms. Repetition of the sequence removes successive two-carbon units from a saturated even-chain fatty acid.

For palmitate, a saturated sixteen-carbon fatty acid, seven beta-oxidation cycles produce eight acetyl-CoA, seven NADH and seven FAD-linked reducing equivalents. Using conventional approximate oxidative phosphorylation yields of 2.5 ATP per NADH and 1.5 ATP per FAD-linked pair of electrons, each cycle contributes approximately four ATP equivalents through reducing equivalents. Eight acetyl-CoA entering the citric acid cycle yield approximately eighty ATP equivalents, using ten per acetyl-CoA. Adding twenty-eight and eighty and subtracting two for activation gives approximately 106 ATP per palmitate under the specified assumptions. The actual biological yield varies with mitochondrial coupling and the energetic costs of transport and cellular processes.

The calculation illustrates why stored fat is energetically concentrated. It should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of ATP yield from a particular living muscle fibre. A fatty acid may be partially oxidised, stored, exported in a lipid, or used for synthesis instead of completing oxidation to carbon dioxide. Furthermore, ATP equivalents in a biochemical accounting model are approximate and do not imply perfectly efficient conversion of chemical energy into mechanical work.

During fasting, oxidative organs can obtain much of their ATP by using fatty acids. This shift helps spare circulating glucose for cells that depend on it more strongly. The liver simultaneously uses fatty acid oxidation to fund gluconeogenesis and generate acetyl-CoA for ketogenesis. Thus lipolysis is not merely a local process in adipose tissue: its products reshape energy metabolism in several distant organs. The glycerol and fatty acid portions of the same stored triacylglycerol molecule enter distinct pathways but can cooperate in maintaining systemic fuel availability.

7. Ketogenesis and the adaptation to sustained fasting

When fasting continues and the delivery of fatty acids to the liver increases, hepatic mitochondria generate substantial quantities of acetyl-CoA. The ability of the citric acid cycle to process acetyl units depends on the availability of oxaloacetate, the redox state of the mitochondrial matrix, and the rates of downstream energy metabolism. At the same time, the liver is supporting glucose production. Under a low-insulin, relatively high-glucagon metabolic environment, excess acetyl-CoA is directed into ketogenesis. The resulting water-soluble compounds can leave the liver and supply oxidative energy to other organs.

The principal ketone bodies are acetoacetate, D-beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetone. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is grouped with the ketone bodies for physiological reasons, although its chemical functional group is a hydroxyl rather than a ketone. Acetone is formed by decarboxylation of acetoacetate and is relatively volatile. Acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate are the major circulating ketone fuels. Their solubility and transport characteristics differ from those of the long-chain fatty acids from which much of their carbon originates.

Ketogenesis occurs principally in hepatic mitochondria. Two acetyl-CoA molecules condense through thiolase to form acetoacetyl-CoA. Mitochondrial HMG-CoA synthase, HMGCS2, combines acetoacetyl-CoA with another acetyl-CoA and water to generate 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA. HMG-CoA lyase then cleaves this compound to form acetoacetate and acetyl-CoA. Although three acetyl-CoA molecules participate in the sequence, one is released again; the net carbon incorporated into acetoacetate corresponds to two acetyl units.

The HMG-CoA synthase involved in mitochondrial ketogenesis must be distinguished from the cytosolic pathway leading to cholesterol synthesis. The two processes share an intermediate name but occur in different compartments and involve different regulatory arrangements. In a question involving a defect in mitochondrial HMGCS2, the most direct prediction is impaired ketone body production, not necessarily a direct block of cytosolic cholesterol synthesis. Compartmentation is an essential part of enzyme specificity.

Acetoacetate can be reduced to beta-hydroxybutyrate by mitochondrial beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase. The reaction uses NADH and produces NAD+. The relative concentrations of acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate are therefore related to mitochondrial redox conditions, although their circulating ratio is also influenced by transport, peripheral metabolism and sampling. Increased fatty acid oxidation can supply NADH and favour beta-hydroxybutyrate formation. Ketogenesis consequently connects carbon disposal and redox metabolism rather than simply producing a single end product of beta-oxidation.

A ketone body released by the liver reaches an oxidative tissue through the circulation. Monocarboxylate transporters contribute to cellular uptake. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is oxidised to acetoacetate, yielding NADH. Succinyl-CoA:3-oxoacid CoA transferase, commonly abbreviated SCOT and encoded by OXCT1, transfers CoA from succinyl-CoA to acetoacetate to form acetoacetyl-CoA. Thiolase cleaves acetoacetyl-CoA into two acetyl-CoA molecules, which can enter the citric acid cycle. Ketone body oxidation thus supplies energy through mitochondrial oxidative pathways.

The liver lacks physiologically significant SCOT activity for ketone utilization. This is why the organ can produce and export ketones without simultaneously consuming the same fuel through the principal ketolytic pathway. In contrast, heart, skeletal muscle, renal tissue and, after adaptation, brain can oxidise ketones. Mature erythrocytes cannot oxidise them because ketolysis and subsequent ATP production require mitochondria. The capability to use ketones is therefore determined by the organ's enzyme repertoire and cellular architecture.

A classic physiological question was how the human brain continues to function during extended nutrient deprivation without an unsustainably high demand for glucose synthesis from body protein. Owen and colleagues reported cerebral substrate exchange in people undergoing prolonged fasting in 1967. Their study used measurements of blood entering and leaving the brain to investigate which circulating fuels were consumed. The evidence supported a major increase in cerebral use of acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate. The study was important because it linked a change in circulating substrate to actual organ uptake rather than relying only on ketone concentration in peripheral blood. Its small selected cohort and prolonged fasting conditions limit generalisation to every individual or to ordinary overnight fasting.

As ketone use by the brain rises, its demand for glucose falls. This adaptation helps reduce the requirement for gluconeogenic amino acids and thereby limits the rate of body protein loss. Protein degradation does not become zero, because proteins are continuously turned over and glucose-dependent cells remain. The adaptation is best understood as a redistribution of metabolic demand. The liver and adipose tissue increase delivery of an alternative oxidative fuel, the brain uses a larger fraction of that fuel, and the need to generate glucose from tissue protein decreases.

The transition toward substantial ketone use is gradual. Overnight fasting, prolonged fasting and severe starvation are not interchangeable states. A person who has just missed one meal will generally have a different balance of glycogen contribution, gluconeogenesis and ketone oxidation from a person who has undergone several days of fasting. Exercise, prior carbohydrate intake and hormonal conditions influence this trajectory. The physiological mechanism is more reliable for exam reasoning than a rigid fasting timetable.

Ketone body concentration reflects both production and disposal. At a given time the liver may produce ketones rapidly while oxidative organs consume them rapidly; the circulating concentration could be lower than in a situation where disposal is impaired. Exhaled acetone, blood beta-hydroxybutyrate and urinary acetoacetate report related but nonidentical aspects of metabolism. The concentration of one compound cannot by itself establish the net rate of hepatic ketogenesis. The same distinction between concentration and flux that applies to glucose applies to ketones.

Physiological fasting ketosis should also be distinguished from pathological ketoacidosis. During controlled fasting, residual insulin action and metabolic feedback usually constrain the delivery of fatty acids and the production of ketone acids. In severe insulin deficiency, uncontrolled lipolysis, hepatic ketogenesis and disturbances of glucose metabolism may lead to dangerous acid accumulation. Ketone bodies are therefore neither intrinsically pathological nor always harmless. Their physiological effect depends on production, utilization, acid-base buffering and excretion. Detailed disease mechanisms belong to later chapters on diabetes and metabolic disorders, but the contrast clarifies why hormonal restraint is important.

The fasting liver is often described as oxidising fat to support glucose synthesis and ketone production simultaneously. The phrase does not imply that every acetyl-CoA molecule generated by beta-oxidation becomes a ketone body. Some acetyl-CoA continues to enter the citric acid cycle, while other molecules contribute to ketogenesis. Changes in relative flux are more informative than an absolute on-or-off picture. The balance is affected by substrate delivery, energy demand, mitochondrial redox conditions, regulation of enzymes and utilization of glucose precursors.

8. The Cori cycle: moving carbohydrate carbon between organs

Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate and produces ATP through substrate-level phosphorylation. At the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase step it also reduces NAD+ to NADH. Glycolysis can continue only if the oxidised form of this cofactor is regenerated. Lactate dehydrogenase provides a rapid cytosolic mechanism: pyruvate accepts reducing equivalents from NADH and becomes lactate while NAD+ is regenerated. This reaction supports ongoing glycolytic ATP production in cells without mitochondria and in cells where glycolytic flux exceeds the ability of oxidative processes to handle pyruvate and cytosolic reducing equivalents.

The conversion of pyruvate to lactate does not itself produce extra ATP. Its benefit is indirect: it regenerates NAD+ so that the ATP-producing reactions of glycolysis can continue. In a mature erythrocyte, mitochondrial oxidation is unavailable, so lactate production is a persistent feature of glucose metabolism. In muscle, lactate can be generated in the presence of oxygen because the rate of glycolytic production of pyruvate and NADH may exceed the rate at which the particular cell oxidises them. Oxygen absence is one reason for enhanced reliance on glycolysis, but it is not a necessary condition for lactate formation.

Lactate can cross cell membranes through monocarboxylate transporters, which couple the movement of monocarboxylate anions with protons. The direction of net transport depends on concentration gradients and the physiological conditions of the tissue. A rapidly glycolytic muscle fibre may release lactate while a neighboring oxidative fibre takes it up. The heart can also use lactate, especially when circulating delivery is high. This transfer of usable carbon between sites of production and oxidation is described by the lactate shuttle concept.

The Cori cycle identifies a specific fate of lactate. Lactate released from glycolytic tissues reaches the liver, where lactate dehydrogenase converts it to pyruvate. Gluconeogenesis converts two pyruvate molecules into glucose. The glucose is released into the blood, where it can again be taken up by muscle or erythrocytes. Carbon is recycled through the systemic circulation, and the ATP cost of glucose restoration is paid by the gluconeogenic organ. Renal gluconeogenesis can also contribute to lactate recycling, although the classical diagram emphasizes the liver.

The energetic consequence is especially useful for reasoning. One free glucose molecule yields two lactate molecules and a net two ATP in peripheral glycolysis. Regeneration of one glucose from two lactate requires six high-energy phosphate equivalents in the liver, consisting of four ATP and two GTP. The NADH needed for gluconeogenesis is generated when lactate is oxidised back to pyruvate. Combining the two parts of the cycle gives a net cost of four high-energy phosphate equivalents to the organism. Energy has not been created by recycling glucose. Rather, the cycle moves some of the energetic burden away from the glycolytic tissue toward an organ that can fund glucose synthesis through oxidative metabolism.

This cost is biologically worthwhile in situations where the peripheral tissue needs rapid ATP production or has no mitochondrial machinery. The liver can oxidise fatty acids and provide the required energy even while peripheral glucose is converted to lactate. The cycle therefore represents cooperation between tissues with different biochemical capabilities. If hepatic energy generation is impaired, the ability to sustain gluconeogenesis from lactate can be reduced, and lactate accumulation may become more likely.

There is an important distinction between lactate turnover and Cori cycling. Not all lactate leaving muscle is converted to glucose. Some is directly oxidised by the heart, other muscles or other suitable tissues. Isotope-tracer research and measurements during exercise support substantial direct lactate oxidation. Therefore an observed increase in plasma lactate concentration cannot be assumed to mean that the liver is generating an equal carbon-equivalent amount of new glucose. The relative contributions of oxidation, gluconeogenesis and other metabolic fates depend on physiological conditions.

The original conceptual model associated with Carl and Gerty Cori established the importance of conversion between carbohydrate and lactate across tissues. Later physiological studies refined this view by examining lactate turnover and oxidation in living animals and humans. Brooks's work on lactate shuttling provides a useful example of how a textbook model can be expanded without rejecting its valid core. The Cori cycle remains a meaningful interorgan pathway, but it does not describe every molecule of lactate produced during exercise.

An examination may distinguish the carbon balance of the Cori cycle from its energetic cost. In an idealized complete cycle, the six carbons present in a molecule of glucose are distributed between two three-carbon lactate molecules and returned to glucose. There is no net synthesis of glucose carbon from an external noncarbohydrate source at the whole-body level if the original lactate came from glucose. At the level of the liver, however, the formation of glucose from lactate is genuinely gluconeogenesis. Whether a process represents new carbon input or recycling depends on where the system boundary is drawn.

The cycle also should not be confused with the citric acid cycle. The Cori cycle involves different organs and circulating intermediates, and its principal energetic significance is the transfer of the cost of carbohydrate regeneration. The citric acid cycle is an intracellular mitochondrial network that oxidises acetyl units and generates reduced electron carriers. The word cycle in each case indicates recurrence, but the scale and physiological function differ.

When evaluating an exercise experiment, it is helpful to separate the rate of lactate appearance into the circulation from the rate of lactate disappearance. If appearance exceeds disappearance, the circulating pool increases; if the two rates are equal, concentration can remain stable despite high turnover. A fall in concentration during recovery may result from increased oxidation, decreased production, increased gluconeogenesis or combinations of these processes. Measuring only one blood lactate value cannot distinguish them.

9. The glucose–alanine cycle and nitrogen transport

Working muscle must manage nitrogen as well as carbon. Amino acids are constituents of proteins, but their carbon skeletons can also participate in energy metabolism. When an amino acid transfers its amino group to another molecule, the remaining carbon skeleton can enter an appropriate metabolic pathway. This separation allows the nitrogen to be handled through specialized transport and disposal pathways. The glucose–alanine cycle is a particularly useful example because it connects nitrogen transfer from skeletal muscle with hepatic glucose synthesis.

In skeletal muscle, transamination reactions commonly transfer amino groups to alpha-ketoglutarate, producing glutamate. This creates a pool of transferable amino nitrogen without requiring each amino acid to release free ammonia directly. The enzyme alanine aminotransferase transfers the amino group from glutamate to pyruvate. The products are alanine and alpha-ketoglutarate. The reaction is reversible, and its direction is governed by the relative availability of substrates and products. Pyridoxal phosphate, derived from vitamin B6, participates as the coenzyme that enables transfer of the amino group between carbon skeletons.

Pyruvate is particularly suitable for this purpose in active skeletal muscle because it is generated by glycolysis. Transamination converts a three-carbon keto acid into a three-carbon amino acid without changing the length of the carbon skeleton. Alanine can then enter the blood and carry both glucose-derived carbon and amino nitrogen toward the liver. This differs from lactate, which is a nitrogen-free product of pyruvate reduction. Although lactate and alanine both supply three-carbon precursors for glucose synthesis, their generation and physiological functions are different.

Hepatic alanine aminotransferase can catalyse the reverse reaction. Alanine transfers its amino group to alpha-ketoglutarate and yields pyruvate and glutamate. The pyruvate carbon can enter gluconeogenesis, while the nitrogen retained in glutamate can enter disposal pathways. Glutamate may undergo oxidative deamination to release ammonium, or transfer an amino group to oxaloacetate to produce aspartate. Both ammonium and aspartate serve as sources of nitrogen for the hepatic urea cycle. Thus the liver can separate the two useful aspects of the alanine molecule: reusable carbon and nitrogen requiring controlled elimination.

The urea cycle incorporates two nitrogen atoms into each urea molecule. One nitrogen enters through carbamoyl phosphate formation from ammonium, while the second is supplied by aspartate. The carbon atom in urea is derived from bicarbonate. Three ATP molecules are used in the cycle, but one of them is cleaved to AMP and pyrophosphate, so the conventional energetic cost corresponds to four high-energy phosphate bonds. This is a cost of nitrogen disposal distinct from the ATP and GTP required for gluconeogenesis. In a whole-organ energy balance, fumarate produced during urea formation and connected redox reactions can influence net energy accounting, so a simple addition of nominal pathway costs must be understood as a simplified biochemical budget.

The liver releases glucose formed from alanine, and peripheral muscle can take it up again. Two alanine carbon skeletons can in principle supply the six carbons of one glucose molecule. In this idealized situation, two amino groups have also been transferred to the liver. They could ultimately account for the two nitrogen atoms in a urea molecule if both are appropriately directed into the urea cycle. Actual nitrogen disposal involves mixed pools and multiple amino acids, so an individual urea molecule cannot ordinarily be assigned to a particular pair of circulating alanine molecules without specific isotope evidence.

The glucose–alanine cycle shifts some metabolic work to the liver. Muscle can obtain ATP by glycolysis and transfer amino nitrogen away from the site of contraction. The liver invests energy in synthesising glucose and converting nitrogen to a less toxic excretory product. Fatty acid oxidation can help provide this energy during fasting. Like the Cori cycle, the glucose–alanine cycle is not an energetically free way to generate ATP. Its benefit lies in the division of metabolic functions among tissues and the regulation of potentially harmful nitrogenous products.

It is important not to infer that all alanine released from muscle comes directly from degradation of an alanine residue in a muscle protein. Alanine can be newly synthesised when a nitrogen donor transfers an amino group to glycolytically derived pyruvate. The carbon and nitrogen in a molecule may therefore have different origins. A study using only carbon labels may establish the fate of the pyruvate-derived carbon without establishing the identity of the amino acid that originally donated the nitrogen. Similarly, measuring a change in plasma alanine cannot uniquely identify a change in the rate of muscle protein breakdown.

A landmark study by Felig and colleagues in 1970 examined amino acid exchange in human tissues. By comparing arterial and venous concentrations, the investigators found evidence of substantial alanine release from peripheral muscle and uptake by the liver region. Subsequent work during exercise supported the interpretation that alanine transports amino nitrogen and provides a precursor for hepatic gluconeogenesis. These findings helped establish the physiological cycle. The measurements are a good example of the value of organ-level exchange studies: a circulating concentration alone would not have identified which organ released alanine and which organ consumed it.

The experimental logic can be reproduced with a hypothetical example. Suppose arterial blood contains 0.30 mmol of alanine per litre and venous blood draining a muscle region contains 0.45 mmol per litre. If blood flow through that region is 0.8 litres per minute, and the measurements use compatible whole-blood concentration and flow units, the net alanine release is 0.8 multiplied by 0.15, or 0.12 mmol per minute. The sign is release because more alanine leaves the tissue in venous blood than enters in arterial blood. This calculation says nothing by itself about the gross rates of alanine production and oxidation inside the region, which could both be larger than the net release.

Muscle also exports nitrogen as glutamine. Glutamine synthetase combines glutamate with ammonium at the cost of ATP, and glutamine carries two nitrogen atoms per molecule. The kidney and intestine are among the important organs using glutamine. In the renal cortex, glutamine metabolism can provide substrates for gluconeogenesis and release ammonium for excretion. The glucose–alanine cycle is consequently an important example of nitrogen exchange but is not the only mechanism of interorgan nitrogen transport. The complete physiological network contains multiple transport forms and destinations.

The relative importance of alanine as a glucose precursor changes with nutritional status, exercise and substrate availability. During early fasting, protein-derived amino nitrogen and carbon can contribute to glucose production. With prolonged fasting, increasing brain use of ketones reduces the glucose requirement and tends to limit the need for amino acid mobilization. This protein-sparing adaptation does not abolish the alanine cycle. Instead, it changes the relative magnitude of the fluxes that support glucose homeostasis.

A student can distinguish the Cori and glucose–alanine cycles by following the reaction at pyruvate in muscle. Reduction by lactate dehydrogenase produces lactate and regenerates NAD+. Transamination by alanine aminotransferase produces alanine and transfers amino nitrogen. Both products can be taken up by the liver and return carbon to blood glucose. Only the alanine route directly couples this carbon exchange to transport of an amino group. The difference predicts distinct effects of changes in redox balance, amino acid availability and hepatic nitrogen disposal.

10. Integration across the time course of fasting

The transition from feeding to fasting is a change in the coordination of multiple organs rather than the isolated activation of a pathway. Initially, the body can support blood glucose using liver glycogen and ongoing gluconeogenesis, while peripheral tissues increase their use of fatty acids. The decline in insulin action simultaneously reduces glucose storage, permits endogenous glucose production and relieves suppression of adipose lipolysis. A hormonal change thus has several mutually compatible consequences, because the receptor machinery in each organ acts on a different set of pathways.

During the early postabsorptive period, hepatic glycogen supplies glucose without requiring construction of a new carbon skeleton. Mobilisation can respond rapidly to changing hormonal signals. At the same time, lactate, glycerol and amino acid carbon contribute to gluconeogenesis. The availability of lactate depends on ongoing glycolysis, which continues in erythrocytes even at rest. Glycerol delivery increases as adipose triacylglycerol is mobilised. The supply of amino acid precursors reflects whole-body protein metabolism and the metabolic needs of peripheral tissues.

In a person at rest, skeletal muscle can decrease glucose use and increase reliance on fatty acids as insulin falls and substrate supply changes. This helps preserve glucose for the brain and erythrocytes. In contrast, contracting muscle may require substantial carbohydrate oxidation even when the person is fasting. The same hormonal environment therefore produces different consequences in resting and active muscle. Local energy demand, calcium signalling and fuel accessibility interact with endocrine regulation.

As hepatic glycogen falls, the contribution of glycogenolysis to circulating glucose becomes more limited. Gluconeogenesis then supplies a larger fraction of endogenous glucose. This shift requires access to precursors and sufficient hepatic energy generation. Increasing adipose fatty acid release supports hepatic oxidative metabolism, while the glycerol portion of released triacylglycerol provides carbon for glucose synthesis. An increase in fatty acid oxidation therefore has two indirect consequences for the carbohydrate economy: it powers glucose production and reduces glucose consumption by other oxidative tissues.

The liver also increases ketone body production. Ketone export provides the brain and other oxidative tissues with an alternative fuel. During a sustained fast, adaptive changes in blood ketone availability, transport and enzyme activity allow cerebral ketone use to become substantial. The resulting reduction in glucose requirement helps limit net protein loss. In this setting, whole-body glucose turnover can decline even while the fraction of glucose supplied by gluconeogenesis increases. A larger fraction does not necessarily mean a higher absolute rate, because the denominator has changed.

The kidney's role becomes increasingly important in sustained fasting. Renal cortical gluconeogenesis uses precursors that include lactate, glycerol and glutamine. Unlike the liver, the kidney does not rely on a large glycogen reserve to provide systemic glucose. It can also couple glutamine metabolism to ammonium excretion, providing a link between substrate use and acid-base homeostasis. The extent of renal glucose contribution is context-dependent, and it cannot be inferred from liver glycogen content alone. Measurements of total endogenous glucose production include renal as well as hepatic output.

Nitrogen balance provides a separate way of interpreting the fasting response. If body protein is being lost, nitrogen excretion exceeds nitrogen intake, which is absent in complete fasting. A decline in urea nitrogen excretion during adaptation may reflect a reduction in amino acid catabolism, but urinary nitrogen measurements require attention to time, renal handling and alternative excretory products. Changes in urine volume can alter concentration without changing total nitrogen loss. The appropriate quantity for estimating loss is the amount excreted over a defined interval, not concentration alone.

The early and late phases of fasting can be contrasted using a deliberately simplified carbon balance. Imagine that endogenous glucose production is 10 arbitrary units per minute early in a fast, with 6 units from glycogen and 4 units from gluconeogenesis. Later, total glucose production is 7 units per minute, with 1 unit from glycogen and 6 units from gluconeogenesis. The gluconeogenic fraction has increased from 40% to about 86%, while absolute gluconeogenic flux has increased from 4 to 6 units. If the later total instead falls to 4 units, all from gluconeogenesis, the fraction reaches 100% but the absolute gluconeogenic flux is unchanged from the early value. These are teaching values rather than measurements; they demonstrate why both numerator and denominator are needed to interpret a reported percentage.

A circulating fuel concentration can also change independently of its production rate. Consider ketone concentration after a long fast. An increase could occur because the liver produces ketones faster, because peripheral disposal has declined, or because production and disposal have changed unequally. If the ketone pool is constant, its appearance and disappearance rates must approximately match. Neither rate can be determined from the constant concentration without further measurements. This general reasoning applies to glucose, lactate, glycerol and fatty acids.

Hormonal counterregulation is particularly important if circulating glucose begins to fall below its normal operating range. Reduced insulin and increased glucagon action help maintain hepatic glucose output. Adrenergic mechanisms can support fuel mobilization. Cortisol and growth hormone can influence longer-term substrate availability and insulin action. The details of endocrine stress physiology belong to related chapters; here the key point is that different hormones act over different time scales and do not replace each other completely. Rapid hepatic responses can be followed by slower adjustments in gene expression and substrate mobilization.

Starvation eventually imposes limits that no metabolic pathway can overcome indefinitely. Ketone use and fatty acid oxidation conserve carbohydrate and protein, but body fat stores are finite, essential nutrients may become limiting, and proteins cannot be sacrificed without consequences for organ function. Metabolic adaptation is not synonymous with absence of physiological stress. Textbook fasting experiments describe mechanisms of survival, not a recommendation to undertake extended fasting.

Refeeding changes the direction of interorgan traffic again. Nutrient absorption resumes, insulin secretion rises, hepatic glucose output is suppressed and glycogen synthesis increases. Adipose fatty acid release and hepatic ketogenesis generally decline as the body shifts toward utilization and storage of incoming fuels. In a severely depleted or malnourished organism, refeeding may also impose substantial electrolyte and fluid challenges. That special clinical context should not be conflated with ordinary recovery after a missed meal. The physiological principles here concern normal changes in fuel availability and the limits of their regulation.

11. Exercise: matching ATP supply to contraction

Skeletal muscle contraction begins when electrical excitation triggers release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium permits interaction between actin and myosin, and myosin ATPase activity converts chemical energy into force and movement. Additional ATP is required to pump calcium back into intracellular stores and restore ion gradients. The resulting increase in ATP demand can be very rapid. Muscle cannot wait for a new meal or for a slow endocrine adjustment, so it uses local energy-buffering mechanisms before and during the recruitment of systemic fuel supply.

The phosphocreatine system provides a readily available buffer. Creatine kinase catalyses the reversible transfer of a phosphate group between phosphocreatine and ADP to form ATP and creatine. When ATP hydrolysis increases, phosphocreatine can rapidly regenerate ATP and temporarily limit changes in ATP concentration. As phosphocreatine availability declines, glycolytic and oxidative pathways provide increasing support. The creatine kinase reaction does not create energy from nothing: restoration of phosphocreatine during recovery consumes ATP derived from metabolism.

Adenylate kinase provides another connection between nucleotide concentrations. It catalyses the reversible conversion of two ADP molecules to ATP and AMP. This can contribute to ATP buffering and generate AMP, a sensitive indicator of metabolic demand. AMP and ADP participate in regulation of energy-sensing pathways, including AMP-activated protein kinase. A small change in ATP concentration can therefore be accompanied by a more informative change in AMP or the AMP-to-ATP ratio. In muscle, this arrangement helps translate increased work into adjustments in glucose transport and substrate oxidation.

The three familiar sources of ATP during exercise are phosphagen reactions, glycolytic substrate-level phosphorylation and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. They do not take turns as completely separate switches. All can contribute from the onset of activity, but their relative contributions vary with intensity, duration, training and substrate availability. The phosphagen system can provide ATP rapidly but has limited capacity. Glycolysis can provide rapid ATP from carbohydrate, while mitochondrial oxidation provides high ATP yield from both carbohydrate and lipid over sustained work when oxygen delivery and mitochondrial capacity permit.

Muscle glycogen is especially useful during high-intensity work because it is located within the tissue and does not require glucose delivery from blood before it can be used. Glycogen phosphorylase and other enzymes respond to contraction-associated calcium, AMP and adrenergic signals. Glycogen-derived glucose-6-phosphate enters glycolysis, supplying pyruvate and ATP. Glycolytic flux can increase more rapidly than mitochondrial handling of pyruvate and reducing equivalents. Under these conditions lactate production and release increase, even though mitochondrial oxidation may also be operating at an elevated rate.

Blood glucose contributes to muscle metabolism through increased transport across the muscle cell membrane. Contraction stimulates GLUT4 translocation through signalling pathways that are not identical to insulin signalling. Calcium-dependent signals, energy stress and additional regulatory mechanisms participate. It would therefore be incorrect to assume that low insulin during exercise necessarily prevents muscle glucose uptake. The combination of increased perfusion, transporter availability and intracellular phosphorylation can promote glucose uptake despite a hormonal environment that supports hepatic glucose release.

At the whole-body level, increased glucose uptake creates a need for increased glucose delivery. The liver can respond by mobilising glycogen and increasing gluconeogenesis. Changes in insulin, glucagon and catecholamines participate in this response. When exercise is prolonged, the available hepatic glycogen reserve may decline, and gluconeogenic precursors become more important. Lactate released by active tissues, glycerol from adipose tissue and alanine from muscle can contribute to hepatic glucose synthesis. This illustrates the simultaneous use of the Cori and glucose–alanine cycles within a larger physiological network.

A rapidly contracting muscle can export lactate while another tissue uses it as a fuel. Muscle fibres with high oxidative capacity can take up lactate and convert it to pyruvate. The heart can also oxidise lactate supplied through the circulation. Consequently lactate production does not indicate that all working muscles lack oxygen. Nor does a high lactate concentration demonstrate that every lactate molecule is destined to return to glucose. The distribution of lactate between oxidation and gluconeogenesis is a question about pathway fluxes, not a consequence that can be read directly from the chemical identity of the metabolite.

Fatty acid oxidation often contributes strongly during sustained low-to-moderate intensity exercise. Adipose lipolysis increases the delivery of albumin-associated fatty acids, while intramuscular lipid droplets can provide another source. Fatty acids are activated, transferred to the mitochondrial matrix as appropriate and oxidised through beta-oxidation. The resulting acetyl-CoA and reduced electron carriers contribute to oxidative ATP production. The relative use of intramuscular and circulating lipid depends on muscle properties and the exercise conditions.

As exercise intensity rises, carbohydrate use generally increases because the demand for rapid ATP turnover becomes greater. Carbohydrate oxidation can also produce more energy per unit oxygen than oxidation of many fatty acids. This helps explain why a high oxidative capacity does not imply exclusive use of fat at every workload. Recruitment of more glycolytic fibres, regulation of pyruvate dehydrogenase, substrate transport and changes in fatty acid delivery all influence the mixture of fuels. The relation between intensity and fat oxidation is not a simple rule in which every increase in exercise intensity increases absolute fat oxidation.

At low intensity, the fraction of total energy supplied by fat may be high even though total energy expenditure is modest. At a higher intensity, the fraction of energy derived from fat may fall while absolute fat oxidation remains similar or increases for a period because total energy expenditure has increased. At still higher workloads, absolute fat oxidation may decline. The distinction between fractional and absolute fuel use is essential for interpreting exercise metabolism graphs. It is also important to distinguish the relative contribution of fat to energy expenditure from changes in the total amount of body fat, which depend on energy balance across a much longer interval.

Fuel use during prolonged activity changes as glycogen reserves and substrate availability change. Muscle glycogen may become depleted within recruited fibres, while fatty acid delivery and oxidation support a larger portion of sustained work. However, fatty acid oxidation alone may not support the same high rate of ATP production required for an intense workload. Reduced carbohydrate availability can therefore impair the ability to sustain a particular power output even when substantial body fat remains. This is an example of the distinction between total stored energy and the maximal rate at which energy can be made available to a tissue.

The availability of dietary carbohydrate during extended activity can modify the contribution of endogenous glucose sources. Absorbed glucose can enter the circulation and support muscle uptake, reducing the requirement for hepatic glucose release under some conditions. The resulting fuel mixture depends on absorption, hormonal responses and exercise intensity. Exercise performed after feeding and exercise performed after an overnight fast therefore should not be interpreted as metabolically identical, even if the external workload is matched.

The cardiovascular and respiratory systems help coordinate these processes by increasing the delivery of oxygen and substrates and removing carbon dioxide and heat. Changes in blood flow can alter substrate uptake independently of changes in arterial concentration. A muscle that extracts the same fraction of arterial glucose while receiving more blood per minute can take up more glucose per minute. Conversely, substrate extraction may rise if transporter activity or metabolism increases. Both delivery and extraction matter for organ exchange.

Consider two muscles with identical arterial glucose concentration. If one receives twice the blood flow and has the same arterial-minus-venous glucose difference as the other, its net glucose uptake is twice as large. If the more active muscle increases blood flow while its extraction difference falls, the net effect depends on the product of these quantities. Therefore neither a venous glucose measurement nor a flow measurement in isolation fully describes substrate uptake. This is why integrated physiology combines circulatory variables with biochemical concentrations.

During recovery, ATP demand for contraction falls, but metabolic adjustments persist. Phosphocreatine is replenished using ATP produced by oxidative metabolism. Muscle glycogen may be resynthesised, especially when carbohydrate becomes available. Insulin sensitivity can remain enhanced after exercise, supporting restoration of carbohydrate stores. Lactate can be oxidised or converted to glucose, and some of the resulting carbon may contribute to glycogen restoration. The time course depends on exercise type, depletion, diet and recovery conditions. Acute exercise responses should be distinguished from adaptations to repeated training, which involve changes in mitochondrial capacity, transporters and regulatory systems and are considered in the next metabolic flexibility chapter.

12. Measuring interorgan fuel flux and substrate oxidation

Physiological metabolism is difficult to infer from static measurements because organs exchange metabolites continuously. An investigator may measure normal plasma glucose at rest and during exercise, yet the underlying rates of glucose delivery and uptake may be very different. An appropriate experiment must identify which quantity is being measured and what assumptions permit it to be converted into a physiological conclusion. Three particularly valuable approaches are organ blood-flow measurements, isotope tracers and indirect calorimetry. Each answers a different question.

The arterial–venous balance method estimates the net exchange of a substance by a defined region. Blood entering an organ contains a concentration of substrate, and blood leaving the organ contains another concentration. If the inflow and outflow volumes are equal over the measured interval and the substance is measured in a compatible fluid compartment, net uptake equals flow multiplied by the arterial-minus-venous concentration difference. When this quantity is negative, net release is occurring. For an organ supplied by several vessels, the input must account for each relevant blood supply. The liver, for example, receives blood from both the hepatic artery and portal vein; a simple systemic arterial–hepatic venous comparison cannot automatically substitute for a full hepatic balance.

For a hypothetical exercising leg, let blood flow be 4 litres per minute, arterial glucose concentration 5.0 millimoles per litre and venous glucose concentration 4.5 millimoles per litre. The difference is 0.5 millimoles per litre, and net uptake is 2.0 millimoles per minute. Multiplying by the molar mass of glucose, approximately 180 milligrams per millimole, gives 360 milligrams per minute. Suppose another condition has blood flow of 2 litres per minute and an extraction difference of 1 millimole per litre. The calculated uptake is identical. A larger concentration difference does not establish a larger uptake rate unless flow is considered.

The method measures net exchange, not all intracellular metabolic processes. Muscle may simultaneously produce and consume lactate; the measured difference reflects the imbalance between the two. An organ may show zero net exchange while a substantial amount of the substance is entering and leaving. Similarly, blood glucose taken up by a muscle may be oxidised, incorporated into glycogen or transformed into products that leave the tissue. Arterial–venous measurements alone cannot establish the proportion going to each destination. More detailed experiments are needed to distinguish these fates.

Stable isotope tracers allow molecules to be followed through a metabolic network. A glucose molecule containing carbon-13 or deuterium can be distinguished from the much larger pool of naturally abundant glucose using mass spectrometry. If a tracer is infused at a known rate and mixes with endogenous glucose, the dilution of its enrichment can provide information about the rate of glucose appearance. At isotopic and metabolic steady state, a simple one-pool approximation relates the tracer infusion rate to tracer enrichment. The approximation depends on the precise definition of enrichment and on accounting for tracer input separately from endogenous tracee appearance.

For illustration, suppose a tracer is infused at 0.05 millimoles per minute, and the steady tracer-to-tracee ratio is 0.025, after correcting for background abundance. In an ideal well-mixed system with no recycling of the label and negligible other tracer sources, the tracee appearance rate equals 0.05 divided by 0.025, or 2.0 millimoles per minute. The total molecular appearance rate including infused tracer is 2.05 millimoles per minute under this convention. An experiment that reports a tracer fraction of total rather than a tracer-to-tracee ratio requires a different expression. Numerical interpretation therefore begins by defining the denominator.

The ideal steady-state assumption becomes less reliable immediately after a meal or at the onset of exercise because pool sizes and enrichments may change rapidly. Tracer carbon can also recycle: labelled glucose can generate labelled lactate, which returns to glucose through gluconeogenesis. The reappearance of labelled carbon can complicate estimation of glucose production if the chosen label is retained through the cycle. Comparing tracers with distinct labelling positions or using appropriate non-steady-state models can help address these limitations. A student need not master specialist tracer kinetics to understand the fundamental inference: labelled atoms provide evidence of a route, but enrichment is not automatically equal to pathway flux.

Indirect calorimetry estimates aspects of whole-body fuel oxidation by measuring oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production. Complete oxidation of glucose is represented by the reaction of one glucose molecule with six oxygen molecules to produce six carbon dioxide molecules and six water molecules. The ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed is therefore one for pure glucose oxidation. Complete oxidation of palmitic acid requires twenty-three oxygen molecules and generates sixteen carbon dioxide molecules and sixteen water molecules. Its theoretical respiratory quotient is sixteen divided by twenty-three, approximately 0.70. The difference arises because fatty acids are more reduced than carbohydrates and require more oxygen per carbon atom oxidised.

The respiratory exchange ratio measured at the lungs is the volume of carbon dioxide expired divided by the volume of oxygen consumed, using matching units and measurement conditions. Under suitable steady-state conditions it approximates the respiratory quotient of tissue metabolism. Values near 0.70 suggest predominant lipid oxidation, and values near 1.0 suggest predominant carbohydrate oxidation when protein oxidation and other complications are minor. At high exercise intensity, bicarbonate buffering and changes in ventilation can increase measured carbon dioxide output independently of complete substrate oxidation. Respiratory exchange ratios above one do not imply that a cellular fuel has a normal oxidative respiratory quotient greater than one.

For a hypothetical steady-state exercise measurement, oxygen consumption is 2.0 litres per minute and carbon dioxide output is 1.6 litres per minute. The respiratory exchange ratio is 0.80. This supports mixed substrate oxidation under the usual assumptions. It does not identify which organs use carbohydrate, whether the oxidised fatty acids came from adipose tissue or intramuscular lipid, or how much glucose was recycled before oxidation. Answering those questions requires further evidence. This distinction prevents overinterpretation of a convenient whole-body measurement.

A complementary intervention is the euglycemic hyperinsulinemic clamp, introduced by DeFronzo, Tobin and Andres as a means of studying insulin action. Insulin is raised under controlled conditions while glucose infusion is adjusted to maintain a target glucose concentration. If endogenous glucose production is effectively suppressed and glucose concentration is steady, a larger requirement for infused glucose indicates greater glucose disposal under the specified insulin stimulus. If endogenous glucose production persists, the glucose infusion rate alone cannot quantify total disposal accurately without accounting for that production. Isotope tracers can be combined with the clamp to address this issue. The experimental principle is relevant here because it separates a controlled hormonal stimulus from the normal variation of glucose concentration after meals.

An experimental design comparing a meal with exercise should standardise the factors that affect interpretation. Previous activity can change muscle glycogen and insulin responsiveness; recent diet affects liver glycogen and circulating substrate; exercise intensity changes the recruitment of muscle fibres. Appropriate comparison conditions could include a resting trial at the same time of day and a matched exercise trial with controlled nutritional status. Measurements of glucose, lactate, fatty acids, ketones, respiratory exchange and selected hormones provide complementary evidence. It is important not to infer that a hormone caused a metabolic change merely because the hormone and the flux varied together. Controlled perturbations or convergent evidence are needed to support causation.

13. Mechanistic predictions and integrated problem solving

The pathways studied in this chapter can be assembled into a causal model. Begin by specifying the physiological demand: continuous glucose provision, rapid muscular work or storage of absorbed nutrients. Next identify the organs that can provide the required substrates and the tissues that must consume them. Then determine which hormonal and local regulatory signals alter the direction or magnitude of the relevant pathways. Finally test the prediction against carbon balance, energy balance and the known enzyme repertoire of each tissue. This sequence is more reliable than memorising separate lists of pathways associated with fasting and feeding.

For example, imagine selective inhibition of hepatic fatty acid oxidation during fasting. Adipose lipolysis could still release fatty acids and glycerol, so circulating substrate availability would not necessarily disappear. Nevertheless, the liver would have reduced access to ATP and reducing equivalents derived from fat oxidation, and acetyl-CoA-dependent stimulation of pyruvate carboxylase could be diminished. Glucose production from lactate and alanine would consequently be vulnerable. Ketogenesis would also tend to fall because the supply of hepatic acetyl units from fatty acids is restricted. The combined pattern of inadequate glucose production and relatively low ketone availability is mechanistically different from a situation of uncontrolled ketone production due to severe insulin deficiency.

Consider a second intervention that selectively reduces adipose triglyceride lipase activity during a fast. The first direct effect would be reduced mobilization of stored triacylglycerol, assuming no compensatory pathways. Fatty acid supply to oxidative tissues would decline, and glycerol delivery to gluconeogenic organs could also fall. The liver would receive less substrate for ketogenesis and potentially less energy from fatty acid oxidation. Increased dependence on glucose might result in some peripheral tissues if alternative fuel delivery declined. The size of the response would depend on the availability of other fuels and on how strongly compensatory mechanisms respond.

In a third experiment, a contracting muscle increases its glucose uptake despite a reduction in insulin. This observation need not contradict the established role of insulin in glucose transport. Contraction provides a parallel route to increased GLUT4 surface availability. If insulin signalling is selectively impaired but contraction signalling remains functional, muscle can retain some contraction-induced glucose uptake. If the transporter itself is absent, both routes may be compromised because they share an important downstream component. Comparing interventions at different levels of a pathway helps identify where regulatory signals converge.

An examination may ask how two individuals can have the same blood glucose but different glucose turnover. One person may be resting after an overnight fast, with moderate endogenous glucose release matched by tissue uptake. Another may be exercising, with greater hepatic release and greater uptake by active muscle. If glucose appearance equals disappearance in each person, the measured concentration can be similar. The correct interpretation is that homeostatic regulation can maintain a pool size while underlying metabolic flux changes. This principle applies equally to lactate and ketones and explains the need for tracers or organ balance measurements.

The carbon–nitrogen distinction is useful when comparing recovery from exercise with prolonged fasting. Lactate derived from glycolysis supplies carbon but does not directly transport amino nitrogen. Alanine supplies a three-carbon skeleton and an amino group. Glycerol supplies carbon from triacylglycerol without carrying nitrogen. Fatty acids primarily supply oxidative energy and ketogenic acetyl units, with the odd-chain exception discussed earlier. A question that asks which precursor increases urea production under comparable conditions is therefore asking something different from a question about which precursor contributes to glucose carbon. The answer requires identifying what is carried by the molecule, not merely recalling its place in a pathway.

The integrated response is also influenced by species and tissue differences. Mammalian erythrocytes lack mitochondria, whereas mature erythrocytes of many nonmammalian vertebrates retain organelles and differ in metabolic capabilities. Ruminants obtain much of their absorbed energy from microbial fermentation products and depend substantially on gluconeogenesis even during feeding. The human fed–fasted model is therefore a valuable representative mammalian example rather than a universal description of all animals. For a comparative physiology problem, identify the species and relevant cellular features before transferring conclusions from one organism to another.

The central lesson is that the metabolic state is a coordinated distribution of work. After feeding, intestinal nutrient delivery and endocrine signals favour uptake, oxidation and storage. During fasting, glucose-releasing organs mobilise glycogen and construct glucose from appropriate substrates, adipose tissue releases fuel, and oxidative tissues change their substrate preferences. During exercise, local contraction-related mechanisms raise muscle ATP supply and systemic regulation supports fuel delivery. These conditions can overlap, and the pathway responses are graded rather than mutually exclusive. The next chapter extends these concepts to the capacity of the organism to switch fuels appropriately and to the consequences of impaired metabolic flexibility.

Part B: conceptual foundations

1. Which enzyme enables hepatic glycogen to support circulating free glucose?

  1. Glycogen synthase
  2. Glucose-6-phosphatase
  3. Hexokinase
  4. Phosphofructokinase 1
Answer and explanation

B. Glucose-6-phosphatase. Glucose-6-phosphatase hydrolyses glucose-6-phosphate. Glycogen synthase stores residues, hexokinase consumes free glucose, and PFK-1 directs phosphorylated carbon toward glycolysis.

2. The immediate principal product of glycogen phosphorylase is:

  1. Glucose
  2. Glucose-6-phosphate
  3. Glucose-1-phosphate
  4. UDP-glucose
Answer and explanation

C. Glucose-1-phosphate. Phosphorolysis of alpha-1,4 bonds yields glucose-1-phosphate. Free glucose is released at branch hydrolysis, glucose-6-phosphate is generated by phosphoglucomutase, and UDP-glucose is a synthetic precursor.

3. Which glucose transporter is recruited to skeletal muscle membranes by insulin and contraction?

  1. GLUT4
  2. SGLT1
  3. GLUT5
  4. GLUT1
Answer and explanation

A. GLUT4. GLUT4 is the regulated vesicular transporter in skeletal muscle. SGLT1 transports glucose with sodium, GLUT5 primarily transports fructose, and GLUT1 provides much basal uptake rather than the characteristic GLUT4 response.

4. Which fasting signal directly promotes hepatic cAMP signalling?

  1. Glucagon
  2. Insulin
  3. Leptin acting on hypothalamus
  4. Aldosterone
Answer and explanation

A. Glucagon. Hepatic glucagon receptors activate Gs and adenylyl cyclase. Insulin generally opposes hepatic glucose output; leptin and aldosterone have different principal regulatory roles.

5. Which pair is exclusively ketogenic in standard amino acid classification?

  1. Alanine and glycine
  2. Leucine and lysine
  3. Valine and aspartate
  4. Glutamine and serine
Answer and explanation

B. Leucine and lysine. Leucine and lysine provide ketogenic products rather than net glucogenic carbon. The other listed amino acids can furnish pyruvate or citric acid cycle intermediates.

6. Which product of adipose lipolysis supplies glucose carbon most directly?

  1. Palmitate
  2. Acetyl-CoA
  3. Glycerol
  4. Acetoacetate
Answer and explanation

C. Glycerol. Glycerol becomes glycerol-3-phosphate and DHAP. Even-chain palmitate yields acetyl-CoA, which cannot supply net glucose in mammals; acetoacetate is a ketogenic fuel.

7. Which enzyme initiates the major sequence of stored triacylglycerol hydrolysis?

  1. Lipoprotein lipase
  2. ATGL
  3. Glucokinase
  4. CPT2
Answer and explanation

B. ATGL. ATGL hydrolyses intracellular TAG to DAG. Lipoprotein lipase acts on circulating lipoproteins, glucokinase phosphorylates glucose, and CPT2 regenerates mitochondrial acyl-CoA.

8. What is the immediate effect of malonyl-CoA on the carnitine system?

  1. Stimulates CPT1
  2. Inhibits CPT1
  3. Hydrolyses acylcarnitine
  4. Activates glucose-6-phosphatase
Answer and explanation

B. Inhibits CPT1. Malonyl-CoA inhibits CPT1 and limits long-chain acyl entry for oxidation. It is not an acylcarnitine hydrolase or a direct activator of glucose-6-phosphatase.

9. Which ketone-utilizing enzyme is effectively absent in normal liver?

  1. HMG-CoA lyase
  2. SCOT
  3. Pyruvate carboxylase
  4. Glycerol kinase
Answer and explanation

B. SCOT. SCOT activates acetoacetate for ketolysis. Liver possesses enzymes required for ketogenesis, gluconeogenesis and glycerol use but cannot use ketones through the principal SCOT-dependent route.

10. Which mature mammalian cell necessarily depends on glycolysis for ATP?

  1. Cardiomyocyte
  2. Hepatocyte
  3. Erythrocyte
  4. Oxidative skeletal muscle fibre
Answer and explanation

C. Erythrocyte. Mature mammalian erythrocytes lack mitochondria. The other cells can perform mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and use a mixture of fuels.

11. What is the direct redox function of converting pyruvate to lactate?

  1. Generates NADPH
  2. Regenerates NAD+
  3. Produces ATP directly
  4. Oxidises FADH2
Answer and explanation

B. Regenerates NAD+. LDH reduces pyruvate using NADH, regenerating NAD+ for glycolysis. LDH does not directly phosphorylate ADP or use NADPH or FADH2 in this reaction.

12. Which enzyme transfers amino nitrogen from glutamate to pyruvate?

  1. Alanine aminotransferase
  2. Lactate dehydrogenase
  3. Pyruvate kinase
  4. Glycogen phosphorylase
Answer and explanation

A. Alanine aminotransferase. ALT produces alanine and alpha-ketoglutarate. LDH transfers reducing equivalents, pyruvate kinase generates pyruvate, and glycogen phosphorylase releases sugar phosphate.

13. Which nutrient transport route initially carries most long-chain dietary lipid from enterocytes?

  1. Portal blood as free palmitate only
  2. Lymph as chylomicrons
  3. Renal vein as ketones
  4. Bile ducts as glycogen
Answer and explanation

B. Lymph as chylomicrons. Enterocytes package most long-chain dietary lipid into chylomicrons that enter lymph. Glucose and amino acids instead mainly enter portal blood.

14. Pyruvate carboxylase requires which coenzyme?

  1. Biotin
  2. Folate only
  3. FAD only
  4. Pyridoxal phosphate
Answer and explanation

A. Biotin. Biotin carries activated carbon dioxide during pyruvate carboxylation. PLP supports transamination, whereas FAD and folate serve different reaction classes.

15. Which condition generally increases hepatic fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase activity through phosphorylation of the liver bifunctional enzyme?

  1. High hepatic insulin signal
  2. Glucagon–PKA signalling
  3. Direct GLUT4 insertion
  4. SCOT inhibition
Answer and explanation

B. Glucagon–PKA signalling. In the liver isoform, PKA phosphorylation favours FBPase-2 activity, lowering fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. This favours gluconeogenesis over glycolysis.

16. Which is the usual carrier of circulating nonesterified long-chain fatty acids?

  1. Haemoglobin
  2. Albumin
  3. Glycogenin
  4. Insulin
Answer and explanation

B. Albumin. Albumin binds hydrophobic nonesterified fatty acids. Haemoglobin carries respiratory gases; glycogenin initiates glycogen formation; insulin is a signal.

17. What is the principal immediate phosphate donor in the creatine kinase ATP-buffering reaction?

  1. Glycogen
  2. Phosphocreatine
  3. Glucose-6-phosphate
  4. Malonyl-CoA
Answer and explanation

B. Phosphocreatine. Phosphocreatine transfers its phosphate to ADP. The other compounds are metabolic intermediates, not the creatine kinase phosphate donor.

18. The hepatic mitochondrial enzyme central to ketogenesis is:

  1. HMGCS2
  2. PFK-1
  3. GLUT4
  4. Lipoprotein lipase
Answer and explanation

A. HMGCS2. HMGCS2 forms mitochondrial HMG-CoA for ketogenesis. PFK-1 is glycolytic, GLUT4 is a transporter and LPL hydrolyses lipoprotein TAG.

19. Which organ besides the liver is a physiologically important glucose producer during prolonged fasting?

  1. Renal cortex
  2. Mature erythrocytes
  3. Adipocyte lipid droplet
  4. Skeletal muscle fibre
Answer and explanation

A. Renal cortex. Renal cortical cells have glucose-releasing gluconeogenic machinery. Erythrocytes lack mitochondria; adipocytes and muscle do not normally provide substantial free glucose output.

20. What is the theoretical respiratory quotient for complete glucose oxidation?

  1. 0.70
  2. 0.85
  3. 1.0
  4. 1.5
Answer and explanation

C. 1.0. Six CO2 molecules are produced per six O2 molecules consumed. Approximately 0.70 is typical of complete fat oxidation, and values greater than one at the lungs may reflect non-steady-state effects.

21. How many high-energy phosphate equivalents are required to make one glucose from two pyruvate?

  1. 2
  2. 4
  3. 6
  4. 8
Answer and explanation

C. 6. Pyruvate carboxylase consumes two ATP, PEPCK two GTP, and reverse phosphoglycerate kinase two ATP. Two NADH are additionally required but are not counted as phosphate equivalents.

22. Which compound is not chemically a ketone despite being classified as a ketone body?

  1. Acetone
  2. Acetoacetate
  3. D-beta-hydroxybutyrate
  4. Acetyl-CoA
Answer and explanation

C. D-beta-hydroxybutyrate. Beta-hydroxybutyrate contains a hydroxyl group rather than a ketone carbonyl. Acetone and acetoacetate contain ketone groups; acetyl-CoA is not one of the three usual ketone bodies.

Part C: analytical and experimental reasoning

23. A selective hepatic glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency is imposed during fasting. Which observation is most directly predicted?

  1. Normal hepatic free glucose release
  2. Reduced hepatic glucose export despite G6P formation
  3. Inability of muscle to hydrolyse glycogen
  4. Increased erythrocyte ketolysis
Answer and explanation

B. Reduced hepatic glucose export despite G6P formation. Both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis converge at G6P, but hepatic export needs hydrolysis to glucose. Muscle phosphorylase and erythrocyte mitochondrial absence are independent of this hepatic intervention.

24. During a complete Cori cycle, muscle produces two ATP from free glucose. The liver restores glucose using four ATP and two GTP. Whole-body net phosphate balance is:

  1. +4
  2. +2
  3. −4
  4. −6
Answer and explanation

C. −4. The liver expends six equivalents and muscle gains two; 2−6=−4. Counting only liver cost gives −6 and ignoring liver cost gives +2.

25. A leg receives 3 L/min blood with arterial glucose 5.2 and venous glucose 4.8 mmol/L. Calculate net uptake.

  1. 0.4 mmol/min
  2. 1.2 mmol/min
  3. 12 mmol/min
  4. −1.2 mmol/min
Answer and explanation

B. 1.2 mmol/min. Uptake = flow × (arterial − venous) = 3 × 0.4 = 1.2 mmol/min. The positive sign indicates uptake. A concentration difference alone lacks flow units.

26. A resting organ has arterial lactate 1.0 and venous lactate 1.0 mmol/L. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Lactate oxidation is impossible
  2. Lactate production is absent
  3. Net lactate exchange is zero under matched flow
  4. All lactate goes to glucose
Answer and explanation

C. Net lactate exchange is zero under matched flow. Equal inflow and outflow concentrations imply zero net exchange, not zero gross uptake or production. Additional tracers are needed to resolve opposing fluxes.

27. Glucose appearance is 2 mmol/min at rest and 5 mmol/min during exercise; glucose concentration is constant in both trials. What follows?

  1. Disappearance is also approximately 2 and 5 mmol/min respectively
  2. Disappearance is zero in both
  3. Liver glycogen is unchanged
  4. All extra glucose derives from glycogen
Answer and explanation

A. Disappearance is also approximately 2 and 5 mmol/min respectively. At constant pool size and distribution volume, input equals output. Source partition and glycogen changes cannot be deduced from total appearance alone.

28. In early fasting, glucose output is 10 units/min, with 4 from gluconeogenesis. Later total output is 5, with 4 from gluconeogenesis. Which statement is correct?

  1. Absolute gluconeogenesis doubles
  2. Fraction falls to 20%
  3. Absolute gluconeogenesis is unchanged; fraction rises from 40% to 80%
  4. Glycogen output is unchanged
Answer and explanation

C. Absolute gluconeogenesis is unchanged; fraction rises from 40% to 80%. The numerator stays at four while total decreases. The glycogen contribution falls from six to one. A percentage increase is not necessarily an absolute flux increase.

29. A subject oxidises palmitate completely. Assuming 2.5 ATP/NADH, 1.5 ATP/FAD equivalent and 10 ATP/acetyl-CoA, what is the net ATP estimate?

  1. 108
  2. 106
  3. 80
  4. 28
Answer and explanation

B. 106. Seven cycles produce 7 × (2.5+1.5)=28 ATP equivalents; eight acetyl-CoA yield 80. Subtract two equivalents for activation: 106. 108 omits activation.

30. An inhibitor blocks CPT1 in liver while adipose lipolysis remains intact. Which paired effect is most plausible during fasting?

  1. Lower long-chain fat oxidation and lower ketogenesis
  2. Higher ketogenesis and higher CPT1 flux
  3. No effect because FA cross inner membrane freely
  4. Erythrocyte ketone oxidation rises
Answer and explanation

A. Lower long-chain fat oxidation and lower ketogenesis. CPT1 blockade limits long-chain acyl transfer and acetyl-CoA supply for ketogenesis. Medium-chain substrates may bypass dependence on CPT1, but red cells cannot oxidise ketones.

31. A muscle has impaired insulin receptor signalling but intact contraction pathways and GLUT4. What is the best prediction?

  1. No glucose uptake under any condition
  2. Some contraction-induced uptake may remain
  3. Glucagon directly replaces insulin receptor
  4. Muscle exports glucose from glycogen
Answer and explanation

B. Some contraction-induced uptake may remain. Contraction can stimulate GLUT4 independently of upstream insulin receptor signalling. This does not imply a glucagon replacement or confer glucose-6-phosphatase activity.

32. After a protein-rich meal, insulin and glucagon both rise. Why can this be physiologically appropriate?

  1. Glucagon supports hepatic glucose provision while insulin supports amino acid utilization
  2. Insulin necessarily stops all protein synthesis
  3. Glucagon converts all amino acids to fat
  4. The two hormones always cancel exactly
Answer and explanation

A. Glucagon supports hepatic glucose provision while insulin supports amino acid utilization. Amino acids can stimulate both pancreatic hormones. Relative hepatic glucose production and peripheral uptake depend on nutrient supply and signalling, not a simple mutually exclusive switch.

33. During prolonged fasting, the brain oxidises more ketones. What downstream change is expected, other conditions equal?

  1. Greater obligatory glucose synthesis from amino acids
  2. Reduced glucose demand and potential protein sparing
  3. Complete cessation of erythrocyte glucose use
  4. Conversion of palmitate to net glucose
Answer and explanation

B. Reduced glucose demand and potential protein sparing. Cerebral ketone use reduces the need for glucose and can reduce amino acid carbon demand. Red cells remain glycolytic, and even-chain fatty acids do not yield net glucose.

34. Two lactate molecules enter hepatic gluconeogenesis. Compared with two pyruvate, what advantage do they provide?

  1. They eliminate ATP expenditure
  2. Their oxidation supplies cytosolic NADH
  3. They carry two amino groups
  4. They bypass glucose-6-phosphatase
Answer and explanation

B. Their oxidation supplies cytosolic NADH. LDH oxidises lactate to pyruvate and generates the two NADH required downstream. ATP/GTP expenditure and glucose-6-phosphatase are still necessary; lactate contains no nitrogen.

35. In a tracing study, 13C-labelled glucose produces labelled lactate and later labelled glucose. What does this demonstrate most directly?

  1. A possible recycling route for glucose carbon
  2. All labelled lactate was converted to glucose
  3. Net glucose carbon arose from even-chain fatty acid
  4. There is no gluconeogenesis
Answer and explanation

A. A possible recycling route for glucose carbon. Reappearance supports carbon recycling through routes such as the Cori cycle. It cannot establish that every lactate molecule followed that route or identify whole-body net flux without quantitative modelling.

36. A tracer enters at 0.06 mmol/min; steady tracer-to-tracee ratio is 0.03. With ideal mixing and no recycling, tracee appearance is:

  1. 0.0018 mmol/min
  2. 0.5 mmol/min
  3. 2 mmol/min
  4. 2.06 mmol/min
Answer and explanation

C. 2 mmol/min. Using infusion divided by tracer-to-tracee ratio gives 0.06/0.03=2 mmol/min. Total appearance including tracer is 2.06; the question asks tracee appearance.

37. A subject has VO2 2.5 L/min and VCO2 2.0 L/min in steady exercise. Which interpretation is strongest?

  1. RER 0.8, compatible with mixed oxidation
  2. RER 1.25, pure protein
  3. RER 0.5, only fat
  4. All lipid originates from adipose tissue
Answer and explanation

A. RER 0.8, compatible with mixed oxidation. RER=2.0/2.5=0.8. Under suitable steady-state assumptions it supports mixed substrate oxidation, but cannot locate the tissue source of lipid.

38. During intense non-steady exercise, measured RER rises to 1.15. Which is most appropriate?

  1. Pure glucose has theoretical RQ 1.15
  2. Bicarbonate buffering and ventilation may elevate measured CO2 output
  3. Pure palmitate oxidation explains it
  4. Mitochondria cannot consume oxygen
Answer and explanation

B. Bicarbonate buffering and ventilation may elevate measured CO2 output. At high intensity, additional CO2 can arise from buffering and changes in gas exchange. Measured RER need not match tissue RQ; glucose RQ is one and palmitate approximately 0.70.

39. A hypothetical adipose depot hydrolyses 5 mmol TAG completely, but only 9 mmol fatty acids are exported. What is a possible explanation?

  1. Stoichiometry requires only one fatty acid per TAG
  2. Six mmol fatty acids were retained or re-esterified if no other fates occur
  3. No glycerol could be released
  4. LPL must be absent
Answer and explanation

B. Six mmol fatty acids were retained or re-esterified if no other fates occur. Complete hydrolysis generates 15 mmol fatty acid and 5 mmol glycerol. A difference of six can be retained/re-esterified under the stated simplification. Net release need not equal gross production.

40. An intervention blocks hepatic SCOT expression. Compared with normal liver, what is the most direct expected change?

  1. Major loss of normal hepatic ketone utilization
  2. Little additional effect on principal hepatic ketolysis because SCOT is already negligible
  3. Direct loss of HMGCS2
  4. Immediate erythrocyte ketone utilization
Answer and explanation

B. Little additional effect on principal hepatic ketolysis because SCOT is already negligible. Normal liver already lacks effective SCOT-mediated ketolysis. The intervention does not directly inhibit ketogenesis or create mitochondrial machinery in red cells.

41. Two alanine molecules donate their carbon to one glucose. Their two amino groups are ultimately incorporated into one urea molecule in an idealized model. Nominal phosphate cost for glucose plus urea synthesis is:

  1. 4
  2. 6
  3. 8
  4. 10
Answer and explanation

D. 10. Gluconeogenesis from pyruvate costs six phosphate equivalents; urea formation costs four, giving ten nominal equivalents. Redox and linked pathway contributions are excluded from this simplified count.

42. A researcher observes high circulating lactate but unchanged hepatic glucose production. Which alternative is plausible?

  1. Lactate can be oxidised by other tissues
  2. Lactate necessarily accumulates only without oxygen
  3. All lactate must become glucose
  4. LDH produces ATP directly
Answer and explanation

A. Lactate can be oxidised by other tissues. Lactate has multiple fates including oxidative disposal by heart and muscle. An increased lactate concentration does not uniquely predict hepatic gluconeogenic flux.

43. Muscle glycogen decreases 30 g over an experiment, while synthesis during the interval is 8 g. What is gross degradation, assuming no other inventory changes?

  1. 22 g
  2. 30 g
  3. 38 g
  4. 8 g
Answer and explanation

C. 38 g. Net change = synthesis − degradation = −30. Therefore degradation=8+30=38 g. Pool depletion alone underestimates gross breakdown when synthesis continues.

44. During a euglycemic clamp, glucose infusion is 4 mg/kg/min and endogenous production remains 1 mg/kg/min. At steady concentration, total disposal is:

  1. 3
  2. 4
  3. 5
  4. 0 mg/kg/min
Answer and explanation

C. 5. Total appearance equals exogenous infusion plus endogenous production, 4+1=5. Assuming all endogenous production is suppressed would underestimate disposal.

45. An exercising subject expends 400 energy units with 50% fat contribution. At higher intensity expenditure is 600 units with 35% fat contribution. Absolute fat contribution changes from:

  1. 200 to 210
  2. 200 to 35
  3. 50 to 35
  4. 400 to 600
Answer and explanation

A. 200 to 210. Fat energy is total × fraction: 400×0.5=200 and 600×0.35=210. Percentage fell but absolute contribution increased slightly.

46. Which prediction is most consistent with severe impairment of hepatic beta-oxidation during a fast?

  1. Increased ATP supply for gluconeogenesis
  2. Potential hypoglycemia with reduced ketogenesis
  3. Complete loss of muscle glycogen at rest
  4. Increased glucose from palmitate carbon
Answer and explanation

B. Potential hypoglycemia with reduced ketogenesis. Reduced hepatic fat oxidation can limit ATP and acetyl-CoA required to support glucose production and ketogenesis. Muscle glycogen and carbon conservation do not imply the other options.

SET, assertion–reason and numerical practice

47. SET: Which sequence correctly follows adipose triacylglycerol hydrolysis?

  1. TAG → DAG → MAG → glycerol
  2. TAG → glycogen → glucose
  3. MAG → TAG → DAG → ketone
  4. TAG → pyruvate → alanine
Answer and explanation

A. TAG → DAG → MAG → glycerol. Sequential lipases remove one fatty acid at each step, leaving glycerol. The other sequences confuse storage molecules with unrelated pathways.

48. Assertion–reason: A: During prolonged fasting, ketones can spare body protein. R: Increased cerebral ketone oxidation can lower glucose demand. Choose the relationship.

  1. Both true; R explains A
  2. Both true; R unrelated
  3. A true, R false
  4. A false, R true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; R explains A. Both are true. Lower glucose requirement may reduce dependence on gluconeogenic amino acids, though protein turnover continues.

49. Assertion–reason: A: Erythrocytes can use ketones during fasting. R: Ketolysis requires mitochondrial enzymes.

  1. Both true; R explains A
  2. Both true; R unrelated
  3. A true, R false
  4. A false, R true
Answer and explanation

D. A false, R true. Mature mammalian red cells lack mitochondria and cannot perform ketolysis. The requirement for mitochondrial machinery explains why the assertion is false.

50. SET: Two glycerol molecules can enter glucose synthesis through:

  1. DHAP
  2. Acetyl-CoA only
  3. Lactate dehydrogenase only
  4. SCOT
Answer and explanation

A. DHAP. Glycerol kinase produces glycerol-3-phosphate, which is oxidised to DHAP; two triose phosphates can form a six-carbon sugar.

51. Numerical: 2 mmol of phosphorylase-released glycogen glucosyl residues are converted entirely to lactate. Direct net glycolytic ATP production is:

  1. 2 mmol
  2. 4 mmol
  3. 6 mmol
  4. 12 mmol
Answer and explanation

C. 6 mmol. Each residue bypasses the hexokinase ATP expenditure, so 2×3=6 mmol ATP. The cost of original glycogen synthesis is excluded.

52. Experimental reasoning: To estimate net hepatic uptake of lactate, which information is required?

  1. Hepatic vein concentration only
  2. Arterial and portal concentrations and flows, plus hepatic venous concentration and appropriate outflow balance
  3. Urine glucose only
  4. Muscle glycogen concentration alone
Answer and explanation

B. Arterial and portal concentrations and flows, plus hepatic venous concentration and appropriate outflow balance. The liver has dual blood supply; full input and output mass balance needs both inflows and the outflow. A single concentration cannot establish organ exchange.

Revision and mastery

Core model

Feeding favours storage and insulin-dependent uptake. Fasting promotes hepatic and renal glucose provision, adipose mobilization, fatty acid oxidation and ketone delivery. Exercise increases muscle ATP demand and local glucose transport while liver, adipose tissue and circulation support fuel availability. Lactate transfers carbon; alanine transfers carbon and nitrogen.

Equations and high-yield relationships

  • Net uptake = blood flow × (arterial − venous concentration).
  • Gluconeogenesis from two pyruvate: 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH.
  • Complete Cori cycle: 2 − 6 = −4 high-energy phosphate equivalents.
  • Palmitate oxidation: 7 cycles, 8 acetyl-CoA; approximate net 106 ATP.
  • RER = VCO2 / VO2; approximately 0.70 for fat and 1 for glucose under appropriate conditions.
