1. Why an animal cell needs an adjustable attachment

A typical animal cell has a flexible plasma membrane rather than a rigid external wall. Its shape and position depend not only on the components enclosed by that membrane but also on its physical relationships with neighboring cells and with material secreted into the extracellular space. A fibroblast in connective tissue, for instance, is surrounded by a network that can withstand pulling forces. If it could neither recognize nor hold that network, its own contractile activity would produce little useful movement of the surrounding tissue. Similarly, an epithelial cell at the basal surface of a sheet must resist forces that tend to separate the sheet from its underlying support. The biological difficulty is therefore not merely keeping cells together; it is establishing specific attachments that remain appropriate as the environment changes.

The extracellular matrix, abbreviated ECM, provides both the material to which cells attach and information about their surroundings. Collagen fibers can bear tensile loads; basement membrane laminins provide recognizable surfaces for epithelial attachment; fibronectin helps organize adhesive sites. These macromolecules are not freely permeable through a lipid bilayer. A cell consequently requires a membrane protein that can recognize a particular extracellular structure while connecting that recognition to events in the cytoplasm. An integrin is one important solution to this requirement. Its outer region binds a suitable molecular partner, its membrane-spanning region maintains physical continuity, and its inner regions interact with cytoplasmic proteins that organize force and signaling.

An attachment cannot always be permanent. During wound repair, a fibroblast may have to move into a region containing newly deposited matrix. It needs to establish attachments in the direction of travel, generate force against those attachments, and release attachments that would otherwise hold the rear of the cell in place. A leukocyte faces a different version of the same problem. It normally travels in circulating blood but must sometimes develop a stable attachment to a vessel wall. If the receptor were permanently adhesive under every condition, the cell might attach in inappropriate places. If the receptor could never increase its ability to bind, useful arrest would be difficult. Adjustability is therefore a necessary property of many integrin systems.

A receptor recognizes a ligand, meaning a molecule or molecular feature to which that receptor binds. A ligand for an integrin is not necessarily a small soluble messenger of the kind encountered in hormone signaling. It may be a region within a large insoluble matrix glycoprotein. It may instead be a protein on another cell. The location and immobilization of the ligand matter because an attached cell can pull against a ligand fixed to a matrix more effectively than against a freely moving molecule in solution. Recognition, force transmission and the activation of biochemical pathways are related properties of the integrin system, but none is simply interchangeable with the others.

Consider two cells that carry equal numbers of the same integrin at their surfaces. The first might attach firmly to a fibronectin-coated surface whereas the second attaches weakly. This observation alone does not show that one cell expresses more receptors. The receptors may differ in conformation, their ability to recruit intracellular partners or their organization into groups at the membrane. The distinction between receptor abundance and receptor activity will recur throughout this chapter. It explains why a change in adhesion can occur rapidly without waiting for transcription and translation of new proteins. It also explains why measurement of receptor quantity is insufficient to establish how a cell behaves.

An integrin-dependent attachment differs in purpose from a gap junction. A gap junction contains channels through which certain ions and small molecules can travel between adjacent cytoplasms. Integrins provide no such channel. Instead, their principal role is recognition and mechanical coupling across a plasma membrane, accompanied by regulation of intracellular responses. They also differ from cadherins, which commonly support adhesion between cells expressing compatible cadherins. Although integrins can participate in cell-cell contacts, their usual partners are not another copy of the same integrin on the neighboring membrane. The precise pair of molecules involved is more informative than simply calling a contact a junction.

It is helpful to separate three questions whenever an adhesion experiment is presented. First, can the cell identify and bind the substrate? Second, can the bond be connected to intracellular structures so that it withstands a load? Third, can the cell interpret the attachment as information and alter its behavior? Loss of any one of these abilities may make a cell appear defective in a general attachment assay. However, the molecular explanation and the prediction for other experiments differ. A central objective of studying integrins is learning to identify which step has changed rather than interpreting all adhesion defects as identical.

2. How the molecular bridge was discovered

The development of the integrin concept began with observations of how cultured cells interact with extracellular proteins. Fibronectin was particularly useful because it could promote the attachment and spreading of cells on an otherwise poorly adhesive surface. Its presence outside the cell and the organization of actin filaments inside the cell suggested a connection spanning the membrane. Such a correlation, however, did not establish a direct molecular bridge. Fibronectin and actin were separated by the lipid bilayer, and many membrane proteins could potentially account for the relationship. Scientists had to find an intermediate protein and distinguish a genuine recognition molecule from components that merely happened to occur near the contact.

One approach was to determine which region of fibronectin was necessary for attachment. Fibronectin is a large modular protein. A small portion could retain cell-binding activity even when other portions were absent. Work by Michael Pierschbacher and Erkki Ruoslahti in 1984 identified a short peptide containing the sequence arginine, glycine and aspartate, abbreviated RGD, within the cell-binding region. The significance of a short recognition sequence is that a cell need not recognize an entire matrix molecule as an indivisible shape. It can identify a chemically suitable surface feature presented in an appropriate structural context. Other regions of a large matrix protein can serve distinct functions, such as assembly into a matrix.

The difference between an immobilized peptide and a soluble peptide gives this discovery a mechanistic interpretation. Suppose a substrate presents a recognized peptide on its surface. Receptor binding can create a physical connection between the cell and the substrate. If an excess of the same peptide is provided freely in solution, it may occupy receptors that would otherwise bind to the substrate. The soluble molecules cannot necessarily supply an equivalent stationary anchor. Thus a ligand can promote attachment in one physical presentation while interfering with attachment in another. Competition is a particularly informative observation because it shows that attachment is not simply an indiscriminate attraction to any protein-coated surface.

Controls are critical to such an interpretation. A peptide of similar size and charge but a different sequence can help determine whether inhibition depends on specific residues. A cell that attaches to an independent ligand through a different receptor provides a further comparison. If both cell attachment systems collapse when the peptide is introduced, a nonspecific effect on viability or the physical properties of the medium must be considered. If only attachment through the RGD-recognizing system is impaired, the evidence for receptor recognition is stronger. These are principles for interpreting competition experiments; they are not numerical results attributed to the historical work.

A second line of investigation used antibodies that interfered with cell attachment and candidate membrane proteins associated with fibronectin-binding activity. An antibody can bind an exposed molecular surface and obstruct its function, alter its conformation or change its organization. If an antibody reproducibly inhibits adhesion, its antigen is a candidate component of the machinery. It is not automatically proved to be the direct ligand-binding protein because the antibody might disrupt an associated protein instead. Isolation of the relevant complex, ligand-binding measurements and molecular identification provide complementary evidence. Different experimental approaches became particularly persuasive when they converged on related membrane protein complexes.

In 1986, Tamkun and colleagues published molecular characterization of the membrane glycoprotein involved in connecting extracellular fibronectin and intracellular actin. Their paper introduced the name integrin. The name captured the idea that the protein participates in integration of external and internal structures. Cloning and comparison of related subunits made it possible to recognize a family of receptors rather than treating every adhesive interaction as a completely separate phenomenon. The discovery also created new questions that could be investigated genetically: which subunit combination determines specificity, which portions communicate with the cytoplasm, and which changes impair adhesion without eliminating the receptor?

Studies of platelets helped reveal a property that a static model of a matrix receptor would not explain. Platelets in blood contain the receptor now commonly called αIIbβ3, which can bind fibrinogen when activated. Resting circulating platelets should not normally aggregate simply because fibrinogen is already present in the plasma. A mechanism was required by which intracellular activation could rapidly modify extracellular binding behavior. The explanation of integrin regulation therefore grew from both adhesion studies and physiological systems in which attachment is switched on in response to a signal. The direction from cytoplasm toward external ligand-binding properties is called inside-out signaling.

Structural and biochemical research later clarified how different conformations of integrin ectodomains and interactions of cytoplasmic tails can be associated with altered ligand binding. In his 2002 review Richard Hynes emphasized integrins as receptors capable of transmitting information in both directions across a membrane. This perspective unifies physical anchorage and regulation rather than regarding them as unrelated functions. A ligand can influence intracellular assembly and signaling, while an intracellular regulatory protein can modify external recognition without traversing the membrane. The underlying mechanism is an alteration of the distribution of molecular states and interactions.

These historical observations also explain why no single method supplies every part of the modern model. A peptide competition experiment establishes a recognition requirement, an antibody experiment identifies an accessible candidate, sequence analysis reveals membrane topology and protein relationships, and functional reconstitution tests whether particular components can reproduce a proposed molecular event. Cellular experiments test whether the reconstructed mechanism operates under physiological conditions. A good examination answer relates each conclusion to the evidence that actually supports it. It should not claim, for example, that the discovery of RGD proved that all integrins recognize RGD or that an image of a receptor beside actin proved direct binding between those molecules.

3. The αβ receptor: molecular organization and family diversity

An integrin is a heterodimer, a functional protein complex containing two different polypeptide chains. Its chains are named alpha and beta, written α and β. The complete receptor designation identifies both chains. For example, α5β1 contains the alpha-5 subunit and the beta-1 subunit. Calling it simply beta-1 can be misleading because the same beta subunit participates in several different receptors. A heterodimer forms through noncovalent interactions between the two subunits. Although these interactions stabilize a functional complex, the chains remain distinct polypeptides with their own extracellular, transmembrane and intracellular portions.

The human integrin family is conventionally described as eighteen alpha subunits and eight beta subunits forming twenty-four established heterodimers. Multiplying eighteen by eight gives 144 mathematically possible pairs, but the calculation does not give the number of biological receptors. Most hypothetical pairings do not form naturally occurring functional heterodimers. The distribution of interfaces and compatible subunit combinations imposes molecular constraints. Tissue-specific gene expression adds another layer of selectivity. A cell need not express all members of the integrin family, and the presence of a particular alpha chain cannot by itself establish which receptors are present without knowledge of compatible beta chains.

Each typical subunit traverses the lipid bilayer once. The extracellular domain is much larger than the usual cytoplasmic tail. Together the outer regions form a ligand-binding head connected by leg-like domains to the membrane. The transmembrane helices are arranged in the hydrophobic interior of the bilayer, with cytoplasmic portions available to interact with intracellular proteins. This topology creates a continuous route for conformational changes and force. It does not create a continuous aqueous channel from the outside to the inside. There is no requirement for a ligand or a divalent cation to cross the membrane in order to alter the receptor's signaling behavior.

The alpha subunit contains a large beta-propeller domain in its head region. The term beta-propeller describes a structural arrangement of protein elements and should not be confused with the beta subunit of the receptor. A subset of alpha subunits carries an inserted domain known as the alpha I domain, or alpha A domain. This inserted domain can provide the principal ligand-recognition surface. Collagen-binding integrins such as α2β1 and leukocyte integrins such as αLβ2 illustrate this architecture. Other alpha subunits lack the inserted alpha I domain. In those receptors the recognition site generally involves the head interface between the alpha and beta subunits.

The beta chain has an extracellular I-like domain that participates in metal-dependent recognition or transmission of the conformational change, depending on the receptor family. Its lower extracellular domains contribute to leg flexibility and the organization of receptor states. A structural domain is a portion of a protein that can form a recognizable three-dimensional unit with particular functions and interactions. Understanding domain architecture allows a student to predict how mutations can separate functions. A substitution near the extracellular recognition site may prevent ligand binding even though the cytoplasmic tail remains capable of associating with an adaptor. A mutation at the other end may instead preserve the recognition surface while changing activation or anchorage.

The extracellular regions of integrins are glycosylated. Carbohydrate chains added during biosynthesis influence the properties of many membrane proteins, including their folding, stability, transport and surface behavior. It would be too strong to conclude that every sugar attached to an integrin is directly part of its ligand-binding site. Rather, changes in glycosylation may affect the amount of correctly folded receptor that reaches the membrane or affect binding in receptor-specific ways. When studying a mutation or an inhibitor, one should therefore distinguish total synthesis of integrin protein from delivery of mature functional heterodimers to the cell surface.

Most integrin cytoplasmic tails are short compared with the catalytic domains of classical receptor tyrosine kinases. They generally lack intrinsic kinase activity. Instead, they contain binding regions for adaptor and regulatory proteins that recruit or organize enzymes elsewhere in the complex. This difference is important because an experiment showing increased tyrosine phosphorylation after integrin engagement does not imply that the integrin itself transferred a phosphate group. The catalytic work may be performed by focal adhesion kinase or Src-family kinases associated with the adhesion. The receptor provides the physical and organizational context in which those enzymes become regulated.

An important exception to the short-tail pattern is the β4 subunit, which has an unusually large cytoplasmic region. In α6β4 integrin, this region supports association with the keratin intermediate-filament system through specialized linker proteins in epithelial hemidesmosomes. The exception demonstrates that the name integrin does not specify a single type of cytoskeletal connection. Most textbook examples emphasize actin-linked adhesions, and for good reason, but a general statement that all integrins are linked only to actin would incorrectly exclude a central form of epithelial anchorage. Molecular organization must be interpreted for the specific heterodimer and junction.

The distinction between family membership and functional identity is also apparent in tissue biology. Many cell types express beta-1-containing integrins, but an immune cell may employ a beta-2-containing receptor to recognize a vascular ligand whereas a platelet relies on αIIbβ3 for aggregation. Some integrins bind several ligands, and some extracellular proteins offer recognition sites for more than one integrin. Consequently the loss of a single heterodimer need not remove every adhesive interaction on a given substrate. The remaining receptors may provide partial compensation, or the loss may produce a particular defect that depends on where the missing receptor was expressed.

The proper level of analysis thus depends on the experimental question. If an antibody recognizes the beta-1 chain, it may identify or perturb a whole subset of heterodimers. If it recognizes an epitope present only on α5β1, the interpretation can be narrower. Likewise, a knockout of a beta gene potentially affects several receptors, whereas mutation of a specific alpha chain may affect fewer. This combinatorial organization gives the integrin family both diversity and shared properties. It also means that examination questions asking for the function of an unspecified alpha or beta subunit should be approached cautiously unless the relevant partner is given.

4. Recognition of matrix and cell-surface ligands

Ligand specificity arises from the molecular fit between a receptor's binding surface and chemical groups presented by its ligand. Integrin recognition is neither wholly nonspecific adhesion nor a simple universal rule in which any protein that contains a particular short sequence will necessarily bind every receptor. A useful way to understand specificity is to consider several well-established ligand classes. These classes relate receptor diversity to extracellular matrix organization rather than requiring memorization of an exhaustive list of all twenty-four human integrins.

Fibronectin provides a classical example. The integrin α5β1 recognizes fibronectin using the RGD region together with interactions influenced by a neighboring synergy region. The three letters refer to arginine, glycine and aspartate in the one-letter amino acid notation. The arginine side chain has a positively charged group at physiological pH, the aspartate side chain has a negatively charged carboxylate, and glycine contributes a small residue within the peptide backbone. The arrangement of these groups helps establish receptor recognition. The sequence functions as part of a larger folded region, so both its chemical identity and its accessibility influence the outcome.

Vitronectin, another adhesive glycoprotein, is a familiar ligand for αvβ3. Several integrins can recognize RGD-containing sites in appropriate proteins. Therefore a synthetic RGD-bearing surface may support attachment through more than one receptor. If a researcher observes that free RGD peptide reduces attachment, it is reasonable to infer involvement of an RGD-sensitive adhesive interaction under the given conditions, but not to identify α5β1 specifically without further evidence. A receptor-specific blocking antibody, a genetic deletion or an independently validated binding measurement is needed to distinguish between plausible candidates.

The collagen-binding receptors illustrate a distinct recognition principle. α2β1 is a representative integrin that recognizes triple-helical collagen. An established recognition motif is GFOGER, where O represents hydroxyproline in this collagen sequence notation. The geometry of the collagen triple helix contributes to recognition by the integrin alpha I domain. A short unstructured peptide containing the same ordinary amino acids is not necessarily an equivalent ligand because it may fail to present the residues in the native arrangement. Similarly, heat denaturation of collagen can change adhesive recognition even though the sample still contains collagen polypeptides. The substrate's conformation is a functional part of its identity.

Basement membrane laminins are recognized by integrins such as α3β1, α6β1 and α6β4, with receptor preferences varying across laminin forms and tissues. The biological context is particularly clear in epithelial sheets, whose basal surfaces contact a basement membrane rather than the fibrillar interstitial matrix encountered by many fibroblasts. Recognition of laminin can connect a basal cell surface to cytoskeletal structures and thereby contribute to the maintenance of tissue organization. This is a selective relation between matrix composition and receptor expression. A cell that recognizes laminin efficiently need not attach equally well to every type of collagen or fibronectin presentation.

Certain integrins mediate attachment to another cell's membrane rather than to matrix. The leukocyte integrin αLβ2, often called LFA-1, recognizes intercellular adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1. ICAM-1 is an immunoglobulin-superfamily adhesion protein; it is not an integrin. Another important pair is α4β1, also called VLA-4, and VCAM-1. The α4β1 receptor also recognizes appropriate regions of fibronectin. These examples show why describing integrins exclusively as cell-matrix receptors is an incomplete generalization. The same family architecture supports interactions with structurally different extracellular partners, including proteins on other cells.

Platelet αIIbβ3 recognizes fibrinogen and other appropriate adhesive ligands. Fibrinogen has multiple interaction sites and can participate in bridging neighboring activated platelets. The physiological function depends not merely on the presence of ligand but on receptor activation and on the multivalent nature of the bridging molecule. An isolated receptor-ligand bond can form without producing macroscopic aggregation. Aggregation requires sufficiently effective connectivity across a population of platelets. This distinction between individual binding and organization of a multicellular structure helps explain why experiments using soluble ligand binding and experiments measuring aggregation are related but not equivalent.

Ligand density and presentation strongly influence the biological consequences of specificity. On a surface with many appropriately exposed recognition sites, a cell may bind multiple integrins. On a surface with sparse sites, an individual receptor may recognize its ligand with unchanged intrinsic affinity, yet the cell may be unable to build an attachment strong enough to persist. If a protein coating changes its conformation when adsorbed to a surface, the accessible density of recognition sites may differ from the total amount of protein applied. Thus equal coating mass does not guarantee equal biological ligand availability. A rigorous comparison controls the identity, accessibility and physical presentation of the substrate.

When interpreting attachment to a mixture of matrix proteins, the existence of more than one receptor-ligand relationship is particularly important. Suppose a cell expresses collagen-binding and fibronectin-binding integrins and is placed on a mixture containing both collagen and fibronectin. A selective inhibitor of the fibronectin receptor may reduce one set of contacts without eliminating collagen-dependent attachment. The residual adhesion does not prove that the inhibitor is ineffective. Repeating the experiment on separately defined substrates and measuring receptor surface abundance provides better evidence. Conversely, complete failure on every substrate could suggest a shared regulatory component, a more general cytoskeletal defect or compromised cell health.

These relationships also reveal a boundary of the RGD model. An RGD sequence is a valuable experimental tool because a subset of integrins recognizes it, but collagen receptors, laminin receptors and leukocyte integrins cannot be reduced to the same rule. Even among RGD-recognizing receptors, adjacent residues, peptide geometry and the conformation of the receptor influence binding. The correct conclusion is that integrins have diverse, overlapping recognition preferences. The identity of both subunits, the ligand context and the experimental conditions determine which interaction is likely to dominate.

5. Divalent ions and the chemistry of ligand recognition

Integrins illustrate a form of molecular recognition in which a metal ion can participate directly in an interaction between two proteins. An ion with a charge of +2, such as magnesium, has a strong electrostatic attraction for appropriate electron-donating oxygen atoms. In water it is surrounded by water molecules, but it can also be coordinated by oxygen-containing chemical groups within a folded protein. The spatial arrangement of those groups is imposed by the structure of the protein. The metal ion therefore becomes part of a well-defined chemical environment rather than simply increasing the positive charge of the receptor surface.

MIDAS is the abbreviation for metal ion-dependent adhesion site. In an integrin alpha I domain, several receptor residues contribute to the coordination environment around a divalent ion. A suitable ligand can contribute a carboxylate group to that environment. For a receptor lacking an alpha I domain, the corresponding metal-dependent recognition surface is generally located in the beta I-like domain. In RGD-recognizing receptors, the aspartate carboxylate of the ligand provides a familiar example of a metal-interacting group. Such an arrangement makes an appropriately oriented chemical group important to binding even though most of the ligand's molecular mass may lie far from the site.

A metal-dependent interaction is not the same as an indiscriminate attraction between molecules of opposite charge. A protein can carry a net negative charge yet fail to bind because its acidic groups are not appropriately arranged. Conversely, a ligand can contain many residues that are irrelevant to receptor recognition but still bind through a properly presented recognition region. Changing an aspartate to an amino acid of similar size but without a carboxylate may remove an interaction required for productive binding. Altering a nearby residue can change recognition by changing how the coordinating group is presented rather than by removing the coordinating group itself.

Magnesium commonly supports ligand recognition in integrin systems. Manganese can favor stronger binding in many experimental systems, which is why it is frequently used as an extracellular manipulation of integrin activity. Calcium has receptor- and condition-dependent effects. Integrin ectodomains contain multiple metal-binding sites, and ion occupancy at one site can affect recognition at another. Consequently a blanket statement that calcium activates every integrin, or that it inhibits every integrin, is not defensible. For examinations it is more useful to remember metal dependence as a general principle and interpret the effects of particular ions in the conditions specified by the question.

The situation is different for classical cadherins. Calcium binds between successive extracellular cadherin domains and helps maintain the structure needed for adhesion. Removing calcium can compromise cadherin-mediated contacts, but it does not follow that cadherins possess the same recognition mechanism as integrins. Both receptor families may be sensitive to chelators for different molecular reasons. Thus observing that a tissue separates after calcium removal cannot, by itself, establish which adhesion molecule was responsible for maintaining the tissue. Receptor identity must be investigated using additional evidence.

A chelator is a compound that binds metal ions through multiple coordinating groups. EDTA can lower the free concentrations of several divalent ions in an experimental solution. If cells attached to fibronectin lose adhesion following EDTA exposure, metal-dependent adhesion is a plausible explanation. However, the effect of EDTA is broader than a specific block of the integrin MIDAS. A loss of adhesion could involve more than one receptor system, and a long treatment may affect other cellular functions. The experiment is strongest when the attachment substrate is defined, receptor-specific perturbations are included, and the effect of removing the chelator and restoring suitable ions is measured.

The free concentration of an ion must be distinguished from the amount added to a solution. For example, two media with identical total magnesium can have very different free magnesium if one contains a chelator. In considering a metal-dependent binding reaction, it is the ion accessible to the receptor that matters. Exact calculation of free metal concentration would require information about complex formation and competing solutes. In the simplified experiments used at examination level, one can compare media containing available ions with media in which those ions have been effectively removed, without pretending to know an exact free concentration.

An extracellular magnesium requirement should not be confused with a requirement for an elevation of cytoplasmic calcium. In the first case the relevant ion contributes to the chemistry or conformation of the extracellular receptor domain. In the second case the cytoplasmic ion can regulate signaling proteins within the cell. A cell may possess both mechanisms at the same time. If an inhibitor is confined to the extracellular solution, it may disrupt the recognition site without directly inhibiting calcium-dependent enzymes inside the cytoplasm. Conversely, a cytoplasmic signaling defect can impair activation even when extracellular metal availability is normal.

Metal replacement experiments are useful for distinguishing reversible environmental effects from structural receptor defects. A receptor that lost binding because free magnesium was removed may recover after suitable conditions are restored. A receptor with a mutation that destroys its coordinating site may not recover simply because magnesium is abundant. The second failure illustrates that ligand recognition requires both the appropriate chemical participant and a receptor able to position that participant. The absence of rescue does not alone identify the precise mutated residue, but it narrows the explanations and motivates a direct test of the receptor's extracellular binding function.

6. Conformational states and allosteric regulation

Proteins can undergo changes in three-dimensional arrangement without being newly synthesized or chemically degraded. Their covalent backbones establish a basic molecular architecture, but noncovalent interactions permit a range of conformational states. For integrins this flexibility is especially significant because extracellular ligand recognition can be regulated by proteins that interact with the opposite side of the plasma membrane. Allostery describes such communication between different regions of a molecule. A regulator need not occupy the same site as a ligand to influence the ligand's binding properties.

A standard structural model distinguishes bent-closed, extended-closed and extended-open configurations of an integrin. In a bent configuration the receptor's outer region folds toward the membrane. In an extended configuration the outer head is displaced farther away from the membrane. Opening of the ligand-binding headpiece is a further structural change often associated with a high-affinity state. These are related but different descriptions of molecular geometry. If a probe establishes that receptors are extended, it has not necessarily established that every recognition head has opened or that every receptor has bound ligand.

The resting population of receptors should not be imagined as a rigid collection of identical shapes. Even in the absence of an activating signal, molecular motions and interactions generate a distribution of states. Changes in regulatory partners, ligand occupancy or force can alter that distribution. A higher fraction of binding-competent states can produce stronger observed binding without requiring any increase in the number of receptors. A receptor population can return toward its original distribution when the activating condition is removed. This mechanism provides an efficient way to control cell attachment over a short interval.

The structure of the extracellular head is physically connected to the leg domains, transmembrane helices and cytoplasmic tails. In many well-studied integrins, interactions between the transmembrane and membrane-proximal portions of the alpha and beta subunits favor resting configurations. Changing those interactions can reduce structural constraints, allowing an increased probability of active configurations. This is why an intracellular mutation can modify an extracellular ligand-binding measurement. A chemical signal does not have to cross the membrane through a pore; rearrangement of a continuous membrane protein complex can transmit the effect.

Ligand binding itself can stabilize favorable receptor configurations. A receptor with a flexible ectodomain can therefore respond not only to intracellular regulators but also to the ligand it encounters. These effects are reciprocal: changes in receptor state alter the probability of ligand recognition, while ligand occupancy alters the stability of receptor states. A simplistic description in which every receptor must first become fully extended and only then can bind any ligand overlooks receptor-specific differences and the continuous nature of conformational equilibria. The common structural model remains a useful framework when these limitations are recognized.

A conformation-sensitive antibody is an experimental probe whose recognition depends on the accessibility or shape of a particular epitope. If its binding increases following a short cellular stimulus, a conformational alteration may have occurred. To interpret the result, a second antibody that measures total surface receptor is useful. Without that comparison, an increase in signal could simply result from more receptor molecules at the cell surface. It is also important that the mutation or treatment under investigation does not independently change the antibody's own binding site. A conformation-sensitive readout is evidence about the state it recognizes rather than a universal measurement of all receptor functions.

An extracellular activating manipulation can sometimes help separate defects in regulation from defects in the actual ligand-binding surface. A cell whose intracellular activation machinery is defective may still carry a receptor capable of binding if an external condition favors active conformations. A receptor whose recognition surface is structurally defective may remain unable to bind under the same manipulation. These are conditional predictions, because different mutations may alter the receptor's response to the manipulation itself. If soluble ligand binding is restored but cell spreading remains impaired, the result suggests that activation and downstream mechanical attachment have been separated.

Constitutive activation is another instructive perturbation. A mutation that destabilizes a resting conformation may increase ligand binding without the ordinary regulatory stimulus. That might seem advantageous for a cell placed on an adhesive substrate, but in an organism adhesion must occur at suitable sites and times. A circulating cell that cannot keep its adhesion receptors appropriately restrained may form unwanted contacts or fail to detach when necessary. Stronger binding in a laboratory assay therefore does not necessarily imply improved physiological function. The biological importance of regulation includes the capacity to turn adhesion down as well as to turn it up.

Allostery must also be distinguished from changes in receptor clustering. A cell may accumulate several integrins in a small region without changing the affinity of any individual receptor. Conversely, individual receptors may become stronger binders without a large change in their distribution across the surface. Either process can increase apparent adhesion in a whole-cell assay. Observations of bright receptor clusters by microscopy are therefore insufficient to demonstrate a specific conformational transition. Together, structural probes, controlled ligand-binding experiments and measurements of receptor organization can establish which contributions are responsible for a changed adhesive behavior.

7. Inside-out signaling and rapid control of adhesiveness

Inside-out signaling describes a regulatory transition in which events at the cytoplasmic side of an integrin change its extracellular ligand-binding behavior. The initial stimulus to the cell can itself be extracellular. A chemokine, for example, may activate a conventional signaling receptor, producing an intracellular response that modifies an integrin. The term inside-out then refers specifically to how the integrin is regulated. It does not require the original message to have originated in the cytoplasm, nor does it mean that an extracellular ligand is transported outward through the integrin.

Talin is a large intracellular protein with an important role in the activation of many integrins and the organization of their connections to actin. Its head region can bind to the cytoplasmic tail of a suitable beta integrin subunit. Other regions of talin participate in additional interactions, including those with the cytoskeleton. This molecular organization allows talin to influence the receptor's state at the membrane while also helping connect it to cellular force-generating structures. These roles are related, but experiments can sometimes separate the ability of talin to promote ligand binding from the ability of an adhesion to withstand tension.

In a widely used model, talin engagement of the beta cytoplasmic tail changes membrane-proximal interactions that help stabilize the resting receptor. Alterations of alpha-beta tail and transmembrane associations are transmitted through the membrane and extracellular legs toward the headpiece. The distribution of receptor configurations changes, potentially increasing the fraction with favorable ligand affinity. This is an allosteric effect because talin and the ligand bind at different regions of the receptor. Talin is not a ligand that competes directly for the extracellular recognition pocket, and it is not a kinase that must phosphorylate the ligand to activate adhesion.

Kindlins cooperate in many cellular systems with talin. They bind regions of beta-integrin cytoplasmic tails distinct from the principal talin-binding site and can contribute to receptor activation, organization and stabilization of adhesive responses. Their contribution depends on the integrin and cell context. A mutation that selectively prevents one interaction may therefore change one or more aspects of activation even if the other adaptor can still associate with the receptor. It would be misleading to describe kindlin as merely a smaller copy of talin or to assume that talin binding always makes every other regulator unnecessary.

Upstream pathways provide a link between extracellular signals and this intracellular machinery. A small GTPase such as Rap1 can participate in signaling that promotes talin recruitment or activation in appropriate cells. Like other regulatory GTPases, Rap1 can adopt different functional states depending on its bound guanine nucleotide. Signals that favor an active Rap1 state can influence the localization and interactions of downstream components. The purpose of including Rap1 here is to understand the connection between a signaling receptor and an adhesion receptor, rather than to memorize every intermediary found in a particular cell type.

A rapid activation response can use proteins that were already synthesized before the signal arrived. Talin, kindlin, integrins and upstream regulators may all be present in the resting cell. The stimulus changes their activity, accessibility or distribution. If receptor expression at the membrane remains constant while ligand binding increases within a short time, the observation is consistent with this kind of post-translational regulation. It is not absolute proof of talin involvement, because other mechanisms can also change the activity of existing receptor molecules. Specific perturbations are required to identify the causal regulator.

Platelet αIIbβ3 provides a standard physiological example. Fibrinogen is already present in blood, yet circulating resting platelets normally do not form large fibrinogen-mediated aggregates. Appropriate platelet activation promotes intracellular events that increase the ability of αIIbβ3 to engage adhesive ligands. Fibrinogen can then participate in connections between activated platelets. A receptor expressed continuously at the membrane can thus provide an adhesion response only when needed. A mutation affecting intracellular activation could produce weak stimulation-dependent fibrinogen binding even if the surface abundance of αIIbβ3 remained normal.

One can distinguish such an activation defect from loss of receptor expression by measuring two independent properties. An antibody recognizing total surface αIIbβ3 reports receptor abundance, whereas a suitable soluble fibrinogen-binding measurement reports functional binding under specified conditions. If abundance is markedly reduced, fewer receptors are available to bind. If abundance is comparable to normal but agonist-induced binding is impaired, the activation machinery or ligand-binding surface becomes a more plausible target of investigation. An extracellular activating manipulation and additional structural measurements can help separate these possibilities.

Ye and colleagues reported in 2010 that talin binding could activate and extend membrane-associated αIIbβ3 in a reconstructed experimental system. By controlling which components were present, the study addressed whether talin could reproduce a terminal activation event. Such evidence is different from an experiment in which talin is removed from a living platelet or cultured cell. A reconstitution experiment can establish sufficiency for a specified biochemical response, whereas a loss-of-function experiment tests whether the protein is required for a response under the tested conditions. The cellular environment may impose further requirements for localization, regulation, clustering or force transmission.

Consider an integrin beta-tail mutation that disrupts talin binding but does not affect surface expression or the extracellular recognition site. A stimulation-dependent increase in ligand binding might be reduced. If a condition that directly favors extracellular activation restores binding, the result supports a defect in the normal intracellular activation route. However, the same cells might still be unable to organize a normal actin-linked adhesion. The mutation can affect mechanical coupling in addition to activation. An experiment that records only soluble ligand binding would miss the latter defect, whereas an experiment measuring cell shape alone might conceal the restoration of receptor recognition.

Regulation has a second half: declining activation and release of contacts. When an intracellular stimulus diminishes, receptor states can shift again. Changes in adaptor interactions, membrane distribution and cytoskeletal loading can also affect adhesive behavior. A cell does not necessarily have to destroy its integrins to stop using a particular contact. Reversible control of receptor activity is therefore useful for cells whose task requires attachment at one moment and release at another. In directed movement, this temporal control can be coupled to spatial regulation so that different regions of the same cell form and release contacts at different times.

8. Outside-in signaling and the molecular interpretation of attachment

Outside-in signaling begins with the integrin's engagement with an appropriate extracellular partner and describes the resulting influence on intracellular events. Ligand binding provides a local indication of the cell's environment. Changes in receptor organization and interactions of the cytoplasmic tails provide a route by which that information affects enzymes and structural proteins. Because an integrin can transmit load as well as bind a chemical ligand, the physical nature of the contact can influence the response. This creates a mechanism by which the cell can respond to both the composition and the mechanical properties of its surroundings.

Integrins are not generally receptor tyrosine kinases. Their short cytoplasmic tails usually have no intrinsic catalytic kinase domain. Instead, their engagement can organize complexes containing enzymes such as focal adhesion kinase, abbreviated FAK. FAK is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase that participates in signaling at many adhesion sites. A common early signaling event involves phosphorylation of FAK at tyrosine 397, creating a site for association with Src-family kinases. Further phosphorylation and interactions within the complex can influence adhesion assembly, turnover and cell behavior.

Phosphorylation in this setting illustrates the importance of identifying the enzyme responsible for a molecular change. A cell may exhibit an increase in tyrosine-phosphorylated proteins after attachment to fibronectin. This does not establish that the integrin itself transfers phosphate from ATP. The receptor can recruit, position or regulate a separate enzyme. Catalytic proteins and structural receptors have different functions within a complex. A mutation that removes an enzyme's catalytic activity may affect phosphorylation even if the initial ligand-recognition event is intact.

Paxillin is an example of an adhesion-associated adaptor or scaffold. Its interaction sites help organize proteins within focal adhesion complexes. A scaffold need not perform a chemical transformation to influence signaling; by altering where molecules assemble, it can change the opportunities for regulatory interactions. Talin, vinculin and other linkers contribute to mechanical organization, whereas FAK and Src contribute catalytic activity. The assembly is not simply a collection of identical proteins surrounding an integrin. Its organization brings together distinct biochemical and mechanical functions at a site of cellular contact.

Adhesion-associated signaling can influence actin through the Rho family of small GTPases. Rac and Cdc42 contribute to particular forms of protrusive and polarized actin organization, while RhoA can contribute to contractile structures through downstream regulators. The shape of a cell depends not just on which proteins are present, but on where and when these regulators operate. Local signaling near a newly formed attachment can promote changes that would have very different consequences if they occurred uniformly across the cell. The connection between external attachment and the distribution of cytoskeletal activity is an important part of the biological meaning of outside-in signaling.

Integrin signals also cooperate with signals supplied by soluble growth factors. In many anchorage-dependent cells, adhesion-associated pathways can contribute to activation of survival-promoting signaling such as PI3K-Akt. The matrix contact is not equivalent to a complete growth-factor signal, and its presence does not guarantee that the cell will proliferate. Different pathways may be necessary in combination. The physiological response reflects a decision based on several inputs: whether the cell is attached appropriately, whether growth factors are available, and whether internal conditions support survival or division.

A basic outside-in experiment compares otherwise equivalent cells placed on an adhesive substrate or maintained under a nonadhesive condition. If FAK phosphorylation rises rapidly on the adhesive substrate, the observations support a relationship between adhesion and signaling. Receptor-specific blocking, equal cell input and measurement of total FAK help strengthen the interpretation. A result obtained after many hours may be affected by differences in cell survival, spreading or the proportion of cells retained. Early time points can help distinguish an immediate adhesion-associated signaling event from secondary changes in cell composition.

Different inhibitors can reveal different stages of the process. A ligand-binding inhibitor prevents formation of contacts needed for many downstream events. An inhibitor of FAK catalytic activity may instead reduce particular phosphorylation responses even though a receptor can still recognize extracellular ligand. Consequently immediate receptor binding could be maintained while mature spreading or directed movement is impaired. This prediction is not universal because signaling can feed back onto receptor organization. Nevertheless, using distinct readouts helps determine whether the first failure occurs at recognition or later in the response.

A single physiological sequence can contain both directions of signaling. A soluble mediator can initiate intracellular activation of an integrin; the activated receptor can then bind a substrate; engagement can alter cytoplasmic signaling and force generation. Changes in these cytoplasmic events can influence other nearby integrins, strengthening or remodeling the attachment. There is no contradiction in describing different transitions within the same sequence as inside-out and outside-in. The distinction becomes clear when each arrow is labeled with its starting compartment and the functional change at its destination.

The two directions also explain why adhesion cannot be adequately described as a static physical bridge. If an extracellular ligand were merely an inert anchor, changing it would not be expected to influence intracellular kinase activity or survival. If the receptor were only a signaling switch, defects in physical coupling would not be expected to alter a cell's capacity to pull on the matrix. Integrin function unifies recognition, force transmission and biochemical communication while preserving important distinctions between them. Understanding those distinctions is necessary for interpreting both molecular perturbations and whole-cell behavior.

9. Affinity, occupancy and avidity: distinguishing molecular and cellular measurements

To understand why one cell adheres more firmly than another, begin with the simplest possible molecular reaction. Let R represent an unoccupied receptor and L a freely available ligand that has a single effective binding site. The bound complex is RL. Binding can be represented by R + L ⇌ RL. At equilibrium, molecules continue to associate and dissociate, but the average amounts of each species are constant because the two opposing rates balance. This description concerns a reversible binding reaction. It is not a complete description of a cell adhering to a solid substrate, where receptor movement, clustering, cytoskeletal forces and substrate geometry introduce additional variables.

For the simplest mass-action model, the association rate is k_on[R][L] and the dissociation rate is k_off[RL]. Square brackets represent molar concentration. The association rate constant has units of inverse molar concentration per unit time, for example M⁻¹ s⁻¹, and the dissociation rate constant has units of inverse time, for example s⁻¹. At equilibrium the equality of these rates gives k_on[R][L] = k_off[RL]. Rearrangement defines the equilibrium dissociation constant K_d = k_off/k_on = [R][L]/[RL]. Its units are concentration because one concentration remains after cancellation. A smaller dissociation constant corresponds to a greater equilibrium affinity under the specified conditions.

The total receptor concentration in this simple system is [R]_T = [R] + [RL]. The fraction of receptor occupied, θ, equals [RL]/[R]_T. Substituting [R] = K_d[RL]/[L] yields θ = [L]/(K_d + [L]). This is the familiar saturating binding relation for independent sites of a single class. When the free ligand concentration equals K_d, half the receptors are occupied at equilibrium. When free ligand greatly exceeds K_d, the bound fraction approaches one. At much lower ligand concentration than K_d, the bound fraction is approximately [L]/K_d. Each limiting case follows directly from the relative sizes of the two terms in the denominator.

Consider two hypothetical states of an integrin with dissociation constants of 100 nM and 10 nM for the same monovalent ligand. At a free ligand concentration of 10 nM, the first state's equilibrium occupancy is 10/(100 + 10), or about 0.091. The second state's occupancy is 10/(10 + 10), or 0.50. The change in affinity therefore produces a substantial change in occupancy at the same ligand concentration and with the same number of receptors. These values are illustrative rather than reported measurements of any particular integrin. They show how a conformational change that shifts affinity can produce a functional difference without changing receptor expression.

The difference between the two states is not equally conspicuous at all ligand concentrations. At 1,000 nM, the illustrative occupancies become 1,000/1,100, approximately 0.909, and 1,000/1,010, approximately 0.990. Both are close to saturation. A measurement made only at a very high concentration might therefore underestimate the functional difference between the states. To compare affinities meaningfully, ligand-binding measurements should span concentrations below and above the expected half-saturation point. A single signal at saturation is more useful for estimating binding capacity than for distinguishing closely related affinities, provided the other assumptions are met.

Binding capacity and affinity correspond to different properties of a population. If cells contain twice as many available receptors, they may bind twice as much ligand when all sites are occupied. The concentration at which half of each population's own sites become occupied need not change. Conversely, a receptor population whose affinity increases without a change in receptor number can display a leftward shift in a normalized binding curve without a change in the theoretical saturation capacity. This distinction is useful when interpreting a graph comparing resting and stimulated cells. Absolute binding and fractional occupancy answer different questions, so normalization must be understood before drawing a conclusion.

The model assumes that the free concentration of ligand is known. If ligand is supplied in large excess, the free concentration may be close to the total concentration added. If receptors bind a substantial fraction of the ligand molecules, that approximation fails. Binding then reduces the free ligand concentration, and the mass balance for ligand must be included in the calculation. The elementary occupancy equation is still valid in terms of free ligand concentration, but substituting total ligand concentration can introduce an error. Examination problems often state that ligand is in excess to make this distinction explicit.

Affinity is related to thermodynamics. With a standard concentration C° of 1 M, the dimensionless equilibrium association constant for the simple reaction is K_a° = C°/K_d when K_d is expressed in molar units. The standard Gibbs free energy of binding is ΔG° = −RT ln K_a° = RT ln(K_d/C°). R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature and the logarithm acts on a dimensionless ratio. At a fixed temperature a smaller K_d corresponds to a more negative standard binding free energy. For the illustrative tenfold reduction in K_d above, the difference in ΔG° is RT ln(0.1), about −5.7 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. The result expresses an equilibrium free-energy difference, not the amount of ATP directly consumed by an integrin.

Kinetics offers another distinction. A bond with dissociation rate constant k_off has, in a simple first-order dissociation model without rebinding or applied force, a mean lifetime of 1/k_off. Its half-life is ln(2)/k_off. If k_off is 0.2 s⁻¹, the half-life is about 3.47 s. If it is 0.02 s⁻¹, the half-life is about 34.7 s. Slower dissociation can support a longer-lived individual interaction. However, two interactions can have the same K_d while possessing different association and dissociation rate constants if both constants change proportionately. Equilibrium affinity alone does not tell how quickly a cell will attach or release a bond.

Real cell-matrix attachment is not usually a single independent soluble-ligand reaction. Many receptor-ligand bonds may form simultaneously in a contact region. Avidity describes the effective strength of multivalent binding or the combined effect of multiple interactions. When one bond breaks, another may continue to hold the surfaces close together, increasing the probability that a broken bond reforms before the cell separates. Receptor clustering, ligand density, mobility and geometry can contribute to this collective behavior. Avidity can increase while the affinity of an individual molecular pair remains unchanged.

A deliberately simple probability example helps distinguish the two scales. If each of four independent bonds has a probability of 0.5 of remaining intact at a specified instant, the probability that all four have broken is 0.5 raised to the fourth power, or 1/16. The probability that at least one remains intact is therefore 15/16, or 0.9375. With one bond alone, the corresponding probability is 0.5. This calculation is not a general law of cell attachment. Real bonds share forces, may rebind and may not be statistically independent. Its purpose is to show why a contact supported by several bonds can persist more effectively than a single bond even if the individual bonds have identical properties.

At a cell-matrix interface, receptors and ligands are confined to surfaces or to locally accessible regions. Their effective densities may be expressed per unit area rather than in molar concentration. The geometry can affect association, especially where the distance between the membrane and substrate limits access. A dissociation constant measured with soluble purified molecules should not automatically be used as though it directly predicts the strength of a whole cell on a coated dish. The former measures a molecular equilibrium under defined conditions; the latter integrates molecular binding, receptor organization and mechanics.

The force applied to a bond can also alter its lifetime. For many bonds increasing a pulling force favors dissociation, but some integrin-ligand interactions exhibit catch-bond behavior over particular force ranges, meaning that lifetime initially increases with force under those conditions. This observation is important because it prevents the indiscriminate claim that every receptor-ligand bond necessarily becomes weaker as soon as it is loaded. Neither behavior should be generalized to all receptors at all forces. The specified molecular pair, range of applied force and measurement method matter. Equilibrium occupancy calculations do not account for a continuously loaded, dynamically changing adhesion.

When an examination graph reports the number of cells retained after washing, the measured quantity is not automatically proportional to K_d. The wash imposes a mechanical challenge, the contact may contain several kinds of receptors, and the time allowed for attachment may influence how far adhesion assembly has progressed. A reduction in retained cells could reflect decreased affinity, fewer receptors, lower ligand density, disrupted actin coupling or failure of the cells to survive. A second experiment specifically measuring monovalent ligand binding, together with surface expression and imaging of contact organization, can help separate these explanations. The quantitative models supply reference points for reasoning, not substitutes for knowledge of the experimental design.

10. Focal adhesions: building a load-bearing link to actin

A cell attached to a matrix must solve a mechanical problem beyond the initial recognition of a ligand. If the ligand is immobilized and an integrin binds it, the receptor can hold a small region of the plasma membrane near the substrate. However, the lipid bilayer alone is not an efficient structure for transmitting contractile force over the entire cell. A connection from the receptor to the cytoskeleton allows forces generated inside the cell to act against the external matrix. The connection is assembled from proteins on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane. The result can be an organized, dynamic adhesion complex rather than an isolated receptor-ligand bond.

Focal adhesions are integrin-associated structures that link the extracellular matrix to actin filaments through a set of intracellular adaptor and regulatory proteins. They are frequently studied in cultured cells spread on suitable substrates, where they can be visualized as localized regions containing integrins and characteristic cytoplasmic proteins. A focal adhesion is not itself a separate membrane protein. It is an assembly of many molecules, including transmembrane receptors, linkers and signaling components. Its functional identity follows from the continuity between matrix, receptor, cytoplasmic linkage and actin.

Talin contributes an important part of this connection. Its head can interact with an integrin beta tail, while other regions participate in interactions with actin and adhesion-associated proteins. Vinculin can associate with talin and actin, helping reinforce the mechanical linkage in many contexts. These proteins are not interchangeable with actin, which is the filament-forming cytoskeletal protein itself. A useful conceptual representation begins with an ECM ligand, passes through an integrin, connects through a talin-containing adaptor system and ends at an actin filament. Additional proteins regulate and stabilize the connection, and their precise arrangement varies among adhesions.

A newly formed integrin-matrix contact need not immediately possess the organization of a large mature focal adhesion. Small nascent contacts can develop near advancing regions of a cell. Some are transient and disappear; others become more stable and recruit additional components. Actomyosin-generated tension can contribute to maturation in appropriate contexts. The presence of a receptor on the membrane therefore does not prove that it is part of a mature focal adhesion, and the presence of ligand-bound receptor does not establish that the cytoskeleton is fully connected. Adhesion assembly is a process with intermediate states.

The mechanical behavior of the linkage helps explain why some contacts become stronger when tension is applied. Talin contains regions that can change conformation under force. In a common model, pulling can expose sites that bind vinculin, increasing opportunities for reinforcement of an actin-linked attachment. The mechanism makes force more than a challenge to an existing bond; force can also become a regulatory input that changes the composition and stability of the contact. This is one aspect of mechanotransduction, the conversion of mechanical information into changes in cellular organization or biochemical signaling.

Mechanical reinforcement does not imply that unlimited force is beneficial. If the applied load exceeds the capacity of the receptor-ligand bonds, the intracellular linkers or the supporting matrix, the attachment can fail. Even an integrin-rich contact will transmit little useful force if the matrix itself tears or moves freely. The biological response reflects the properties of a complete series of mechanical connections. In the same way, a strong extracellular bond may contribute little to whole-cell traction if the cytoplasmic linkage is defective. The weakest or most dynamic component may limit the force that a contact can transmit.

A simple spring model provides intuition about the physical environment. Suppose a cell pulls on an elastic substrate and the relationship between force F and displacement x can be approximated locally by F = kx, where k is an effective stiffness with units of force per length. For a displacement of 100 nm and stiffness of 0.1 pN nm⁻¹, the force is 10 pN. For the same displacement on a substrate with stiffness 0.5 pN nm⁻¹, the force is 50 pN. The comparison assumes linear elasticity and the same displacement imposed by the cell. Real cellular contractions need not impose the same displacement on every substrate, but the model illustrates why resistance to deformation can change mechanical loading at a contact.

In an experiment comparing cells on two gels, stiffness must be separated from other properties. A stiffer gel may differ from a softer gel in ligand density, surface chemistry or porosity. If more fibronectin is exposed on the stiffer gel, a change in spreading cannot be attributed uniquely to mechanical stiffness. The ideal comparison keeps the accessible adhesive ligand and other relevant conditions as similar as possible while varying the mechanical property of interest. Measuring cell area, adhesion organization and substrate deformation can provide complementary information rather than assuming that a single visible shape represents the entire mechanical response.

Actin filaments interact with myosin motors to generate contractile forces using chemical energy. This is an important energetic distinction: the integrin itself is not a motor that hydrolyzes ATP to pull the cell forward. It participates in a coupling system through which forces generated by actomyosin can be transmitted to the matrix. ATP consumption in actin remodeling, motor activity and kinase reactions contributes to the dynamic behavior of the cell. The energy of extracellular ligand binding and the energy used to sustain intracellular force generation must not be conflated.

An experiment disrupting actin polymerization can reveal the distinction between ligand recognition and focal adhesion organization. Depending on the treatment and time scale, a cell may still have integrins capable of binding their extracellular ligands, yet fail to spread normally or maintain strong traction. If receptor abundance and ligand-binding activity are preserved while actin-dependent mechanical behavior is reduced, the evidence points toward a defect downstream of recognition. However, cytoskeletal changes can also feed back on receptor distribution and activation. One should avoid assuming that an actin perturbation affects only a single step under all conditions.

Focal adhesions also provide locations for biochemical signaling. A receptor-bound region may recruit FAK, Src-family kinases, paxillin and other regulators. The organization of these proteins is coordinated with the physical attachment, so the mechanical and signaling functions cannot be fully separated in a living cell. Nevertheless, distinguishing them analytically is useful. For example, an adaptor mutation may reduce traction more severely than early phosphorylation, whereas a kinase mutation may alter signaling without immediately eliminating every physical bond. Comparing multiple functions can help identify which role of the adhesion complex was affected.

Not every integrin-dependent cell-matrix contact forms a large focal adhesion of the type observed in a fibroblast cultured on a flat surface. Cell type, matrix geometry and physiological setting influence adhesion structure. Cells moving within a three-dimensional tissue may use contacts that differ in size or persistence from those seen on rigid laboratory dishes. The standard focal adhesion model is valuable because it describes a well-established mode of linking integrins to actin and organizing signals. It should not be generalized into a claim that every integrin molecule in every animal tissue occupies the same type of plaque.

11. Hemidesmosomes: stable epithelial attachment to a basement membrane

The basal cells of an epithelium face a special mechanical requirement. They form part of a sheet that must remain attached to an underlying basement membrane while resisting stresses generated by movement of the tissue. A rapidly remodeling actin-linked contact is useful in many settings, but stable anchorage of an epithelial sheet benefits from a linkage to the intermediate-filament system. Keratin intermediate filaments form an extensive network within many epithelial cells and contribute to their mechanical resilience. Hemidesmosomes connect that network to the extracellular basement membrane through a specialized protein assembly.

The name hemidesmosome reflects the appearance of a junction at one basal membrane surface, but a hemidesmosome is not simply one molecular half of a desmosome. A desmosome connects neighboring cells and involves desmosomal cadherins, whereas a hemidesmosome attaches a cell to the extracellular matrix. Both structures are associated with intermediate filaments, which is one reason their distinction can be confusing. The identity of the extracellular partner and the transmembrane receptor resolves the ambiguity. Cadherin-mediated cell-cell adhesion and integrin-mediated cell-matrix attachment are different molecular relationships.

The key integrin in epithelial hemidesmosomes is α6β4. Its extracellular region recognizes laminin in the basement membrane, notably laminin-332 in the familiar skin example. The beta-4 chain has a long cytoplasmic domain that provides binding opportunities for specialized linker proteins. Plectin is an important component connecting the hemidesmosomal complex to keratin intermediate filaments. In many epithelia additional components contribute to the organization and stability of the junction. A student at NET or SET level should be able to trace the essential connection from basement membrane laminin through α6β4 and plectin to keratin.

The geometry of this linkage explains the effect of a defect at different positions. If laminin is absent or structurally unsuitable, an otherwise normal α6β4 receptor may lack a productive extracellular anchor. If α6β4 is absent, the cell cannot use the normal integrin-dependent connection even when the basement membrane remains intact. If the interaction with plectin or the keratin system fails, ligand recognition may remain possible but mechanical continuity with the intracellular filament network is compromised. Each defect can contribute to epithelial fragility, yet the molecular site of failure differs.

Hemidesmosomes are often contrasted with focal adhesions because both involve integrins but connect to different cytoskeletal systems. In the standard focal adhesion example, β1- or β3-containing receptors engage matrix and connect to actin through talin-associated machinery. In the hemidesmosomal example, α6β4 engages laminin and connects to keratin through plectin-associated machinery. The contrast is functional as well as structural. Focal adhesions often participate in dynamic spreading, traction and migration, whereas hemidesmosomes are particularly important for stable epithelial anchorage. This is a typical comparison rather than an assertion that one structure can never remodel or that the other can never be long-lived.

Epithelial repair illustrates why even a stable attachment must be regulated. At the edge of a damaged sheet, cells may need to alter their basal contacts to change shape and migrate. Hemidesmosomal organization can be remodeled as cells change their adhesive behavior. The integrin α6β4 can have signaling roles outside its usual structural context, so receptor identity alone does not prove the presence of a fully assembled hemidesmosome. The observed partners and junctional organization establish what role the receptor is performing in that cell at that time. The specialized stable attachment is not an immutable structure.

An imaging experiment can test the relation among these components. Suppose a section of an epithelial tissue is stained for α6β4, laminin and keratin. A basal concentration of integrin near the laminin-rich basement membrane and the ends of keratin-associated structures would be consistent with hemidesmosomal organization. Colocalization at the resolution of ordinary fluorescence microscopy does not establish direct molecular binding between every stained component. The adaptor relationship can be tested through selective mutations, biochemical interactions or changes in organization when the relevant linker is absent. Structural and functional evidence are complementary.

The contrast with a gap junction is even more direct. A gap junction contains paired channels between neighboring cells and provides a route for selected small molecules or ions. A hemidesmosome connects one cell to matrix and is not a communication channel between two cytoplasms. A focal adhesion likewise does not establish such a pore. In an examination diagram showing a basal epithelial membrane, basement membrane laminin and intracellular keratin, a hemidesmosome is the appropriate interpretation. If the figure instead shows two opposed plasma membranes with aligned channels, the key problem is direct intercellular coupling rather than integrin-mediated matrix attachment.

Tissue integrity often depends on several junction types functioning together. An epithelial cell can use cadherin-based lateral contacts to maintain association with neighboring cells and hemidesmosomal basal contacts to remain attached to its supporting matrix. Weakening the basal attachment may cause separation from the basement membrane even if lateral cell-cell contacts are initially retained. Weakening lateral cadherin-mediated contacts may disrupt sheet organization while some basal attachments remain. Thus the position and plane of tissue failure can provide clues to which structural relationship has been affected, although direct molecular evidence is still needed.

The hemidesmosome exemplifies a central lesson of integrin biology: family membership does not impose a single intracellular architecture. The unusual cytoplasmic domain of β4 permits a specialized connection to intermediate filaments. The same general organization of extracellular recognition and membrane-spanning receptor is therefore adapted to a different cellular mechanical task. A correct comparison should identify the receptor, ligand, intracellular linker, filament system and biological purpose rather than simply memorizing the names of two junctions.

12. Adhesion turnover, migration and tissue organization

A migrating cell must coordinate movement of its internal structures with movement of its body relative to the extracellular environment. The central physical difficulty is that contraction within an isolated object cannot produce a sustained displacement of the whole object in a particular direction unless it can exchange forces with its surroundings. In many animal cells integrin-dependent contacts provide a route for this exchange. The front of a cell can extend a protrusion, establish adhesive contacts, and then use internal forces to advance the cell body relative to the substrate. Existing contacts must be remodeled or released as the rear moves forward.

The protruding edge of a cell is supported by the organization of actin. New actin filaments can contribute to extension of the membrane, while the establishment of adhesive connections allows the cell to stabilize part of the protrusion. If a protrusion fails to attach effectively, it may retract without contributing substantially to net movement. On a suitable matrix, newly formed adhesions can couple internal movement of actin to the substrate. This connection is often described in terms of a molecular clutch. The analogy emphasizes engagement and disengagement of a force-transmitting linkage, rather than claiming that a cell contains a rigid mechanical clutch identical to that in a machine.

The migration cycle includes events occurring in different regions of the same cell. At the front, protrusion and new adhesion may be prominent. More centrally, contractile forces can help move the cell body. At the rear, disassembly or release of contacts permits advancement. These events can overlap in time and their precise organization varies among cells. A receptor system that is uniformly activated across the entire cell may therefore have a different consequence from one whose activity is regulated locally. The location of adhesion is important because traction must be coordinated with a direction of movement.

An increase in the strength of adhesion need not produce a proportional increase in migration speed. When adhesion is too weak, the cell cannot efficiently transmit force to the substrate. When adhesion is excessively stable, the cell may have difficulty releasing its rear or remodeling its contacts. Intermediate levels of attachment can support more effective movement in some experimental systems. The relationship between adhesion strength and migration speed can therefore be nonmonotonic. This is a conditional biological pattern, not a universal curve for every cell type and matrix. The shape of the relationship depends on cell mechanics, receptor expression, substrate and the migration mode being studied.

To see how this distinction could be tested, imagine a series of surfaces with increasing accessible fibronectin density. The number of cells that remain attached after a brief wash may increase with density. The average distance traveled over a longer interval might initially increase but later decline if adhesions become difficult to release. There would be no contradiction between these two observations because attachment and movement are different outcomes. Before attributing the pattern to integrin-dependent turnover, one would need controls for cell viability, matrix presentation and other factors that could change with the preparation of the surfaces.

A cell moving in a three-dimensional tissue encounters geometry different from that of a flat culture dish. Adhesions may be distributed around the cell rather than concentrated at a flat basal surface, and the physical constraints of surrounding material can influence the way forces are generated and transmitted. Some modes of cell migration use weaker or less persistent integrin-dependent anchorage, and some movement in confined environments can occur with limited integrin involvement. Consequently it is not accurate to claim that every form of animal cell motility requires large focal adhesions. The textbook focal adhesion model explains a major mode of movement while allowing alternative strategies in different environments.

Chemotaxis and haptotaxis illustrate two sources of directional information. Chemotaxis involves a response to a gradient of a soluble chemical, while haptotaxis refers to directional movement influenced by spatial variation in substrate-associated cues. A migrating cell may encounter a matrix in which adhesive recognition sites are more accessible in one region than another. Integrins can contribute to the perception and use of this difference, together with downstream cytoskeletal regulation. A gradient of ligand density does not guarantee movement toward the region of greatest density, since very strong attachment or other directional signals may alter the outcome. The observed behavior must be interpreted in its experimental context.

Adhesion also participates in organization of the matrix itself. A fibroblast can use integrin-dependent contacts to exert force on fibronectin and other matrix-associated structures. The pulling forces can influence the organization of extracellular material, affecting the environment encountered by the same cell and its neighbors. This creates a reciprocal relationship: matrix properties influence cell behavior, and cellular activities modify matrix organization. A static diagram showing a fixed extracellular ligand is useful for explaining recognition but does not capture this longer-term interaction between cells and tissues.

The ability to regulate cell-matrix adhesion is important during normal development and wound repair. Cells must sometimes move relative to an extracellular scaffold and later establish more stable associations. A defect that prevents movement can impair formation or restoration of a tissue even if individual cells remain metabolically active. Conversely, excessive release of contacts may impair maintenance of tissue architecture. Detailed developmental pathways and malignant invasion belong to their respective curriculum chapters, but the underlying adhesion principles apply to both. The relevant question at this stage is how receptor specificity, activation and mechanical coupling affect the capacity of a cell to move or remain anchored.

Palecek and colleagues (1997) compared migration in systems where ligand level, integrin expression or binding affinity was varied. Their work linked changes in migration speed to changes in short-term cell-substrate adhesiveness, rather than treating each manipulation as an unrelated phenomenon. This provides experimental support for considering an intermediate adhesion regime in the relevant system. It does not justify assigning a universal optimum to all cells, substrates or migration modes. 13. Anchorage-dependent survival and physiological integration

Many normal animal cells receive signals from their surroundings that help maintain survival and appropriate growth. An epithelial cell in a tissue is not simply supplied with nutrients; it is also held in a particular structural context by contacts with neighbors and the extracellular matrix. If the cell detaches from that context, it may lose an important set of regulatory inputs. The cell's response to detachment can therefore be more complex than a temporary change of shape. In some anchorage-dependent cells, loss of appropriate adhesion can initiate a form of programmed cell death known as anoikis.

Anoikis is not synonymous with death due to nutrient deprivation, although both conditions can eventually affect cell viability. In a controlled example, cells in an adhesive culture and cells kept in suspension may receive the same soluble nutrients and growth factors. If the suspended population has reduced survival, the loss of anchorage-associated regulation is a potential explanation. To attribute the change specifically to integrin-dependent signaling, one must examine the relevant receptor interactions and consider other differences between the conditions, including cell-cell contact and the physical effects of suspension. Not every suspended cell dies by anoikis; circulating blood cells are an obvious physiological example of cells adapted to survival without continuous matrix anchorage.

Integrin-associated pathways may cooperate with soluble growth-factor pathways to sustain appropriate intracellular signals. For example, a cell may require both matrix-dependent signaling and a growth factor to maintain a survival-promoting pathway. The two signals can act in parallel or influence one another at shared downstream components. If either input is removed, the combined output may be inadequate. This type of integration explains why observing an activated growth-factor receptor does not automatically establish that a detached cell will survive. A cellular response depends on the entire regulatory state rather than on the presence of one activated protein.

A simple factorial experiment can reveal the interaction. Suppose an anchorage-dependent cell population is tested under four conditions: adhesive matrix with growth factor, adhesive matrix without growth factor, nonadhesive surface with growth factor, and nonadhesive surface without growth factor. If high survival occurs only when both matrix and growth factor are present, the observations suggest that the two inputs cooperate under those conditions. If growth factor alone restores survival, a different relationship is indicated. Such data do not by themselves show that two pathways act directly on the same protein. Additional measurements of phosphorylation, cell death markers and receptor-specific perturbations would be needed to locate the point of convergence.

A decrease in cell number after detachment should not automatically be interpreted as increased programmed cell death. The cell population might instead divide less frequently, detach during handling or become difficult to recover for counting. A suitable assay distinguishes viability and death from proliferation and retention. For example, a separate measure of a cell-death-associated process combined with an initial cell count and an estimate of proliferation helps identify why the population changed. The biological significance of a survival pathway is therefore linked to the reliability of the measurements used to infer survival.

Platelet aggregation provides a complementary example in which adhesion must be rapidly induced rather than continuously maintained. Activated platelets can bind fibrinogen through αIIbβ3 and create connections with other platelets. Following initial aggregation, ligand engagement can contribute outside-in signals involved in stabilization and cytoskeletal responses. This demonstrates that a physiological adhesion event can involve first a change in ligand-binding competence and then a response to the newly formed contact. A defect in the receptor itself and a defect in the intracellular activation machinery may both impair aggregation but can produce different results in receptor-expression and binding assays.

Glanzmann thrombasthenia is a classical biological illustration of impaired platelet aggregation associated with deficient or dysfunctional αIIbβ3. The purpose of considering this condition is not to study clinical treatment but to connect molecular recognition with a physiological function. If platelets cannot establish effective fibrinogen-dependent bridges, the process of aggregation is compromised. Other aspects of platelet behavior may still occur, illustrating that defects in one receptor need not eliminate every response of the cell. When an examination question describes normal platelet number but defective aggregation, it is important to consider receptor function rather than assuming that the only possible problem is cell production.

Leukocyte adhesion provides a second example of rapid regulation under physiological conditions. Selectins help mediate rolling contacts in many vascular settings, as discussed in the earlier chapter on cell adhesion molecules. Local signaling can then promote activation of leukocyte integrins. Binding of an activated integrin such as αLβ2 to an appropriate Ig-superfamily partner can contribute to firmer attachment. The sequence does not imply that selectins are physically converted into integrins. Instead, different receptor families contribute distinct functions that cooperate in the behavior of the whole cell.

A defect in the common β2 subunit can impair several leukocyte integrins rather than only one alpha-beta combination. Leukocyte adhesion deficiency type I illustrates the consequences of deficient β2 integrin expression or function. In a simplified examination example, rolling contacts might remain relatively preserved while efficient firm arrest is impaired. The prediction follows from the division of roles between receptor families, although the precise behavior in a particular tissue depends on the adhesion molecules and signals present there. A selective loss of one adhesion step is more informative than a statement that all intercellular communication has failed.

Different tissues employ integrins for different purposes while retaining the same broad molecular architecture. A fibroblast may emphasize actin-linked traction and matrix organization. An epithelial cell may employ α6β4-dependent stable anchorage to a basement membrane. A platelet uses regulated ligand binding to participate in multicellular aggregation, and a leukocyte adjusts attachment to cell-surface partners during movement between compartments. In each example the relationship between extracellular partner and intracellular regulation is suited to the physiological task. Learning these examples as variations on a shared mechanism is more useful than memorizing unrelated lists of receptor names.

Frisch and Francis (1994) reported apoptosis following disruption of matrix interactions in normal epithelial cells under their experimental conditions, and showed that sensitivity depended on the cellular state. Their findings helped establish the concept of anoikis. Separately, a 1993 study of neutrophils from patients with distinct leukocyte adhesion deficiencies used intravital microscopy to distinguish initial rolling from later attachment and emigration. In the CD18-deficient case, rolling was preserved while stimulated firm adhesion and emigration failed. The observations support the distinction between selectin-dependent initial contacts and integrin-dependent arrest in the tested system; they are not a claim that all leukocyte trafficking is mediated by a single receptor.

14. Comparative biology, perturbation analysis and experimental reasoning

Integrin-mediated adhesion is a prominent feature of animal cells, whose extracellular matrices support tissue organization in the absence of the rigid cell walls characteristic of many other organisms. Related integrin machinery is evolutionarily ancient, and the presence of components in some unicellular relatives of animals cautions against treating complex animal tissues as the only possible evolutionary origin of the system. The familiar mammalian receptor repertoire is a particular elaboration of a broader adhesion strategy. Invertebrates such as Drosophila use a smaller repertoire of integrins that nevertheless contributes to tissue attachment and development. A conserved physical principle can therefore operate with different numbers of receptor subtypes and distinct ligand preferences.

Plants and bacteria provide a useful functional contrast. Plant cells are commonly enclosed in cellulose-containing walls and interact with their surroundings through mechanisms adapted to that architecture. They do not use the canonical animal αβ integrin family as their standard matrix-adhesion system. Bacteria can use adhesins, pili and extracellular polymeric material for attachment, but these are not automatically integrins simply because they perform an adhesive function. Functional analogy should be distinguished from molecular homology. Similar biological problems can be solved by molecular systems with different evolutionary histories and structures.

Experimental analysis of an integrin system benefits from ordering possible failures along a causal chain. Receptor protein must be produced and delivered to the cell surface. It must be able to adopt suitable configurations, recognize its ligand and engage the appropriate cytoplasmic partners. The cell must then be able to organize mechanical and signaling responses. A defect at an early stage can propagate to every later stage. A defect at a later stage can leave earlier functions intact. This asymmetry explains why a normal downstream response can be informative about an upstream step, whereas a missing downstream response rarely identifies a unique upstream cause without further experiments.

Surface abundance can be measured with an antibody chosen to recognize a suitable extracellular epitope independently of the activation state. Total cellular protein can be assessed separately. If total receptor protein is abundant but little is detected at the surface, impaired folding, assembly, trafficking or retention are possibilities. If both amounts are normal, altered function of the surface population should be considered. The comparison becomes particularly informative when a receptor-specific mutation is known and a matched wild-type receptor is used as a control. A single Western blot measuring whole-cell protein cannot establish that the receptor is present in its functional membrane compartment.

A soluble ligand-binding assay can test a different stage. With controlled ligand concentration and appropriate specificity controls, it may reveal an altered ability of the surface receptor to engage ligand. Multivalent ligand and clustering can influence the result, so a purified monovalent system may be more suitable when the question concerns intrinsic affinity. Flow-based measurement of fluorescent ligand binding can provide a population-level readout, but a change in fluorescence could reflect several variables, including receptor number and cell size. Normalization and an independent measurement of abundance improve interpretation.

Cell-spreading and adhesion assays extend the analysis to whole-cell behavior. Cells can be placed on a defined matrix and the fraction retained after a controlled wash measured. Microscopy can reveal whether the remaining cells spread or organize actin-associated contacts. If a mutant population binds soluble ligand normally but remains rounded and poorly resistant to washing, a defect in receptor-cytoskeleton coupling or downstream regulation is plausible. However, one should avoid assuming that rounded cells are necessarily dead or that a spreading defect always proves disruption of actin. Separate measurements of viability and cytoskeletal organization are valuable.

A rescue experiment tests whether replacing a missing component or bypassing a particular defect can restore the measured function. Suppose removal of an integrin alpha chain reduces adhesion to collagen. Reintroducing a wild-type copy that restores surface receptor and adhesion supports a causal role for the missing chain. Reintroducing a copy with a defective recognition domain may fail to restore attachment even when expression is recovered. An expression control is necessary because failure of rescue could result simply from poor production or delivery of the replacement receptor. The comparison tests function more specifically when the two constructs reach the surface in comparable amounts.

One must also consider compensation. A cell may possess several receptors recognizing related extracellular ligands. Deletion of one receptor can have little effect on a broad attachment assay if another receptor provides sufficient recognition. That observation does not prove the deleted receptor has no physiological role. The effect might be more evident on a different ligand, at a lower ligand density or under a particular mechanical challenge. Conversely, loss of a common beta subunit may have extensive consequences because several heterodimers depend on it. Comparing single and combined perturbations can reveal overlapping functions, but broad damage to cell health must still be excluded.

The design of engineered adhesive surfaces illustrates a practical application of ligand recognition. A material that presents an appropriate integrin-binding sequence can encourage attachment of cells carrying compatible receptors. The sequence alone is insufficient to predict every outcome; ligand density, orientation, accessibility, substrate stiffness and cell identity also matter. A surface intended to support stable attachment may be designed differently from one intended to support cell migration. The underlying principles of receptor specificity and mechanical coupling are therefore directly relevant to biotechnology without requiring specialist fabrication protocols. The same reasoning is useful when evaluating why cells grow differently on two culture substrates.

Finally, the central examination strategy is to identify the level of organization represented by each observation. A binding curve concerns molecular recognition under defined conditions. A conformation-sensitive probe reports accessibility of a particular structural feature. An actin image provides evidence about cellular organization. A phosphorylation measurement reports a biochemical event, and a tissue separation or migration defect reports an integrated physiological outcome. By connecting these levels in causal order, one can make specific predictions without claiming more than the data establish. This is also the appropriate bridge to the following chapter on gap junctions: both integrins and junctional channels participate in communication, but their molecular structures, transmitted information and functional readouts are fundamentally different.

Worked numerical examples

Occupancy at equal ligand concentration

A monovalent ligand is present at free L = 30 nM. Compare Kd = 90 nM and Kd = 10 nM.

Solution. θ = L/(Kd + L). The first state has 30/120 = 0.25; the second has 30/40 = 0.75. A threefold increase in fractional occupancy is compatible with unchanged receptor number. Assumes independent sites, equilibrium and negligible ligand depletion.

Binding capacity

Two cell populations have 2,000 and 4,000 equivalent surface sites per cell, with Kd = 20 nM. At L = 20 nM, calculate bound sites.

Solution. Each has θ = 0.5, giving 1,000 and 2,000 sites occupied. The apparent absolute binding differs because capacity differs, not because intrinsic affinity differs. Assumes identical receptor behavior.

Kinetic difference

Two preparations have kon = 10⁶ M⁻¹ s⁻¹. Their koff values are 0.1 and 0.01 s⁻¹.

Solution. Kd = koff/kon gives 10⁻⁷ M (100 nM) and 10⁻⁸ M (10 nM). The half-lives are ln 2/0.1 = 6.93 s and ln 2/0.01 = 69.3 s. No force or rebinding is included.

Free energy comparison

At 298 K, Kd decreases tenfold for the same ligand. Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Solution. ΔΔG° = RT ln(0.1) = −5,705 J mol⁻¹, approximately −5.71 kJ mol⁻¹. More favorable equilibrium binding does not imply that integrin hydrolyzes ATP.

Force on an elastic support

A local effective spring stiffness of 0.3 pN nm⁻¹ and a displacement of 50 nm are given.

Solution. F = kx = 15 pN. Doubling displacement in the linear regime doubles force. This model does not describe a whole cell's active adaptation to stiffness.

Basal detachment with retained lateral contacts points toward a basal anchorage system. It does not uniquely prove the defect without receptor and linker measurements.

A researcher measures total β1 by immunoblot and finds normal amounts, but surface β1 signal is very low. Which explanation should be tested first?

  1. A pure extracellular Kd change is already proved
  2. Impaired assembly, delivery or retention at the cell surface
  3. An increase in surface receptor number
  4. Normal total protein establishes normal receptor localization
Answer and explanation

B. Impaired assembly, delivery or retention at the cell surface

Whole-cell abundance does not specify surface availability. Trafficking or assembly is a plausible early defect; direct localization studies are needed.

Question 38

Soluble RGD blocks adhesion to a fibronectin-coated surface. Which conclusion is strongest?

  1. Only α5β1 can be involved
  2. An RGD-sensitive interaction contributes; the exact receptor still needs identification
  3. All integrins bind RGD
  4. Fibronectin is absent from the surface
Answer and explanation

B. An RGD-sensitive interaction contributes; the exact receptor still needs identification

Several integrins recognize RGD-containing ligands. Competition establishes sensitivity but cannot uniquely identify the receptor without receptor-specific evidence.

Question 39

In a four-condition survival assay, viable fraction is high only with both adhesion and growth factor. What is supported?

  1. Growth factor alone is sufficient for the observed high survival
  2. Matrix adhesion alone is sufficient for the observed high survival
  3. The two inputs cooperate under these conditions
  4. The assay proves direct binding of the growth factor receptor to the integrin
Answer and explanation

C. The two inputs cooperate under these conditions

High survival only in the joint condition supports cooperative requirements for this cell system. It does not locate the molecular point of convergence or establish direct receptor interaction.

Question 40

An α2 chain knockout reduces attachment to native collagen but not fibronectin. Reintroduction of wild-type α2 restores collagen attachment. What does this support?

  1. Every matrix receptor requires α2
  2. The collagen assay proves direct kinase activity by α2
  3. Fibronectin is recognized only by α2β1
  4. A specific role of α2-containing receptors in collagen attachment
Answer and explanation

D. A specific role of α2-containing receptors in collagen attachment

Selective loss and rescue support the causal receptor contribution. The unaffected fibronectin response argues against a generalized cell-viability defect.

Question 41

In a hypothetical migration assay, increasing ligand density increases wash-resistant attachment monotonically, but migration speed peaks at intermediate density. Which interpretation fits?

  1. The data are impossible because movement must increase with binding
  2. Receptors must be degraded at every density
  3. Traction and rear release impose different requirements
  4. Migration speed is a direct equilibrium Kd measurement
Answer and explanation

C. Traction and rear release impose different requirements

Adhesion can support traction yet excessive persistence can restrict release. Attachment and migration measure different integrated cellular functions.

Question 42

A receptor conformation antibody reports increased binding but a ligand-binding assay is unchanged. What is the best interpretation?

  1. The increased antibody signal proves increased ligand occupancy
  2. The ligand-binding result excludes every conformational change
  3. The results establish increased receptor synthesis
  4. The antibody may report a structural transition not sufficient for higher ligand affinity
Answer and explanation

D. The antibody may report a structural transition not sufficient for higher ligand affinity

A conformation-sensitive antibody reports a specific epitope. Ligand binding, expression and conformational transitions are distinct measurements, so further probes are needed.

Question 43

A receptor variant has unchanged extracellular recognition and surface abundance but fails to recruit a particular scaffold after ligand attachment. What is the most direct affected stage?

  1. Biosynthesis of all matrix proteins
  2. Intrinsic extracellular ligand specificity
  3. Direct cytoplasmic coupling through gap junctions
  4. Assembly of the intracellular outside-in signaling complex
Answer and explanation

D. Assembly of the intracellular outside-in signaling complex

The initial recognition stage is preserved. The measured failure is the recruitment of a downstream intracellular component after engagement.

Question 44

A single α-chain deletion causes little change in adhesion to mixed collagen and fibronectin. Which experiment best tests compensation?

  1. Conclude the deleted receptor has no function
  2. Measure only total matrix mass
  3. Compare defined single-ligand surfaces and targeted disruption of alternative receptors
  4. Remove all nutrients and repeat
Answer and explanation

C. Compare defined single-ligand surfaces and targeted disruption of alternative receptors

A mixed substrate may permit alternative contacts. Separating ligands and receptors can expose overlapping adhesive functions while controlling cell health.

Question 47

Assertion: A left shift in an ideal normalized equilibrium binding curve can occur without more receptors. Reason: A reduction in Kd increases occupancy at the same nonsaturating ligand concentration.

B. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion.

From θ = L/(Kd+L), reducing Kd increases fractional occupancy without changing receptor number.

Assertion: Gap junctions and focal adhesions are equivalent because both connect two cytoplasms. Reason: Gap junctions can permit direct passage of selected small molecules between cells.

The reason is a property of gap junctions, not focal adhesions. Focal adhesions couple a cell to matrix rather than connecting cytoplasms.

Question 52

In the occupancy graph, the solid curve has Kd = 10 nM and the dashed curve has Kd = 100 nM. What happens as free ligand becomes very large?

  2. The dashed curve becomes zero
  3. Both fractional occupancies approach one
  4. Both curves remain at 0.5

C. Both fractional occupancies approach one

The simple independent-site model approaches saturation when L greatly exceeds Kd. The curves differ most at intermediate concentrations.

Question 53

Which sequence best orders a standard stimulated platelet adhesion event?

C. Upstream activation → cytoplasmic integrin regulation → fibrinogen engagement → outside-in stabilization

The standard sequence contains both integrin signaling directions. The other alternatives introduce unrelated structures or impossible enzyme locations.

Question 54

A matched molecular binding comparison isolates ligand chemistry more directly. The other tests are indirect or introduce new variables.

Question 55

  4. A genetic knockout alone proves the direct binding mechanism

Sufficiency and necessity are logically distinct and context-dependent. Living cells add spatial, mechanical and regulatory requirements.

Question 56

Which experimental finding most directly supports the prediction that a matrix-recognition mutation affects binding rather than receptor expression?

  2. Normal surface abundance, reduced specific ligand binding and failure of the recognition-defective construct to rescue under matched expression

B. Normal surface abundance, reduced specific ligand binding and failure of the recognition-defective construct to rescue under matched expression

The combination separates abundance from ligand-binding competence and provides a controlled functional comparison. Morphology or viability alone cannot localize the defect.

Misconceptions and examination traps

All integrins are cell–matrix receptors

Correction: Cell-surface Ig-family ligands such as ICAM-1 can also bind integrins. Why the error occurs: The family is introduced using matrix examples. Exam application: A question can pair αLβ2 with ICAM-1.

All integrins recognize RGD

Correction: Only a subset does; collagen, laminin and Ig-family recognition use distinct surfaces. Why the error occurs: RGD is a memorable historical motif. Exam application: Ask whether RGD competition identifies one receptor.

All alpha/beta pairings exist

Correction: Only compatible established heterodimers are found. Why the error occurs: Multiplication is mistaken for biological assembly. Exam application: Distinguish 144 possible combinations from 24 established human receptors.

Integrins are intrinsic kinases

Correction: They recruit associated kinases such as FAK and Src. Why the error occurs: Adhesion causes phosphorylation. Exam application: Identify the actual catalytic component.

Calcium activates every integrin

Correction: Cation effects are receptor- and condition-dependent. Why the error occurs: Cadherin calcium dependence is overgeneralized. Exam application: Interpret a cation-specific assay.

EDTA identifies integrin adhesion uniquely

Correction: Chelation also affects other systems. Why the error occurs: Chelation is assumed receptor-specific. Exam application: Compare cadherin and integrin attachment.

Extended means fully open

Correction: Extension and headpiece opening differ. Why the error occurs: Two-state cartoons omit intermediate configurations. Exam application: Interpret a conformation-sensitive probe.

More adhesion always means higher affinity

Correction: Avidity, abundance and mechanics can alter cellular adhesion. Why the error occurs: Whole-cell attachment is treated as molecular binding. Exam application: Compare normalized binding with retention.

Inside-out means original ligand must be intracellular

Correction: It describes the direction of regulation through the integrin. Why the error occurs: The stimulus and receptor-side event are conflated. Exam application: Classify chemokine-induced activation.

Outside-in requires integrin to phosphorylate itself

Correction: Associated kinases mediate phosphorylation. Why the error occurs: Receptor tyrosine kinase analogy is overextended. Exam application: Predict selective FAK inhibition.

All integrins link only to actin

Correction: α6β4 links to keratin through specialized machinery. Why the error occurs: Focal adhesion is treated as universal. Exam application: Identify the hemidesmosome exception.

Hemidesmosome is half a desmosome molecularly

Correction: Their receptors and external partners differ. Why the error occurs: The names suggest identity. Exam application: Distinguish cell–cell from cell–matrix junctions.

Focal adhesion is a channel

Correction: It is an adhesive assembly, not a pore between cells. Why the error occurs: Both structures are junctions. Exam application: Compare with gap junctions.

Maximum adhesion always maximizes migration

Correction: Release is needed as well as traction. Why the error occurs: Attachment is confused with translocation. Exam application: Interpret a peaked migration curve.

All detached cells undergo anoikis

Correction: Sensitivity depends on cell type and context. Why the error occurs: Epithelial examples are generalized. Exam application: Compare normal blood cells with anchorage-dependent epithelia.

Total receptor abundance proves surface function

Correction: Localization and activation require separate measurements. Why the error occurs: Immunoblot quantity is treated as activity. Exam application: Predict a trafficking defect.

Colocalization proves direct binding

Correction: It indicates spatial proximity within resolution limits. Why the error occurs: Overlapping fluorescence is mistaken for molecular interaction. Exam application: Require biochemical or perturbation evidence.

Equal Kd means equal kinetics

Correction: Different kon and koff can yield the same ratio. Why the error occurs: Equilibrium is confused with rates. Exam application: Compare bond lifetimes for equal Kd.

Revision and mastery

Integrins coordinate ligand recognition, structural anchorage and signaling. Receptor identity determines potential partners; extracellular ions and conformational equilibria influence binding; intracellular adaptors organize activation and mechanical linkage. Outside-in signaling communicates engagement to internal regulators. The biological outcome depends on cell type, ligand presentation, cytoskeletal coupling and other signals.

Essential relations

Kd = koff/kon; θ = L/(Kd + L); t½ = ln 2/koff; ΔG° = RT ln(Kd/C°); F = kx for a linear spring. Know the assumptions and units before applying any relation.
