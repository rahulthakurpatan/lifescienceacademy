1. Why a gene need not resemble its final message

Imagine comparing a DNA sequence that contains the information for a protein with the messenger RNA isolated from cells making that protein. It seems reasonable to expect the entire messenger to match one continuous stretch of DNA. That expectation works for many bacterial protein-coding genes. Yet many nuclear genes in animals and plants fail this test: portions of the DNA match the mature RNA, but intervening stretches of DNA have no counterpart in that particular RNA molecule. The DNA itself remains intact. What has changed is the arrangement of information in the RNA product.

This chapter examines that structural relationship. The preceding study of operons explains how several bacterial coding regions may share one transcription unit. An interrupted gene poses a different organizational question: how can the sequence specifying one functional product be distributed across separate intervals of a single transcription unit? The distinction between the organization of genes and the organization of their products will be important throughout genetics and molecular biology.

A gene is a genomic region whose expression gives rise to one or more functional RNA or protein products. A locus is its genomic position. A transcription unit is the interval copied into an RNA from a defined initiation region to a downstream endpoint. These terms overlap but are not interchangeable. A transcription unit can contain several bacterial genes; a eukaryotic gene may support more than one transcript with different boundaries. Therefore a gene cannot always be described by a single pair of RNA coordinates. Regulatory sequences associated with a gene may lie outside a particular transcribed interval.

The elementary prerequisites are DNA strand polarity, complementary base pairing and the distinction between transcription and translation. Transcription uses a DNA strand as a template to synthesize RNA; translation uses nucleotide triplets in a suitable RNA reading frame to specify amino acids. RNA is not simply a smaller chromosome. It is a separately synthesized molecule whose sequence is related to the DNA template and may subsequently be processed. This distinction resolves the apparent paradox of an interrupted gene without requiring any rearrangement of chromosomal DNA.

By the end of the chapter, a learner should be able to reconstruct a DNA–primary RNA–mature RNA map, identify regulatory and transcribed landmarks, distinguish an exon from a coding segment, interpret hybridization and sequence data, calculate lengths and coding capacity, and predict the structural consequences of alternative RNA arrangements or changes at a boundary. The detailed catalytic mechanism of RNA splicing is a separate subject; here the important objective is understanding the architecture on which that mechanism acts.

2. Continuous and interrupted gene arrangements

Begin with a simple continuous protein-coding gene. Its coding nucleotides occur in one uninterrupted stretch of the DNA strand used as a sequence reference. Its mature RNA may contain additional untranslated nucleotides at either end, but the part corresponding to the coding region does not need internal sequence removal. Many bacterial genes have this general architecture. It does not imply that the RNA is never processed or that promoter and terminator sequences are absent.

Now consider a gene with three segments that contribute to a mature RNA, separated by two intervening segments. A transcription event can copy the three segments and both intervening regions into a single precursor. During RNA processing the intervening portions are removed from that RNA and the retained portions become adjacent. The retained intervals are called exons and the removed intervening intervals are called introns, with reference to a particular transcript. The term split gene or interrupted gene describes the discontinuous genomic arrangement of the sequences retained in the processed product.

The difference is best visualized in terms of sequence continuity, rather than in terms of whether the chromosome is physically broken. The DNA is a continuous double helix through the exons and introns. Likewise a primary transcript is initially synthesized as a continuous RNA chain, even though some portions can be removed before transcription finishes. The mature molecule has a different adjacency relationship between nucleotides. For example, the final nucleotide of the first exon can become the immediate neighbor of the first nucleotide of the second exon, even if they are thousands of base pairs apart in the genome.

An intronless gene has no intron in the transcript under consideration. It may still possess a 5′ untranslated region, coding sequence, 3′ untranslated region and regulatory elements. Conversely an intron can be located entirely within untranslated sequence, so a gene with an uninterrupted protein-coding region can have an interrupted transcript structure. State precisely whether a question concerns the continuity of the coding sequence or the presence of introns anywhere in the transcript.

The simple alternating exon–intron model also has a useful counting rule. A single linear transcript with three distinct retained exon blocks and no unusual processing topology contains two internal intervening gaps. More generally, n consecutive exon blocks are separated by n − 1 introns in that transcript model. This relationship concerns a selected RNA arrangement with distinct, nonoverlapping blocks separated by positive-length gaps; one should not count all overlapping exon annotations at a genomic locus and automatically apply the rule. Alternative first and last exons can change the relevant transcription span itself.

As a complementary comparison, a bacterial operon with three structural genes may contain three translation start and stop pairs within one polycistronic RNA. A nuclear gene with three exons and two introns can instead have just one main translation start and stop pair. Neither the number of exon blocks nor the number of transcription units determines the number of protein-coding genes. For a map with several boxes, inspect the labels and determine whether each box represents a separate coding unit, a retained transcript interval or simply an annotation of a regulatory element.

3. Read a gene map in the correct direction

DNA strands are antiparallel: if one runs from its 5′ end to its 3′ end, the complementary strand runs in the opposite direction. For any particular transcript, RNA polymerase reads the template DNA in the 3′ to 5′ direction while making the RNA 5′ to 3′. The other DNA strand is often called the coding or non-template strand because, apart from thymine in DNA and uracil in RNA, its sequence matches the corresponding unprocessed RNA. For noncoding RNA genes, non-template or sense strand is the more general term.

A published gene diagram often draws just one horizontal line. This is a simplification of a double-stranded molecule, not an indication that the gene consists of single-stranded DNA. The diagram may represent the non-template strand, or it may represent genomic coordinates increasing from left to right. These conventions are not equivalent when a gene lies on the reverse strand. The arrow defining transcription direction is therefore more reliable than the page orientation.

Upstream means toward the 5′ side of the transcript-oriented reference; downstream means toward its 3′ side. For a reverse-strand gene, upstream may correspond to a larger numerical genomic coordinate. Numbering from the transcription start site often assigns +1 to the first transcribed nucleotide and negative numbers to positions upstream. In conventional relative numbering there is no zero position. Genomic coordinates instead refer to positions on a named reference sequence. A student must first determine which convention the problem uses.

Consider a reverse-strand transcript whose first retained interval occupies genomic positions 901–1000 and second retained interval occupies 601–700, with the intervening segment occupying 701–900. In transcript order the first exon is the higher-coordinate interval. Its RNA starts opposite reference position 1000 and proceeds toward 901, then the next retained interval begins opposite 700. The RNA is still synthesized 5′ to 3′; only the numerical direction on the reference chromosome has reversed. A diagram that labels the low-coordinate block exon 1 solely because it is on the left would be incorrect.

Sequence comparisons require the same care. If a coding-strand DNA segment is written 5′-ATG GCT TAA-3′, its RNA counterpart is 5′-AUG GCU UAA-3′. Its complementary template is 3′-TAC CGA ATT-5′. When a question supplies the template strand in the opposite written direction, first reverse the order and then apply complementary base pairing, or equivalently compute its reverse complement. Exon joining follows the order of the transcript, not an arbitrary alphabetical or coordinate ordering.

Direction matters when examining landmarks at both ends of a gene. A promoter is generally located at or near the initiation region upstream in transcriptional orientation; it does not have to be located to the left of an arrow drawn on a reference genome. Similarly the 3′ RNA end corresponds to the downstream side even when the coordinate numbers decrease. The following chapters on transcription and chromosome architecture use the same directional vocabulary.

4. How RNA–DNA hybrids revealed split genes

The expectation of sequence continuity was tested particularly clearly in studies of adenovirus type 2, a DNA virus that uses the gene expression machinery of its host cell. Adenovirus provided a relatively tractable genome and abundant viral RNAs. In 1977, Susan Berget, Claire Moore and Phillip Sharp investigated the relationship of late viral messenger RNA to the viral genome. Independently, work by Louise Chow and colleagues associated with Richard Roberts established unusual arrangements of adenovirus messenger sequences. The primary reports showed that a single mature messenger included sequences mapping to separated positions on viral DNA. The 1993 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine recognized Roberts and Sharp for the discovery of split genes.

The underlying experimental principle is complementarity. Single-stranded RNA can pair with a complementary DNA strand. Under appropriate conditions it can also invade a duplex DNA region, forming an RNA–DNA hybrid and displacing the other DNA strand. This three-stranded arrangement is an R-loop. Electron microscopy can reveal the paths of the paired and unpaired nucleic acid strands. If a continuous RNA sequence matches consecutive DNA positions, there is no need to invoke intervening DNA intervals to explain the alignment.

Suppose instead that the RNA matches DNA regions A, B and C while DNA intervals X and Y lie between them. A single RNA molecule can align with all three matching regions, but the intervening DNA has no complementary RNA counterpart in that molecule. The RNA remains continuous at its exon junctions; the DNA takes a longer path at each junction. With a suitable hybrid preparation, the extra DNA is visible as loops or intervening unpaired regions. In a different preparation, RNA ends lacking complementarity to the DNA fragment used may remain unpaired. These two observations must not be confused: an unpaired RNA tail can signal that the corresponding DNA is outside the fragment, whereas a DNA loop within an alignment can indicate a genomic interval absent from the RNA.

The historic hexon messenger study is a useful concrete example. The body of the purified viral RNA mapped to one region of the viral genome, while short sequences at its 5′ end corresponded to separated upstream positions on the same strand. This was not what a simple model of an uninterrupted RNA copied from one short local interval predicted. Combining hybrid mapping with the known genomic fragments allowed the investigators to conclude that separate genomic sequence blocks contributed to a single mature RNA. A separate 1977 paper by Chow, Gelinas, Broker and Richard Roberts described the discontinuous 5′ terminal sequences of several late adenovirus messengers. These independent results support the same organizational principle.

The discovery did not by itself reveal every chemical reaction responsible for RNA splicing. It established a structural mismatch between DNA and processed messenger and supported the existence of precursor processing. The original work used particular viral RNA preparations and mapping conditions; the general conclusion was subsequently strengthened by observations in cellular genes, including globin genes, and by direct comparison of genomic and complementary DNA sequences.

For reasoning about an experimental diagram, distinguish the material used from the inferred result. A purified mature RNA should give a different alignment pattern from a corresponding intron-containing precursor; an RNA from an unrelated gene should not produce the same specific alignment under stringent conditions. A continuous gene of known structure provides a useful conceptual comparison. These are examples of controls for understanding the method, not a claim that all such controls were performed in exactly this form in the historical experiments. A noncomplementary segment could also arise through sequence variation, a wrongly chosen genomic fragment or a technical artifact. Confirmation by independent sequence mapping reduces ambiguity.

The evidence thus changes the conceptual model of a gene: its functional RNA can be assembled from discontinuous genomic intervals without physically excising those intervals from the chromosome. The crucial distinction between a genomic DNA loop and an excised RNA intron will recur in later chapters. A loop in a hybrid map is a structural consequence of the experimental pairing, not a direct image of the biochemical splicing intermediate.

5. Promoters, transcription boundaries and gene landmarks

A typical nuclear protein-coding gene has several landmarks that refer to distinct biological events. The promoter is a DNA region involved in selecting where transcription can begin and recruiting the relevant transcription machinery. Promoters differ in their organization. Some have a recognizable TATA element and many do not. Some support a narrow initiation site and others a broader region of starts. Promoter elements can overlap the initiation region, so it is inaccurate to define every promoter as a stretch that is entirely upstream and untranscribed. For elementary diagrams the promoter is usually shown to the upstream side of the transcription start site.

The transcription start site, or TSS, marks the first nucleotide of a particular RNA transcript. It is not the translation start site. In a typical protein-coding messenger, additional RNA sequence precedes the AUG used for synthesis of the main protein product. That preceding retained sequence is the 5′ untranslated region. A promoter helps determine the initiation of RNA synthesis, whereas the translation start codon marks the beginning of the main protein coding sequence. These two landmarks refer to different molecular machines and are often separated by tens or hundreds of nucleotides or by introns in genomic DNA.

The protein coding sequence, abbreviated CDS, is the interval of the processed RNA containing the codons used for the specified protein product, with conventions about the stop codon explicitly stated. Translation terminates at a stop codon, leaving downstream retained nucleotides as the 3′ untranslated region. The stop codon therefore does not terminate transcription. A mutation that changes a stop codon can affect protein length while leaving the original RNA endpoints intact. A mutation affecting transcription initiation can change the RNA population without changing the nucleotide sequence of the coding region itself.

At the downstream end of a typical RNA polymerase II transcript, an RNA cleavage event establishes the 3′ end of many mature nuclear messenger RNAs. A poly(A) tail is then added to many such messages. Transcription may continue beyond the cleavage site before polymerase releases the template. Therefore the end of a mature transcript, the end of a primary transcription event and the translation stop codon must be kept separate. The sequence of the added poly(A) tail is not generally represented by a corresponding continuous tract of adenines in the genomic DNA. The cap at the 5′ end is also a modification rather than a simple genomic length contribution.

An enhancer is another regulatory DNA element that can influence transcription from a promoter. Its position is not fixed relative to the transcribed interval: it can be upstream, downstream or within an intron. An enhancer located in an intron is both a regulatory region in the DNA and part of an interval transcribed into a precursor RNA; its RNA sequence may be removed from that particular mature messenger. Functional and positional classifications are not mutually exclusive.

When calculating a gene's length, identify the endpoints supplied in the problem. A span from the first transcribed nucleotide to the mature 3′ cleavage site can be computed from the annotated transcript blocks and intervening intervals. The total length of nascent transcription may extend farther. A regulatory-domain span might be wider still. The term gene length in a database often refers to the genomic span of annotated transcripts, not to a precisely bounded collection of every regulatory sequence that can affect expression.

6. Exons are not synonymous with protein-coding sequence

The most common introductory error is to identify exons with coding DNA and introns with all noncoding DNA. That is a useful first approximation for some examples but it is not a valid definition. An exon is a segment retained in a specified mature RNA. A coding sequence is a segment whose codons specify a protein product, including the termination signal according to the chosen counting convention. The exon classification asks what is retained; the coding classification asks what is translated.

In a three-exon protein-coding gene, the first exon might contain a 5′ UTR followed by the first portion of the coding sequence. A middle exon might be entirely coding, and the final exon might contain the end of the coding sequence, the stop codon and the 3′ UTR. All three are exons even though some of their nucleotides are not translated. A first exon may be entirely untranslated when translation starts in a later exon. A last exon can likewise be entirely untranslated if the stop codon lies in a preceding exon.

The 5′ UTR lies between the mature RNA's 5′ beginning and the start of the main coding region. Its sequence can influence the accessibility of the start site and the behavior of the messenger. Some UTRs contain upstream open reading frames, so the name untranslated region is defined relative to the main annotated protein product, not an assertion that no ribosome can ever translate a nucleotide there. The 3′ UTR follows the main stop codon and can contain elements affecting RNA localization, stability and translation. These functions show why being outside the main coding sequence is not equivalent to being biologically useless.

Introns can interrupt a coding region, a UTR or a noncoding RNA transcript. An intron inside a UTR is copied into the precursor but removed from the processed RNA in the same broad structural sense as a coding-region intron. It does not interrupt a protein reading frame because it lies outside the coding region. Conversely an intron can separate the nucleotides of one codon, so neither exon alone needs to contain an integral number of codons. The relevant reading frame is that of the joined coding sequence.

Genes whose functional product is a noncoding RNA offer a particularly strong test of terminology. A long noncoding RNA can have multiple exons but no annotated protein-coding sequence. A tRNA precursor can contain an intron yet its mature product is not messenger RNA. The word exon therefore does not imply protein synthesis. Similarly, DNA lying between two genes is not automatically an intron: it must be an intervening transcribed interval removed from the corresponding RNA. The same genomic interval can even have different annotations in different transcripts or overlapping genes.

A useful habit is to classify every interval in a gene map along two independent axes. First decide whether it is transcribed and whether it is retained in the selected RNA. Then decide whether it belongs to the coding sequence of the selected protein. This two-step procedure prevents nearly all confusion among exons, introns, untranslated regions, regulatory DNA and intergenic DNA.

7. Exon–intron boundaries and the logic of joining

Consider an exon ending in the sequence AC and the following exon beginning in GU. In the mature RNA the local sequence is ACGU, but in the precursor an intron lies between AC and GU. To produce the expected sequence the cell must remove precisely the intervening nucleotides and join the two retained ends. Removing one nucleotide too many or leaving one intronic nucleotide behind can change the reading frame if the junction lies within the coding sequence. Even if the reading frame is maintained, the amino acid sequence or regulatory information may change.

The 5′ splice site is the boundary at the upstream end of an intron in transcript orientation, and the 3′ splice site is at its downstream end. They are sometimes called donor and acceptor sites respectively. For the common class of nuclear spliceosomal introns, the intron begins with GU and ends with AG in RNA notation. On the non-template DNA strand these are GT and AG. These dinucleotides are parts of larger sequence contexts, not sufficient signals by themselves. Many GU and AG occurrences within a transcript are not used as splice boundaries. Other sequence elements, including a branch region and often a pyrimidine-rich tract near the downstream boundary, contribute to recognition in the common system.

The GU–AG pattern is not universal. Rare classes and noncanonical boundaries exist, and organellar or self-splicing introns have different recognition logic. An exam question that gives a conventional nuclear intron model can reasonably use GU–AG; an assertion that every intron in every organism must begin and end with those bases is false. Exact identification requires a transcript alignment or appropriate evidence, rather than a search for the first occurrence of GU followed by AG.

At the structural level, processing changes the RNA but leaves the corresponding gene's DNA sequence in place. The joined RNA has phosphodiester continuity across the exon junction. The removed interval is no longer part of that mature RNA product, although excised intron-derived RNA may persist briefly or give rise to other RNAs in certain cases. Do not mistake a mature RNA for an unmodified primary transcript simply because both were derived from the same locus.

RNA synthesis and removal of intervening sequence can overlap in time in a living nucleus. The familiar textbook diagram showing a complete primary RNA followed by a completely processed RNA describes the relationship between their sequences. It need not be a literal snapshot of every molecule at a single stage. Some introns may already have been removed while a downstream region is still being transcribed. The sequence accounting remains valid for the defined complete transcribed interval.

The detailed assembly of small nuclear ribonucleoproteins, the chemical role of the branch-point nucleotide, and the reaction intermediates are part of RNA processing. Knowing the structural boundary vocabulary first makes those later biochemical steps easier to understand: the machinery must distinguish the intervals to retain from the intervals to remove and join the appropriate ends with nucleotide-level precision.

8. Coding continuity and intron phase

An intron can interrupt the coding sequence at different positions relative to the triplet reading frame. The phase of a coding-region intron describes where it occurs within that triplet pattern. A phase 0 intron lies between complete codons. A phase 1 intron lies after the first nucleotide of a codon. A phase 2 intron lies after the second nucleotide of a codon. These terms refer to a particular coding transcript; an intron in an untranslated region has no coding phase in the ordinary sense.

For example, take the processed coding sequence AUG GCU UAC UAA. It encodes methionine, alanine and tyrosine, followed by a stop signal. A phase 0 intron could lie between AUG and GCU. A phase 1 intron could lie between the G and CU of GCU. A phase 2 intron could lie between GC and U of that same codon. In all three cases accurate joining can restore the identical mature coding sequence. The phase describes the location of the interruption, not an alternative genetic code.

This distinction explains why coding exons do not all have lengths divisible by three. Suppose the first coding exon contains 100 nucleotides starting at the initiation codon. Dividing 100 by 3 gives 33 complete codons with one remaining nucleotide, so the first intron is phase 1. The next coding exon must supply the remaining two nucleotides of that codon before the subsequent complete triplets begin. If the first two coding segments together contain 252 nucleotides, then the next intron is phase 0. Phase is determined from the cumulative coding length measured from the start codon, not from the length of an isolated exon unless the exon begins at a codon boundary.

The same arithmetic helps interpret exon skipping. The terms in-frame and out-of-frame describe continuity of triplet grouping; they do not guarantee a particular biological activity. If an internal fully coding exon of length divisible by three is omitted, the downstream reading frame is preserved, provided that the start site and surrounding junctions remain as specified. If the exon has a length not divisible by three, the downstream frame changes under those assumptions. Frame preservation does not guarantee a functional protein: a deleted segment may be essential for folding, interaction or catalytic activity, and a new junction can itself introduce a stop codon. Conversely a frameshift may lead to an early stop, but its exact position requires the sequence.

In evolutionary discussions, compatible coding phases can help explain why some rearrangements preserve a reading frame. However, an exon is not necessarily equivalent to a complete protein domain. Domains are folded structural units, whereas exon boundaries are defined by RNA organization. One domain can span several exons, and an exon can contribute to more than one structural region. The architectural relationship provides possibilities, not a one-to-one rule.

When a problem asks for the phase at a boundary, write down the number of coding nucleotides already retained upstream and find the remainder after division by three. Exclude UTR nucleotides from this count. If a problem uses a different phase convention, such as the number of bases required to complete a codon in a particular annotation format, follow the definition given rather than equating the two automatically.

9. Quantitative accounting: gene span, RNA length and protein capacity

Each base pair along the transcribed DNA interval corresponds to one nucleotide in the RNA copied from it. The number of nucleotides in a fully transcribed interval is therefore the number of base pairs along that interval, not twice that number. Both DNA strands are present, but only one serves as the template for the specified transcript. If the transcribed region contains n retained exons of lengths E1 through En and n − 1 internal introns of lengths I1 through I(n − 1), define E as the sum of exon lengths and I as the sum of intron lengths.

Under the simplified assumption that the first and last exonic endpoints define the entire interval being measured, the genomic span G equals E + I. The corresponding unspliced RNA contains G nucleotides, while the genome-derived portion of its completely spliced RNA contains E nucleotides. Thus G − E = I. This identity is not an empirical approximation; it follows from partitioning the interval into nonoverlapping retained and removed portions. It does not include a promoter, an unmeasured downstream termination region, the cap or a nontemplated poly(A) tail.

The fraction of the interval retained in that mature transcript is E/G, and the removed fraction is I/G. Multiplication by 100 converts each fraction into a percentage. Notice that the denominator is the original measured span. If a question asks instead for the intron-to-exon length ratio, the denominator is E; it is a different quantity and can exceed one.

Worked example 1. A gene model contains exons of 150, 210 and 240 bp, separated by introns of 800 and 1,600 bp. E = 150 + 210 + 240 = 600 bp and I = 800 + 1,600 = 2,400 bp. The genomic span is 3,000 bp; the complete unspliced interval corresponds to 3,000 nt; the genome-derived mature RNA is 600 nt. The retained fraction is 600/3,000 = 0.20 or 20%. The intron-to-exon ratio is 2,400/600 = 4. The transcript has three exons and two internal introns. It is not legitimate to infer 199 amino acids merely from the mature length because the UTR lengths and coding boundaries were not specified.

Worked example 2. In that 600-nt RNA, the 5′ UTR is 90 nt and the 3′ UTR is 60 nt. Define the remaining CDS length to include the three-nucleotide stop codon. It contains 600 − 90 − 60 = 450 nt or 150 codons. One of these is the stop, which contributes no amino acid. The predicted primary protein product contains 149 amino acid residues, assuming a conventional complete open reading frame (ORF: a sequence of codons extending to a stop in the selected frame), no recoding and no premature stop. The initiation methionine counts as a residue; subsequent protein processing can remove it or other segments, but this does not change the initial translation prediction.

Worked example 3. A reference annotation gives exons at positions 101–200, 401–550 and 901–1100, all one-based and inclusive. A closed interval from a to b contains b − a + 1 bases because both endpoints are counted. The exons therefore contain 100, 150 and 200 bp, for E = 450 bp. The introns occupy 201–400 and 551–900, of lengths 200 and 350 bp, giving I = 550 bp. The outer span is 1100 − 101 + 1 = 1,000 bp, and 450 + 550 = 1,000 checks the accounting. A zero-based half-open coordinate system would use a different endpoint formula; do not apply both conventions in the same calculation.

Worked example 4. A mature messenger of genome-derived length 1,200 nt is associated with a transcribed span of 7.2 kb under the same simplifying assumptions. Convert 7.2 kb to 7,200 bp. The difference is 6,000 intervening nucleotides. If the transcript contains four introns of equal length, each is 1,500 bp; without the equality assumption the mean is still 1,500 bp but individual lengths cannot be determined. The mature retained fraction is 1,200/7,200 = 1/6 or approximately 16.7%. A length of 1,200 nt does not reveal the exon count unless additional structural evidence is given.

These relationships provide a way to audit examination data. A claimed mature genome-derived transcript longer than the sum of its annotated retained intervals suggests an unaccounted exon, a different transcript, added sequence or a measurement/convention error. It does not imply that every intron must be retained. Conversely a long DNA span can encode a small protein, because much of the span may be removed or remain untranslated. Keep length, gene number and coding capacity distinct.

Accounting relationships

G = E + I; retained fraction = E/G; removed fraction = I/G. For a complete conventional CDS of length C including the stop codon, initial polypeptide length = C/3 − 1.

10. One genomic locus, more than one transcript architecture

The alternating exon–intron diagram describes one possible processed transcript. But a genomic locus may produce several mature RNA variants, called transcript isoforms. Some differ in which internal exon is included, others in where an exon begins or ends, and still others in which first or last region is used. The same DNA can support different arrangements of retained sequence without a change in the nucleotide sequence of the chromosome.

The simplest example is cassette exon skipping. Suppose the genome contains intervals A, B and C, separated by intervening regions. One mature RNA contains A–B–C, while another contains A–C. The second RNA lacks B, which is an exon of the first RNA. In the second transcript the entire genomic interval between A and C is removed in the corresponding splice event. This is why a statement such as “this region is an exon” is often shorthand for “this region is an exon in at least one specified transcript.” It may not have identical status in every isoform.

Alternative boundaries are another possibility. Two different donor positions can give a longer or shorter upstream retained segment; two different acceptor positions can change the beginning of a downstream retained segment. The resulting RNAs differ by part of an exon rather than by an entire block. Intron retention produces an RNA that includes sequence removed from another RNA. Not every intron-containing RNA is a functional mature isoform, however. Some molecules are incompletely processed precursors or are destined for degradation. Evidence of expression, full transcript structure and processing status is required before drawing a functional conclusion.

Different transcription start sites and different 3′ cleavage sites can also yield different RNA architectures. These alternatives can create changes in UTRs without altering the main encoded protein. They may also change the coding sequence when a different initial or terminal coding segment is used. Alternative promoter use and alternative cleavage are not themselves synonymous with alternative splicing, although a single transcript may combine several kinds of variation.

A well-known biological consequence is that different RNA isoforms from a single gene can encode proteins differing in a small sequence region. In other cases the proteins are identical, because the alternative segment lies within a UTR. Thus neither the number of annotated exons nor the number of RNA variants equals the number of distinct protein products. An RNA must be translated in a suitable frame and be sufficiently stable for its coding potential to be realized.

Consider a teaching model in which a transcript contains fixed exons A and D and two optional internal blocks B and C. If B and C can independently be included or excluded and all four combinations are permitted, the number of arrangements is 2 × 2 = 4: A–D, A–B–D, A–C–D and A–B–C–D. If B and C are mutually exclusive and exactly one must be present, only two combinations are allowed. If a question merely says that two optional blocks exist without telling us their dependencies, four is a theoretical upper bound for this simple binary model rather than an observed biological count.

The structural diversity of transcripts makes genome annotation more complex than a one-gene, one-message picture. When studying a locus, distinguish the overall union of intervals used across isoforms from the exact exon list of one selected transcript. Only the latter can be used directly to calculate its mature length, coding phase and specific protein product.

11. From genomic DNA to cDNA: testing a gene model

Direct comparison of sequences provides a more precise way to infer the architecture first revealed by hybrid observations. Genomic DNA contains the chromosomal sequence with both retained and intervening regions. Complementary DNA, abbreviated cDNA, is DNA synthesized from an RNA template using reverse transcription. If the starting RNA is a fully spliced messenger and the cDNA represents its full genome-derived sequence, the cDNA lacks the introns removed from that messenger. It can also include untranslated sequences. A cDNA is not necessarily just a protein coding sequence.

Imagine a genomic sequence containing a 120-base interval A, a 500-base interval X, a 180-base interval B, a 300-base interval Y and a 200-base interval C. A full-length cDNA aligning to A, B and C in that order, but not to X or Y, supports a three-exon transcript with two introns. The genomic outer span is 1,300 bp and the cDNA's genome-derived portion is 500 bp. The precise ends of the aligned blocks identify candidate exon boundaries. A cDNA consisting only of B and C might instead have come from a shortened RNA or from an incomplete reverse transcription event. The absence of A in one cDNA is not enough to establish that all RNA products lack A.

A reverse-transcription PCR comparison makes the same logic visible through fragment sizes. Select two primers within retained flanking exons, facing toward the interval. A PCR product amplified from genomic DNA contains all genomic sequence between the primers, including an intervening intron. A product amplified from fully spliced cDNA lacks the intervening interval. If the primer locations and ends are otherwise identical, the size difference corresponds to the removed length between the primers. The primers need not span the entire gene, so this difference cannot automatically be interpreted as total intron length for the gene.

For example, imagine a genomic DNA product of 960 bp and a product from cDNA of 360 bp using the same flanking primer pair. If there is one internal gap and the products are specific, the gap is 600 bp. If there are two gaps, their combined length is 600 bp, but the fragment sizes alone cannot reveal their separate lengths. Sequencing the amplified cDNA and aligning it to genomic DNA can distinguish these possibilities. Similarly, two different RNA arrangements can have the same length if equal-sized blocks are exchanged; a single gel band does not establish an exact exon sequence.

Controls make these conclusions stronger. A no-template reaction checks for contamination of PCR reagents. A reverse-transcription-minus control checks whether a product could arise from contaminating genomic DNA in the RNA preparation. An independently verified genomic product provides a length comparison. Correct primer placement matters: if a primer lies entirely within a skipped exon, the shorter isoform may not be amplified at all. If a primer spans an exon–exon junction, it may preferentially amplify a joined RNA-derived sequence rather than the ordinary intron-containing genomic sequence. Such selectivity is useful but not automatically absolute.

A band intensity is not a direct measure of the number of original RNA molecules unless the method and assumptions support quantitative interpretation. Amplification efficiency, template length, sample quality and the detection range can differ. The architectural questions in this chapter rely primarily on the presence, size and identity of specific products, not on quantitative expression profiling.

Hybridization, sequence alignment and PCR length comparison are complementary. Hybridization gives physical evidence of noncontiguous correspondence, sequence alignment identifies nucleotide boundaries, and PCR can test defined intervals or junctions. The most convincing conclusion comes when multiple observations support the same transcript model rather than when a single ambiguous measurement is treated as proof.

12. Biological examples and important exceptions

The beta-globin gene provides a familiar cellular example of an interrupted protein-coding gene. Its common gene model contains three exons separated by two introns. The mature RNA contains the retained coding segments together with untranslated terminal sequences. Comparison with the genomic sequence makes clear that the amino acid chain is specified by joined coding segments, not by translation through the entire genomic span. Globin genes also illustrate related genes within a family, but that is a separate issue: the three exons of one beta-globin gene are not three separate globin genes.

A useful contrast is provided by many replication-dependent histone genes in animals. These genes typically lack introns, yet they give rise to functional proteins required for chromatin assembly. Their messages also commonly use a specialized nonpolyadenylated 3′ end rather than the usual long poly(A) tail. Histone variants and other histone genes can differ from this pattern. The example shows that neither intron removal nor polyadenylation is universally necessary to produce a functional eukaryotic protein-coding messenger.

Intron abundance varies substantially among eukaryotic lineages and among genes in the same organism. Many animal and plant nuclear genes are interrupted, while the nuclear genome of budding yeast contains relatively few intron-bearing genes compared with many multicellular eukaryotes. The presence of a nucleus does not require every gene to contain introns. A species can maintain many intronless genes and still use RNA processing on other transcripts.

Bacterial protein-coding genes are commonly continuous and may be grouped into operons, but prokaryotes are not absolutely intron-free. Certain bacteria and their phages carry self-splicing introns, and some archaeal RNA genes contain introns removed by specialized processing systems. Eukaryotic organelle genomes can also contain introns, particularly in some fungal and plant lineages. These examples do not mean that all organisms use the same splicing machinery or that the introns are identical in origin and mechanism. They show why general tendencies should not be presented as universal rules.

Another useful biological distinction is between a gene's architecture and the function of its intronic sequence. Some introns contain regulatory elements that influence gene expression or sequences that give rise to functional RNA products. Others may have no individually established specific function. The fact that a segment is removed from a particular mature RNA tells us what happens in that transcript, not whether every nucleotide has a selected biological role. Introns can also provide regions in which genetic variation occurs without altering the main coding sequence directly, though a variation can still change boundaries or regulatory behavior.

It is tempting to claim that introns exist in order to create protein diversity, but the evolutionary history is more complex. Alternative exon use can indeed expand transcript and sometimes protein diversity. Introns can provide opportunities for changes in gene organization. Neither observation establishes that every intron originated for that purpose or that introns are required for complex organisms. Distinguish a possible current consequence of architecture from an explanation of its evolutionary origin. The exam-relevant conclusion is that interrupted genes create additional relationships between genomic sequence, mature RNA and possible products, and these relationships vary across biological systems.

13. Predict the structural effects of a mutation

A DNA substitution within a coding exon may change an amino acid, produce a premature stop or leave the encoded amino acid unchanged. But the consequence depends on more than its position in an exon. A substitution can also influence the selection of an adjacent exon boundary. Likewise an intronic substitution can matter if it disrupts a recognition sequence, changes a regulatory element or creates a newly used boundary. It is therefore incorrect to assume that only coding DNA changes can influence the mature protein.

Consider a simple interrupted gene with a normal mature sequence A–B–C. A boundary mutation could lead to skipping B, retention of an intervening segment or use of an alternative nearby boundary. The particular outcome depends on the surrounding sequence and the cell's processing machinery. Without RNA evidence one should predict plausible classes of outcome, not assume a unique product from every mutation at the same dinucleotide. Changes to the mature coding length can then be analyzed with the reading-frame rule.

For example, if a fully coding 90-nt internal exon is lost and the junctions preserve the surrounding coding sequence, the downstream frame remains the same because 90/3 = 30. The initial predicted protein loses 30 residues relative to the original, provided the new junction introduces no stop and no other changes occur. A loss of 91 coding nucleotides changes the downstream frame because the remainder upon division by three is one. An exact new protein length cannot be inferred without knowing where the altered reading frame encounters a stop.

Some beta-thalassemia alleles illustrate the biological importance of changes that affect globin RNA splicing. A variant may disrupt a normal splice site or cause an abnormal boundary to be used, reducing the supply of correctly processed beta-globin RNA. This can decrease production of functional beta-globin and disturb the balance of globin chains. The broad chain of causation is DNA variant, altered RNA architecture, altered supply of usable protein, and physiological consequence. Not all beta-thalassemia alleles act through splicing, and a clinical diagnosis cannot be made from the diagram alone.

Changes in regulatory DNA offer a different class of prediction. A promoter change may reduce initiation or shift the set of transcription starts. A change near a 3′ cleavage signal may alter the terminal boundary or RNA stability. These can affect expression even when every amino acid codon remains intact. In an examination, identify the earliest level of organization directly affected by the change, then follow the causal steps to possible RNA and protein outcomes. A small protein amount is not itself proof of an intron defect; it could also reflect transcription, RNA stability or translation.

The distinction between structural and functional evidence also applies to rescue experiments. If a construct containing an intronless cDNA restores a protein in a cell with a boundary defect, the result is compatible with a defect in normal RNA processing. It does not by itself identify the exact responsible factor because the construct may differ in regulatory context and abundance. Comparing genomic and cDNA sequences, checking RNA products and monitoring protein output together provides stronger inference.

14. Integrate the maps and prepare for chromosome organization

The entire chapter can be reconstructed as a sequence of questions. Start with a double-stranded genomic locus and identify its transcript direction. Locate the initiation landmark and the mature RNA endpoint. Within that interval distinguish regions copied into the precursor from regions retained in the selected mature RNA. Within the retained regions, distinguish the main coding sequence from untranslated sequence. Then interpret alternative transcripts as different arrangements of those genomic intervals rather than as necessarily different genes.

When drawing a map from memory, include at least two rows, genomic DNA and mature RNA, and ideally a third row representing the complete transcribed interval. Mark 5′ and 3′ directions, distinguish exons from intervening sequence by shape or label, and put the translation start and stop inside the appropriate retained blocks. Use a separate symbol or label for the promoter and any mature 3′ cleavage site. If the polymerase termination site is included, draw it separately. This exercise reveals whether the distinction among regulatory, transcribed and translated intervals is truly understood.

To solve a length problem, first identify whether a given number describes a genomic span, an unspliced transcript, a genome-derived mature RNA or a coding sequence. Convert kilobases to bases and distinguish DNA base pairs from RNA nucleotides. Count inclusive endpoints only when the coordinate convention requires it. Subtract introns to obtain retained length, subtract UTRs to obtain the specified coding length, and subtract the stop codon from the amino acid count only when it is included in the given coding length. These steps are independent of how many introns a gene contains.

To reason from data, identify which molecule was measured. A genomic DNA band reports a DNA interval; a cDNA band reports an RNA-derived interval under the stated assumptions; an RNA–DNA hybrid can reveal separated complementary blocks. Alternative hypotheses such as incomplete cDNA synthesis, genomic contamination or equal-length alternative exons must be evaluated using controls. Never replace a precise molecular inference with the vague statement that a gene is “expressed differently” when the data actually concern sequence structure.

The next chapter investigates the abundance and arrangement of unique and repetitive DNA. An intron is defined by the fate of a transcribed sequence, not by its copy number in the genome. It can contain unique DNA, repeated motifs or mobile-element-derived sequence. Conversely a repetitive sequence can occur in an exon, an intron or between genes. Keeping these independent classifications separate will make genome organization much easier to interpret. The later study of RNA synthesis and processing will explain how the cell recognizes and joins the boundaries established here.

15. Part B — Conceptual discrimination

Question 1

Which statement defines an exon?

A. A sequence retained in a specified mature RNA; B. Every protein-coding DNA segment only; C. DNA upstream of all promoters; D. Every noncoding DNA segment

Answer and explanation

A. Retention in a particular processed RNA defines an exon. B excludes UTR and noncoding RNA exons; C confuses location with processing; D includes intergenic and intronic DNA.

Question 2

Which feature directly identifies the first nucleotide of a transcript?

A. AUG; B. TSS; C. Stop codon; D. Poly(A) tail

Answer and explanation

B. The TSS is a transcription landmark. A and C refer to translation, while D concerns 3′ RNA processing.

Question 3

A conventional single linear RNA model contains six retained exon blocks. How many intervening gaps separate them?

A. 6; B. 7; C. 5; D. 12

Answer and explanation

C. Six blocks have five internal separations. A counts blocks, B adds one, D incorrectly doubles the number.

Question 4

Which observation directly challenges a continuous-gene model?

A. One mature RNA specifically matches separated intervals on one DNA strand; B. DNA has two strands; C. RNA contains uracil; D. RNA polymerase moves on DNA

Answer and explanation

A. Noncontiguous correspondence requires an explanation beyond simple continuous copying. B–D are compatible with both continuous and interrupted genes.

Question 5

Which sequence pair is typical of common nuclear introns in RNA orientation?

A. AG–GU; B. GT–AG; C. GU–AG; D. AUG–UAA

Answer and explanation

C. GU is at the 5′ intron end and AG at the 3′ end in the common class. A reverses them; B uses DNA notation; D denotes translation signals.

Question 6

An intron occurs after the second nucleotide of a coding triplet. Its phase is:

A. 0; B. 1; C. 2; D. 3

Answer and explanation

C. Phase 2 follows two coding nucleotides of a triplet. Phase 0 is between codons and phase 1 follows one; phase 3 is not a distinct category.

Question 7

Which region is transcribed and retained but usually outside the main protein CDS?

A. 3′ UTR; B. A wholly upstream untranscribed promoter; C. An excised intron; D. Intergenic DNA by definition

Answer and explanation

A. The 3′ UTR is retained downstream of the main stop. B is untranscribed as specified, C is removed, D is not defined by retention.

Question 8

Which statement about intron distribution is correct?

A. All eukaryotic genes have introns; B. Bacteria never have introns; C. Intronless nuclear genes exist; D. All organelles use identical splicing machinery

Answer and explanation

C. Many replication-dependent histone genes illustrate intronless nuclear genes. A and B are false universal claims; D ignores distinct organellar systems.

Question 9

Assertion: Exons can contain untranslated sequences. Reason: Exon status depends on retention in a particular processed RNA, not on translation.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Retention and translation are separate classifications, directly explaining UTR exons. B misses that relationship; C and D contradict the definitions.

Question 10

What does a full-length cDNA prepared from a fully spliced messenger normally contain?

A. Every upstream enhancer; B. Only excised introns; C. Retained transcript sequence including represented UTRs; D. Both genomic DNA strands plus all neighboring genes

Answer and explanation

C. cDNA copies the RNA; it does not recreate flanking or removed genomic intervals. A, B and D confuse cDNA with genomic DNA.

Question 11

Which pair is not necessarily coincident in a typical Pol II gene?

A. Translation stop and mature 3′ cleavage; B. First transcribed nucleotide and TSS; C. An internal intron's 5′ end and its donor boundary; D. Mature exon junction and the adjacency of its two retained sides

Answer and explanation

A. Translation stops before the 3′ UTR; cleavage and polymerase termination are also separate. B–D describe corresponding landmarks under the definitions supplied.

Question 12

Which claim is justified by a genomic intron in a 5′ UTR?

A. It must interrupt a codon; B. It can be removed without directly interrupting the main reading frame; C. It must be an intergenic sequence; D. It cannot be transcribed

Answer and explanation

B. A UTR intron is inside a transcribed non-main-coding interval. A places it inside the CDS, C outside the gene, and D denies precursor transcription.

16. Part C — Experimental and quantitative reasoning

Question 1

Exons have lengths 100, 200 and 300 bp, introns 1,000 and 400 bp. Which pair gives measured genomic span and genome-derived mature RNA length?

A. 1,400 bp and 600 nt; B. 2,000 bp and 600 nt; C. 2,000 bp and 1,400 nt; D. 4,000 bp and 600 nt

Answer and explanation

B. E = 600; I = 1,400; G = 2,000. A omits exon lengths from the span, C mistakes intron length for retained RNA, D incorrectly counts both DNA strands.

Question 2

A 975-nt mature RNA has a 75-nt 5′ UTR and 150-nt 3′ UTR. The CDS includes the stop. How many amino acids in the initial product?

A. 250; B. 249; C. 324; D. 750

Answer and explanation

B. CDS = 975 − 75 − 150 = 750 nt, corresponding to 250 codons, of which the last is stop. A counts stop, C uses total RNA, D confuses nucleotides and residues.

Question 3

Three inclusive exon intervals are 10–109, 210–309 and 510–609. What is the total intronic length?

A. 300 bp; B. 299 bp; C. 600 bp; D. 301 bp

Answer and explanation

A. Each exon is 100 bp, the outer span is 609 − 10 + 1 = 600 bp. Thus I = 600 − 300 = 300 bp. B and D use incorrect endpoint arithmetic, C is the entire span.

Question 4

The first coding exon contains 74 coding nt and the second contains 49; coding sequence continues in a third exon. What are the phases of the first and second coding-region introns?

A. 2 and 0; B. 2 and 1; C. 1 and 2; D. 0 and 0

Answer and explanation

A. 74 mod 3 = 2; cumulative 74 + 49 = 123, divisible by 3. B incorrectly uses the second exon independently; C reverses phase logic; D ignores the first remainder.

Question 5

An internal coding exon of 84 nt is skipped; the start and downstream junction are otherwise unchanged and no new stop is introduced. Which consequence follows?

A. Downstream frame shifts by one; B. Downstream frame is maintained and 28 residues are lost; C. All proteins must retain function; D. The genomic exon is deleted

Answer and explanation

B. 84/3 = 28, so frame is maintained and initial product length is reduced by 28. A ignores divisibility; C asserts function without evidence; D confuses RNA choice and DNA deletion.

Question 6

A genomic amplicon is 1,200 bp and a cDNA amplicon from the same exon primers is 450 bp. What can be inferred if both are specific?

A. Exactly one 750-bp intron; B. 750 bp of removed genomic interval between the primers; C. The entire gene is 1,200 bp; D. The mature protein has 150 residues

Answer and explanation

B. The size difference is 750 bp; one or several gaps may account for it. A assumes one gap, C assumes full-gene coverage, D ignores UTRs and stop conventions.

Question 7

A no-reverse-transcription control yields the same large band as genomic DNA. What is the most relevant concern?

A. Genomic contamination; B. All introns are translated; C. Polymerase always terminates at AUG; D. The gene is necessarily intronless

Answer and explanation

A. A DNA product in a no-RT reaction can arise from DNA contamination. B and C confuse molecular processes; D is not established by a control band.

Question 8

A reverse-strand gene has retained intervals 700–800 and 300–400 in one transcript. Which interval comes first in transcript order?

A. 300–400; B. 700–800; C. Both begin simultaneously; D. Orientation cannot affect ordering

Answer and explanation

B. Transcription on the reverse strand proceeds toward smaller reference coordinates. A assumes reference order equals transcript order; C and D contradict the supplied direction.

Question 9

Two internal optional exons are mutually exclusive and exactly one is selected, while both terminal exons are fixed. How many permitted exon combinations?

A. 4; B. 2; C. 8; D. 1

Answer and explanation

B. There are precisely two choices. A assumes independent inclusion/exclusion; C counts extra unconstrained choices; D ignores the alternative.

Question 10

A mutation eliminates a conventional donor site. Which result is justified without transcript data?

A. The entire gene must be deleted; B. Only the adjacent exon can ever be skipped; C. Several RNA architecture changes are possible and need testing; D. The protein must be unchanged

Answer and explanation

C. Retention, skipping or alternative site selection may occur. A confuses DNA and RNA; B assumes one obligatory outcome; D denies possible coding consequences.

Question 11

Two cDNA products have identical length, but align to distinct equal-sized middle exons. What explains the observation?

A. Equal length establishes identical nucleotide sequence; B. Different exon arrangements can share a length; C. All genomic DNA is repetitive; D. No alternative transcript exists

Answer and explanation

B. Length alone cannot resolve isoform identity. A and D ignore the sequence alignment; C is irrelevant.

Question 12

A 600-nt genome-derived mature RNA corresponds to 3,000 bp of genomic span. A second RNA retains 120 nt of an intervening interval but is otherwise identical. What is its genome-derived length and retained fraction?

A. 720 nt, 24%; B. 720 nt, 20%; C. 480 nt, 16%; D. 3,120 nt, 104%

Answer and explanation

A. 600 + 120 = 720; 720/3,000 = 0.24. B uses the original fraction, C subtracts rather than adds, D incorrectly changes the genomic denominator.

Question 13

An exon is present in two RNAs, but lies entirely before the main AUG in RNA 1 and contributes coding bases in RNA 2. Which conclusion follows?

A. It cannot be an exon of RNA 1; B. Retention and coding status depend on the selected transcript; C. RNA 2 must originate from a different chromosome; D. Exons always encode full protein domains

Answer and explanation

B. Both RNAs retain the interval, but their selected CDS boundaries differ. A equates exons with CDS; C is unsupported; D confuses genomic intervals with folded domains.

Question 14

A cDNA library contains a short clone missing its expected first exon, but a full-length RNA alignment includes it. Which next inference is justified?

A. The exon is deleted from every cell; B. A short clone can reflect incomplete synthesis or a distinct RNA, so further evidence is needed; C. The gene is an operon; D. The first exon must be an intron in all products

Answer and explanation

B. A partial cDNA does not define the full RNA architecture. A and D overgeneralize; C invokes an unrelated organizational concept.
