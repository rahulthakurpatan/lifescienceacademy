1. From digestion to absorption

The alimentary canal presents a remarkable physiological problem. A meal contains usable chemical energy, minerals and molecular building blocks, but most of these materials cannot be delivered directly to the tissues in the form in which they are swallowed. A piece of starch is not a transportable blood glucose molecule; a triglyceride droplet cannot readily traverse an aqueous extracellular environment; iron may be present in a form that is poorly soluble. Digestion changes the physical and chemical form of food, whereas absorption transfers the resulting substances across an epithelial barrier into the internal environment. Assimilation is a further process: cells use or incorporate the absorbed material. These are distinct events even when they overlap in time. An animal may digest a nutrient effectively yet fail to absorb it if an appropriate membrane transporter is absent.

An intestinal epithelial cell is a particularly useful example of the relationship between cell biology and organ physiology. It has a lumen-facing membrane that encounters a changing mixture of digestive products and a tissue-facing membrane exposed to interstitial fluid. A nutrient crossing the apical membrane has not necessarily reached the blood. It may be metabolized by the epithelial cell, stored temporarily, returned to the lumen or transferred across the opposite side of the cell. Thus an experiment measuring accumulation of radiolabel in enterocytes is not equivalent to an experiment measuring net transfer of the label into portal blood. The distinction is especially important for lipids and iron, because the intracellular processing steps influence whether a substance ultimately enters the circulation.

Absorption is selective rather than an indiscriminate transfer of dissolved matter. The epithelial barrier must exclude many harmful agents while permitting water and physiologically useful solutes to pass. Selectivity emerges from transport proteins, junctions, chemical transformations and concentration gradients acting together. The transport capacity for any particular substance is also finite. At low luminal concentrations a small increase in substrate may produce a nearly proportional increase in uptake; at high concentrations, a carrier-mediated component can approach saturation. A large luminal load can therefore exceed effective absorptive capacity even in an otherwise healthy tissue.

The normal mammalian small intestine is the principal site for absorption of dietary nutrients. The large intestine receives material that escapes small-intestinal absorption, together with endogenous secretions and products of microbial fermentation. Its roles include electrolyte and fluid recovery as well as uptake of short-chain fatty acids. This division is functional rather than absolute: different regions share many transport pathways, and disease, development and species differences change their relative contribution. The following discussion uses adult mammalian physiology as the main model; an assertion about a named transporter should not automatically be generalized to all animals.

The balance of matter across the digestive tract

A useful way to investigate absorption begins with conservation of matter. Imagine an intestinal segment into which a substance is introduced at a known rate. At steady state, the amount leaving the lumen per unit time is the difference between the amount supplied and the amount passing out of the segment. For a nutrient that is neither generated nor degraded within the lumen, this difference represents net removal from the luminal compartment. The qualification is important because the disappearance of an original molecular form may simply reflect digestion. When lactose disappears, for example, some of the carbon may still be present in the lumen as glucose or galactose. A valid absorption experiment must account for the carbon-containing products or measure their appearance on the other side of the epithelium.

The distinction between a fraction and an absolute rate also matters. A preparation exposed to a low nutrient load may absorb nearly all of the available substrate but deliver less total nutrient per minute than a preparation exposed to a larger load. Conversely, a large load can increase the total absorbed amount while lowering the fraction absorbed. For example, suppose a segment absorbs 8 of 10 mmol supplied during one interval and 15 of 25 mmol during another. The respective fractional absorptions are 80 and 60 percent. Nevertheless, the second interval yields more absorbed nutrient. A statement that absorption has increased or decreased is therefore incomplete unless the investigator specifies whether the outcome is the amount, rate, fraction or concentration change.

The lumen is not a homogeneous vessel. Digestion, water absorption, secretion and movement of contents change the composition encountered by different regions of the epithelium. The concentration at the epithelial surface may differ from the concentration in the bulk luminal contents. A slowly mixed solution develops a local concentration boundary layer when substrate is removed rapidly at its surface. Nutrient delivery to the surface can then limit uptake even though the transport protein itself has spare capacity. Mixing, diffusion, membrane transport and blood flow are consequently steps in a series, and the most restrictive step may change as the experimental conditions change.

One consequence is that the same molecule may face different restrictions in different preparations. In a purified membrane vesicle, the experimenter can eliminate much of the diffusion path and directly manipulate an ion gradient. In an intact intestine, the mucus layer, digestive reactions, perfusion and intracellular metabolism influence the result. In an entire animal, hormonal state and motility provide further regulation. These preparations answer related but distinct questions. A carrier demonstrated convincingly in vesicles does not by itself establish the quantitative contribution of that carrier to whole-animal nutrient balance. It establishes a mechanism whose physiological importance must be tested at additional levels of organization.

2. Absorptive anatomy and epithelial polarity

To understand why the small intestine is so effective, consider a smooth cylinder and one with a corrugated inner surface. At the same outer dimensions the corrugated surface can provide more area for interaction with luminal contents. The mammalian intestinal mucosa achieves enlargement at several scales. Circular folds involve mucosal and submucosal organization; villi are fingerlike projections of mucosa into the lumen; microvilli are much smaller projections of the apical membrane of individual absorptive cells. These structures are not interchangeable. Damage to a villus removes numerous epithelial cells and their microvilli, whereas damage restricted to microvilli can reduce membrane area and associated proteins even if the gross villus remains recognizable.

The absorptive cells, or enterocytes, form a continuous epithelial layer. Capillaries and a central lymphatic vessel known as a lacteal lie within the connective tissue of a typical small-intestinal villus. The short diffusion distance from epithelium to these vessels helps maintain transfer of absorbed substances into the internal circulation. Nutrients entering a blood capillary are carried away in blood, whereas large lipid-rich particles enter lymphatic vessels. A villus is therefore not merely a device to maximize surface area; it combines an exchange surface with distribution pathways. The arrangement prevents the tissue-facing side from simply accumulating all of the products delivered by the apical membrane.

Microvilli form a brush border. The brush border contains membrane-associated hydrolytic enzymes and transporters, placing the final stages of digestion close to the uptake machinery. This coupling allows a disaccharide to be hydrolysed near the membrane that transports its constituent monosaccharides. However, the enzyme and transporter are separate entities. An enzyme deficiency may leave a substrate unhydrolysed, whereas a transporter defect may leave already hydrolysed products unabsorbed. A distinction between these mechanisms can be tested by presenting the product of the enzyme directly to the epithelium. If uptake resumes, the hydrolytic step is implicated; if the product still fails to cross, a transport limitation becomes more plausible.

Tight junctions near the apical ends of adjacent epithelial cells contribute both a barrier and a fence. Their barrier function determines aspects of paracellular permeability; their fence function helps preserve differences in the composition of apical and basolateral membranes. The plasma membrane facing the lumen can therefore express a different set of proteins from the membrane facing extracellular fluid. Directional movement emerges when uptake and release mechanisms are arranged in series. If a glucose carrier were distributed symmetrically over both surfaces, net transport would depend differently on the prevailing gradients and could not be inferred from its mere presence.

The crypt-villus axis supports epithelial renewal. Proliferating cells in crypt regions supply cells that differentiate and migrate along the villus in the conventional mammalian small-intestinal arrangement. Mature absorptive cells express many brush-border transporters. Other cell types, including mucus-secreting goblet cells, enteroendocrine cells and Paneth cells in appropriate regions, contribute to the tissue environment. Secretory and absorptive activities should be viewed as interacting epithelial functions, not as perfectly segregated jobs confined to every crypt and every villus without exception. The large intestine lacks the characteristic small-intestinal villi but possesses a substantial absorptive epithelial surface and crypts.

When villous surface area is lost, fewer functional uptake sites may be available. According to a simple diffusion model, flux is proportional to area when the concentration difference, permeability and other factors are held constant. Living tissue is more complicated because transporter abundance per unit area, intestinal transit, blood flow and remaining segment capacity can all change. The observation that one tissue has twice the surface area of another does not establish that it will absorb twice as much of every nutrient. A useful mechanistic prediction is conditional: increasing effective exchange area raises the potential capacity of a pathway if other limiting factors are unchanged.

Exchange area, distance and maintenance of the receiving compartment

For an ideal passive pathway, the rate of movement can be written as J = PA(C1 − C2), where P is an effective permeability coefficient, A is area, and C1 and C2 are concentrations in the source and receiving compartments. If P has units of centimetres per second, A is in square centimetres and concentration is expressed in moles per cubic centimetre, the product is in moles per second. The expression assumes that the permeability is constant and that the concentration difference represents the gradient relevant to transfer. It is not the correct general equation for a saturable active carrier, but it clarifies the distinct contributions of exchange area and driving force.

As a numerical illustration, an epithelial sheet with area 10 square centimetres and an effective permeability of 0.001 centimetres per second separates two compartments with concentrations differing by 2 micromoles per cubic centimetre. The calculated passive transfer is 0.02 micromoles per second, or 1.2 micromoles per minute. If half of the exchange area is lost, the predicted rate is 0.6 micromoles per minute, provided the gradient and permeability remain unchanged. If the damaged tissue simultaneously becomes leakier, a particular paracellular solute could behave differently. Changes in permeability and area must therefore be measured separately.

Blood flow contributes to the concentration gradient on the tissue side. When rapidly absorbed glucose enters the interstitium, capillary removal limits local accumulation. If perfusion decreases substantially, the concentration immediately outside the basolateral membrane may rise and reduce favorable glucose exit. Reduced perfusion may also compromise ATP production and alter the operation of ion pumps. These are two mechanisms, one related to removal of absorbed material and another related to metabolic support of epithelial cells. In experimental physiology they must not be conflated. A change in flow can affect both without proving that a particular apical transporter is directly sensitive to pressure.

The structural integrity of the villus also depends on the epithelial barrier. Tight junction permeability varies with the particular ions or molecules involved. A junction that allows a small ion to cross more easily need not allow a protein of much larger size to pass. Conversely, injury that compromises barrier selectivity can lead to loss of normal gradient maintenance even if many brush-border carriers remain present. A measurement of transepithelial electrical resistance gives information about ionic conductance, but it is not a direct census of all routes available to macromolecules. A tracer of known size or charge can provide complementary information.

Absorptive regionalization is best understood as overlapping capacity. Proximal small-intestinal segments are exposed to recently digested food and support substantial absorption of many nutrients. More distal segments recover material remaining in the lumen, and the ileum has specialized roles in bile acid and cobalamin uptake. A regional specialization does not imply that all other nutrient pathways disappear from that segment. When a proximal region is lost, the remaining gut can sometimes increase effective capacity over time. The physiological interpretation of a resection experiment thus depends on both the missing specialized function and the opportunity for compensation by the remaining mucosa. An immediate response and a response after adaptation are not necessarily identical.

3. Transport energetics and the sodium gradient

The intestinal wall uses both transcellular and paracellular routes. A transcellular pathway enters a cell through one membrane and exits through another. A paracellular pathway passes between epithelial cells through junctional spaces. In both cases the driving forces determine the direction of net movement. Diffusion of an uncharged molecule tends to lower its chemical potential, often corresponding to movement from higher to lower concentration in dilute solutions. A charged ion responds to electrical as well as chemical forces. For sodium, the negative intracellular potential of an enterocyte generally favors inward movement in addition to the concentration difference produced by active extrusion.

The basolateral sodium-potassium ATPase couples ATP hydrolysis to extrusion of three sodium ions and uptake of two potassium ions per cycle. It directly consumes chemical energy, and thus is a primary active transport system. Potassium channels and other transport proteins contribute to electrical and ionic steady states. Because the pump maintains low cytosolic sodium, apical sodium entry may be energetically favorable even when an associated nutrient is moving uphill. Secondary active transport exploits that favorable movement. The apical cotransporter itself need not hydrolyse ATP. Its operation nevertheless depends indirectly on metabolism because the ion gradient must be restored after each transport cycle.

For movement of sodium from lumen to cell, the electrochemical free energy per mole is RT ln([Na+]cell/[Na+]lumen) + F(Vcell − Vlumen). R is the gas constant in joules per mole per kelvin; T is absolute temperature; F is the Faraday constant in coulombs per mole; V is electrical potential in volts. This form approximates chemical activities using concentrations. A negative value means inward transfer is favorable. Suppose temperature is 310 K, luminal sodium is 140 mmol/L, cytosolic sodium is 14 mmol/L and the cell is 40 mV negative relative to the lumen. The concentration term is approximately −5.93 kJ/mol, and the electrical term is approximately −3.86 kJ/mol. Their sum is −9.79 kJ/mol. Both terms favor entry, and the negative sign is not an arbitrary convention: it indicates that free energy is released by movement in the specified direction.

For an idealized transporter that obligatorily carries two sodium ions inward with one neutral glucose molecule, the free energy of a full cycle is the sum of twice the sodium term and RT ln([glucose]cell/[glucose]lumen). At the reversible limit the sum is zero. With the illustrative sodium gradient above, the glucose concentration ratio at that limit would be exp(19.58/2.577), or about 2,000. This is an equilibrium upper bound for an idealized coupled process, not the actual gradient in a working intestine. Glucose metabolism, basolateral exit, changing ion gradients, kinetic limitations and leakage prevent the tissue from operating at this theoretical limit. The calculation demonstrates why coupling permits accumulation against a chemical gradient without violating thermodynamics.

Pump inhibition does not instantaneously abolish every absorption pathway. An established sodium gradient can persist briefly, and passive routes may remain. Over time the cell loses the gradient if ongoing sodium entry is not balanced by extrusion. Consequently, a sodium-dependent uptake component declines. Experimental time matters: a measurement immediately after inhibition may differ from one after substantial ionic redistribution. The same inhibitor can also affect cell volume, membrane voltage and viability, so a decline in net flux is not proof that every affected carrier is itself an ATPase.

Coupling, reversibility and interpreting transport measurements

A gradient is stored free energy, not a fixed substance that a cotransporter consumes without consequences. Each transport cycle changes the distribution of the coupled ion and the nutrient. In a closed membrane preparation the driving force can therefore decline. In an intact epithelial cell the basolateral pump and other ion transport mechanisms continually maintain a nonequilibrium state. At steady state, sodium enters and leaves the cell at equal total rates when intracellular sodium content is constant. This does not mean that no sodium is moving. It means the two opposing fluxes balance over the observation interval. The tissue can sustain substantial directional transfer through the cell while keeping its intracellular composition nearly constant.

Coupled transport is reversible in thermodynamic principle. Under unusual imposed conditions, such as an inverted ion gradient and a sufficiently large substrate gradient, the net direction of a reversible carrier may change. The normal direction follows from the combined free-energy changes of all transported species and from carrier kinetics. It is therefore preferable to say that SGLT1 normally mediates luminal uptake in the mammalian intestinal context, rather than to define the molecule as a device that is intrinsically capable of moving glucose in only one direction. The biological architecture and maintained gradients give the pathway its usual directionality.

Electrical coupling introduces an additional observable. Entry of positively charged ions without compensating negative charge produces current. A transporter that carries two sodium ions with a neutral glucose molecule transfers two positive elementary charges per ideal completed inward transport cycle. If an isolated current component of 19.3 microamperes were entirely due to this process, its corresponding glucose molar rate would be I/(2F), where F is about 96,485 coulombs per mole. The result is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol/s, or 0.10 nanomoles per second. This calculation only applies to the specifically isolated current component under the stated stoichiometry. Total tissue current can contain contributions from sodium channels, chloride secretion and other electrogenic pathways, and not every sugar molecule must use the same transport route.

The thermodynamic maximum derived earlier also changes if the membrane potential changes. Using the previous sodium concentrations but setting the cell potential equal to the luminal potential removes the electrical contribution. The sodium concentration term is still favorable, and a perfectly coupled two-sodium glucose carrier can still theoretically establish a glucose concentration ratio of 100 at equilibrium. Thus depolarization does not necessarily abolish secondary active uptake; it reduces the available electrical driving force. Conversely, increasing the magnitude of a negative cell potential can enhance the energetic favorability of inward sodium movement. Actual transport rates also depend on the carrier's voltage-dependent kinetic behavior.

Primary active and secondary active processes should be distinguished from facilitated diffusion by their energetic coupling, not merely by whether they involve a protein. A facilitative glucose carrier may mediate rapid transfer and may saturate, but it does not use an independently favorable ion gradient to maintain an uphill glucose distribution at equilibrium. A cotransporter may also saturate, so saturation by itself cannot distinguish the two. Evidence that uptake depends on an imposed sodium gradient, that substrate accumulates beyond passive equilibrium and that a specific transporter carries both sodium and substrate provides stronger mechanistic support than a saturating concentration-response curve alone.

Some experimental conditions simultaneously alter the driving force and the number of carriers operating at the membrane. A decrease in maximal observed uptake could arise from fewer transport proteins, decreased turnover, reduced effective membrane area or an ion gradient that is no longer maintained. A change in the apparent half-maximal concentration could also reflect interactions with coupled ions or changes in experimental geometry. A student should therefore resist identifying molecular binding affinity directly from every whole-tissue half-saturation value. The parameter is operational: it describes the relationship measured under specified conditions.

4. Carbohydrate absorption and monosaccharide carriers

Most dietary digestible carbohydrates must be reduced to monosaccharides before substantial absorption. Starch hydrolysis produces smaller carbohydrates that are further processed by brush-border enzymes. Lactase hydrolyses lactose into glucose and galactose, whereas sucrase activity yields glucose and fructose from sucrose. The resulting monosaccharides need not use identical membrane transport systems. This is a key example of why chemical classification alone does not identify a physiological route.

In the canonical mammalian intestinal model, SGLT1 on the apical membrane couples the inward movement of sodium to that of glucose or galactose. The human transporter typically couples two sodium ions to one sugar molecule. The sodium electrochemical gradient drives uptake of sugar even when the cytosolic sugar concentration exceeds that in the lumen. Sodium is extruded across the basolateral membrane by sodium-potassium ATPase, and glucose and galactose can exit via facilitated transport mediated principally by GLUT2. These three proteins therefore constitute a linked functional system: apical uptake, maintenance of the driving force and basolateral delivery. Disrupting any one of the three can alter net transepithelial absorption.

Fructose illustrates another mechanism. It enters principally through the facilitative transporter GLUT5 in the mammalian small-intestinal brush border. As a facilitated diffusion pathway, this route depends on the fructose gradient rather than obligatorily coupling entry to sodium. It does not necessarily have a linear flux-concentration relationship because the protein has a finite turnover capacity. Fructose can leave toward the tissue through facilitative mechanisms including GLUT2. The standard teaching model emphasizes these principal localizations. Changes in transporter distribution with luminal conditions and differences among experimental models mean that a rigid assertion that GLUT2 can never be apical is not justified. Exam questions usually specify the canonical model unless they provide additional experimental context.

A carrier-mediated uptake system can be represented approximately by v = Vmax C/(Km + C), with C the substrate concentration, v the uptake rate, Vmax the maximum rate and Km the substrate concentration giving half of that maximum in this simplified kinetic description. At C = Km, uptake is Vmax/2. At C = 9Km, uptake is 0.9Vmax. An increase of ninefold from Km to 9Km therefore increases rate by only 1.8-fold, not ninefold. This relation is a useful model for saturable transport, not a complete thermodynamic description of SGLT1; sodium concentration and voltage can also modify the observed kinetics.

Imagine an isolated intestinal preparation supplied with glucose or fructose at matched concentrations. Replacement of luminal sodium with a poorly transported substitute, while preserving osmotic conditions, should strongly reduce the sodium-dependent component of glucose uptake. The directly sodium-independent fructose component should be less affected initially. A long-duration substitution could impair tissue physiology and indirectly change fructose transfer too. Controls for viability, osmolarity and epithelial electrical properties are necessary to interpret the comparison. A different experiment uses a specific inhibitor of SGLT1: the predicted reduction in glucose uptake supports carrier involvement, provided the inhibitor specificity and alternative pathways are considered.

The historical sodium-glucose coupling hypothesis associated with Robert Crane helped explain why intestinal sodium and water absorption can be enhanced by glucose. The concept linked energy-dependent epithelial physiology to a practical biological problem: a secreting intestine can still retain functioning absorptive pathways. Human intestinal perfusion studies have compared glucose-containing solutions with control solutions in the presence and absence of a secretory stimulus. The resulting measurements support the idea that glucose-sensitive sodium transport and osmotic water movement can coexist with stimulated secretion. A net flux measured across a segment reflects the difference between opposing processes, not an assertion that secretion has stopped.

Substrate specificity and separating enzyme defects from transport defects

The glucose-galactose and fructose distinction is not simply a distinction between two sweet-tasting substances. Molecular recognition depends on the arrangement of hydroxyl groups and other structural features in the sugar. Glucose and galactose differ stereochemically, yet both can serve as substrates for SGLT1. Fructose has a different carbonyl chemistry and is handled mainly by GLUT5 at the apical surface. The selective recognition of related molecules allows experimental comparisons in which the nutrient classes are similar in size but their dependence on a particular ion gradient differs.

Competition is informative only when the competing molecules share a relevant step. If glucose and galactose are presented together at concentrations near a carrier's capacity, they may compete for the sodium-coupled transporter. Fructose supplied at the same time uses a distinct major apical route, so competition at SGLT1 is not the principal explanation for any interaction. However, all three sugars can influence the overall physiological state of the tissue and share some downstream handling. An observed reduction in net glucose flux in the presence of fructose is therefore not sufficient evidence that both bind to the same apical transporter.

A disaccharidase defect and an absorption defect can be compared by a substrate-product challenge. Imagine an intestinal tissue in which glucose and galactose individually cross at normal rates but uptake of labelled carbon from lactose is poor. The result suggests failure to hydrolyse lactose or to deliver it effectively to the relevant enzyme. If lactase activity is measured independently and found reduced, this interpretation is strengthened. Now consider a different tissue that fails to absorb labelled carbon from lactose and also fails to absorb glucose or galactose directly, while fructose transfer remains substantial. The pattern is more compatible with a common glucose-galactose transport defect. These are predictions from the canonical model, not diagnostic rules applicable to every possible tissue disorder.

The consequences of a nonabsorbed sugar are partly osmotic. Hydrolysis itself changes the number of dissolved particles. In a simplified dilute solution, one molecule of lactose yields one molecule each of glucose and galactose. If both products remain in the lumen, the contribution to particle concentration may increase rather than decrease. When the monosaccharides are efficiently removed, however, the luminal osmotic load falls. Thus digestion alone does not guarantee improvement in fluid balance. The coupled sequence of hydrolysis and absorption determines the net outcome.

The time course of the response matters in an intact animal. Rapid removal of glucose from proximal intestine may prevent substantial amounts from reaching distal regions, while poorly absorbed sugars can proceed farther. Microbial fermentation in the distal gut can produce additional osmotically active compounds and gases, and short-chain fatty acids may subsequently be absorbed. These effects are secondary to the original transport problem and can obscure simple interpretations of stool composition. A low concentration of the original sugar in final faeces does not exclude earlier malabsorption if that sugar has been metabolized by microorganisms before collection.

A clear examination strategy is to draw the pathway as a chain of chemically identified substrates. Start with a disaccharide, label the enzyme producing monosaccharides, then identify each apical route, the driving force and basolateral exit. If a question describes a selective perturbation, mark the exact step and trace consequences upstream and downstream. If substrate accumulates upstream but the next product is scarce, the location of the bottleneck is narrowed. If substrate and product accumulate together inside cells, consider a later transfer restriction. This method is more reliable than memorizing isolated transporter names because it accommodates unfamiliar experimental scenarios.

5. Amino acids, dipeptides and tripeptides

Protein digestion delivers a mixture of free amino acids and short peptides to the small intestine. Transport of amino acids must accommodate side chains with different electrical properties and chemical structures. Consequently, a single universal amino acid transporter would be an inadequate model. Several apical carrier systems recognize different or overlapping classes of substrates, including neutral, basic and acidic amino acids. Many depend on sodium gradients, while some use other coupling mechanisms or exchange substrates. A mutation affecting one carrier can therefore produce a selective absorption phenotype rather than abolishing uptake of every amino acid.

PEPT1 provides a complementary route for dipeptides and tripeptides. It uses the electrochemical gradient of protons to drive the movement of small peptides across the apical membrane. This pathway has broad substrate recognition and can deliver the amino acid content of many different short peptides into enterocytes. Cytosolic peptidases usually hydrolyse these peptides, and the liberated amino acids can cross the basolateral surface through several transporter systems. The usual nutrient delivered into portal blood is thus free amino acid even when the absorbed luminal form was a peptide. The apical transport substrate and the vascular transport form need not be identical.

A proton gradient may be maintained in part by apical sodium-hydrogen exchange. This exchanger can use the sodium gradient to move protons toward the lumen. The basolateral sodium-potassium pump contributes indirectly to that sodium gradient. One may therefore identify a chain of energetic dependencies: ATP supports sodium extrusion, sodium entry can support proton extrusion, and proton entry drives peptide uptake. This sequence does not imply that PEPT1 directly transports sodium. It illustrates how an apparently different secondary active transport mechanism can still be dependent on the overall ion homeostasis of the epithelium.

Consider a tissue with impaired uptake of a particular free neutral amino acid but an intact PEPT1 pathway. The same amino acid supplied as part of a transportable dipeptide may enter by the peptide route and become available after intracellular hydrolysis. Conversely, specific inhibition of PEPT1 should decrease the peptide-dependent component without eliminating all free amino acid uptake. To identify the responsible pathway experimentally, compare the disappearance of labelled free amino acid and labelled dipeptide from the lumen, the chemical form of label within the cell, and its appearance in the receiving compartment. Label in a cell may be incorporated into other metabolic products, so analytical separation is important.

Protein breakdown in the lumen and peptide absorption work in parallel. A large dietary polypeptide is generally not transported intact in bulk as a nutritional substrate in adult mammals. Small amounts of larger molecules can be sampled by specialized epithelial and immune mechanisms; this does not make endocytosis the dominant adult nutritional route. Developmental and species-specific exceptions, such as macromolecule transfer in certain neonatal animals, should be distinguished from the normal mature intestinal mechanism. The major physiological conclusion is that multiple convergent routes increase the efficiency of nitrogen acquisition.

Transporter overlap, nitrogen balance and peptide experiments

Amino acids differ in charge state at physiological pH. Their amino and carboxyl groups can exist in ionized forms, and their side chains distinguish neutral, positively charged and negatively charged categories. An epithelial transport protein recognizes a substrate through its molecular structure and electrostatic properties. Consequently, uptake of one free amino acid can be affected by another that uses the same transport system. Overlapping specificity means that the effect of a competitor may vary with the relative expression of alternative carriers. A measured transport deficit for one amino acid cannot automatically be extended to the entire pool of dietary nitrogen.

Peptide transport creates a partly independent entry route. For example, a dipeptide containing two neutral amino acid residues differs from either free amino acid in molecular structure because the residues are joined by a peptide bond. PEPT1 recognizes small peptides through features of the complete substrate rather than simply acting as two free amino acid carriers operating together. Once hydrolysed inside an enterocyte, each absorbed dipeptide can yield two amino acid molecules. This can influence interpretation of rates. An uptake rate of 5 nanomoles of dipeptide per minute corresponds, after complete hydrolysis, to 10 nanomoles of amino acid residues per minute. It does not correspond to 10 nanomoles of transport cycles.

A useful tracer comparison involves radiolabelled free amino acid and a chemically identified peptide containing the same residue. If the tracer is measured without separating chemical forms, radioactivity in the serosal compartment cannot establish whether intact peptide or free amino acid crossed. Chromatographic separation of labelled species can answer that question. The chemical identification of the label is as important as its location. Intracellular conversion of an amino acid to another metabolite can also complicate an experiment if the chosen label tracks an atom rather than the original molecule.

A proton-coupled route is influenced by extracellular pH and by the electrical potential of the cell. A lower luminal pH can increase the favorable proton concentration gradient, but a strong acidic perturbation can affect other proteins, junctional integrity and cell viability. Changes in PEPT1-mediated uptake with pH need to be interpreted within a range that preserves physiological function. A clean experiment changes one driving-force component while monitoring other variables. In a whole tissue, maintenance of local surface pH can differ from the pH measured in the bulk luminal solution because transporters and buffering reactions act near the membrane.

Another important distinction is between protein intake and the amino acid mixture delivered to the body. The intestine uses amino acids for its own protein synthesis and energy metabolism, so the profile in portal blood does not exactly reproduce the composition of dietary protein. Some amino acids are metabolized extensively by intestinal tissue. This does not imply that absorption has failed; it means that the epithelial layer is metabolically active. A complete nitrogen balance must include nitrogen retained in tissue, nitrogen delivered into blood, and material still present in the lumen. Under steady-state conditions, changes in cellular pools can be small, but during a short tracer experiment they may be substantial.

The overlap of free amino acid and peptide routes provides an example of physiological robustness. A system with multiple partially redundant pathways can continue functioning when one pathway is impaired, but the compensation is substrate dependent and finite. If brush-border surface area is severely reduced, both groups of transporters may be lost simultaneously. In such a case the existence of alternative pathways does not guarantee normal total absorption. The nature of the perturbation determines whether compensation is possible.

6. Lipid delivery, micelles and lymphatic transport

Lipids pose a physical challenge distinct from that of sugars and amino acids. Long-chain fatty acids and monoacylglycerols are poorly soluble in water, and the gut lumen contains a substantial aqueous phase. Bile salts are amphipathic molecules, with hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions. They help disperse lipid and form mixed micelles with products of lipid digestion. A micelle is a small molecular aggregate, not a membrane-bound vesicle containing a complete intestinal meal. The distinction between an emulsified oil droplet and a mixed micelle matters: the first presents surface area for enzymatic hydrolysis; the second helps deliver hydrolysis products through the aqueous environment near the epithelial surface.

Pancreatic lipase and colipase enable digestion of dietary triglycerides at an oil-water interface. The resulting lipid products associate with bile salts and other amphipathic molecules. Mixed micelles improve effective delivery across the unstirred aqueous layer adjacent to the brush border. Lipid molecules can leave the micelle and enter epithelial cells through mechanisms involving membrane partitioning and proteins such as fatty acid transport-related systems. The micelle itself does not generally cross the apical membrane intact. Most bile salts continue along the lumen and are efficiently recovered in the terminal ileum for enterohepatic recycling. Thus the lipid cargo and its luminal carrier have distinct fates.

Long-chain fatty acids and monoacylglycerols entering the enterocyte are used to resynthesize triacylglycerols. Lipid assembly in the endoplasmic reticulum involves incorporation of lipids into particles containing apolipoprotein B-48, with a critical role for microsomal triglyceride transfer protein. These nascent particles are further processed and secreted basolaterally as chylomicrons. A chylomicron is structurally different from a luminal micelle. It has a hydrophobic lipid core surrounded by a surface monolayer containing amphipathic lipids and apolipoproteins, enabling lipid transport in an aqueous environment. It is synthesized by the enterocyte rather than being directly absorbed from the food.

Chylomicrons move from the villous interstitium into lacteals. Intestinal lymph travels through collecting lymphatic vessels and ultimately reaches the systemic venous circulation via the thoracic duct in the usual human anatomical arrangement. This route initially bypasses the hepatic portal circulation. Subsequently, circulating lipoprotein lipase facilitates delivery of fatty acids to tissues, and chylomicron remnants can be taken up by the liver. Detailed lipid metabolism belongs in biomolecule chapters; the essential point here is that the route of first vascular appearance differs from that of water-soluble nutrients.

Short-chain and many medium-chain fatty acids are relatively water soluble and can reach portal blood directly, often associated with albumin. This is a tendency related to chain length and metabolic handling rather than a universal rule for every fatty acid under every feeding condition. Short-chain fatty acids produced by microbial fermentation in the colon are absorbed and can be metabolized locally or delivered toward the liver. A tracer study comparing a labelled long-chain fatty acid with a labelled shorter-chain fatty acid can reveal different distributions of label between lymph and portal blood. To draw a mechanistic conclusion, the investigator must also distinguish original tracer from its metabolic products and consider the observation interval.

If bile delivery decreases, absorption of long-chain lipids and fat-soluble vitamins can be impaired even when the enterocyte retains the capacity to assemble chylomicrons. If intracellular lipoprotein assembly is disrupted, luminal micellar delivery and initial epithelial entry can persist while subsequent lymphatic export decreases. The two defects can produce a similar decrease in final lipid delivery despite acting at different steps. Measuring lipid in the lumen, tissue and lymph helps distinguish the location of the bottleneck. This staged approach is a general strategy for understanding physiological transport rather than memorizing a single association between fat and lymph.

The physical chemistry of intestinal lipid delivery

A large lipid droplet has relatively little surface area per unit volume. If a sphere has radius r, its surface area is 4πr² and its volume is 4πr³/3, giving a surface-to-volume ratio of 3/r. Dividing a fixed lipid volume into smaller droplets therefore increases the total oil-water interfacial area. If each original sphere is replaced by spheres with one-tenth its radius, the total surface available per unit lipid volume increases tenfold. This does not mean that the lipase-catalyzed reaction must proceed ten times faster in vivo. Enzyme concentration, adsorption at the interface, colipase, mixing and product removal can each become limiting. The geometric result explains one advantage of emulsification without confusing it with hydrolysis itself.

A detergent-like molecule can associate with lipids because its structure includes regions with different affinities for water and nonpolar substances. Bile salts help stabilize dispersed lipid and support the formation of mixed micelles, which are compositionally dynamic. Long-chain lipid products move between aggregate-associated and free forms. A micelle can transport many different types of molecules simultaneously, including fatty acids, monoacylglycerols and cholesterol. Its capacity depends on the composition of luminal fluid and the amount of available amphipathic material. Thus total lipid concentration in the lumen may be very high while the freely dissolved fraction is small. Micellar delivery helps overcome the low solubility of the hydrophobic products in the aqueous phase adjacent to the membrane.

Imagine that a lipid product crosses the membrane very rapidly whenever it reaches the apical surface. The concentration of freely available lipid near the surface can become depleted. If micelles deliver lipid toward that region and release some of their cargo, the total rate of delivery can increase even though the freely dissolved concentration remains low. Micelles need not be engulfed to perform this function. The analogous situation in blood is the binding of a poorly water-soluble compound to a carrier protein: the associated pool increases the amount that can be transported through an aqueous compartment. The analogy has limits, because a micellar aggregate is not a protein and has different exchange and assembly properties.

Several defects can interfere with this sequence before lipids encounter enterocytes. A reduction in pancreatic lipolytic activity decreases generation of the appropriate products. A reduction in bile salt availability limits the efficiency of mixed micellar delivery, even if hydrolysis proceeds to some extent. Reduced mixing changes the contact between secretions and lipid droplets. Abnormally rapid movement of intestinal contents can shorten the time available for hydrolysis and uptake. These perturbations need not have identical signatures. The chemical composition of material remaining in the lumen may distinguish undigested triglyceride from products that were generated successfully but not efficiently absorbed.

The enterohepatic circulation conserves a physiologically useful pool of bile acids. Bile acids are secreted from the liver into bile, enter the intestinal lumen, and are largely recovered before elimination. The terminal ileum has a specialized sodium-dependent apical bile acid transport system; absorbed bile acids return via portal blood. Hepatic uptake and resecretion support repeated cycles of use. A failure in ileal reclamation can increase fecal bile acid loss. If the liver cannot fully compensate through synthesis, the available bile acid pool can decline and micellar delivery may become less effective. The importance of this mechanism depends on the magnitude and duration of the loss. It also illustrates why damage to a distal specialized absorptive segment may alter the efficiency of digestion and absorption in more proximal regions during later meals.

The luminal and tissue routes must remain separate when drawing this cycle. Bile acids leave the liver in bile and return predominantly through portal circulation. Dietary long-chain lipids enter the enterocyte, where they are processed and packaged into chylomicrons, which leave predominantly through lymph. Both processes are linked during digestion, but they do not represent a single circulating particle moving through the gut wall. This is a particularly useful contrast for interpreting a question containing two tracers, one placed in the bile acid pool and another in dietary long-chain triglyceride.

Intracellular lipid allocation and identifying a blocked step

The enterocyte must reconcile the chemical advantages of lipid transport with the requirement to prevent uncontrolled accumulation of hydrophobic material. Re-esterification of long-chain fatty acids and monoacylglycerols produces neutral lipids that can be allocated to intracellular storage or lipoprotein assembly. Packaging allows the cell to release a large amount of hydrophobic material into an aqueous tissue compartment in a form stabilized by surface lipids and proteins. The balance between storage and export can vary with nutrient delivery and the physiological state of the cell. An enterocyte that contains lipid droplets is therefore not necessarily defective; the significance of storage depends on its magnitude, duration and relationship to normal export.

Apolipoprotein B-48 provides a structural component of the intestinal chylomicron. In the conventional mammalian mechanism, successful lipidation of the nascent apolipoprotein depends on microsomal triglyceride transfer protein. If lipidation fails, the availability of lipid substrate alone cannot guarantee assembly of a functional particle. This creates an experimental contrast with a defect in luminal micelle formation. In the latter, less lipid substrate may arrive at the cell. In the former, lipid may arrive and be resynthesized but not efficiently leave in the expected particle form. A carefully chosen tracer experiment can discriminate upstream failure of substrate delivery from downstream failure of particle assembly.

An illustrative experiment introduces 100 arbitrary units of label as a long-chain fatty acid. After a defined period, a control preparation retains 15 units in the lumen, contains 20 units in epithelium and has released 55 units into collected lymph, with 10 units recovered in other measured products or compartments. A second preparation retains 15 units in the lumen, contains 60 units in epithelium, and releases 15 units into lymph, with the other 10 units unchanged. The identical luminal retention argues against a large difference in total luminal removal over the measurement period. The redistribution between epithelium and lymph suggests impaired transfer downstream of entry. It does not establish which molecular assembly protein is responsible; further identification of intracellular lipid forms and secreted particles is required.

The difference between a chylomicron and a typical cell membrane is structural as well as functional. A bilayer encloses an aqueous cellular compartment, whereas a chylomicron surface monolayer surrounds a predominantly hydrophobic core. Polar headgroups can interact with the surrounding water while nonpolar regions interact with core lipids. This organization explains why a large mass of triacylglycerol can be conveyed through blood without remaining as an unstable bulk oil droplet. Lipoproteins differ in origin, composition and physiological fate. The intestinal chylomicron is associated primarily with recently absorbed lipid, whereas other classes have different origins and functions discussed elsewhere in the curriculum.

Lymphatic export introduces a time delay between uptake and systemic appearance. A water-soluble nutrient released into a blood capillary can reach the portal circulation rapidly, while chylomicron transport requires assembly, secretion and movement through lymphatic vessels. Consequently, comparing systemic concentrations at one early time point can give a misleading impression of total absorption. Time-integrated recovery, tissue-associated tracer and route-specific sampling can provide a more informative comparison. A delayed appearance of labelled lipid is not necessarily proof of decreased cumulative absorption.

Shorter-chain fatty acids provide a contrast because their greater aqueous solubility reduces dependence on incorporation into chylomicrons. Their transfer is not exclusively determined by chain length: esterification, local metabolism and experimental feeding conditions also influence the final route. A simplified question may specify a representative long-chain fatty acid and a representative medium-chain fatty acid, asking which is more likely to appear first in portal blood. The expected comparison is valid as a predominant pathway, not as an absolute statement about every molecule. In the colon, fermentation-derived acetate, propionate and butyrate provide a further example of short-chain products whose absorption and metabolic use depend on epithelial and microbial physiology. Detailed community ecology belongs to the later holobiont subunit.

7. Vitamins, iron and calcium

Vitamins are organic compounds required in relatively small amounts for normal physiological function. Their absorptive routes depend on chemical properties rather than on their common nutritional classification. Vitamins A, D, E and K are fat soluble and usually benefit from bile salt-mediated micellar delivery along with dietary lipid. They may be incorporated into chylomicrons during intestinal transport. Water-soluble vitamins generally use other uptake pathways, including specific carriers. The distinction is a useful starting point but should not be treated as an exhaustive rule for each compound, because different molecular forms, dose and tissue conditions affect transport.

Vitamin B12 absorption is an instructive multiorgan sequence. Food-associated cobalamin is liberated from proteins during digestion and binds salivary and gastric haptocorrin. Pancreatic proteolysis in the small intestine frees cobalamin for binding to intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein produced by gastric parietal cells. The intrinsic factor-cobalamin complex reaches the terminal ileum, where receptor-mediated uptake involving cubilin and amnionless allows internalization. Intrinsic factor is subsequently degraded within the cell, and absorbed cobalamin is delivered into circulation associated with transport proteins including transcobalamin. Gastric intrinsic factor production and ileal absorption therefore represent separate anatomical steps of a single process.

An experimental defect in the stomach and a defect in the terminal ileum can both reduce normal B12 uptake. An ileal epithelial receptor defect cannot be corrected simply by providing more intrinsic factor if receptor-mediated uptake remains impaired. Conversely, an intrinsic factor deficiency may reduce delivery of the ligand complex to an otherwise functional ileal receptor. At high oral concentrations a small passive absorption component can occur independently of intrinsic factor. This exception is important when interpreting physiological claims: intrinsic factor is crucial for normal efficient absorption, but it is inaccurate to say absolutely no cobalamin can cross the intestinal epithelium without it.

Iron illustrates the separation of apical uptake from systemic delivery. Nonheme dietary iron is often present in ferric forms and is more readily transported by the divalent metal transporter DMT1 after conversion to ferrous iron. Luminal conditions and brush-border reductase activity contribute to that conversion. Once within the enterocyte, iron can enter a ferritin storage pool or be exported through basolateral ferroportin. Oxidation of exported ferrous iron facilitates its binding in ferric form to transferrin in blood. Heme-derived iron has partially distinct uptake and processing pathways; the precise molecular identity of all heme entry mechanisms should not be presented as settled.

Hepcidin, a liver-derived regulatory peptide, reduces iron export by binding ferroportin and promoting loss of its functional activity, including internalization and degradation. When hepcidin is elevated, iron can be retained within enterocytes even if apical uptake continues. Because epithelial cells are eventually shed, retention can reduce net systemic acquisition. Conversely, iron deficiency commonly favors lower hepcidin and greater capacity for iron delivery into blood. This is a useful example of a systemic feedback signal controlling one step in a transepithelial transport pathway. It also explains why tissue-associated iron tracer and circulating iron tracer can change in opposite directions.

Calcium enters the internal environment by transcellular and paracellular routes. The classical regulated transcellular pathway includes entry through apical calcium channels such as TRPV6, intracellular calcium handling involving calbindin in appropriate contexts, and basolateral extrusion mediated by calcium pumps and other transport mechanisms. Calcitriol, the active hormonal form of vitamin D, enhances intestinal calcium absorption by regulating relevant transport and epithelial processes. This mechanism is especially important when dietary calcium availability is limited. Passive paracellular transport can contribute substantially when luminal calcium concentration is high. The relative contributions are conditional, and an examination question that assumes every calcium ion necessarily traverses TRPV6 oversimplifies the physiology.

These micronutrient pathways reveal three distinct principles: selective complex recognition in B12 uptake, regulated basolateral export in iron absorption, and the coexistence of regulated transcellular and passive paracellular routes in calcium absorption. A useful prediction about absorption must identify which stage is altered, rather than treating all micronutrients as though they entered blood by one generic diffusion process.

Why micronutrient absorption is regulated

An animal must acquire enough of an essential micronutrient while limiting potentially harmful excess. Unlike a glucose molecule that can contribute energy or be stored in large pools of carbohydrate and lipid, some mineral ions are needed in smaller amounts and can become harmful when free concentrations increase. Control of intestinal entry and systemic delivery is therefore particularly important. The regulated export of iron is a classic example. Iron is required for haem proteins and numerous enzymes, but unbound redox-active iron can participate in undesirable chemical reactions. Binding, storage and controlled export help preserve availability while limiting exposure to unregulated iron.

When interpreting dietary iron, total elemental content should not be equated with bioavailability. A food can contain abundant iron in poorly soluble or tightly bound forms. Luminal pH, ligands and redox conditions affect how much nonheme iron is accessible to the absorptive pathway. A reducing environment can favor conversion of ferric iron to ferrous iron for DMT1-mediated uptake. Some dietary ligands stabilize soluble forms, whereas others can form complexes that limit access. This means that chemical analysis of a meal does not directly predict how much iron reaches blood. Nutrient form, epithelial transport and systemic regulation all contribute.

Ferritin storage and ferroportin export represent different fates of an intracellular iron pool. The availability of an apical transport pathway does not establish the magnitude of systemic iron uptake because ferroportin is subject to regulation. Consider two epithelial cells with identical rates of iron entry during a short interval. If one exports most incoming iron while the other stores much of it, the cells deliver different amounts to blood. In a longer experiment, the retained iron may leave the body with shed epithelial cells. This is why the body's control of a basolateral export protein can influence whole-animal absorption even when the initial luminal membrane transport remains possible.

Hepcidin links the liver's regulatory role to the intestinal barrier. An increase in functional hepcidin generally reduces ferroportin-mediated export, while a reduction in hepcidin can favor iron mobilization. Inflammatory signals can increase hepcidin, illustrating how a non-nutritional condition may alter systemic iron availability. Iron demand associated with red cell production also influences the regulatory system. The detailed systemic network is outside the present node, but the transport-level inference is directly examinable. A high iron content within duodenal epithelium is not proof that circulating iron delivery is high; the direction of the flux across the basolateral membrane remains decisive.

The calcium pathway illustrates a different balance between regulated cellular uptake and concentration-dependent paracellular movement. Calcium ions carry two positive charges, and cytosolic free calcium serves as an important signaling variable. A large unbuffered rise in intracellular calcium could interfere with signaling and other cellular functions. A transcellular pathway therefore requires coordinated entry, intracellular handling and extrusion. Calbindin can contribute to intracellular buffering and movement in the classical intestinal model. Basolateral extrusion helps maintain low free cytosolic calcium while supplying the extracellular fluid. The relevant transport proteins and their relative importance may vary by species, segment and physiological condition.

Calcitriol influences intestinal calcium handling by changing expression and function of epithelial components. Its effects are not restricted to a single channel in every biological context. Suppose dietary calcium is low and an experimental perturbation prevents the normal enhancement of transcellular absorption. A reduction in fractional uptake is plausible even when a large concentration gradient could support passive transfer under a different condition. Conversely, supplying a higher luminal calcium concentration may increase passive paracellular contribution. This does not mean that hormonal control becomes physiologically irrelevant; it means that the proportion of transfer assigned to the two pathways can change with substrate availability.

A quantitative example makes the distinction clear. If a tissue transfers 3 units of calcium by a regulated transcellular pathway and 1 unit by a passive pathway at low luminal calcium, total transfer is 4 units and the transcellular share is 75 percent. At higher luminal calcium, suppose the regulated route remains near 3 units while the passive route increases to 7 units. Total transfer is 10 units and the regulated share falls to 30 percent. The regulated route has not stopped functioning. Its proportional contribution has fallen because the other route has increased. This reasoning applies more broadly whenever a saturable carrier and a concentration-dependent alternative pathway operate in parallel. The numbers are hypothetical and not measurements of human calcium absorption.

The vitamin B12 pathway adds an important time dimension. Failure of ongoing absorption need not produce an immediate systemic deficiency because existing body stores and recycling can buffer the effect. An absorption test addresses current acquisition from the intestine; a measurement of the body's status integrates prior intake, storage, use and loss. The two observations need not change simultaneously. For example, an experimental reduction in intrinsic factor-mediated uptake could be detected in a tracer study before the total cobalamin pool becomes substantially depleted. This is not peculiar to B12; the timing of a physiological consequence depends on the size and turnover of the relevant reserve.

Other water-soluble vitamins are often transported through selective membrane carriers rather than a uniform passive route. Folate forms can require processing before uptake, and vitamin C transport depends on its chemical form. It is useful to recognize that the term water soluble describes a physicochemical property, not the identity or energetics of a transporter. Some compounds may use sodium-dependent transport, others proton-dependent pathways or other carrier mechanisms. A claim that all water-soluble vitamins freely diffuse across the lipid bilayer is not supported by that classification. A charged or highly polar molecule may have poor intrinsic membrane permeability even if it dissolves readily in luminal fluid.

The major distinction between fat-soluble and water-soluble vitamin handling therefore concerns the initial delivery environment and the prevalent downstream transport route. Lipid-associated vitamin delivery can decline when bile availability or fat absorption is impaired. B12 provides a prominent exception to a simplistic water-soluble category because of its specialized binding-protein and receptor sequence. Iron and calcium illustrate yet other patterns: mineral absorption depends on ionic form, carrier selectivity, epithelial export and regulation. The student should be able to explain each pathway independently and compare the stages at which a perturbation would be expected to act.

8. Water, electrolytes and colonic recovery

Intestinal fluid balance depends on both exogenous water intake and endogenous secretions. Saliva, gastric juice, pancreatic juice, bile and intestinal secretions contribute fluid to the lumen. Much of this water must be recovered; otherwise daily losses would greatly exceed the volume contained in food and drink alone. The gastrointestinal tract is therefore an exchange organ, with simultaneous absorptive and secretory fluxes. Measuring net luminal water loss does not reveal the total amount absorbed in one direction unless the opposing secretion is separately quantified.

Sodium absorption is supported by nutrient-coupled cotransport, sodium-hydrogen exchange and other mechanisms. In some regions coordinated sodium-hydrogen and chloride-bicarbonate exchange produces net sodium chloride absorption with little direct net electrical charge transfer. In distal colonic epithelium, electrogenic sodium entry through ENaC can contribute to sodium conservation under aldosterone-sensitive regulation. The basolateral sodium-potassium pump maintains the gradient supporting these routes. Chloride may follow through junctions or use transcellular transport mechanisms, depending on segment and electrochemical conditions. Charge balance and electroneutrality impose constraints on sustained net ion transfer.

Water moves in response to differences in water chemical potential, commonly described in terms of osmotic and hydrostatic gradients. Transport of sodium, chloride and organic solutes into the tissue can create conditions favoring water movement from the lumen. Aquaporins and paracellular pathways contribute to water permeability. The statement that water follows solute is useful when the solute transport produces a suitable effective osmotic gradient, but it is not a universal directional rule. A large concentration of poorly absorbed solute in the lumen can instead retain water or favor movement into the lumen.

Secretion is essential for hydration of luminal contents and normal digestive processes. In a conventional intestinal chloride-secretory model, basolateral sodium-potassium-chloride cotransport accumulates chloride in epithelial cells, and apical CFTR channels permit chloride exit when activated. Sodium and water can follow in response to the resulting electrochemical and osmotic conditions. Cholera toxin is a canonical example of a stimulus that promotes excessive secretion via cAMP-dependent pathways. Importantly, the sodium-glucose cotransporter can remain functional during such secretion. Thus the observation that an epithelium is secreting fluid does not mean all absorptive pathways have switched off.

Osmotic and secretory mechanisms can be distinguished conceptually. An unabsorbed carbohydrate creates a luminal osmotic load; fermentation of that carbohydrate can further alter the chemical contents of the colon. A secretory stimulus increases the active epithelial movement of ions toward the lumen. In practice both mechanisms can coexist and the measured net water movement reflects their combined effects. Experimental interpretation should identify which solutes are retained, which transport proteins are activated, and whether the observed change persists when the luminal substrate is removed.

For a simplified fluid balance, suppose 8.0 L of fluid enters a small-intestinal segment system per day, 6.5 L is recovered there, and the colon receives the remaining 1.5 L. If the colon recovers 1.3 L, the final luminal loss is 0.2 L/day. If small-intestinal recovery decreases by 1.0 L and colonic recovery remains fixed, loss rises to 1.2 L/day. These are illustrative values, not measured physiological constants. The calculation shows that a modest decrease in the fraction absorbed upstream can produce a disproportionately large change in final loss. It does not account for compensatory changes in colonic absorption or intestinal secretion.

9. Portal circulation, hepatic first pass and experimental inference

Most absorbed sugars and amino acids enter villous blood capillaries and are conveyed by mesenteric venous drainage toward the hepatic portal vein. The liver is thus exposed to nutrient concentrations that may differ from those in systemic arterial blood. Hepatic uptake, storage, conversion and release help regulate the composition of blood subsequently delivered to tissues. First-pass processing is not synonymous with complete extraction: the fraction of a nutrient removed depends on hepatic metabolic state, delivery rate and other properties of the substance. In contrast, chylomicrons initially enter the lymphatic circulation and subsequently the systemic venous blood.

A concentration measurement in portal blood alone is insufficient to establish the absolute delivery rate of a nutrient, because blood flow contributes to transport. In an ideal steady-state organ balance, net release of a substance into blood equals flow multiplied by the venous-minus-arterial concentration difference. If mesenteric flow is 0.50 L/min and the concentration of a nutrient is 2 mmol/L higher in venous than arterial blood, net release is 1 mmol/min. The calculation assumes a representative inflow and outflow, conservation of measured material over the observation period, and consistent use of blood or plasma concentration with the corresponding flow. It describes net release from the sampled territory, which can be smaller than total luminal absorption because some nutrient is metabolized within the intestine.

An Ussing chamber provides a conceptual model of directional epithelial transport. A sheet of epithelium separates two bathing solutions, conventionally representing mucosal and serosal sides. Electrical potential and current can be monitored while ionic composition is controlled. Under voltage-clamped conditions, changes in short-circuit current can reflect changes in net electrogenic ion movement. Addition of glucose to the mucosal solution may increase sodium-coupled electrogenic uptake. However, electrical current is not a direct measure of total glucose flux because other ionic currents contribute, and electrically neutral pathways are not represented directly. Combining electrical observations with labelled nutrient flux strengthens interpretation.

Brush-border membrane vesicles offer a different kind of evidence. An experimentally imposed sodium gradient can transiently drive substrate accumulation in the vesicles. Uptake may exceed its eventual equilibrium value while the gradient is strong, creating an overshoot. As the gradient dissipates, substrate redistributes. Such observations support coupling between the ion gradient and substrate movement even in a preparation separated from cellular ATP generation. Vesicle orientation, integrity and the absence of basolateral structures limit extrapolation to net transport across the whole intestine.

Consider a hypothetical tracer experiment in which a nutrient enters isolated enterocytes normally but its appearance on the serosal side falls sharply after a targeted perturbation. The first explanation to test is a defect in intracellular processing or basolateral export, not necessarily failure of apical uptake. Conversely, loss of initial uptake with preserved cellular viability and normal downstream export capacity implicates the luminal or apical step. These predictions are hypotheses that must be tested by measurements at multiple locations, rather than conclusions obtained by naming a transporter from a single final endpoint.

The integrated view of absorption prepares the student for energy balance. Dietary energy becomes metabolically available only after digestion and acquisition across the epithelial barrier; the amount absorbed is not necessarily the amount stored. The liver, adipose tissue, muscle and other organs determine subsequent use of absorbed substrates in relation to energy demands. Those processes belong to the next chapter. Here, the central conclusion is that epithelial structure, membrane energetics, molecular selectivity, vascular architecture and whole-body regulation jointly determine nutrient delivery.

Misconceptions

Digestion equals absorption
Digestion changes the form of food; absorption crosses the epithelial barrier.
All nutrient uptake is passive
Secondary active carriers can drive uptake uphill using ion gradients.
SGLT1 hydrolyses ATP
Its sodium gradient is sustained indirectly by the ATPase.
Fructose uses SGLT1
The major canonical apical pathway is GLUT5.
PEPT1 transports sodium directly
PEPT1 couples peptides to protons.
Micelles enter lymph intact
Micelles deliver luminal lipids; enterocytes synthesize chylomicrons.
Every fatty acid enters lymph
Many shorter-chain fatty acids preferentially enter portal blood.
Intrinsic factor is made in the ileum
It is produced by gastric parietal cells; the complex is absorbed in the terminal ileum.
DMT1 exports iron to blood
Ferroportin mediates cellular iron export.
All calcium crosses TRPV6
Paracellular absorption also contributes.
Secretion switches all absorption off
Secretory and absorptive fluxes may coexist.
Water is actively pumped by ATP
Water movement follows chemical potential gradients.
Tissue tracer equals net absorption
Basolateral transfer and cellular metabolism must be assessed.
Portal concentration equals flux
Blood flow and the inflow concentration are needed.
Microvilli and villi are identical
Villi are tissue projections; microvilli are membrane projections.

Practice questions

1. Part B

Which protein directly hydrolyses ATP to maintain the sodium gradient?

Answer and explanation

Basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase. SGLT1 uses the gradient rather than hydrolysing ATP.

2. Part B

Which apical transporter is associated with fructose uptake?

Answer and explanation

GLUT5 is the principal facilitative fructose transporter in the conventional model.

3. Part B

What is the usual human SGLT1 coupling ratio?

Answer and explanation

Two sodium ions per glucose or galactose molecule.

4. Part B

Which ion directly drives PEPT1?

Answer and explanation

Protons. Sodium supports the proton gradient indirectly through epithelial ion exchange.

5. Part B

Which anatomical structure receives most newly secreted intestinal chylomicrons?

Answer and explanation

Villous lacteals; most glucose enters the blood capillary instead.

6. Part B

Where does receptor-mediated intrinsic factor-cobalamin uptake occur?

Answer and explanation

Terminal ileum; intrinsic factor is produced in the stomach.

7. Part B

Which protein mediates basolateral iron export?

Answer and explanation

Ferroportin. DMT1 principally mediates apical uptake of ferrous iron.

8. Part B

Which channel contributes to electrogenic chloride secretion?

Answer and explanation

CFTR on the apical surface in the canonical secretory model.

9. Part C

A tracer accumulates in enterocytes but disappears from serosal fluid after a selective intervention. Which stage is most directly implicated?

Answer and explanation

Intracellular processing or basolateral release; initial entry was preserved. Distinguish these possibilities by identifying the chemical form of tissue label.

10. Part C

Luminal sodium replacement reduces glucose uptake rapidly but has little early effect on fructose. Explain.

Answer and explanation

SGLT1 uses sodium coupling, whereas GLUT5 uses facilitated diffusion. Osmolarity, viability and nonspecific voltage changes require controls.

11. Part C

At C = 4Km, what fraction of maximum rate is predicted by the simple saturable model?

Answer and explanation

4/(1+4) = 0.8. The model assumes fixed conditions for other factors that influence rate.

12. Part C

Portal blood flow is 0.4 L/min and the venous-minus-arterial glucose difference is 3 mmol/L. Find net release.

Answer and explanation

0.4 × 3 = 1.2 mmol/min; this is not gross luminal uptake if intestinal metabolism occurs.

13. Part C

Bile delivery is reduced while epithelial chylomicron assembly is intact. Predict the change in labelled long-chain fatty acid transfer.

Answer and explanation

Lymphatic appearance is likely reduced because micellar delivery to the brush border is impaired upstream of assembly.

14. Part C

Hepcidin increases during an experiment. Iron tracer rises in epithelium while portal iron tracer falls. Explain.

Answer and explanation

Ferroportin export is inhibited; entry can continue while delivery declines. Tissue uptake is not equivalent to systemic acquisition.

15. Part C

A preparation displays a glucose-dependent increase in short-circuit current. Can total glucose absorption be calculated directly from that current?

Answer and explanation

Not without isolating the SGLT1-related current and accounting for stoichiometry and other transport routes. Current measures charge rather than all solute flux.

16. Part C

Intestinal input is 7 L/day and absorption is 6 L/day; secretion adds another 0.5 L/day not included in input. What is output?

Answer and explanation

7 + 0.5 − 6 = 1.5 L/day. The definition of input must avoid counting secretion twice.
