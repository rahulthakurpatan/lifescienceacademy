1. Why a cell needs an internal delivery system

A pancreatic cell secretes digestive enzymes, a neuron releases a neurotransmitter, and a fibroblast exports collagen. Each accomplishes a difficult physical task: a large molecule must travel from the region where it is made to a particular destination, but the aqueous interior of each membrane-bound compartment is separated from the cytosol by a hydrophobic lipid bilayer. A large folded protein cannot simply dissolve in that bilayer and diffuse through it. If all newly made proteins accumulated in the cytosol or if every internal membrane fused indiscriminately, the cell could not preserve the different chemical environments on which these functions depend.

Consider a receptor on the surface of a cell. Its ligand-binding domain must face the extracellular fluid, whereas a signaling domain may face the cytosol. Both domains are made by the same ribosome. To become functional, the receptor must be inserted into a membrane with the correct orientation, travel to the surface, and eventually be removed or recycled. In contrast, an enzyme that digests proteins inside a lysosome must be sent to the lysosomal lumen rather than released outside. Neither protein can reach its intended location merely because it was synthesized correctly. The sequence of amino acids contains information not only about the folded protein but often about where that protein should go.

There are three distinct physical solutions to intracellular transport. Gated transport through nuclear pores connects nucleus and cytosol and can accommodate folded macromolecules. Transmembrane transport across protein translocators introduces selected proteins into compartments such as the ER and mitochondria; depending on the system, the transported protein may need to be unfolded or partially unfolded. Vesicular transport, the central subject of this chapter, encloses soluble cargo in a small membrane-bound carrier or incorporates membrane cargo into its surface. The carrier buds from one compartment and delivers its contents by fusing with another. No soluble protein inside the vesicle has to cross its lipid bilayer during that journey.

The secretory and endocytic pathways form a connected network rather than a straight pipeline. Newly made proteins and lipids travel outward from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), while material enters from the cell surface through endocytosis. Endosomes serve as sorting stations; their contents may be recycled, returned to the Golgi or delivered for degradation. Many of the proteins that perform the delivery must themselves be recycled. The cell therefore maintains differences between compartments in the face of continuous traffic, not because their membranes are static.

The key questions are how cargo is selected, how a small carrier is formed, how its destination is recognized, how two lipid bilayers merge without leakage, and how the cell adjusts the rate and direction of traffic. Each question refers to a different stage of a single coordinated cycle. A defect at any one stage can leave the protein at the wrong location even when all the other stages are intact.

2. Localization signals and the first decision: entry into the ER

Protein sorting starts before a vesicle exists. Most nuclear-encoded proteins begin synthesis on cytosolic ribosomes. A ribosome is not intrinsically an ER ribosome or a free ribosome; the destination of its growing polypeptide determines whether it is temporarily recruited to the ER surface. A newly emerging hydrophobic signal sequence on a secretory protein is recognized by the signal recognition particle (SRP). SRP binds both the signal and the ribosome and transiently slows elongation. The complex is directed to the SRP receptor on the cytosolic face of the ER, where the ribosome is transferred to the Sec61 translocon. GTP binding and hydrolysis by SRP and its receptor coordinate this transfer and reset the components.

Translation resumes while the chain passes through the channel. A typical soluble secretory protein carries an amino-terminal signal peptide that is cut by signal peptidase on the lumenal side. The mature protein then becomes a soluble resident of the ER lumen, at least initially. It can undergo folding, disulfide formation and glycosylation there. Removing an ER signal sequence usually leaves the corresponding soluble protein in the cytosol instead of sending it directly to the Golgi. Conversely, attaching a functional ER signal to a suitable cytosolic reporter can send the reporter into the secretory pathway. These manipulations illustrate necessity and sufficiency, but successful targeting also depends on signal accessibility and the folding characteristics of the reporter.

Membrane proteins pose an additional problem: a hydrophobic segment must leave the translocon laterally and remain within the bilayer, while hydrophilic portions must be directed to the appropriate side. A stop-transfer sequence arrests transfer of the growing chain through Sec61 and becomes a membrane-spanning helix. A protein with a cleaved amino-terminal signal peptide followed by a stop-transfer sequence commonly has an N-terminal lumenal domain and a C-terminal cytosolic domain (type I). An internal signal-anchor can both target the ER and remain in the membrane. Its orientation depends on the sequence and the distribution of nearby charges, with positively charged residues tending to remain on the cytosolic side. Thus a type II protein commonly has an N-terminal cytosolic domain; a type III protein can have the opposite orientation. Multiple start and stop transfer elements generate multipass proteins, although actual insertion can also require accessory machinery.

It is useful to distinguish the signal for entering the ER from the signals that operate after entry. A signal peptide does not by itself guarantee lysosomal localization, nor is a sequence that promotes clathrin-mediated internalization an ER-entry signal. A membrane receptor might carry both an internal signal-anchor for ER insertion and a tyrosine-containing motif in its cytosolic tail for endocytosis. A lumenal lysosomal enzyme can acquire a carbohydrate-based address later in the Golgi. The order in which sorting signals become accessible is therefore part of their biological meaning.

Not all proteins use the secretory pathway. A nuclear localization signal is read by nuclear transport machinery, many mitochondrial and plastid precursor proteins use organelle-specific import signals, and peroxisomal proteins can be imported using peroxisomal targeting sequences. Some unconventional secretory proteins lack a classical ER signal. These exceptions matter because the familiar ER–Golgi route is not a universal explanation for every protein found outside the cytosol. The detailed translocation mechanisms of those organelles are treated separately; here the central question is what happens to cargo once it has reached the endomembrane system.

3. Membrane sidedness is preserved during budding and fusion

Imagine coloring the two faces of an ER membrane differently. The cytosolic leaflet always faces the cytosol; the opposite leaflet faces the ER lumen. When a region buds into the cytosol, the cytosolic face remains outside the vesicle and the lumenal face lines its inside. When that vesicle merges with a target membrane, the two cytosolic leaflets join each other and the two lumenal leaflets join each other. There is no need to flip the bilayer inside out. The interior of the vesicle becomes continuous with the lumen of the receiving compartment.

This topological conservation explains a seemingly curious feature of surface proteins. A carbohydrate chain attached to a receptor on the ER lumenal side remains lumenal during transport through the Golgi. After an exocytic vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane, that carbohydrate faces the extracellular solution. The external leaflet of the plasma membrane is thus topologically equivalent to the lumenal leaflet of secretory organelles, even though their physical locations and lipid compositions differ. The cytosolic tail stays cytosolic throughout; it can interact with adaptor proteins or signaling enzymes without crossing a membrane.

Endocytosis follows the complementary direction. The extracellular domain of a receptor becomes enclosed inside an endocytic vesicle, which is topologically equivalent to the endosomal lumen. It does not become exposed to the cytosol simply because the receptor has moved inside the cell. This distinction prevents a frequent error: internalization of a receptor is not the same as translocation of its ligand into the cytosol. An internalized ligand generally remains in the lumen unless another specific mechanism releases it.

A standard experimental test of topology uses protease accessibility. Suppose sealed microsomes carry a protein with two antibody-detectable tags, one at each terminus. An externally added protease cannot cross an intact microsomal bilayer. If it removes the C-terminal tag but leaves the N-terminal tag, the C-terminal region is exposed on the cytosolic surface and the N-terminal region is protected in the lumen. Addition of detergent disrupts the bilayer and should make both susceptible. Without the detergent control, apparent protection might instead arise from an inaccessible folded domain or resistance to the chosen protease. Glycosylation of an appropriate lumen-facing sequence can provide complementary evidence, but lack of a glycan alone does not prove a cytosolic location because not every potential site is occupied.

The principle of conserved topology extends to lipids, subject to the activities of lipid translocases. Vesicular traffic carries lipids in both leaflets. However, synthesis, selective retention, flippases, scramblases and lipid transfer proteins can change local composition. Consequently topology is conserved by the geometry of ordinary budding and fusion, not a guarantee that every individual lipid stays permanently in the same leaflet. Inward budding into an endosome during formation of intralumenal vesicles is an important special geometry, discussed later.

4. How the secretory pathway was discovered

The order of the secretory pathway was established experimentally before its molecular machinery was identified. George Palade and colleagues studied cells specialized for protein secretion, particularly pancreatic acinar cells, by pulse–chase labeling combined with electron microscopic autoradiography. During a brief pulse, radiolabeled amino acids entered newly synthesized proteins. A chase with excess unlabeled amino acids reduced further incorporation of the label, allowing the movement of a cohort of proteins to be followed. Electron microscopy provided the structural map; autoradiographic grains indicated where radioactivity was concentrated.

Label appeared initially in rough ER-associated regions, later in Golgi compartments, and subsequently in secretory granules. This time-ordered redistribution was consistent with transfer of newly synthesized proteins through the ER and Golgi to secretory granules. It was not simply a comparison of how much total protein each organelle contained. Following the same labeled cohort makes the sequence informative: appearance at one location before another, followed by loss from the first and gain at the next, suggests passage through the first. A single static micrograph cannot reveal that direction.

A second influential approach used yeast temperature-sensitive secretion mutants (sec mutants). At a permissive temperature the mutant cells secreted cargo; at a restrictive temperature certain mutants accumulated secretory proteins in particular compartments or accumulated transport vesicles. Different defects blocked distinct stages. Genetic complementation and biochemical studies then linked particular gene products to budding, targeting and fusion. Yeast and mammalian systems share many of the underlying mechanisms even though their detailed organelle organization differs.

A pulse–chase experiment does not prove that every labeled protein takes exactly the same route. Label recovery can change due to degradation or incomplete sampling, and the time resolution is limited by pulse length and fractionation quality. If compartments are not cleanly separated, a labeled signal in a fraction need not be uniquely located there. Appropriate controls include organelle markers, recovery of label from the medium, and verification that the pulse itself does not alter secretion. Modern fluorescent reporters allow living cells to be observed, but a fluorescent tag can itself alter cargo sorting. Using multiple complementary methods strengthens an interpretation.

5. The transport cycle: cargo capture, budding and scission

The first requirement of vesicular transport is selectivity. Imagine a small patch of membrane chosen for budding. If it contained a random sample of every protein in the donor, the donor would lose its identity with each round of traffic. Instead, the cell recruits proteins that create a local budding zone and preferentially gather cargo. A transmembrane cargo molecule can present a sorting signal directly on its cytosolic tail. A soluble protein in the lumen cannot contact a cytosolic coat; it may bind a transmembrane cargo receptor whose cytosolic tail is recognized by the budding machinery. Some soluble material enters by bulk flow, meaning that it is carried with the lumenal fluid without obligatory specific recognition.

Cargo selection and curvature are coupled. Coat proteins assemble on the cytosolic face of the donor membrane, recruit other components, and help deform the initially flatter bilayer into a curved bud. Accessory proteins and lipids contribute to the energetics of bending and scission. Once a narrow neck forms, the carrier separates from its donor. The exact mechanism of neck severing differs between coat types and pathways. Dynamin is a classic GTPase for scission of many plasma membrane clathrin-coated pits, but it is incorrect to impose a requirement for dynamin on every COPI- or COPII-dependent carrier.

Many coats are recruited by small GTPases. These act as switches rather than long-distance motors. A guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) promotes GDP release and GTP binding, generating a membrane-associated active state that recruits effectors. A GTPase-activating protein (GAP) accelerates hydrolysis of GTP, returning the switch toward the inactive state. By localizing the exchange factor and the binding partners, a compartment can recruit a particular transport machinery at the correct time and location. A mutation that prevents GTP hydrolysis can therefore be disruptive even though the switch is in its active form: the cycle requires timely assembly and disassembly.

Following separation, a carrier generally sheds much of its coat, exposing the proteins required for productive tethering and fusion. Coat components return to the cytosol for reuse. The carrier may move along microtubules or actin filaments with appropriate motors, or undergo short-range movement. Motor proteins consume ATP to generate directed mechanical movement, whereas coat-associated GTPases coordinate assembly and turnover. Motors do not alone specify the ultimate fusion target; a vesicle delivered close to the wrong compartment still needs compatible targeting and fusion machinery.

A final step completes the cycle when the recipient incorporates the carrier membrane and receives the soluble contents. Cargo can then remain, proceed farther, or be sorted for a return route. The membrane proteins used for transport, such as SNAREs and cargo receptors, must be recovered or reused. The cycle thus consumes energy, transports material and regenerates its own machinery. To understand a trafficking defect, ask which stage has failed: cargo capture, budding, scission, movement, tethering, fusion, or recycling. Distinct stages can produce similar reductions in final secretion but different intracellular distributions.

6. COPII and selective export from the endoplasmic reticulum

The ER is the entry compartment for a large portion of proteins that are to be secreted or incorporated into other membranes. It contains folding assistants and resident enzymes that must remain there, while correctly assembled export cargo must leave. The transitional ER contains ER exit sites, spatially organized regions at which COPII coats form. These regions are largely distinct from the ribosome-rich regions engaged in synthesis, although they are connected by the same ER membrane.

COPII coat assembly begins when the ER-localized GEF Sec12 promotes the GTP-bound state of Sar1. Sar1 exposes an amphipathic amino-terminal helix that inserts into the cytosolic leaflet, helping initiate curvature and recruit the inner coat Sec23/Sec24. Sec24 recognizes export information carried by membrane cargo and cargo receptors; Sec23 serves as a GAP for Sar1 and contributes to the timing of coat turnover. The outer coat complex Sec13/Sec31 forms a scaffold that helps curve and organize the growing carrier. Its assembly also promotes Sar1 GTP hydrolysis in cooperation with Sec23.

A cargo receptor is especially useful for soluble proteins that would otherwise rely entirely on bulk flow. By binding the soluble cargo on the lumenal side and presenting export determinants to Sec24 on the cytosolic side, the receptor bridges the physical barrier of the membrane. Membrane proteins can carry signals such as acidic or hydrophobic export motifs; no one short motif applies to all COPII cargo. Export also depends on proper conformation, assembly of oligomers, and availability of the relevant sorting partners.

Quality control in the ER and export are linked but not identical. A newly made glycoprotein may repeatedly associate with chaperones until it reaches an export-competent conformation. Misfolded proteins often remain associated with quality-control components or are directed toward degradation through ER-associated degradation, which commonly involves movement to the cytosol and the proteasome. Blocking quality control or excessively loading the ER can impair transport without directly mutating COPII. Conversely, a correctly folded cargo can remain in the ER because its export signal is defective. Testing folding and comparing other cargoes helps distinguish these cases.

In mammalian cells, COPII-derived carriers can feed an ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), and transport then proceeds toward the cis-Golgi. The diagram of a tiny spherical vesicle moving directly to the Golgi is useful for understanding the direction but is not a literal description of every carrier. Some cargo, such as long procollagen assemblies, requires specialized export machinery and larger or differently shaped intermediates. This is not evidence that COPII is irrelevant; it is evidence that coat-dependent transport can accommodate different physical demands.

If Sar1 cannot bind GTP effectively, recruitment of the inner coat and subsequent export are compromised. If Sec24 fails to recognize a particular cargo signal, the defect may be selective, leaving other export cargoes relatively unaffected. If a GTP-locked Sar1 mutant disrupts coat turnover, abnormal buds or carriers may accumulate and productive export can fall. These predictions show why simply increasing the fraction of active GTPase does not necessarily increase net transport.

7. COPI retrieval and the preservation of ER and Golgi identity

Forward transport would be unsustainable if every ER component that entered an outbound carrier remained permanently outside the ER. ER proteins can escape with the flow of cargo. Some membrane proteins are needed repeatedly at ER exit sites. Their continuous synthesis would be wasteful, and the receiving compartment would accumulate proteins that do not belong there. Retrograde transport solves this problem by returning escaped residents and reusable transport components.

COPI coats are recruited by the small GTPase ARF1. A membrane-associated ARF GEF promotes exchange of GDP for GTP, enabling ARF1 to associate strongly with the membrane and recruit the coatomer complex. Coatomer assembles on appropriate Golgi and intermediate-compartment membranes and participates in selecting retrograde cargo. ARF GTP hydrolysis, stimulated by GAPs, contributes to coat turnover. Unlike COPII, which is particularly associated with ER exit, COPI is central to retrieval from early Golgi and intermediate compartments toward the ER and participates in intra-Golgi recycling. It should not be described as the universal coat of every retrograde route.

Consider a soluble ER chaperone carrying KDEL (Lys-Asp-Glu-Leu) at its C terminus. Because the chaperone is lumenal, cytosolic coat proteins cannot bind KDEL directly. A transmembrane KDEL receptor recognizes the motif in the more acidic environment of the Golgi lumen. The receptor couples recognition to retrograde sorting; on returning to the relatively neutral ER lumen, it can release the chaperone. The receptor is then available for another cycle. KDEL is therefore predominantly a retrieval signal, not an absolute prohibition on leaving the ER. A soluble ER protein lacking its retrieval sequence can be secreted even if its original ER targeting signal still works.

Some ER membrane proteins carry C-terminal cytosolic dilysine retrieval motifs such as KKXX or KXKXX. Their location on the cytosolic face enables direct or indirect interaction with coatomer. This is a different geometry from the lumenal KDEL signal. Reversing the orientation of a membrane protein can make an otherwise intact motif inaccessible to the sorting machinery. Moreover, ER localization can also involve retention through interactions, oligomerization and partitioning; not every ER protein uses a canonical KDEL or dilysine signal.

Retrograde traffic also makes cisternal maturation possible. If a Golgi cisterna gradually changes its identity while moving from an early to a late stage, earlier resident processing enzymes must be recycled backward to younger compartments. COPI-mediated recycling can contribute to this movement. A block in retrieval can consequently affect both resident localization and the sequence in which newly synthesized cargo is modified. The predicted phenotype is broader than an isolated failure to return one KDEL-bearing protein.

A useful conceptual control is to compare deletion of the KDEL motif on one fluorescent chaperone with inhibition of the coatomer machinery. In the first case, the reporter may be mislocalized but many other retrieval cargoes still cycle. In the second, many cargoes and resident proteins are affected and Golgi organization may change. The distinction between a sorting signal and a shared transport component is central to interpreting mutant experiments.

8. Golgi progression, compartment identity and sorting at the exit

A newly exported secretory protein must pass through an ordered processing environment. The Golgi has an entry side (cis), intermediate medial regions and a trans side that leads to the trans-Golgi network (TGN). The differences among these compartments are functional. Different resident enzymes modify cargo at different steps, so a protein arriving at a late compartment may have undergone transformations that are absent from an early compartment.

Two classical models explain how cargo advances. In the stable-compartment or vesicular transport model, cisternae retain their identity and cargo moves forward between them in transport intermediates. In cisternal maturation, an early cisterna progressively acquires later properties while resident enzymes recycle backward. These ideas need not be mutually exclusive: the relative contribution of cisternal progression, tubules and vesicular exchange depends on cargo and cell type. Large cargo that cannot fit into a conventional small carrier motivates cisternal maturation; live imaging in yeast has demonstrated changes in resident markers within individual cisternae. Neither observation implies that all intracellular transport is performed by cisternae.

Glycan processing provides an experimentally useful readout of this journey. Many ER N-linked glycans initially contain high-mannose structures that are sensitive to endoglycosidase H (Endo H). Some cargo develops complex glycans during Golgi processing that resist Endo H. A pulse-labeled reporter changing from sensitive to resistant therefore can indicate that it has reached a region capable of the relevant modification. Endo H resistance is not a universal Golgi passage test: some proteins retain high-mannose glycans even after reaching a later destination, and a glycosylation enzyme defect can prevent resistance despite normal movement. It is necessary to use a validated reporter, organelle markers and an independent measure of localization.

The TGN is an important branching point. Soluble secretory proteins may proceed in a constitutive secretory route, become concentrated in regulated secretory granules, or travel toward endosomes and lysosomes. Membrane proteins may be sorted toward particular domains of the cell surface. Some residents are retained or returned to an earlier compartment. The underlying principle is that a sequence or molecular feature in the cargo becomes meaningful only when its binding partner is present at the correct location. Trans-Golgi sorting does not mean that a finished protein chooses a direction without participating in molecular interactions.

Lipid composition contributes to sorting. Membranes differ in thickness, curvature, sterol content and phosphoinositide species. The length and properties of a protein's transmembrane domain can influence partitioning and retention, and cytosolic adaptors can recognize particular phosphoinositides. These influences act alongside specific protein interactions; no single membrane thickness or lipid domain explains all Golgi sorting. The next two routes illustrate how a chemically modified soluble cargo and a cytosolic motif are each recognized by a different receptor or adaptor.

9. Lysosomal enzyme sorting: the mannose-6-phosphate address

Lysosomal hydrolases are soluble proteins that hydrolyze macromolecules in an acidic compartment. They are synthesized into the ER and pass through the Golgi, but simply entering the secretory pathway does not distinguish them from proteins destined for secretion. A special sorting system in many mammalian cells uses a carbohydrate modification, mannose-6-phosphate (M6P), to mark newly synthesized soluble lysosomal enzymes.

In early Golgi regions, GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase recognizes a three-dimensional feature on a lysosomal enzyme rather than just a short linear sequence common to every hydrolase. It transfers a phospho-GlcNAc group from UDP-GlcNAc onto a mannose residue of an N-linked oligosaccharide. A subsequent uncovering enzyme removes the covering GlcNAc residue, exposing the phosphate on mannose. The tag is thus generated by two distinct enzymatic steps. It is not a ribosomal signal peptide or a phosphate added directly to an amino acid side chain.

In the TGN, M6P receptors bind the tagged enzymes on their lumenal side. Their cytosolic tails interact with sorting adaptors, including GGA proteins and AP-1, that recruit clathrin and other budding machinery. A carrier delivers the complex into the endosomal pathway. The increasingly acidic lumen promotes dissociation of enzyme and receptor, allowing the hydrolase to progress to lysosomes while the receptor returns toward the Golgi or cycles through other routes. Receptor recycling matters: if every receptor were degraded after one delivery, efficient enzyme sorting would require continual replacement.

The consequences of a failure in tagging are dramatic. In mucolipidosis II (I-cell disease), defects in the phosphotransferase system prevent efficient M6P tagging of many hydrolases. Untagged enzymes can follow secretion routes instead of being efficiently delivered to lysosomes. Extracellular hydrolase activity can increase while intracellular lysosomal degradation falls, resulting in accumulation of undegraded material. The term inclusion cell reflects the resulting storage inclusions. The logic is not that the enzymes are necessarily absent or catalytically incapable; many are made but sent to the wrong location.

An experimental demonstration compares lysosomal activity inside cells with enzyme activity in the culture medium. A large increase in extracellular enzyme with a decrease in lysosomal enzyme is consistent with defective sorting, particularly if enzyme synthesis is unchanged. Adding an external appropriately tagged enzyme can allow uptake through surface M6P receptors in suitable cells, illustrating a basis for some enzyme replacement approaches. This does not imply every lysosomal protein or every hydrolase depends entirely on M6P; alternative routes exist. Lysosomal membrane proteins generally depend on their own cytosolic sorting information, and some soluble enzymes use other receptors. Plant vacuolar protein sorting also uses different recognition systems.

A defect in the M6P receptor would also impair delivery even if tagging is normal. To distinguish a tagging failure from a receptor failure, examine whether the secreted enzyme carries M6P and test uptake of a normally tagged enzyme. A tagging-defective cell with functional receptors can still internalize properly tagged material; a relevant receptor-defective cell may not. Partial receptor redundancy and alternative routes mean that the result should be expressed as an efficiency difference, not invariably complete loss.

10. Clathrin, adaptor proteins and membrane scission

Clathrin is a coat protein that forms a lattice on the cytosolic surface of selected budding membranes. Its characteristic assembly unit is a triskelion made from three heavy chains associated with light chains. Triskelia assemble into a curved lattice around a developing coated pit. However, clathrin itself is not the universal reader of cargo signals. Adaptor proteins connect the coat to the membrane and to selected cargo.

At the plasma membrane, AP-2 is a major adaptor that recognizes appropriate cytosolic cargo motifs and phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, commonly written PI(4,5)P₂. Motifs such as YXXΦ (Φ representing a bulky hydrophobic residue) and certain dileucine-based motifs can be recognized in the appropriate context. Other adaptors recognize particular cargoes or contribute to coat formation. LDL receptor internalization classically involves an NPXY-related motif and associated adaptors rather than a simplistic universal AP-2 recognition rule. At the TGN and endosomal membranes, AP-1 and GGAs participate in different clathrin-associated sorting routes. The same coat building material can therefore be reused at distinct locations because its recruitment and cargo specificity depend on different molecular partners.

As clathrin and accessory components gather, the membrane bends inward at a plasma membrane pit. The cytosolic coat remains on the outside of the nascent vesicle; the extracellular ligand is enclosed inside. In many mammalian cells dynamin assembles around the narrow neck. GTP hydrolysis is coupled to conformational changes that contribute to neck severing, releasing the carrier. A dominant-negative dynamin mutant can cause accumulation of deeply invaginated pits that remain continuous with the cell surface. This phenotype differs from a mutant that prevents initial adaptor binding, which may reduce recruitment of a selected cargo even while other pits still form.

After scission, auxilin and the ATP-dependent chaperone Hsc70 help remove the clathrin lattice. Changes in phosphoinositide composition also contribute to changing the affinity of coat-associated proteins for the carrier. A coated vesicle is not the final destination: the coat must be recycled and fusion machinery exposed. If uncoating is disrupted, coated carriers can accumulate despite the fact that cargo capture and scission have occurred. Such a defect can be distinguished from failed scission by examining whether the carriers remain connected to the donor membrane.

COPI, COPII and clathrin illustrate a shared principle: a coat organizes a budding event, while a separate targeting system operates downstream. The coat type predicts the donor and usual route, but does not by itself determine the entire cargo itinerary. A clathrin-coated vesicle formed at the cell surface typically enters the endocytic system; one formed at the TGN may carry lysosomal enzyme receptors toward endosomes. Not all endocytosis uses clathrin, and many post-Golgi or recycling carriers have different machinery.

11. Rab GTPases, movement and selective tethering

After budding, the cell confronts a recognition problem. Many small carriers move through the same cytoplasm, and many membrane surfaces have superficially similar lipid bilayers. Productive fusion must be restricted to the appropriate target. Rab proteins, a family of small GTPases, help organize these distinct membrane identities. A Rab is often attached to the cytosolic face of a membrane through prenyl lipid groups, allowing it to recruit effectors at specific locations.

As with Sar1 and ARF, the nucleotide state matters. A Rab GEF promotes formation of Rab-GTP, which binds effectors. GAP-stimulated hydrolysis returns Rab toward the GDP state. GDP dissociation inhibitor (GDI) can extract many prenylated GDP-bound Rabs into the cytosol and stabilize them for reuse. Spatially restricted GEFs, GAPs and interacting proteins establish where Rab is active. Thus the mere presence of a Rab protein is less informative than the place and time at which it occupies its active form.

Rab effectors may help recruit motor proteins, organize membrane domains, or form physical links between a carrier and its target. Tethers can capture a vesicle over a distance greater than that spanned by SNARE proteins alone. Examples include long coiled-coil tethers at the Golgi and multicomponent tethering complexes at various endomembrane compartments. A tether can also coordinate downstream SNARE assembly. Capture is nevertheless different from membrane fusion: two membranes can be physically tethered yet remain separated by a layer of water.

Different Rab proteins are associated with different regions of the network. Rab5 is classically associated with early endosomes, Rab7 with later endosomal identity, and Rab11 with recycling pathways. Rab1 participates in early secretory transport. These are useful markers, not immutable names for exclusive compartments. During endosomal maturation, recruitment of Rab7 and reduction of Rab5 activity help change the set of available effectors and destinations. Cargo can remain in a compartment whose molecular identity changes as it matures.

A GTP-locked Rab5 can produce enlarged early endosomes in suitable experimental systems by altering the balance of fusion and maturation. This does not mean that every active Rab increases net forward transport. A failure of Rab activation can impair targeting, whereas a failure of inactivation can impair progression to the next state. The interpretation must identify the specific Rab and the route under study.

Suppose COPII buds normally in a mutant cell, but ER-derived vesicles accumulate near the Golgi and lumenal cargo remains separate from Golgi markers. The defect could affect tethering or fusion rather than coat assembly. If introducing the appropriate active tethering machinery restores close contact but not mixing of the vesicle contents, the remaining lesion lies downstream. Such experiments establish why a targeting GTPase, a coat and a fusion protein cannot be treated as interchangeable terms.

12. SNARE pairing and the physical mechanism of fusion

Bringing two membranes close together is not enough to make them fuse. Each bilayer has a hydrated polar surface; approaching very closely requires displacement of water and deformation of lipids. An uncontrolled merger could also mix the wrong compartments or leak their contents. The cell uses a protein assembly process to overcome the energy barrier locally while restricting fusion to appropriate membrane partners.

SNARE proteins contain motifs capable of assembling into a stable helical bundle. In a simplified classical picture, a vesicle SNARE (v-SNARE) pairs with target-membrane SNAREs (t-SNAREs). The more general structural classification distinguishes R-SNARE and Q-SNARE motifs according to a characteristic central residue. A common fusion complex contains one R and three Q helices. The v/t terminology is useful for a typical directional fusion event, but R and Q identities are not universally equivalent to vesicle and target locations; some pathways involve more complex arrangements.

Before fusion, SNAREs on opposing membranes assemble into a trans-SNARE complex. Progressive zippering of the helices draws the membranes into close proximity. Tethers and regulatory proteins, including Sec1/Munc18 family proteins in many pathways, coordinate when productive SNARE assembly occurs. As bilayers deform, the contacting leaflets can merge in a hemifusion intermediate, and a fusion pore can then connect the lumenal spaces. This is a useful physical model of fusion, not a claim that every pore follows an identical sequence of detectable intermediates. A complete fusion event allows cargo transfer and membrane mixing.

After fusion, the SNAREs that previously resided in opposing membranes are now in the same membrane, forming a cis-SNARE complex. The ATPase NSF (N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor), recruited through soluble NSF attachment proteins such as α-SNAP, uses ATP hydrolysis to disassemble the stable complex and make SNAREs available for reuse. NSF is not the primary motor that pulls the two membranes together at the moment of fusion. Instead, energy invested in resetting SNAREs enables repeated cycles of assembly and disassembly. This resolves an apparent puzzle: fusion can be driven by favorable protein assembly while the entire sustained trafficking cycle remains energy-dependent.

The distinction between docking and fusion can be tested in a reconstituted system. Artificial liposomes carrying complementary SNAREs may show lipid mixing; however, lipid mixing alone is insufficient to establish full content transfer because hemifusion can mix the contacting leaflets without a fully open pore. Measuring transfer or mixing of enclosed water-soluble markers and checking for leakage helps distinguish full fusion from partial membrane rearrangement or rupture. In cells, accumulation of attached vesicles with failure of lumenal content mixing supports a late fusion defect.

SNARE compatibility is necessary in many routes but not a complete explanation of specificity. Several SNAREs can interact in vitro in combinations that would be inappropriate in a cell. Rab effectors, tethers, regulators and membrane composition constrain which combinations actually form. A mutation in a SNARE may block cargo delivery despite normal coating and targeting; conversely, introducing complementary SNAREs at a wrong location does not guarantee normal fusion if the rest of the machinery is missing. Biological fidelity is produced by a sequence of selective steps rather than a single molecular lock.

13. Endocytosis: bringing extracellular material into the sorting network

Cells must retrieve membrane components and take up extracellular materials without allowing the plasma membrane to lose its selective barrier. Endocytosis solves this by invaginating a portion of the surface and closing it off as an intracellular compartment. An extracellular molecule is thereby moved into an internal lumen. This mechanism differs fundamentally from facilitated diffusion or active transport through a membrane protein because a patch of membrane moves together with its cargo.

Receptor-mediated endocytosis makes uptake selective. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particles carry cholesterol esters and bind LDL receptors on the cell surface. When receptors are concentrated in coated pits, many LDL particles can be internalized despite low extracellular concentration. The receptor's extracellular domain binds ligand while its cytosolic tail interacts with internalization machinery. In early endosomes, the acidic environment promotes dissociation of LDL from its receptor. The receptor is commonly returned to the plasma membrane while LDL proceeds toward lysosomes, where components are degraded and cholesterol becomes available to the cell.

LDL receptor defects illustrate the difference between binding and internalization. A receptor with an intact extracellular ligand-binding domain but a defective cytosolic internalization signal can bind LDL at the surface yet fail to concentrate efficiently in coated pits. Surface binding assays would appear much less affected than measurements of internalized LDL. In contrast, a ligand-binding defect reduces initial binding itself. Brown and Goldstein's work on familial hypercholesterolemia provided important evidence for receptor-mediated uptake and its role in feedback regulation of cholesterol metabolism.

Other endocytic modes serve different needs. Fluid-phase uptake captures extracellular solution without requiring each molecule to bind a receptor. Some forms of clathrin-independent endocytosis involve distinct membrane components and regulators; caveolae are flask-shaped invaginations enriched in caveolin and other structural proteins, but not every caveola undergoes rapid constitutive internalization. Macropinocytosis uses actin-driven membrane ruffling to enclose relatively large volumes of extracellular fluid. Phagocytosis, specialized in cells such as macrophages, uses receptors and actin remodeling to envelop large particles. It produces a phagosome that subsequently interacts with the endolysosomal system. The internalization mechanism should be inferred from the actual cargo and evidence, not from the word endocytosis alone.

One way to distinguish surface-bound from internalized ligand is to compare labeling at a low temperature that slows trafficking with labeling after warming, followed by removal of accessible surface ligand using a suitable assay. Internalized label becomes inaccessible to the surface removal step. Controls for membrane integrity and ligand binding are necessary because low temperature also affects membrane properties and binding equilibria. The important inference is the separation of uptake from attachment: a ligand can bind strongly without crossing into an endocytic compartment.

14. Endosomal sorting, acidification, recycling and degradation

The early endosome receives incoming material from multiple uptake routes and then separates cargo according to its fate. Its lumen is acidified by V-type H⁺-ATPases, which use ATP to pump protons into the lumen. Continued proton movement creates both a pH difference and an electrical gradient; counterion movement and proton leakage influence the final steady pH. Acidification is therefore an active, regulated process. It supplies the chemical environment required for many sorting reactions, not simply the final conditions for enzymatic degradation.

A pH-sensitive interaction can separate ligand from receptor. LDL dissociates from its receptor in early endosomes, allowing recycling of the receptor. Transferrin behaves differently: iron is released from transferrin in the acidic endosome, but iron-free transferrin can remain bound to its receptor until it reaches the neutral extracellular environment. This contrast demonstrates that low pH does not universally cause all ligand–receptor pairs to dissociate. The precise molecular response of the pair determines whether the receptor and ligand recycle together or separate.

Recycling can occur through relatively direct routes from early endosomes or through a recycling endosomal compartment, often involving Rab11. Recycling of transporters, receptors and membrane lipids sustains the surface without requiring new synthesis for each use. Cargo returning toward the Golgi instead follows endosome-to-TGN retrograde routes. Retromer and associated sorting machinery contribute to several such retrieval pathways, although retromer also participates in other endosomal sorting processes. These routes must not be conflated with COPI-mediated retrieval from the Golgi to ER.

Cargo intended for degradation may be retained in the maturing endosome. A membrane receptor activated by a growth factor can be ubiquitinated on its cytosolic region, allowing endosomal sorting machinery to recognize it. ESCRT complexes participate in recognition and inward budding of selected membrane regions to form intralumenal vesicles within a multivesicular body. This inward budding is geometrically different from ordinary outward budding into cytosol. The receptor's cytosolic tail, which formerly contacted cytosolic signaling partners, becomes enclosed inside the intralumenal vesicle and is thus sequestered from cytosolic signaling machinery. Later, fusion of the endosomal compartment with lysosomes or maturation into degradative compartments permits breakdown of both lumenal and membrane cargo.

Ubiquitin is not exclusively a proteasomal degradation signal. The form, location and context of ubiquitination determine which recognition machinery acts. A ubiquitinated surface receptor can be directed to lysosomal degradation without being directly fed into the cytosolic proteasome. This is especially important for exam questions that compare membrane-protein down-regulation with ER-associated degradation. In the latter, misfolded ER-associated proteins often reach cytosolic proteasomes; in endosomal down-regulation, the integral membrane cargo can remain membrane-embedded until sequestration and lysosomal digestion.

If V-ATPase activity is reduced, endosomes tend to become less acidic. Ligand dissociation, receptor recycling and lysosomal enzyme activity may all be impaired, but the severity depends on the particular cargo and compartment. If ESCRT function is impaired instead, cargo may reach the endosome yet remain on its limiting membrane, potentially continuing to signal or being abnormally recycled. Distinguishing these stages requires measuring both pH and cargo distribution rather than assuming that every lysosomal sorting phenotype is caused by deficient acidification.

15. Exocytosis, constitutive delivery and stimulus-dependent release

The outward counterpart of endocytosis is exocytosis. A secretory carrier fuses with the plasma membrane, releases its lumenal contents outside the cell, and contributes its membrane components to the cell surface. A soluble protein that occupied the vesicle lumen becomes extracellular, while a membrane protein becomes part of the plasma membrane with its orientation preserved. Exocytosis therefore serves both secretion and membrane renewal.

In constitutive secretion, transport carriers continuously deliver material to the plasma membrane without requiring a specialized acute secretory trigger for each event. Examples include the ongoing release of many extracellular matrix proteins and delivery of newly made membrane proteins. Constitutive does not mean unregulated: cargo selection, membrane fusion, transport rate and cellular demand still affect the pathway. In an unpolarized cell, some soluble TGN cargo enters this route when it lacks signals for retention or alternative destinations. The route is not necessarily completely nonselective.

Regulated secretion operates in cells that need to release a concentrated product at particular times. Secretory cargo is sorted into immature granules, which mature through changes in composition, acidification, cargo condensation, processing and selective removal of unwanted proteins. Mature granules may be retained near the cell surface until a stimulus activates fusion. Pancreatic endocrine cells release insulin from such granules, while certain neurons release neurotransmitter from synaptic vesicles that are regenerated by endocytic recycling. The precise mechanisms of cargo storage differ between those examples, but both illustrate stimulus-dependent exocytosis.

A rise in cytosolic Ca²⁺ is a common trigger for rapid regulated secretion. In synapses, calcium entry through voltage-gated channels activates calcium-sensitive machinery, including synaptotagmin in many forms of rapid vesicle release. This promotes productive fusion of prepared vesicles through SNARE-dependent mechanisms. The cargo can already be present in the vesicle before stimulation; the stimulus changes the probability or rate of release, not necessarily the rate of cargo synthesis. This distinction explains why a cell can produce a rapid secretory burst within a time too short for transcription, translation and ER-to-Golgi transit.

A mutation that reduces calcium sensing may leave mature granules present but impair triggered release. A mutation that prevents sorting of cargo into granules may instead reduce the stored pool and cause premature constitutive secretion. A mutation in a shared fusion component may affect multiple outward routes. Measuring the size of the stored pool, basal release and stimulated release therefore helps localize the defect. Membrane capacitance measurements can detect changes associated with fusion because membrane area increases, although compensating endocytosis can obscure net changes.

The cell must balance outward addition and inward removal of membrane. Neurons recycle synaptic vesicle components to sustain repeated release, and other cells adjust surface protein numbers to changing conditions. Full fusion can be followed by retrieval, and transient fusion pore behavior can occur in certain secretory systems. It is not necessary to assume that every exocytic event permanently adds a complete vesicle's area to the plasma membrane.

16. Control by membrane lipids, signaling and cellular demand

Membrane traffic is not an automatic conveyor running at a fixed speed. The cell changes the rates of uptake, recycling, degradation and outward delivery to meet its physiological needs. Such changes can occur in seconds by controlling an existing transporter pool or more slowly through altered synthesis and degradation. The location of a protein is therefore a regulated property just as important as its abundance and catalytic activity.

Phosphoinositides are low-abundance membrane lipids with phosphorylated inositol headgroups that provide binding sites on the cytosolic leaflet. PI(4,5)P₂ at the plasma membrane helps recruit endocytic machinery; PI4P contributes to TGN-associated recruitment; PI3P is characteristic of early endosomal domains and helps recruit proteins with appropriate lipid-binding regions. Kinases and phosphatases continually modify these lipids. Altering their spatial distribution can change which adaptor, Rab effector or tether is recruited even when the amount of that protein remains normal. A lipid identity is not a permanent label: enzymes and trafficking continually renew it.

Receptors can be removed from the surface after activation. Internalization can reduce exposure to extracellular ligand, but internalized receptors may continue to signal from endosomal membranes in some contexts. Recycling restores surface responsiveness, whereas lysosomal sorting reduces receptor abundance and often terminates signaling. These are distinct regulatory outcomes. Measuring the total amount of receptor after stimulation cannot by itself distinguish a change in internalization from recycling; measuring surface and internal pools separately is more informative.

The insulin-responsive glucose transporter GLUT4 provides another illustration. In muscle and adipose cells, a substantial fraction of GLUT4 can reside in intracellular compartments. Insulin signaling increases its presence at the cell surface by changing trafficking, particularly mobilization and fusion of GLUT4-containing carriers, with endocytosis and recycling also contributing. Once at the surface, GLUT4 allows facilitated diffusion of glucose down its gradient. Insulin does not turn GLUT4 into an ATP-powered glucose pump. The regulated event is the number of transporters available at the membrane, linking this chapter directly to passive transport.

Epithelial cells impose an additional spatial requirement. The apical membrane faces a lumen or external surface, while the basolateral membrane faces neighboring cells and underlying tissue. Tight junctions help restrict mixing of membrane components, but selective delivery and retrieval also maintain domain identity. Cytosolic motifs, lumenal features, lipid interactions and specialized sorting machinery contribute in cargo-dependent ways. A protein delivered to the wrong domain can compromise directional transepithelial transport even when it functions perfectly as a molecular transporter. Plant cells likewise rely on regulated delivery and retrieval of cell wall components and polarized membrane proteins, with distinct vacuolar and cell-plate pathways. The broad principles apply without making animal-specific M6P or LDL routes universal.

Regulation also includes quality control and feedback. Persistent ER stress can reduce the efficiency of export, and a cell may adjust synthesis or degradation when cargo overloads the pathway. Nutrient availability and signaling can affect delivery to degradative compartments. Motor activity and cytoskeletal arrangement influence the speed and direction of long-range transport. None of these regulators acts in isolation; a change in vesicle number may reflect increased formation, impaired fusion, or slowed turnover. The following quantitative models explain how to distinguish the possibilities.

17. Quantitative reasoning: enrichment, surface balance and turnover

Transport questions often provide numbers rather than ask for a detailed physical derivation. A small set of conservation equations can convert a descriptive pathway into a testable model. Every model requires the student to identify the measured pool, the fluxes entering and leaving it, and the assumptions under which the calculation applies. These equations do not describe all molecular steps; they express necessary relationships among the rates and quantities.

Cargo enrichment compares concentrations or surface densities, not merely the number of molecules in two differently sized membranes. Suppose a donor membrane contains 1,000 receptor molecules across 100 µm², giving a density of 10 molecules/µm². A carrier whose membrane area is 0.10 µm² contains six receptor molecules. Its density is 6/0.10 = 60 molecules/µm², giving a sixfold enrichment relative to the donor average. If the carrier contained only one molecule, its density would equal the donor average. Enrichment suggests preferential cargo selection but can also reflect localized donor domains; comparison with a nonselected membrane marker helps distinguish enrichment during budding from pre-existing clustering.

Area conservation provides another useful relation. Approximate a spherical transport vesicle of radius r as having membrane area A = 4πr². The formula follows from the surface area of a sphere, and r must be in the same length units as the desired area. For r = 50 nm = 0.05 µm, A = 4π(0.05)² = 0.0314 µm². If 200 such vesicles fuse completely per minute and 160 equal-sized vesicles are retrieved, net surface area increases by (200 − 160) × 0.0314 = 1.256 µm²/min. This approximation ignores membrane folds, transient fusion, changes in vesicle size and tension-dependent feedback. If retrieval vesicles differ in area, the correct expression is fusion rate times mean area added minus retrieval rate times mean area removed, rather than simply subtracting vesicle counts.

A membrane capacitance calculation links area to electrical measurements. For a uniform bilayer, capacitance is approximately C = cₘA, where cₘ is specific membrane capacitance. If cₘ ≈ 1 µF/cm², then 1 µm² = 10⁻⁸ cm² and the corresponding capacitance is 10⁻¹⁴ F = 10 fF. The net 1.256 µm²/min increase above predicts a capacitance increase of approximately 12.56 fF/min. In an actual capacitance recording, electrical accessibility, membrane geometry and simultaneous endocytosis complicate interpretation. Capacitance estimates surface change, not the identity or amount of lumenal cargo released.

Now follow the surface receptor pool. Let S be the number of surface receptors and I the number of internal receptors in a simplified closed two-pool system. If internalization is first order with rate constant kᵢ and recycling first order with constant kᵣ, then dS/dt = kᵣI − kᵢS. The internal pool changes in the opposite direction. Since S + I = T, substituting I = T − S gives dS/dt = kᵣT − (kᵢ + kᵣ)S. At steady state, dS/dt = 0, so S/T = kᵣ/(kᵢ + kᵣ). This is a steady-state fraction, not the probability that any single receptor never leaves the surface. Equal opposing fluxes can be large even though S is constant.

For 1,200 total receptors, kᵢ = 0.20 min⁻¹ and kᵣ = 0.10 min⁻¹, the surface fraction is 0.10/0.30 = 1/3, or 400 surface receptors. The flux internalized is 0.20 × 400 = 80 receptors/min and the recycling flux is 0.10 × 800 = 80 receptors/min. If recycling doubles while internalization is unchanged, the eventual surface pool becomes 600 receptors. The change arises without any new receptor synthesis. If synthesis and degradation occur, T is not necessarily constant and the equations must include those fluxes; forcing the closed-pool formula onto an open system can produce an incorrect prediction.

A pulse–chase measurement can estimate a first-order departure rate under appropriate conditions. Let N(t) be labeled cargo in an isolated donor compartment after labeling ends, assume no return or degradation and a constant proportional exit rate k. Then dN/dt = −kN. Dividing by N and integrating from zero to t gives ln[N(t)/N(0)] = −kt, hence N(t) = N(0)e⁻ᵏᵗ and t½ = ln2/k. If 80 units become 20 units in 12 minutes, two half-lives have elapsed, so t½ = 6 min and k ≈ 0.1155 min⁻¹. If cargo returns or is degraded, disappearance from the donor no longer measures export alone. A receiving-compartment signal is required to establish destination.

The acidification of an endosome can also be expressed quantitatively using pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], with activity approximated by concentration in dilute solution. A shift from pH 7 to pH 5 corresponds to a hundredfold rise in free proton concentration. It does not mean precisely a hundredfold increase in total protons pumped, because buffers bind many of the added protons and membrane leakage or counterion transport affects the steady state. The mathematical lesson is to separate a measured concentration from the energy-dependent flux that maintains it.

18. Diagnosing a blocked transport pathway

The usual CSIR-NET analytical problem is not to name a coat from memory, but to infer a likely stage of failure from several observations. Start by asking whether the affected protein was synthesized, whether it entered the correct initial compartment, whether it formed a mature conformation and whether the transport machinery works for other proteins. A protein found in the ER could have a defective export signal, be retained by quality control or be affected by a general budding defect. The observation alone does not distinguish them.

A localization mutant in a single cargo can be compared with a wild-type reporter transported in the same cell. If a mutant receptor stays in the ER while a control receptor reaches the surface, a global defect in COPII is less likely. If both accumulate in the ER, a common machinery defect or widespread stress is more plausible. Restoration of the normal sorting sequence and rescue by a wild-type transport gene offer complementary ways to identify the cause. Rescue must be interpreted with appropriate expression controls because overexpression may bypass an otherwise limiting step.

A temperature-sensitive mutant can be shifted to the restrictive condition during a pulse–chase, then returned to the permissive condition. If labeled cargo accumulates in an intermediate compartment during the block and moves onward after release, that compartment lies upstream of the final destination and the block may be reversible. Electron microscopy may show whether membrane buds remain attached or vesicles have separated. A fusion mutant can produce many detached carriers, whereas a budding mutant may decrease the production of carriers and increase donor cargo. The number of vesicles is therefore not a direct measure of the amount of successful transport.

Consider two independent mutant lines with normal protein synthesis. In line A, a newly synthesized secretory reporter remains Endo H sensitive and colocalizes with ER markers; in line B, the reporter becomes resistant, accumulates at TGN markers and scarcely appears in the medium. Assuming the reporter normally acquires resistance during Golgi passage, line A has a defect at or before the relevant processing stage. The observed localization narrows it to ER export or quality control. Line B has reached the Golgi, and a defect in late sorting, carrier formation, targeting or fusion is more plausible. Additional surface and carrier measurements would be required to specify one of these.

Perturbations can also be used cautiously. Brefeldin A interferes with certain ARF GEFs and can cause redistribution of many Golgi components toward the ER in sensitive cells, rather than acting simply as a universal inhibitor of all vesicular transport. Inhibitors of V-ATPase alter endolysosomal acidification. Microtubule-disrupting treatments alter organelle positioning and long-range traffic, but need not abolish every local transport event. The effect of a perturbation is evidence about its target and the downstream system, not a license to infer that every process disrupted is directly acted on by the reagent.

Several controls help avoid overinterpretation. Verify viability and overall protein synthesis when secretion falls. Confirm that compartment markers still identify distinct organelles after a treatment. Use an appropriate time course and normalization to the initial amount of labeled cargo. Distinguish surface binding from uptake and lipid mixing from content mixing. Measure total label recovery when possible. These controls turn a seemingly simple diagram into a scientifically defensible explanation. The goal is to identify the earliest directly supported defect while acknowledging alternatives that have not yet been excluded.

19. Integrating the network: why direction and identity require recycling

The transport system can now be reconstructed from the perspective of one molecule. A newly made membrane receptor first acquires its orientation at the ER. Correctly assembled cargo is selected at exit sites by COPII. The receptor advances through the Golgi, where its lumenal domain may be modified. Sorting at the TGN directs it toward the appropriate surface domain. Rab proteins and tethers organize target recognition, and SNAREs promote fusion. The receptor binds extracellular ligand and may later enter an endocytic pit through adaptor-mediated cargo selection. Endosomal sorting returns it to the surface or directs it toward degradation.

At each stage, traffic is selective but not absolutely perfect. Escaped ER residents can be retrieved by COPI. M6P receptors return after delivering hydrolases. SNAREs are disassembled and recycled after fusion. Endosomal recycling replenishes surface proteins. Continuous local lipid synthesis, transport and modification help maintain compartment-specific composition. The resulting organization is a dynamic steady state, sustained by repeated sorting and energy consumption.

Energy appears in different places for different purposes: nucleotide exchange and hydrolysis regulate coat and Rab switches; dynamin uses GTP during many scission events; motor proteins use ATP to move cargo; Hsc70 and NSF use ATP to reset machinery; V-ATPases establish lumenal proton gradients. The energy for fusion includes favorable SNARE complex assembly and membrane rearrangement, whereas the entire cycle requires energy to restore starting conditions. It would be misleading to assign a single fixed ATP cost to all vesicles because carrier size, composition, movement distance, number of active proteins and route vary.

This framework also explains why compartment morphology and function are connected. A secretory cell has prominent ER and Golgi because it processes large amounts of cargo. Endosomes and lysosomes provide controlled environments for incoming molecules and degradation. Cytoskeletal networks position compartments and transport carriers. In the following subunit, the individual organelles can be studied in more detail. The present chapter supplies the organizing principle: an organelle is not merely a bag of enzymes but a compartment whose identity is maintained by selective transport, retrieval and regulated molecular exchange.

20. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Question 1

A soluble secreted reporter loses its ER signal peptide. What is the most direct prediction?

A. Cytosolic accumulation; B. Obligatory lysosomal localization; C. Nuclear import; D. Increased COPII capture

Answer and explanation

A. Without the signal, the reporter is not normally recruited to Sec61. B requires ER entry and downstream sorting; C requires an appropriate nuclear import signal; D cannot occur for soluble cytosolic cargo.

Question 2

Which coat normally supports early secretory export from ER exit sites?

A. COPI; B. COPII; C. Clathrin/AP-2; D. ESCRT

Answer and explanation

B. Sar1 and Sec23/24 recruit COPII at ER exit sites. COPI is prominent in retrieval, AP-2 in surface uptake, and ESCRT in inward endosomal budding.

Question 3

Which combination correctly identifies a lumenal retrieval signal and its reader?

A. KDEL and KDEL receptor; B. KDEL and cytosolic Sec24 directly; C. M6P and SRP; D. NPXY and lumenal receptor

Answer and explanation

A. KDEL is recognized in the lumen by a transmembrane receptor. B violates topology; C confuses lysosomal tagging with ER entry; D confuses a cytosolic internalization determinant with a lumenal ligand.

Question 4

A receptor glycosylated in the ER reaches the plasma membrane. Where is the glycan?

A. Cytosolic; B. Extracellular; C. In the bilayer core; D. Always removed

Answer and explanation

B. The lumenal face of secretory membranes is topologically equivalent to the outer surface. A reverses orientation, C ignores aqueous carbohydrate location, and D assumes universal processing that does not occur.

Question 5

Which enzyme primarily disassembles cis-SNARE complexes?

A. NSF; B. Sec12; C. V-ATPase; D. GlcNAc phosphotransferase

Answer and explanation

A. NSF with α-SNAP uses ATP to reset SNAREs. Sec12 activates Sar1; V-ATPase pumps H⁺; phosphotransferase creates lysosomal sorting information.

Question 6

Which pair is most characteristic of clathrin-mediated uptake at the mammalian plasma membrane?

A. COPII and Sar1; B. AP-2 and clathrin; C. COPI and KDEL; D. Rab7 and M6P alone

Answer and explanation

B. AP-2 couples cargo and membrane to clathrin. A mediates ER export; C retrieval; D concerns other compartments and is not a surface coat.

Question 7

Which event is expected during ESCRT-dependent inward budding?

A. Receptor cytosolic tail becomes enclosed in an intralumenal vesicle; B. Tail flips to extracellular space; C. COPII coat forms on ER; D. Cytosolic proteasome directly digests the intact endosome

Answer and explanation

A. Inward budding sequesters the tail away from cytosolic signaling partners. B confuses topological sequestration with flipping, C is unrelated, and D misidentifies the degradative machinery.

Question 8

Assertion: KDEL-bearing soluble ER proteins may leave the ER and return. Reason: The KDEL receptor captures escaped cargo in the Golgi and participates in retrieval.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Retrieval explains why temporary escape is compatible with ER localization.

Question 9

Assertion: Active Rab-GTP always increases final cargo delivery when GTP hydrolysis is blocked. Reason: GTPase cycling enables timed changes in effector association.

A. Both true and explanatory; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

D. Sustained activation can block turnover or maturation; the reason explains why the assertion fails.

Question 10

Assertion: LDL receptor recycling can occur without new protein synthesis. Reason: After endosomal separation from LDL, the receptor can return to the surface.

A. Both true and explanatory; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

A. Reuse of the existing receptor allows renewed uptake.

Question 11

Which is an example of changing membrane transport by altering transporter number rather than its inherent transport chemistry?

A. Insulin-stimulated GLUT4 delivery; B. Na⁺/K⁺ pump hydrolysis; C. Osmosis through bare lipid; D. Cleavage of a signal peptide

Answer and explanation

A. More surface GLUT4 increases facilitated glucose transport capacity. B changes ion gradients by active pumping, C is water flux, and D is an entry-processing event.

21. Part C — Data interpretation and quantitative reasoning

Question 1

A sealed microsome contains an N-tag and a C-tag. Protease removes only C; protease plus detergent removes both. Infer topology.

Answer and explanation

N lumenal and C cytosolic is supported. Detergent exposes the protected tag; intact microsomes are essential to the inference. A resistant folded tag would require additional controls.

Question 2

A temperature-sensitive mutant accumulates labeled reporter in rough ER at restrictive temperature. The reporter is folded and two unrelated secretory cargoes also fail to exit. Identify the likely shared stage.

Answer and explanation

An early secretory transport machinery defect, such as COPII assembly, is more plausible than a reporter-specific signal mutation. Normal folding reduces the quality-control explanation, but additional budding and rescue data are needed.

Question 3

A soluble lysosomal hydrolase is secreted and lacks M6P. Uptake of an externally supplied normally tagged enzyme remains efficient. What lesion best fits?

Answer and explanation

A tagging defect is supported, for example defective phosphotransferase or uncovering. Functional uptake suggests the relevant receptor machinery remains available. Distinguish the two tagging enzymes by testing the covered intermediate.

Question 4

In a different cell line the secreted hydrolase has exposed M6P, but uptake of labeled tagged enzyme is poor. Which stage is favored?

Answer and explanation

Recognition or receptor-mediated uptake rather than tag synthesis. Receptor abundance, binding and internalization can distinguish receptor loss from a downstream trafficking defect.

Question 5

A donor membrane has 900 receptors in 90 µm². A 0.2 µm² vesicle contains eight receptors. Find enrichment.

Answer and explanation

Donor density = 10 molecules/µm². Vesicle density = 8/0.2 = 40 molecules/µm². Enrichment = 40/10 = fourfold. Count alone is not the relevant quantity.

Question 6

There are 600 total receptors in two closed pools. Internalization kᵢ=0.3 min⁻¹; recycling kᵣ=0.2 min⁻¹. Find surface amount and both fluxes at steady state.

Answer and explanation

S=600×0.2/(0.3+0.2)=240; I=360. Internalization=72 receptors/min; recycling=72 receptors/min. Steady state is not absence of movement.

Question 7

The recycling rate constant in the preceding model falls to 0.1 min⁻¹. What is the eventual surface fraction?

Answer and explanation

0.1/(0.3+0.1)=0.25; S=150 receptors if T stays 600. The model excludes synthesis and degradation.

Question 8

A pulse-labeled donor pool falls from 120 to 30 units in 20 min with first-order export and no return. Compute half-life and k.

Answer and explanation

Two half-lives elapsed, so t½=10 min. k=ln2/10=0.0693 min⁻¹. Disappearance alone cannot demonstrate export if degradation is permitted.

Question 9

Fusion adds 50 spherical vesicles of radius 100 nm per minute; endocytosis retrieves 100 vesicles of radius 50 nm. Find net area change if all fuse or bud completely.

Answer and explanation

Added=50×4π(0.1)²=6.283 µm²/min. Removed=100×4π(0.05)²=3.142 µm²/min. Net≈3.142 µm²/min. Twice as many retrieved vesicles do not balance area because each is smaller.

Question 10

Specific capacitance is 1 µF/cm². Predict the net change from the preceding question.

Answer and explanation

1 µm² corresponds to 10 fF. Therefore 3.142 µm²/min corresponds to about 31.4 fF/min, assuming electrically accessible membrane and fixed specific capacitance.

Question 11

An endosomal pH changes from 6.5 to 5.0. By what factor does free H⁺ concentration rise?

Answer and explanation

10^(6.5−5.0)=10^1.5≈31.6. Total H⁺ pumped cannot be calculated without buffer capacity and leakage.

Question 12

A mutant accumulates deep coated pits continuous with the surface. A second accumulates detached coated vesicles. Compare lesions.

Answer and explanation

First suggests scission failure, potentially dynamin-related. Second suggests uncoating or later turnover failure. Establish membrane continuity and coat persistence before identifying the protein.

Question 13

Complementary SNARE liposomes show lipid mixing but no content mixing. Can complete fusion be concluded?

Answer and explanation

No. Hemifusion or another partial lipid-mixing event may explain the result. Content mixing plus a leakage control is required for strong evidence of a complete fusion pore.

Question 14

A mutant secretes normal basal amounts of a reporter but has reduced triggered secretion despite a large mature granule pool. Which stage is plausible?

Answer and explanation

Stimulus coupling, Ca²⁺ sensing, priming or regulated fusion is implicated. Granule formation is less likely to be the primary problem because the stored pool is present.

Question 15

After growth-factor treatment, total receptor abundance stays constant but surface receptor falls rapidly and internal pool rises. What can be inferred?

Answer and explanation

Net internalization exceeds return to the surface at that time; total degradation is not established. Separate recycling and degradation assays are required to distinguish altered uptake from reduced recycling.

Question 16

A cargo acquires Endo H resistance but accumulates at TGN markers, with little extracellular label. Can a block at ER entry explain the dominant labeled cohort?

Answer and explanation

Unlikely: Golgi processing and TGN localization indicate the reporter passed early steps, assuming the reporter and markers are valid. Late budding, targeting or fusion needs discrimination.

Question 17

After a Rab7 perturbation, cargo accumulates in early-endosomal regions but clathrin-mediated uptake is normal. Where should investigation focus?

Answer and explanation

Post-internalization maturation and late endosomal identity are plausible. Normal uptake argues against a primary plasma membrane coat defect; compare Rab5/Rab7 markers and lysosomal delivery.

Question 18

A surface receptor carrying a ubiquitination-site mutation is internalized normally but remains signaling for longer and recycles excessively. Explain.

Answer and explanation

Loss of the relevant endosomal degradation signal may prevent efficient ESCRT-mediated sequestration and lysosomal sorting, leaving more receptors accessible to cytosolic signaling or recycling. Test receptor localization and degradation before claiming a direct ESCRT defect.

Question 19

A Golgi reporter becomes Endo H resistant normally, but its secretion is reduced. Electron microscopy shows many uncoated vesicles attached to the plasma membrane; their lumenal fluorescent contents remain separate from extracellular fluid. Which defect best explains all observations?

A. Loss of SRP recognition; B. COPII assembly failure; C. Late fusion defect; D. Increased M6P tagging

Answer and explanation

C. Golgi processing excludes A and B as primary defects in this cohort; attachment without contents release implicates fusion. D could redirect some cargo but does not account for docked carriers failing to release.

Question 20

Two closed receptor pools contain 2,000 receptors. At baseline 500 are at the surface and internalization is 100 receptors/min. A treatment halves internalization rate constant but leaves recycling rate constant unchanged. What is the new steady surface pool?

A. 400; B. 500; C. 800; D. 1,000

Answer and explanation

C. Initial kᵢ=100/500=0.2 min⁻¹, and kᵣ=100/1500=1/15 min⁻¹. New kᵢ=0.1 min⁻¹. S=2000×(1/15)/(0.1+1/15)=800. A predicts the wrong direction, B assumes no change, D assumes equal rate constants.

Question 21

A protein has a cleavable ER signal peptide, a stop-transfer helix, and a C-terminal cytosolic internalization motif. The motif is deleted without disturbing folding or other signals. Which outcome is most likely?

A. ER entry abolished; B. N-terminal domain becomes cytosolic; C. Surface delivery can occur but selected uptake is impaired; D. Protein becomes soluble in lysosomal lumen

Answer and explanation

C. The deletion affects the downstream uptake signal, not the upstream ER signal or membrane anchor. A and B confuse targeting with topology; D incorrectly assumes the transmembrane helix disappears.

Question 22

Pulse–chase label in a donor compartment falls from 100 to 40 units, while 20 units appear in the receiver and 40 units are degraded. Assuming complete recovery, what fraction of donor loss reached the receiver?

A. 20%; B. 33.3%; C. 40%; D. 60%

Answer and explanation

B. Donor loss=60; recovered receiver label=20; 20/60=1/3. A uses initial label rather than loss, C uses the remaining donor amount, and D is the total donor loss percentage. Disappearance cannot be equated with delivery.
