1. Why a living membrane needs controlled ion pathways

A cell lives in water, yet its electrical behaviour depends on maintaining different populations of dissolved ions on the two sides of a membrane only a few nanometres thick. Imagine a cell with abundant potassium in its cytoplasm and abundant sodium outside. If the membrane were equally permeable to every ion at all times, these differences would tend to disappear. If the membrane were completely impermeable, the cell could preserve the differences but could not use them to respond rapidly to a stimulus. A selectively permeable membrane resolves this conflict: energy-dependent transport establishes gradients, while ion channels allow the cell to exploit them at a particular place and time. Opening a suitable channel can alter the electrical state of a cell within milliseconds, much faster than synthesizing a new protein.

An ion is an atom or group of atoms carrying net electric charge. Sodium and potassium each carry one elementary positive charge; calcium carries two; chloride carries one negative charge. In water, ions are surrounded by oriented water molecules. The hydrocarbon interior of a phospholipid bilayer provides a very different environment: it cannot stabilize exposed ionic charge nearly as effectively as water. Consequently, ions cross an intact protein-free bilayer extremely slowly compared with many uncharged lipid-soluble molecules. The obstacle is an energetic one rather than simply a question of whether an ion is physically small enough.

An ion channel is a membrane protein or protein complex that supplies a conducting pathway through this barrier. Its interior is chemically different from the surrounding lipid. Polar groups, water molecules and strategically placed charged residues can stabilize ions as they enter and leave the pore. A channel is not merely a hole punctured through the bilayer; a protein-defined pathway has a characteristic preference for certain ions and can change its conducting state. A cell can therefore regulate the ionic composition and electrical behaviour of a compartment without making the entire membrane leaky.

Three questions organize this chapter. Which ions can use the pathway? Under what conditions does the pathway conduct? Once it conducts, in which direction and at what rate do ions travel? The first is selectivity, the second gating and the third permeation driven by an electrochemical gradient. These are distinct properties even though they can influence each other. A potassium channel may retain perfect potassium selectivity while being locked closed, and an open potassium channel may carry no net current at its equilibrium voltage. Separating these possibilities prevents a large fraction of the errors encountered in membrane-transport problems.

Passive transport does not mean that nothing in the system uses energy. A sodium gradient may have been constructed by an ATP-dependent pump; a ligand may have been synthesized using metabolic energy; a mechanically sensitive channel may receive energy from membrane tension. The classification refers to the movement of the permeating ion. If ion movement through the open pore is downhill in electrochemical free energy, the transport event is passive. Maintaining the gradients over time is a different task. We will return to the division of labour between channels and pumps after understanding how a pore can be both fast and selective.

2. Pores, carriers and the architecture of a channel

The distinction between a pore and a carrier begins with the path that a solute takes. A carrier such as a facilitative glucose transporter binds a solute on one side, changes conformation, exposes its binding site to the other side, and releases the solute. Alternating access prevents a continuous aqueous connection between the two reservoirs. Each transport cycle transfers a small, defined number of solute molecules. A conducting channel instead presents an accessible pathway across the membrane. Many successive ions can use this pathway during one opening. Although channel gating involves conformational changes, the protein does not need to complete a full alternating-access cycle for every ion.

A typical narrow ion channel contains several functional regions. The entrance or vestibule connects bulk solution to a narrower region; it can influence local ion concentration through electrostatic attraction or repulsion. The selectivity filter distinguishes between potential permeants. A gate changes the accessibility or conductive conformation of the pathway. The rest of the protein provides structural support and couples regulatory signals to these regions. These divisions are useful for reasoning but are not universally separate physical structures: a filter can itself become a gate, and some channels have more than one gating process.

Membrane-spanning segments must be compatible with the bilayer on their outer surfaces, while the pore-facing surfaces can contain hydrophilic groups. In many potassium channels four subunits contribute to a shared central pore. In vertebrate voltage-gated sodium and calcium channels, four homologous domains of one large principal polypeptide form the analogous central architecture. Some ligand-gated channels assemble as pentamers; others use different subunit arrangements. The number of subunits is not a reliable predictor of how many ions are carried per unit time, and four subunits in a typical potassium channel do not mean four separate pores.

Consider an isolated membrane into which a single purified channel has been incorporated. If its gate opens, ions can diffuse through it repeatedly in either microscopic direction. The net flux is the difference between the two directions. A carrier can also support passive bidirectional transport, but its rate is limited by its turnover cycle and occupation of its solute-binding sites. A channel can support much larger flux per molecule because ions do not have to wait for each complete carrier cycle. Typical single-channel currents of a few picoamperes correspond to millions of elementary charges per second. This comparison is about mechanism, not a universal speed threshold: some channels conduct slowly, and channel conductance can saturate at high ion activity.

Some proteins blur textbook categories. Bacterial outer membrane porins have aqueous pores with varying selectivity and often allow relatively broad classes of small solutes to cross. They are not equivalent to all narrowly selective gated ion channels. An ionophore is another useful contrast: valinomycin can carry potassium across a lipid membrane by shielding its charge, whereas gramicidin forms a conducting pathway. An increase in membrane permeability therefore does not prove that an endogenous protein channel has opened. Structural knowledge and electrical evidence are needed to identify the mechanism.

The rapidity of ion channels also has a cost. Unregulated opening can collapse stored gradients, alter cell volume and disturb electrical signalling. A closed gate solves the problem without removing the channel from the membrane. A population of proteins already positioned at the cell surface can be recruited almost immediately by a stimulus. Conversely, removing or inserting channel proteins changes the maximum number of pathways available over longer times. These two modes of regulation will become distinguishable when we measure the current of a single channel and compare it with whole-cell current.

3. Hydration, dehydration and the energetic basis of selectivity

Why should a protein pore discriminate between sodium and potassium when both ions have the same charge? The answer begins in the surrounding water. Water has a permanent dipole: the oxygen end is partially negative, while its hydrogen ends are partially positive. Water molecules orient their oxygen atoms toward a cation and their hydrogen atoms toward an anion. The resulting hydration environment stabilizes the ion. These molecules exchange with the surrounding solvent; a hydration shell is a dynamic ensemble rather than a permanently attached rigid coat.

For ions of the same charge, a smaller bare radius usually produces a stronger local electric field. Sodium binds its hydration environment more strongly than potassium, and divalent calcium interacts still more strongly with nearby water. It follows that ordering ions by their bare crystallographic radii does not necessarily order the energy needed to move them through a narrow channel. Hydrated size, geometry and solvation energy must be considered together. A pore that admitted a bare potassium ion by size alone would not automatically exclude the smaller sodium ion.

The transition from bulk water to the pore may require partial or substantial removal of waters from an ion's immediate coordination environment. This costs free energy. An appropriately arranged set of protein oxygen atoms, other polar groups or confined water molecules can compensate for the lost interactions. Whether a particular ion crosses efficiently depends on the free energies of the intermediate states and transition barriers along its path. One ion may bind favourably at the entrance but encounter an unfavourable barrier deeper inside. Another may enter and leave rapidly because its interactions are well balanced. Therefore selectivity is not simply the ability to bind an ion tightly.

A helpful energetic picture is to compare the height of the largest relevant barrier for two permeants. If the pathway offers a lower barrier to potassium than to sodium, potassium crosses more frequently under comparable conditions. A simplified rate model assumes comparable attempt frequencies and uses the Boltzmann factor for crossing an activation barrier. Under those restricted assumptions, a difference of ΔΔG‡ between barriers gives a rate ratio approximately exp(ΔΔG‡/RT), where R is the molar gas constant and T is absolute temperature. At 298 K, RT is about 2.48 kJ per mole. A barrier disadvantage of 11.4 kJ per mole gives exp(11.4/2.48), approximately 100. Thus a modest molecular energy difference can produce striking discrimination. This illustrative estimate is not a general formula for permeability ratios of real multi-ion pores, because concentrations, voltage, occupancy and multiple barriers affect measured rates.

An ion also must leave the filter. Suppose a mutation introduces a strong negative charge into a cation-selective pore. Attraction may increase the occupancy of a cation binding site, but excessive stabilization may trap ions and slow their exit. Greater affinity can therefore decrease throughput. This is especially important for understanding calcium channels, where high-affinity calcium interactions coexist with efficient calcium conduction through occupancy-dependent processes. A useful channel combines selective interactions with a low enough exit barrier to support ongoing flux.

Two broad forms of discrimination recur across channel families. Electrostatic interactions can favour cations over anions, or the reverse; coordination geometry and solvation can discriminate between ions with identical charge. Pore diameter can matter, but it does not work independently of chemistry. A wide mechanosensitive pore may pass several ions and even small solutes, whereas a narrow filter may recognize one ion strongly. Channel selectivity is usually relative, not an absolute prohibition. Under unusual ionic conditions, a normally disfavoured ion may carry detectable current. Always interpret a statement of selectivity in the context of the tested solution and channel state.

4. The potassium filter: from structural evidence to rapid conduction

A particularly clear example of molecular selectivity emerged from the bacterial potassium channel KcsA. In 1998 Doyle and colleagues reported its crystal structure, revealing a tetrameric pore with a narrow extracellular filter and a wider internal cavity. The structure provided a physical explanation for many earlier observations of potassium preference. The cavity accommodates hydrated ions and helps reduce the energetic cost of approaching the middle of the membrane. The narrow filter is constructed largely from backbone carbonyl oxygen atoms contributed by each subunit, with threonine hydroxyl groups participating at the inner filter region.

The conserved filter sequence in many potassium channels contains the signature TVGYG. Four copies of the filter segment, one from each subunit, arrange oxygen ligands around a series of positions along the pore. At several of these positions a potassium ion can be coordinated by protein oxygens in a geometry resembling aspects of its hydration environment. The protein replaces the stabilizing waters as potassium enters the narrow part of the pathway. The smaller sodium ion cannot generally obtain equally favourable coordination in that architecture without changes to the filter or coordination pattern. Its stronger hydration further increases the cost of entering a less suitable environment. This explains why the smaller ion need not be the better permeant.

The classical picture of a filter as a fixed-size cage is useful as a first approximation, but not as a complete theory. The protein is flexible, filter oxygen atoms fluctuate, ions interact with one another, and water can participate in different occupancy states. A crystal structure captures arrangements of matter under specific conditions; it does not record the complete path of every ion during physiological transport. The robust examination-level conclusion is that selectivity arises from the combined geometry and energetic interactions of the filter, not from a simplistic comparison of bare radii.

The filter has multiple binding positions, commonly designated S1 through S4 from the outer toward the inner end. More than one ion may occupy the permeation pathway. Interactions among ions help balance the strong stabilizing contacts needed for selectivity with the need for onward movement. A newly arriving ion can influence the departure of another ion farther along the path. This provides an intuitive explanation of how high selectivity can coexist with a rapid conduction rate. The exact balance of water and ion occupancy varies with channel and experimental conditions; it is unnecessary to assume a universal microscopic conduction sequence.

What evidence connects this structure to function? Substituting residues in or near the filter can alter relative permeabilities, ion-dependent block or the stability of conducting states. A functional experiment records the resulting electrical behaviour under controlled ionic conditions. If a mutant acquires a significant sodium current while the wild type strongly favours potassium, the affected residue may participate in filter architecture. A current change alone is not sufficient to locate a selectivity determinant: the mutation could instead reduce membrane expression, alter open probability or cause structural misfolding. Reversal potentials under defined solutions, single-channel recording and expression controls help separate these possibilities.

Different potassium channels use related filter chemistry for different biological purposes. Some provide resting permeability, some open upon depolarization, and others respond to intracellular calcium, ATP-related signals or membrane stretch. KcsA itself is not a model of every gating mechanism: it is activated by acidic conditions on the intracellular side and is useful primarily for understanding the architecture of a selective pore. The distinction is important when connecting a structural experiment to a physiological conclusion.

When a potassium-selective pore is open in an animal cell with high cytosolic potassium, diffusion tends to move potassium outward. As positive charge leaves, the cell interior tends to become more negative. The resulting electrical force pulls potassium inward. Eventually the two effects can balance for that ion, even though potassium concentration remains unequal. Thus the filter determines which ion can travel, but neither its sequence nor the direction of the concentration gradient alone fixes the direction of net current. We need to examine the electrical contribution before predicting what any particular channel opening will do.

5. Sodium, calcium and chloride channels: alternative solutions

Sodium-selective channels show how an ion of the same charge as potassium can be favoured by a different pore. Typical vertebrate voltage-gated sodium channels contain four homologous domains, each contributing to the pore. In their selectivity filter, a characteristic set of residues often called the DEKA motif contributes aspartate, glutamate, lysine and alanine from the four domains. The arrangement differs substantially from the backbone oxygen cage of a potassium filter. Electrostatics, coordination and retained hydration contribute to preferential sodium permeation. Some bacterial voltage-gated sodium channels instead form tetramers with acidic filters, so the DEKA pattern must not be generalized to every sodium-conducting protein.

In many animal cells the external sodium concentration exceeds the internal concentration, while the inside is negative at rest. Both tendencies favour inward sodium movement. Opening sodium channels therefore frequently creates an inward positive current that makes the cell interior less negative. Voltage-gated sodium channels can open rapidly in response to depolarization and then inactivate, limiting the current even when depolarization continues. Epithelial sodium channels, in contrast, can provide regulated sodium entry across the surface of absorbing epithelia and are not the same as the fast voltage-gated sodium channels of excitable cells. Sodium selectivity and stimulus sensitivity are different classifications.

Calcium channels face a demanding coordination problem because calcium carries twice the charge of sodium. Free cytosolic calcium is ordinarily much lower than extracellular or stored calcium. This difference allows even small calcium entry to have large signalling effects. Voltage-gated calcium channels use acidic residues in their pore to favour calcium; many high-voltage-activated vertebrate channels contain a characteristic EEEE locus, while other subfamilies have different acidic motifs. Strong calcium binding can exclude monovalent ions even when sodium is much more abundant. When calcium is available, occupancy and interactions between ions help maintain calcium flux rather than simply trapping one calcium ion permanently.

In the absence of divalent ions, some calcium-selective channels allow appreciable monovalent current. Adding a small amount of calcium can initially reduce that monovalent current because calcium occupies the pore preferentially. Increasing calcium further can support substantial calcium conduction. The lesson is that permeability measured with one ion present is not always sufficient to predict behaviour in mixtures. This qualitative example does not require a detailed multi-ion kinetic model but reveals why a simple size sieve cannot account for every experiment.

Calcium entry also differs functionally from sodium entry. Both bring positive charge inward under many physiological conditions, but calcium is a chemical messenger as well as a charge carrier. A very small elevation in local free calcium can regulate a calcium-sensitive enzyme, promote vesicle fusion or activate a calcium-sensitive potassium channel. Pumps, exchangers and intracellular binding proteins restore low free cytosolic calcium. Calcium channels also exist in intracellular membranes; opening an ER calcium-release channel can move calcium from the lumen into the cytosol without any direct influx from extracellular fluid.

Anions require a different electrostatic and chemical environment. Chloride-selective channels can stabilize a negatively charged permeant through the arrangement of backbone and side-chain groups, including interactions with hydrogen-bond donors and positive electrostatic potential. The ClC family illustrates why a family name is insufficient to infer transport mechanism. Some members, such as ClC-1 in skeletal muscle, are chloride channels; other homologues function as coupled chloride/proton exchangers, not passive chloride pores. Many ClC channels have a dimeric architecture with a separate permeation pathway in each subunit, unlike the shared central pore of a typical potassium-channel tetramer.

Other chloride pathways include GABA-A receptor channels, glycine receptor channels and CFTR. The first two are ligand-gated anion channels, often passing some bicarbonate as well as chloride. CFTR is an unusual member of the ABC protein family that forms an anion channel. Phosphorylation and ATP-dependent gating regulate its activity; once it is open, chloride and bicarbonate movement follows electrochemical forces rather than being pumped uphill by an obligatory ATP-per-ion cycle. This exception is central to distinguishing energy used for gating from energy used for coupled transport.

Chloride is often described as inhibitory because opening chloride-permeable channels frequently stabilizes membrane voltage or moves it toward a more negative value in mature neurons. That is a conditional description, not an intrinsic property of the ion. In a cell with elevated intracellular chloride, the chloride equilibrium voltage can be more positive than the existing membrane voltage. Opening chloride channels can then drive negative charge out of the cell, causing depolarization. Even a depolarizing chloride conductance may reduce the voltage response to a concurrent excitatory input through shunting. The actual effect depends on equilibrium potential, other conductances and the threshold for a cellular response.

Not every cation channel is highly selective among cations. The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor conducts multiple cations; at typical negative membrane voltages, inward sodium and outward potassium contributions yield a net inward current. Some TRP and mechanosensitive channels conduct several cations. We should therefore distinguish selectivity for a charge class from selectivity for one ionic species. This distinction is essential when interpreting the voltage at which total channel current becomes zero.

6. Electrochemical gradients and the sign of ion current

To predict the direction of ion movement, define the membrane voltage V as the electrical potential inside minus the electrical potential outside. A value of −60 mV means the cytosol is electrically negative relative to the external solution. The concentration difference supplies a chemical tendency, while the electrical potential difference supplies an electrical tendency. Positive ions favour movement toward lower electrical potential; negative ions favour movement toward higher electrical potential. The two tendencies may reinforce each other or oppose one another.

Consider sodium at a negative membrane voltage when sodium is abundant outside. Diffusion favours entry and the electrical force on positive sodium also favours entry. Potassium is often abundant inside; its chemical gradient favours exit while the electrical force at negative voltage favours entry. Calcium is commonly much more concentrated outside the cytosol than within it, so both forces favour calcium entry across the plasma membrane at a negative voltage. Chloride is negatively charged: if it is more abundant outside, diffusion favours entry but the electrical contribution at negative voltage favours exit. We cannot resolve the competing forces without knowing their relative magnitude.

The equilibrium potential E for a single ion is the membrane voltage at which the net electrochemical force on that ion is zero under the specified concentration conditions. At this voltage the ion continues to move microscopically in both directions through an open channel, but the mean rates balance. No net flux does not mean no molecules are moving. Nor does equilibrium for one ion imply that every other ion is at equilibrium, or that the concentration gradients have disappeared. Full calculation of E from concentrations will follow in the chapter on membrane electrical properties. Here it is sufficient to treat E as a supplied or experimentally determined voltage.

Electrophysiologists conventionally call outward movement of positive charge positive current and inward movement of positive charge negative current. For anions, the relationship is reversed: inward chloride is a positive outward conventional current, whereas outward chloride is a negative inward conventional current. These conventions reflect the direction in which equivalent positive charge moves, not the direction of movement of all particles. They allow different ion currents to be added in an electrical equation without changing sign definitions for each species.

For a channel with approximately constant conductance, the driving-force expression is i = γ(V − Erev), where i is single-channel conventional current, γ is open-channel conductance and Erev is reversal potential. The latter is the voltage where the channel's net current changes sign. In a channel carrying effectively only one ion, Erev corresponds to that ion's equilibrium potential under the experimental conditions. For a mixed-ion channel, a reversal potential can instead represent cancellation of nonzero component currents. At such a voltage individual ions may continue to have net movement in opposite electrical directions.

The term V − Erev is the electrical driving force expressed in voltage units. If it is positive, the conventional current through an open ohmic channel is positive; if it is negative, the current is negative. For potassium with E = −90 mV and V = −60 mV, the driving force is +30 mV, so net positive charge exits. For sodium with E = +60 mV at the same voltage, the driving force is −120 mV, so positive charge enters. For chloride with E = −75 mV, the driving force is +15 mV, which corresponds to inward movement of chloride. If chloride E were instead −40 mV, the driving force would be −20 mV and chloride would move outward.

An open channel tends to drive V toward its reversal potential if it dominates other membrane currents, but the voltage need not reach that value. Other channels, pumps and external currents may compete. A high density of open potassium channels can pull the voltage close to potassium equilibrium; adding sodium conductance shifts the balance toward sodium equilibrium. Under voltage clamp, however, the experimenter holds V fixed and measures the current needed to maintain that voltage, so the channel's opening need not change V. The distinction between an unclamped living cell and a clamped recording is critical for interpreting experimental traces.

Reversal potential, permeability and conductance are related but not interchangeable. Permeability refers to how readily an ion can cross under specified conditions, and permeability ratios express preference. Conductance measures current response to voltage under defined conditions and depends on ion availability as well as the pathway. Reversal potential is a voltage, not a rate. Changing external potassium shifts the potassium equilibrium potential even when the protein sequence is unchanged. Changing a gate may alter current amplitude without changing ionic selectivity or reversal potential. These distinct outcomes will recur throughout the quantitative section.

7. From single-ion charge to conductance and current

Electric current is the amount of net charge crossing a boundary per unit time. One ampere means one coulomb per second. A single ion carries charge ze, where z is its signed valence and e is the elementary charge, about 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. If a pure ion current corresponds to a net flux of n ions per second in one direction, its magnitude is |i| = n|z|e. Rearranging gives n = |i|/(|z|e). This calculation concerns net movement; it cannot reveal the total number of ions moving in both directions.

Suppose a single sodium channel carries −2.4 pA. Convert picoamperes to amperes: 2.4 × 10⁻¹² A. Dividing by 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C gives approximately 1.50 × 10⁷ net sodium ions entering per second. If an equally large current were carried only by calcium, the number of calcium ions crossing per second would be half that value because each carries twice the charge. If chloride alone carried a current of −2.4 pA, the same magnitude of flux as sodium would be obtained, but the direction of particles would be outward. For mixed-ion pores, the total current cannot be converted into the flux of one ionic species without knowing its contribution.

Ohm's law states that a current through a simple electrical resistance is proportional to voltage. For an open channel whose current–voltage relation is linear over a chosen range, the proportionality applies to voltage measured relative to the reversal point. Write i = γ(V − Erev). The reciprocal of conductance is resistance for an ohmic pathway. Conductance has units of siemens (S = A/V); a picosiemens is 10⁻¹² S. Ion-channel currents are often small enough that picoampere and millivolt units are more convenient than SI base units. Multiplying pS by mV produces 10⁻¹⁵ A, which is 0.001 pA. Therefore i in pA = γ in pS × driving force in mV / 1000.

For example, a 25 pS potassium channel at −50 mV with Erev = −90 mV has a driving force of +40 mV. Multiplication gives 25 × 40 / 1000 = +1.0 pA, an outward potassium current. At −110 mV, the same channel carries 25 × (−20)/1000 = −0.50 pA, inward potassium current. At −90 mV the current is zero despite the open pore. This set of three observations illustrates a linear current–voltage relationship with a constant slope and a zero crossing at −90 mV.

If single-channel current is recorded at two voltages, conductance for a linear pathway can be estimated from the slope. Suppose the open level is −1 pA at −100 mV and +3 pA at −20 mV. The current difference is 4 pA and voltage difference 80 mV. Dividing gives 0.05 pA/mV, equivalent to 50 pS. Substituting −1 pA into i = 0.05(V − Erev), with V in mV, gives −20 = −100 − Erev, so Erev = −80 mV. The slope indicates conductance; the intercept on the voltage axis identifies the reversal point. A single amplitude without Erev cannot determine both.

The ohmic description is a local model rather than a universal property of pores. Ionic concentration asymmetry, multi-ion occupancy and voltage-dependent block can produce nonlinear current–voltage relations even when a channel is held open. For a nonlinear curve, slope conductance di/dV near a chosen voltage is not generally equal to chord conductance i/(V − Erev). The former measures the response to a small voltage change; the latter connects the chosen point to the reversal point. Neither can be assumed constant over all voltages. At reversal, the chord expression as written is undefined, although its limiting value may exist.

Rectification means that current flows more readily in one electrical direction or voltage range than in another. A classical inward-rectifier potassium channel can carry large inward current at sufficiently negative voltages yet restrict outward current at more positive voltages because intracellular magnesium and polyamines can block the pathway in a voltage-dependent manner. It does not reverse the thermodynamic direction of potassium movement, and it is not a pump. Such channels can still carry physiologically important outward potassium current over appropriate voltage ranges. The name inward rectifier describes the asymmetry of its current–voltage behaviour, not an unconditional inward flux.

A whole-cell recording is more complicated because changing voltage may change both single-channel current and the number of open pathways. A steep whole-cell current curve might reflect stronger electrochemical driving force, greater open probability, or both. To interpret it, we must quantify gating separately. This is why recording individual channels and calculating conductance from open levels, rather than from arbitrary averages, is valuable.

8. Gating: closed, open, inactivated and unavailable states

The continuous passage of ions through an open pore cannot account for the abrupt starts and stops of current observed in living membranes. The channel protein itself must occupy conformations with different conducting properties. A gate can be formed by constriction of helices, rearrangement of a selectivity filter, movement of a cytoplasmic segment into the conducting pathway, or other changes that increase the barrier to ion movement. These changes can be triggered by a stimulus, but the channel protein also undergoes thermal motion. Individual opening and closing events are consequently probabilistic.

A minimal description uses two states, closed and open. The closed state is nonconducting but able to respond to an activating stimulus. The open state permits permeation under a driving force. A channel in an activated conformation can still carry zero current at reversal, so electrical silence does not always identify a closed gate. A channel can also have multiple open or closed conformations with different conductance or lifetime. The two-state model is useful when a more detailed scheme is not justified by the available data.

Inactivation is another nonconducting state, distinct operationally from ordinary closure. A voltage-gated sodium channel, for example, may open upon depolarization and then become unavailable during a sustained depolarizing step. Although the activating stimulus remains, the pore does not sustain its initial current. Returning the voltage toward a resting value permits recovery from inactivation, after which the channel is ready to open again. Deactivation refers to closure following removal or reversal of an activating stimulus; recovery refers to restoration of availability. Some channels inactivate from closed states as well, so the simple sequence closed to open to inactivated is an instructional approximation rather than a universal compulsory pathway.

An important distinction is between inactivation and block. A blocker can enter or obstruct an otherwise conducting pore, or bind allosterically to alter gating. Inactivation involves a change in the channel's available conformations. In single-channel recordings both mechanisms can appear as interruptions of current. A ligand-gated receptor can undergo desensitization, becoming less responsive while agonist remains present. This is analogous in its functional outcome to inactivation, but the mechanisms and terminology differ across families. Identifying the cause requires suitable changes in voltage, ligand concentration, recovery time or blocker exposure.

A second distinction is between channel availability and open probability. If half a population is inactivated, the available half might open with very high probability. The measured fraction of all channels open is still limited by the unavailable population. A protocol that first hyperpolarizes the membrane may restore availability before a test depolarization. A different holding voltage may leave many channels inactivated. Thus two recordings from the same cell can show different peak currents at the same test voltage, without any change in channel expression or permeant-ion concentrations.

Gating is best understood as shifts among populations of conformational states. A stimulus can stabilize the open state, destabilize the closed state, or alter the transition barriers between them. An increased opening rate and a decreased closing rate both raise open probability at equilibrium, but their effects on the distribution of open and closed times differ. Exam problems often exploit that distinction. A mutation that makes an open state last longer need not change the amount of current flowing during an individual opening. A mutation that changes pore conductivity need not change how often the channel opens.

9. Voltage gating: coupling an electric field to a protein gate

A membrane voltage of tens of millivolts sounds small, but the electrical potential changes across a very thin membrane. Charged parts of membrane proteins can therefore experience a substantial electrical force. Voltage-gated channels use charged or polar structural elements to couple this electrical change to a different probability of opening. In canonical voltage-gated potassium, sodium and calcium channels, the voltage-sensor region contains four transmembrane segments called S1–S4. The S4 segment includes regularly spaced positively charged residues, commonly arginine or lysine. Their position within the membrane electric field influences the stability of sensor conformations.

In a common voltage-gated potassium channel, four subunits each contain a voltage-sensor domain and a pore-forming domain. S5 and S6 together with an intervening pore loop contribute to the pore; the voltage sensors lie around it. Depolarization changes the energetic preference of the charged S4-containing sensors. Their conformational rearrangement is transmitted to pore-forming helices through electromechanical coupling, often involving the S4–S5 linker and associated structures. The activation gate opens more readily. Hyperpolarization or repolarization can favour return to closed conformations. This account describes a common architecture, not a single identical motion in every voltage-sensitive channel.

The charges within the sensor can themselves generate a very small transient gating current when they move within the electric field, even if ionic permeation is prevented. Such a current is distinct from the larger ionic current carried by ions crossing the whole pore. Evidence for voltage sensing comes from electrical measurements, mutagenesis and structural studies: changing sensor charges can shift activation curves or their steepness, while mutations near the pore can affect selectivity or conductance. A shift is not by itself proof of a specific atomic movement, because sensor–gate coupling can also change.

For a simplified channel that opens more readily upon depolarization, an equilibrium activation curve is often represented by a Boltzmann relation: Popen = 1/[1 + exp((Vhalf − V)/k)], where Vhalf is the voltage producing half of the maximal normalized activation and k is a positive slope factor in the same voltage units. At V = Vhalf, the exponent is zero, so Popen = 1/2 in this idealized two-state representation. At voltages far below Vhalf the exponent is large and positive, making Popen small; far above Vhalf the exponent becomes negative and Popen approaches one. An effective gating-charge description gives k = RT/(z_eff F) when voltage is in volts, with z_eff an effective positive number of elementary charges and F the Faraday constant. It is not necessarily the literal number of moving residues in a complex channel.

If Vhalf = −30 mV and k = 10 mV, at −50 mV the exponent is (+20)/10 = 2 and Popen ≈ 0.119. At −30 mV it is 0.5; at −10 mV the exponent is −2 and Popen ≈ 0.881. A mutation shifting Vhalf to −50 mV while keeping k unchanged raises activation probability at −50 mV to 0.5. It may make a cell more responsive to modest depolarization, but whether this increases or decreases excitability depends on which ions the channel carries. A potassium channel opening earlier can oppose depolarization, whereas sodium entry often reinforces it.

A voltage-dependence curve must not be confused with an I–V curve. For a depolarization-activated sodium channel, open probability may rise as V approaches its positive reversal potential while inward driving force falls. Consequently inward current can peak at an intermediate voltage and then become smaller as the voltage approaches reversal, even though activation continues to increase. Calculating conductance from current divided by driving force, away from reversal, helps isolate activation. If inactivation develops during the measurement, the normalized conductance also depends on the time at which current is sampled.

Not all voltage-gated channels open upon depolarization. Hyperpolarization-activated cyclic-nucleotide-gated channels open more readily at negative voltages and carry a mixed cation current. Some channels combine voltage sensing with binding of intracellular calcium or other ligands. Gating categories describe dominant regulatory inputs, not mutually exclusive boxes. A channel can integrate multiple signals, and changes in membrane composition or auxiliary subunits can alter its gating parameters.

10. Ligand gating and integration of chemical signals

A ligand-gated channel converts chemical information into a change in membrane permeability. A ligand binds to a regulatory site on the protein and alters the relative stability of its conformations. The binding site may be remote from the pore. Mechanical coupling through the protein transmits changes from the ligand-binding domain to the gate. In contrast to a passive carrier, ligand binding in this situation regulates the path taken by other particles; the ligand itself does not necessarily cross the membrane.

The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor provides a classical example. Its pentameric assembly surrounds a central cation-conducting pore. Binding of acetylcholine at extracellular sites increases the probability of the conducting conformation. In a typical muscle membrane at a negative resting voltage, sodium entry and potassium exit contribute to the current, with a net inward positive-charge movement. Ligand concentration influences the probability of opening, while the ionic composition and voltage determine the net current through an open pore. At the mixed-ion reversal potential, acetylcholine may still bind and open the receptor but the total current becomes zero.

GABA-A and glycine receptors illustrate a different coupling between chemical recognition and pore selectivity. Their extracellular ligands activate anion-conducting channels. The biological effect cannot be inferred from ligand binding alone: the same activation can produce different changes in voltage when intracellular chloride differs. It is therefore possible to change the response to a neurotransmitter by altering chloride transporters that establish the gradient, without changing the receptor itself. The detailed synapse is a separate subject; the transferable membrane principle is that ligand recognition and ionic driving force jointly determine a response.

Ligand-binding sites may also face the cytosol. Inositol trisphosphate binds to receptors in ER membranes and promotes calcium release from the lumen. Ryanodine receptor channels respond to regulatory inputs including calcium in appropriate contexts. Calcium-activated potassium channels use intracellular calcium as an activating signal, often coupling a rise in calcium to increased potassium permeability. The calcium is the regulator and potassium is the principal transported ion; a channel need not conduct the ligand that controls it. Cyclic nucleotide-gated channels use intracellular nucleotides as regulatory ligands, and ATP-sensitive potassium channels integrate aspects of metabolic state.

Ligand concentration–response curves often rise and approach a plateau. Binding sites become occupied and the population cannot exceed its maximal possible response. An empirical Hill relation may describe normalized activation f = [L]^h/(Khalf^h + [L]^h), where [L] is ligand concentration, Khalf is the concentration giving half-maximal response, and h is an empirical Hill slope. For h = 1 and Khalf = 10 µM, concentrations of 5, 10 and 30 µM yield f values of 1/3, 1/2 and 3/4, respectively. These values predict normalized response only under the conditions and assumptions of the fitted model. Khalf is not necessarily a microscopic dissociation constant, and h does not necessarily count bound ligands.

At a fixed voltage and ionic composition, increasing ligand may increase the fraction of time each channel is open without altering its unitary current amplitude. Once all responsive channels are maximally activated, further ligand addition does not increase the response indefinitely. Some channels desensitize during prolonged ligand exposure: current falls although ligand remains present. A concentration–response experiment must distinguish peak response from steady response and account for timing. A drug may reduce apparent maximal current by pore block or altered gating, while a competitive antagonist may shift a response curve under suitable equilibrium conditions. Similar-looking curves do not establish identical molecular mechanisms.

ATP-sensitive potassium channels illustrate why the name of a ligand does not specify active transport. In pancreatic beta cells, changes in cellular nucleotide balance influence channel opening through channel and regulatory subunits. ATP can inhibit the potassium-conducting pathway, whereas Mg-nucleotides acting through regulatory components can favour activity in appropriate conditions. The potassium ions move passively when the pore conducts. This creates a link between metabolism and electrical activity without an ATP-hydrolysis requirement for each transported potassium ion. CFTR provides a complementary case in which ATP-dependent gating cycles are part of channel regulation but the anion flux remains passive.

11. Mechanical gating, leak pathways and combined control

A membrane is not merely a chemical interface; it is a deformable material. Stretch, pressure differences and forces transmitted by the cytoskeleton or extracellular structures can change the energetic cost of channel conformations. A mechanosensitive channel responds to such forces by changing its gating probability. Mechanical gating should not be confused with tearing the lipid bilayer: the membrane can remain intact while a protein-defined pore opens reversibly.

A simple physical explanation begins with membrane tension, the energetic cost of increasing membrane area. If the open conformation occupies a larger projected area than the closed conformation, increased tension can favour opening. In an idealized tension-gated system the difference in free energy between states can be written ΔGopen−closed = ΔG0 − σΔA, where ΔG0 is the zero-tension free-energy difference per channel, σ is membrane tension in N/m, and ΔA is the area increase in m². The product has units N·m, or joules per molecule. Greater tension lowers the free-energy cost of opening if ΔA is positive. This model isolates one physical contribution; some channels respond through tethers, curvature or other force-coupling mechanisms.

For a numerical illustration, suppose membrane tension increases by 2 mN/m and opening expands the effective area by 5 nm². Convert 2 mN/m to 2 × 10⁻³ N/m and 5 nm² to 5 × 10⁻¹⁸ m². The change in mechanical work is 1 × 10⁻²⁰ J per channel, approximately 2.43 kBT at 298 K, using kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K. In a simple equilibrium two-state system the open-to-closed odds increase by exp(2.43), approximately 11.4-fold. This does not mean the open probability itself rises by the same factor; probabilities are bounded by one. The area and tension values are illustrative, not measurements of a named channel.

Bacteria use mechanosensitive channels such as MscL and MscS as emergency release pathways. When a bacterium encounters a sudden decrease in external osmolarity, water enters and membrane tension may increase. Opening appropriate channels releases ions and small solutes, reducing the osmotic imbalance and helping protect the cell from excessive stress. A broad selectivity is useful here because the aim is rapid solute release rather than precise discrimination of a single ionic species. A channel with very low activation threshold, however, could leak valuable solutes under normal conditions. The balance between sensitivity and protection is physiologically important.

Animal mechanosensitive channels, including Piezo channels, respond to membrane-associated forces and allow cation movement. The resulting current can change the electrical behaviour of cells involved in sensation or mechanical homeostasis. In plants, mechanically sensitive pathways participate in responses to osmotic and mechanical changes. These examples demonstrate that channels are not restricted to nerve cells and that forces can serve as biological signals. The details of specialized sensory organs and whole-plant water relations belong to their respective physiology chapters.

Some channels are described as leak channels because they contribute to basal conductance without requiring a rapid activating event. This does not imply that they are permanently open or unregulated. Two-pore-domain potassium channels, for instance, can respond to factors such as pH, temperature, lipids or stretch, depending on the member. Basal activity is an experimentally useful description under defined conditions. Channels may also respond to phosphorylation, intracellular pH, membrane lipids and auxiliary proteins. The cell thus regulates existing pores on multiple timescales.

Gating mechanisms are best classified by asking what changes the relative probability of conducting states. A voltage-sensitive channel couples electrical work to conformational changes; a ligand-sensitive channel couples binding energy; a mechanically sensitive channel couples force. The resulting permeation pathway can in each case conduct ions passively. A stimulus that opens a channel is not automatically the source of energy for ion movement across that channel.

12. Open probability and elementary channel kinetics

In a single-channel recording, current often switches unpredictably between a baseline and a discrete open level. Under constant voltage and ion concentrations, an open event of one channel usually has a characteristic amplitude; the durations of openings and closures vary. Repeating the same stimulus does not guarantee identical event timing. The reproducible quantity is the statistical behaviour of the channel. Open probability Popen is the fraction of observation time spent conducting for a single stationary channel, or the probability that a channel is open at a specified time across repeated trials.

If one channel spends 240 ms open during a 600 ms observation, the estimated Popen is 240/600 = 0.4. If its open amplitude is −2 pA and its closed amplitude is zero, the average current is 0.4 × (−2) = −0.8 pA. A brief record may not accurately estimate a low open probability or rare long closures. If two channels are present and their openings overlap, the fraction of time with any current is not the open probability per channel. Instead, for two identical independent channels, count the total open-channel time, weighting the double-open intervals twice, then divide by twice the observation time.

The minimal kinetic model assigns a first-order opening rate α to the closed-to-open transition and a closing rate β to the open-to-closed transition. Both rates have units s⁻¹. If P is the probability of being open, 1 − P is the probability of being closed, because the model contains exactly two states. During a small time dt, openings increase P by α(1 − P)dt and closings decrease P by βPdt. Dividing by dt yields dP/dt = α(1 − P) − βP. This balance equation describes the average behaviour of a population or repeated trials rather than the precise trajectory of one channel.

At steady state dP/dt = 0, so α(1 − P∞) = βP∞. Expanding and collecting terms gives α = (α + β)P∞, hence P∞ = α/(α + β). Increasing α or decreasing β raises steady open probability. The two effects are not kinetically identical. Rearranging the differential equation gives dP/dt = −(α + β)(P − P∞). At constant rates the solution is P(t) = P∞ + [P(0) − P∞]exp(−t/τ), where τ = 1/(α + β). The time constant describes the rate of approach to the new steady state, not the mean open duration.

The mean time spent in an open state before closure is 1/β for this simple exponential waiting-time model, and the mean closed duration is 1/α. A channel with α = 200 s⁻¹ and β = 300 s⁻¹ has P∞ = 0.4, τ = 2 ms, mean open duration ≈ 3.33 ms and mean closed duration = 5 ms. From initially closed conditions after a step imposing these rates, P(2 ms) = 0.4(1 − exp(−1)) ≈ 0.253. One time constant produces about 63% of the total change toward steady state. It does not mean every channel has opened once.

If a mutation doubles both rates, P∞ remains 0.4 because the ratio α/(α + β) is unchanged. The time constant becomes 1 ms, and both individual dwell times halve. Therefore a steady-state activation curve can look normal while channel kinetics change substantially. Conversely, halving β alone increases P∞ to 200/350 ≈ 0.571 and increases the mean open time to 6.67 ms. Such reasoning helps identify whether altered current arises from faster recruitment, prolonged openings, or a change in available population.

Real channels can have several closed states, multiple conductance levels, inactivated states and time-dependent rates. Their dwell-time distributions can require more than one exponential, and the simple two-state model may be inadequate. In many experiments a transient step changes voltage or ligand concentration, so the rates before and after the step differ. The derived solution applies separately within an interval where rates are constant. The point of the model is not to force every record into two states but to connect measurable probabilities with molecular transitions and their consequences.

13. From a single channel to whole-cell current

A cell membrane contains many channel proteins, and a macroscopic current is their combined contribution. Suppose N identical channels are present and each has probability P of being open at a specified time. The expected number open is NP by linearity of expectation. If every open channel has the same current i at the chosen voltage and ionic conditions, the expected current is I = NPi. Substituting i = γ(V − Erev) for an ohmic pore gives I = NPγ(V − Erev). The product NPγ is the effective population conductance at that time. These expressions describe a homogeneous population; a heterogeneous population requires a sum of the contributions from its different channel types.

Consider 400 channels, each with 20 pS conductance, P = 0.25, V = −40 mV and Erev = +50 mV. The expected number open is 100. Single-channel current is 20 × (−90)/1000 = −1.8 pA. The mean whole-population current is −180 pA. If P rises to 0.5 while the other factors remain fixed, current doubles to −360 pA. If instead channel number falls to 200 and P rises to 0.5, the original mean current is restored. A macroscopic measurement alone cannot distinguish these two conditions.

The formula also reveals why changes in current can be misleading near reversal. At V = Erev, I is zero even if N is very large and P is close to one. A large increase in extracellular sodium could change Erev, and hence the current, without any change in the channel number or gating. A mutation that reduces unitary conductance by half can produce the same reduction in whole-cell current as halving the number of channels. Single-channel amplitudes, open durations, surface expression and voltage dependence provide complementary information.

When identical channels open independently with probability P at a fixed time, the number of open channels follows a binomial distribution. For two channels with P = 0.3, the probabilities of zero, one and two open channels are (0.7)² = 0.49, 2(0.3)(0.7) = 0.42 and (0.3)² = 0.09. Their sum is one. If i = −2 pA, the expected current is 0.49(0) + 0.42(−2) + 0.09(−4) = −1.2 pA, matching NPi. The independence assumption is needed for these particular state probabilities, not for calculating the mean sum of identical individual currents.

The variance of the number open in that binomial model is NP(1 − P), so current variance equals Ni²P(1 − P) when instrumental noise is absent and amplitudes are equal. It vanishes when all channels are closed or all are open and is maximal at P = 0.5 for fixed N and i. This elementary observation explains why intermediate activation often produces conspicuous fluctuations. Real recordings also contain electrical noise and biological sources of variability; not every fluctuation can be attributed to independent channel gating. The formula is useful in reasoning problems only when its assumptions are stated.

A further complication arises when channel opening changes the membrane voltage itself. At fixed voltage the single-channel driving force is constant; in an unclamped cell, opening many channels can shift V and hence reduce or increase their driving force. Thus multiplying the initial current by a long time can overestimate total transported charge if the gradient or voltage changes. In voltage-clamp experiments the imposed voltage removes this particular feedback, helping separate the intrinsic gating behaviour from the changing electrical state of the cell.

Finally, currents from different ions add algebraically using the same conventional current sign. If at one voltage sodium carries −100 pA and potassium carries +100 pA, the total ionic current is zero. Both ions nonetheless have nonzero net flux, and their gradients can gradually change. A steady voltage is therefore compatible with ongoing ionic movements. This idea will be fundamental when we develop resting membrane potential in the later electrical-properties chapter.

14. How experiments reveal channels: voltage clamp and patch clamp

Early measurements of membrane current in large excitable cells established that ionic conductance could change with voltage and time. The voltage-clamp approach made it possible to separate the consequences of changing membrane voltage from the current that caused those changes. In the classic work of Hodgkin and Huxley, experimentally separated sodium and potassium current components helped explain electrical excitability. Their macroscopic conductance model was developed before direct molecular observation of individual channels. It inferred the existence of voltage- and time-dependent pathways rather than imaging their protein structures.

The voltage clamp uses feedback to maintain a selected membrane potential. If ions flowing through membrane channels would otherwise change that potential, the apparatus supplies a compensating electrical current. The measured signal is used to infer the membrane current, with the sign convention specified by the recording system. A voltage step also produces a transient capacitive current as charge redistributes across the insulating membrane; leak conductance can add background current. A recorded step response therefore needs interpretation: a brief transient immediately after the voltage change is not automatically evidence of rapid ion-channel activation.

Patch-clamp recording developed by Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann made currents from individual channels experimentally accessible. A small glass pipette contacts the membrane and forms a high-resistance seal, often of gigaohm order. This reduces leakage around the membrane patch and allows the tiny current through a small group of channels to be distinguished from background noise. A single-channel recording can reveal discrete transitions between closed and open levels. The discovery that an individual channel spends variable periods in conducting and nonconducting states provided direct support for probabilistic gating. Their work was recognized by the 1991 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

The patch can be studied in several configurations. In cell-attached recording, the membrane beneath the pipette remains connected to the cell. The intracellular environment is relatively intact, but the absolute voltage across the patch may be uncertain if the resting cell voltage is unknown. In an inside-out excised patch, the cytoplasmic surface faces the bath solution, allowing control of intracellular ligands and ions while the extracellular side remains associated with the pipette solution. In outside-out configuration, the extracellular face is exposed to the bath, useful for changing extracellular ligand concentration. Whole-cell recording is achieved when the membrane patch beneath the pipette is opened, giving electrical access to the cell interior and measuring macroscopic current across the cell membrane.

These configurations answer different questions. To test whether cytosolic calcium directly activates a potassium channel, an inside-out patch allows changes in the solution contacting the intracellular face. Persistence of activation after excision supports a direct or membrane-associated mechanism, though it does not by itself prove binding to a particular protein domain. To test the effect of an extracellular agonist on a ligand-gated receptor, an outside-out patch is appropriate. Whole-cell measurements better reveal the total electrical response but can average over many channels and may alter cytoplasmic composition through exchange with pipette solution.

At a fixed voltage, one channel may show steps of equal height but unequal duration. The step height is the unitary current and depends on conductance and driving force. The total time open provides an estimate of open probability; the number and distribution of events provide kinetic information. If a drug increases the time spent open while leaving the current level unchanged, the evidence favours a gating effect rather than altered unitary conductance. If step amplitude decreases while durations remain similar, altered conduction or rapid unresolved block is plausible. Recordings have finite time resolution: very brief transitions may be missed or averaged, so an apparently smaller amplitude may reflect a fast block rather than a physically narrowed pore.

Two independently opening identical channels can produce three levels: zero, one open-channel current, and twice that current. Multiple levels can also arise from a single channel with subconductance states. The number of observed levels is thus not unambiguous evidence of channel number. One must consider repeated observations, kinetics and channel expression. Likewise, absence of visible openings can result from a closed gate, a lack of driving force, a very low number of channels, failed recording or conductance below detection. Each alternative requires a discriminating control.

An experiment measuring current at several imposed voltages produces an I–V relation. Replacing ions in the bath and measuring the change in reversal voltage can help identify permeant species. A selective blocker can suppress one component of a mixed whole-cell current; comparisons before and after its addition can reveal the blocked component. Neither ion substitution nor pharmacological inhibition alone provides absolute identification, because changes in ionic strength, osmolarity, channel gating and other pathways can affect the result. Controls and independent evidence are central to sound interpretation.

Modern structures, mutagenesis and purified-protein reconstitution complement electrophysiology. A structure suggests which amino acids form a pore and how a regulator might change its conformation. A mutation tests a functional prediction but may also alter folding or localization. Reconstitution can establish that a purified protein is sufficient to generate a conductance under defined conditions; it does not automatically establish its entire role in a living cell. The strongest scientific explanation combines these independent types of evidence without claiming more than each can demonstrate.

15. Reading experiments: distinguish selectivity, gating and driving force

Begin a channel data problem by identifying what was controlled and what was measured. A current measured at fixed voltage is not equivalent to a membrane-voltage change in an unclamped cell. A peak current after a step may differ from a steady current measured later. A single-channel trace provides unitary amplitude; a whole-cell trace sums the activity of many pathways. Before interpreting a drug or mutation, decide whether the evidence concerns N, P, γ, Erev, or a mixture of these.

Consider the following illustrative experiment. A potassium channel is held at different voltages, and its open current and time-averaged current are measured. At −100 mV the open current is −0.4 pA and the average is −0.02 pA; at −60 mV the open current is +0.4 pA and the average is +0.12 pA; at −20 mV the open current is +1.2 pA and the average is +0.96 pA. The open current changes by 0.8 pA for each 40 mV increase, so the conductance is 20 pS. Extrapolating to zero gives Erev = −80 mV. Dividing average by open amplitude gives P values of 0.05, 0.30 and 0.80. The pore is approximately ohmic in this range, but the channel is strongly voltage activated. An average I–V curve alone would hide this separation.

Now imagine a mutant with the same single-channel amplitude at every voltage and the same reversal potential, but with open probability 0.40 rather than 0.80 at −20 mV. Its mean current at that voltage is half the wild-type current: +0.48 pA rather than +0.96 pA because potassium current is outward there. The data support altered gating under these conditions. A different mutant with unchanged P and a smaller single-channel amplitude may have altered pore conduction; additional measurements across voltage can distinguish a reduced slope from a shifted reversal potential. If both are unchanged but whole-cell current falls, reduced number of available channels is one hypothesis.

Ion substitution provides a distinct type of evidence. Suppose a pure channel has a reversal potential of −80 mV in a solution with high cytosolic potassium. Increasing external potassium shifts reversal in the positive direction, while replacing extracellular sodium with an impermeant substitute has little effect under otherwise controlled conditions. These observations support potassium selectivity. They do not alone prove that no other ion permeates. A mixed cation channel might respond to changes in both sodium and potassium. To measure quantitative permeability ratios, the later electrical-properties chapter will introduce the appropriate formal framework and assumptions.

A voltage-step experiment provides insight into inactivation. Depolarize a cell to a fixed potential. A sodium-selective current quickly rises in the inward direction and then declines toward baseline during the maintained step. A potassium current may activate more slowly and persist. Since voltage is held fixed, a rapid decline of sodium current cannot be attributed simply to membrane repolarization. Inactivation is a plausible explanation if gradients remain effectively constant, but ion accumulation, time-dependent block or recording artifacts must be considered. A second pulse after different recovery intervals tests whether the channels regain availability with time. Short intervals may produce smaller test currents and longer intervals restore them.

For example, suppose a conditioning pulse inactivates nearly all of a channel population. A test pulse after 5 ms recovers 40% of the original current, after 10 ms 64%, and after 20 ms 87%. These illustrative values are close to recovery described by 1 − exp(−t/τ) with τ about 9.8 ms: at 5 ms the unrecovered fraction is 0.6, so τ = −5/ln(0.6) ≈ 9.79 ms; the predictions at 10 and 20 ms are 0.64 and 0.87. This single-exponential fit is consistent with a dominant recovery process but cannot uniquely identify the underlying molecular states.

A concentration-response experiment at a fixed voltage provides a different test. If adding extracellular agonist increases opening frequency without changing the unitary current level, the agonist promotes gating. If an antagonist shifts the curve to higher ligand concentrations while the maximum can be regained, reversible competition at the ligand-binding site is one possible mechanism. If the maximum falls and cannot be recovered by more agonist under the tested conditions, pore block or noncompetitive effects become plausible. Timing, receptor desensitization and irreversible loss of functional channels can also affect maximum response. A graph can narrow the hypotheses but should not be used to infer a binding site without additional evidence.

The most reliable analytical procedure is to formulate an explanation that predicts another observable. A gating shift should change normalized activation or dwell times; a conductance change should change the open-level I–V slope; selectivity changes should alter reversal behaviour under suitable ionic substitutions; a change in channel number should affect the maximum possible population current without necessarily affecting individual channels. This predictive reasoning converts a descriptive answer into a testable scientific conclusion.

16. Biological functions and channelopathy reasoning

Ion channels allow cells to convert stored ionic gradients into electrical, chemical and mechanical responses. In a resting animal cell, potassium permeability often makes an important contribution to a negative membrane potential. Recruitment of sodium or other cation conductance can shift voltage in a positive direction; subsequent recruitment of potassium conductance can oppose that change. This cooperation is the cellular basis of rapid electrical signalling, but a full action-potential derivation belongs in the electrical-properties chapter. Here the central idea is that different channels change the relative conductances, not the gradients themselves in every instant.

Calcium channels connect electrical or chemical signals to intracellular reactions. A calcium-permeable pathway can deliver calcium into a small region of cytosol where calcium-sensitive proteins respond. A calcium-activated potassium channel may then open and oppose the original depolarization. This is an example of negative feedback: a stimulus creates calcium entry, which recruits a process that tends to limit further activation of voltage-sensitive calcium channels. The actual result depends on the equilibrium voltages and channel types present. In a nonexcitable cell, calcium entry or release can control secretion, metabolism or gene regulation without a nerve-like action potential.

Epithelial transport provides another illustration. A cell with different membrane domains can place sodium and chloride pathways at one surface and pumps or other transporters at another. Local passive currents, sustained by active transport elsewhere, can create net movement across an entire tissue. Water responds to the resulting osmotic gradients. The direction of an individual channel's current must be analysed at its particular membrane, not inferred from the overall direction of tissue transport. A chloride channel involved in secretion in one epithelium may support absorption in another because the gradients and surrounding transport network differ.

CFTR dysfunction demonstrates how a channel defect can disrupt water balance indirectly. CFTR normally contributes to regulated chloride and bicarbonate conductance in epithelial membranes. Loss of functional surface protein or defective gating can reduce anion transport; in relevant tissues this alters fluid and electrolyte handling. The common F508del variant is associated with folding and trafficking defects as well as additional functional limitations of rescued protein. Other variants can predominantly affect gating. Similar reductions in epithelial current therefore need not arise from identical molecular defects. This is a mechanistic illustration, not a clinical treatment guide.

In skeletal muscle, ClC-1 contributes a large chloride conductance that helps stabilize membrane electrical responses. Loss-of-function variants can reduce this stabilizing conductance and lead to myotonia, in which electrical and mechanical relaxation is delayed after contraction. The mechanism is not that chloride is an intrinsically inhibitory substance. Rather, its available conductance normally limits voltage excursions under the prevailing gradients. Reducing that conductance changes the competition among currents and permits excessive excitability.

Cardiac potassium channels contribute to the timing of electrical recovery. Reduced outward potassium current in certain inherited channelopathies can prolong repolarization, contributing to long-QT syndromes. Conversely, some gain-of-function changes increase outward current and may shorten electrical recovery. A sodium-channel mutation that impairs inactivation can maintain inward current longer than usual, also potentially prolonging depolarization. Opposite defects in different ionic pathways can produce a similar macroscopic electrical phenotype. A strong reasoning answer identifies the direction and duration of the altered current, rather than treating all channel mutations as equivalent.

Selective pharmacological probes help establish such mechanisms experimentally. Tetrodotoxin blocks many voltage-gated sodium channel subtypes, though some are relatively resistant. Tetraethylammonium blocks several potassium channels, with sensitivity dependent on subtype and conditions. Dihydropyridines act on L-type calcium channels. These examples illustrate experimental separation of current components, not universal one-to-one identifications. A blocker-sensitive current provides evidence of the pathway involved, but dose, specificity and expression matter. A change in cell behaviour after applying a blocker does not alone establish all details of the channel's normal role.

Channels can also protect microorganisms and plant cells during environmental change. Mechanosensitive release pathways reduce osmotic stress, while selective potassium and calcium pathways participate in ionic balance and signalling in plant cells. Ion-channel principles therefore connect molecular structure with adaptation across kingdoms. Whenever a phenotype is presented, ask which ion moves, which force drives it, what controls the pore and how the altered conductance changes the function of the whole system.

17. Channels and pumps: two distinct uses of membrane proteins

A channel provides a route for ions to dissipate an electrochemical gradient. A primary active pump directly couples an energy-releasing reaction, such as ATP hydrolysis, to uphill ion movement. A secondary active transporter couples the downhill movement of one solute to uphill transport of another. A facilitated-diffusion carrier moves its substrate downhill by alternating access. All are membrane proteins, and all may be selective; their defining differences lie in the relationship between conformational changes, ion transfer and energy.

Imagine a sealed cell-sized vesicle containing high potassium and low sodium. If potassium channels open and appropriate countercharge movement or electrical constraints permit it, potassium redistributes toward electrochemical equilibrium. The channels do not automatically restore the original concentrations when closed. A pump can rebuild a gradient through coupled chemical work. In an intact cell, the sodium–potassium ATPase and other transporters maintain gradients over longer intervals; ion channels use those gradients for signalling and homeostasis. Pump inhibition may have limited immediate effects on a channel's unitary conductance, while eventually changing driving forces as gradients deteriorate.

A pump can also carry electrical current if it transfers unequal net charge per transport cycle. Current is not exclusive evidence of an ion channel. Conversely, ATP can participate in channel gating, as in CFTR, without driving uphill flux of the permeant ion. A ligand-gated receptor may use binding energy to open, but that does not make the ion movement secondary active transport. The decisive criterion is whether the permeant ion's movement is obligatorily coupled to an energy-yielding process that permits net uphill movement.

A useful exam strategy is to consider reversal. For a passive single-ion pore, changing voltage through its equilibrium potential reverses the net ion flux while the gate remains open. An active pump can sustain a flux against the gradient while its coupled energy source makes the overall cycle thermodynamically favourable; under sufficiently altered conditions some pumps or coupled transporters can reverse their cycles. Neither channels nor pumps violate thermodynamics. They solve different biological problems using different molecular mechanisms.

This distinction prepares the next chapter on active transport. There we will study ATP-coupled stoichiometry and gradient-coupled transport explicitly. The later membrane-electrical-properties chapter will derive equilibrium potentials from chemical and electrical work and examine how many conductances jointly establish membrane voltage. For now, a complete explanation of any ion-channel experiment should connect the pore, gating state, driving force, conductance and population size without confusing them.

18. Consolidation: a diagnostic framework for any channel problem

Start with the permeant ion and its valence. Identify whether the channel is strongly selective or carries several ion species. Determine which side is more concentrated and what electrical potential convention is used. When equilibrium or reversal voltage is given, compute V − Erev before deciding current direction. For an anion, reverse the correspondence between current sign and particle direction. At zero total current, decide whether the channel is a single-ion pathway at equilibrium or a mixed pathway with opposing component currents.

Next identify the regulatory stimulus and the channel's availability. If the stimulus changes voltage, distinguish the change in driving force from the change in open probability. If ligand is added at fixed voltage, a change in mean current can reflect a change in gating, but check unitary amplitude and possible block. If a response fades during a maintained stimulus, consider inactivation, desensitization, block and gradient changes. A recovery experiment or solution control may distinguish them.

When numbers are provided, begin with units. Multiply pS by mV and divide by 1000 to obtain pA; convert pA into A before dividing by elementary charge. For a homogeneous population use I = NPi, with N equal to the number of channels and P defined for the specified population. For two-state kinetics use P∞ = α/(α + β) and τ = 1/(α + β) only when the stated model and constant-rate assumption are appropriate. Remember that doubling both rates preserves steady-state open probability but changes timing.

The chapter's molecular and electrical explanations fit together. Hydration and protein coordination explain why ions prefer different pores. Gating allows a cell to deploy that permeability in response to information. Electrochemical forces decide the direction of net movement, and conductance and open probability determine the rate under a defined model. The experimental ability to measure those quantities separately is what turns ion-channel biology into a predictive science.

19. Part B: conceptual and assertion–reason questions

These original questions test the concepts and reasoning developed in this chapter. They are not reproduced past examination questions.

1. Why is bare ionic radius insufficient to explain K⁺ selectivity over Na⁺?

A. Sodium has greater positive valence; B. Sodium is less hydrated; C. Hydration costs and filter coordination differ; D. The pore consumes ATP to reject sodium.

Answer and reasoning: C. Both are monovalent, so A fails. Sodium generally has stronger hydration, contrary to B. Protein coordination can compensate potassium dehydration more favourably. ATP-driven discrimination is not required, so D fails.

2. A channel conducts at −80 mV but has zero mean net current. Its single-ion reversal potential is −80 mV. What follows?

A. The gate must be shut; B. The chemical gradient must be zero; C. Opposing microscopic ion fluxes balance; D. The channel is a pump.

Answer and reasoning: C. Equilibrium refers to net electrochemical flux. An open channel can be silent, and unequal concentrations can be balanced by voltage. Neither A, B nor D follows.

3. Which feature characterizes canonical voltage sensors in Kv channels?

A. Positive residues in S4; B. TVGYG is an ATP-binding domain; C. Four separate central pores; D. An extracellular agonist is always essential.

Answer and reasoning: A. S4 charges couple voltage to gating. TVGYG is a filter motif, the subunits usually share a central pore, and voltage activation does not universally require an agonist.

4. Which statement about CFTR is correct?

A. It pumps one chloride per ATP; B. ATP regulates gating of a passive anion pore; C. It is a voltage-gated sodium channel; D. It always produces inward chloride flux.

Answer and reasoning: B. ATP-dependent gating is not obligatory uphill anion transport. Flux direction depends on the anion gradient and voltage, excluding D.

5. Assertion: An open chloride channel can depolarize a cell. Reason: Chloride equilibrium potential can be more positive than resting voltage.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true without explanation; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Answer and reasoning: A. If V is below chloride equilibrium, conventional current is negative and chloride can leave. Loss of negative charge depolarizes the cell.

6. What does an inward-rectifier potassium channel name describe?

A. ATP-driven uptake; B. Current–voltage asymmetry; C. An absolute prohibition of K⁺ exit; D. Na⁺ selectivity.

Answer and reasoning: B. Rectification reflects unequal conductance across voltage ranges, often due to voltage-dependent block. The net flux still obeys its electrochemical force.

7. Which patch-clamp configuration exposes the cytosolic membrane face to the bath?

A. Cell-attached; B. Outside-out; C. Inside-out; D. Whole-cell only.

Answer and reasoning: C. Inside-out excision exposes the intracellular face. Outside-out exposes extracellular face; cell-attached retains the cell interior, and whole-cell records the integrated membrane current.

8. Assertion: A channel can have an unchanged equilibrium open probability but altered kinetics. Reason: Doubling both α and β leaves α/(α+β) unchanged.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true without explanation; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Both false.

Answer and reasoning: A. The ratio is unchanged but τ = 1/(α+β) halves. The state occupancy and transition timing are distinct properties.

9. A ligand increases current without changing its single-channel amplitude at fixed voltage. Which interpretation is most directly supported?

A. Greater unitary conductance; B. Shift in ionic reversal potential necessarily; C. Increased open probability or availability; D. Active ion pumping.

Answer and reasoning: C. An unchanged open amplitude argues against A under fixed conditions; current can rise by recruiting more openings. B and D are not justified.

10. Which distinction is accurate?

A. All ClC proteins are passive channels; B. All potassium channels have S4; C. Some ClC proteins are chloride/proton exchangers; D. Every Ca²⁺ channel is strictly impermeable to monovalents under every condition.

Answer and reasoning: C. The family includes channels and exchangers. Some potassium channels lack a canonical S4 sensor; certain calcium channels pass monovalent ions in divalent-free conditions.

11. What happens immediately when an ion channel opens at a fixed voltage with no electrochemical driving force for its sole permeant ion?

A. Net current remains zero; B. Current must rise; C. The ion must be pumped uphill; D. Concentrations instantly equalize.

Answer and reasoning: A. Conductance can increase without net ionic current. This does not remove the concentration gradient or imply active transport.

12. Assertion: A calcium-activated potassium channel can close a negative feedback loop. Reason: Calcium entry can increase K⁺ conductance, which under suitable gradients opposes depolarization.

A. Both true and reason explains assertion; B. Both true without explanation; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false.

Answer and reasoning: A. Where K⁺ equilibrium is negative relative to V, added K⁺ conductance favours repolarization and may reduce further voltage-dependent calcium entry. The gradient condition matters.

20. Part C: quantitative and experimental reasoning

These original questions test the concepts and reasoning developed in this chapter. They are not reproduced past examination questions.

1. Unitary current and flux

A 40 pS calcium-selective pore is held at −20 mV; Erev = +100 mV. Find current and net calcium flux.

Answer and reasoning: i = 40(−120)/1000 = −4.8 pA. Flux = 4.8×10⁻¹²/(2×1.602×10⁻¹⁹) = 1.50×10⁷ Ca²⁺ ions/s inward. Dividing by e alone would overestimate particle flux twofold.

2. Recover a reversal potential

A channel has open currents −2 pA at −120 mV and +1 pA at −60 mV, with linear I–V behaviour. Calculate γ and Erev.

Answer and reasoning: Slope = 3/60 = 0.05 pA/mV = 50 pS. At −60 mV, 1/0.05 = 20 mV = −60 − Erev, so Erev = −80 mV. The intercept, not the negative-current point, identifies reversal.

3. Anion sign

A chloride-only pore has ECl = −35 mV and γ = 10 pS. The cell is clamped at −65 mV. Predict current and ion movement.

Answer and reasoning: i = 10(−30)/1000 = −0.30 pA. Negative conventional current carried by negative charge corresponds to outward chloride movement. If unclamped and dominant, the pore tends to depolarize the cell.

4. Separate expression from gating

A wild-type population of 600 channels has P = 0.3 and i = −1.5 pA. A mutant has 450 channels and the same i, but the measured mean is unchanged. Find mutant P.

Answer and reasoning: WT I = 600×0.3×(−1.5) = −270 pA. Pmut = −270/[450(−1.5)] = 0.4. Loss of channel number can be compensated by increased probability; whole-cell current alone misses both changes.

5. Two-state kinetics

A channel has mean closed time 4 ms and mean open time 1 ms, with exponential dwell times. Estimate α, β, P∞ and τ.

Answer and reasoning: α = 1/0.004 = 250 s⁻¹; β = 1/0.001 = 1000 s⁻¹. P∞ = 250/1250 = 0.20; τ = 1/1250 = 0.0008 s = 0.8 ms. Mean open duration is not τ.

6. Step activation

A two-state channel starts with P = 0.1. After a voltage step P∞ = 0.7 and τ = 4 ms. Find P at 4 and 8 ms.

Answer and reasoning: P(t) = 0.7 − 0.6exp(−t/4 ms). At 4 ms P = 0.479; at 8 ms P = 0.619. An exponential approach does not add the same probability in each time interval.

7. Shift in voltage activation

Two channels have slope factor 8 mV, with half-activation voltages −40 and −24 mV. At −40 mV compare activation.

Answer and reasoning: WT P = 0.5. Second channel P = 1/[1+exp(16/8)] ≈ 0.119. A positive shift requires greater depolarization for equivalent activation. No unitary conductance change follows from this result.

8. Multiple openings

Two independent identical channels each open with probability 0.4. Each open current is +3 pA. Find probability of a 6 pA level and mean current.

Answer and reasoning: Double-open probability = 0.4² = 0.16. One-open probability = 2(0.4)(0.6) = 0.48; zero = 0.36. Mean = 0.48×3 + 0.16×6 = 2.4 pA, also 2×0.4×3.

9. Mixed-ion reversal

At a specified voltage, a ligand-gated pore carries −90 pA sodium and +90 pA potassium current. What is the total, and are gradients necessarily at equilibrium?

Answer and reasoning: Total = 0 pA. Sodium enters and potassium exits, with opposite conventional currents. Zero total does not mean zero net movement of either ion. The mixed-ion reversal point is a balance of currents.

10. Rectification

With Erev = −80 mV, a channel carries −4 pA at −120 mV and +0.4 pA at −40 mV. Is the pathway ohmic?

Answer and reasoning: Equal magnitude driving forces of 40 mV yield unequal amplitudes. Chord conductances are 100 pS inward and 10 pS outward. The curve rectifies inward; it is not evidence of uphill transport.

11. Distinguish three mutants

At a fixed driving force, mutant A has half the open amplitude and unchanged P; B has unchanged amplitude and half P; C has normal single-channel data but half macroscopic current. Give likely explanations and controls.

Answer and reasoning: A suggests lower unitary conduction or fast unresolved block; measure open-level I–V and time resolution. B suggests altered gating; compare dwell distributions. C suggests fewer available channels, but expression and inactivation must be tested separately.

12. Recovery after a conditioning pulse

A channel population recovers from inactivation with time constant 12 ms, initially fully unavailable. What fraction is recovered after 6 and 24 ms?

Answer and reasoning: For this model f = 1−exp(−t/12). At 6 ms f ≈ 0.393; at 24 ms f ≈ 0.865. A second identical pulse can estimate recovered availability if its driving force and activation conditions are unchanged.

13. Energy of discrimination

Under an equal-attempt-frequency single-barrier model, ion A faces a barrier 5.7 kJ/mol lower than ion B at 298 K. Estimate rate preference.

Answer and reasoning: RT ≈ 2.48 kJ/mol. Ratio = exp(5.7/2.48) ≈ 10.0. This is conditional on the model and cannot be identified with an exact permeability ratio in a multi-ion channel.

14. Channel activation versus current maximum

An activated sodium channel has Erev = +60 mV and γ = 20 pS. P is 0.2 at −40 mV, 0.8 at 0 mV, and 1 at +50 mV. Compute mean unitary contributions and explain the trend.

Answer and reasoning: At −40: 0.2×20×(−100)/1000 = −0.4 pA; at 0: 0.8×20×(−60)/1000 = −0.96 pA; at +50: 1×20×(−10)/1000 = −0.2 pA. Activation rises while driving force falls, so inward current peaks at an intermediate voltage among these samples.

15. Design a discriminating experiment

A compound reduces whole-cell potassium current by 70%. Design a minimal strategy to distinguish a selectivity change from altered gating or fewer functional channels.

Answer and reasoning: Measure open-level I–V relations and reversal with controlled potassium substitutions, then estimate single-channel open probabilities and durations. Measure channel abundance at the surface or maximal available conductance with appropriate controls. A shift in reversal under ion substitutions supports altered selectivity; lower P with unchanged amplitude supports gating; unchanged single-channel properties with fewer surface channels supports expression loss. A current reduction alone establishes none of these.

16. Osmotic protection

A bacterial strain lacks major tension-activated release pathways. Predict relative survival after sudden hypoosmotic transfer and justify a control.

Answer and reasoning: Water influx increases membrane tension. The mutant cannot release osmolytes as effectively, so it may be more vulnerable to lysis than the wild type. Compare survival under matched osmotic conditions and rescue with a functional channel gene. A growth defect in all conditions would not uniquely support the mechanosensitive explanation.
