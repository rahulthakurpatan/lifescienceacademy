What does it mean to isolate a nucleic acid?

A cell contains many kinds of molecules, but only a fraction of them are relevant to a particular question. Consider a student who wishes to determine whether two leaves contain the same inherited DNA sequence and whether they express the same messenger RNA. Grinding a leaf and inspecting the mixture cannot answer either question: the mixture contains membranes, proteins, polysaccharides, pigments, metabolites, several genomes and thousands of different RNA species. Isolation is the problem of making the molecules of interest available for measurement while separating them from substances that interfere with the intended measurement. Analysis is the subsequent problem of finding out what the recovered material actually represents. The two operations are closely related but not interchangeable. A transparent solution can contain degraded nucleic acid, and a concentrated preparation can be unsuitable for an enzyme-based assay. The biological question must determine how success is judged.

A nucleotide consists of a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar and one or more phosphate groups. A chain of nucleotides is joined by phosphodiester linkages between adjacent sugars. At the pH ordinarily used for aqueous biochemical measurements, the backbone carries negative charge; the bases are not the main source of that charge. The charged backbone interacts strongly with water and counterions, whereas the aromatic bases absorb ultraviolet light and can participate in stacking interactions. These properties provide distinct handles for separation and detection. Charge helps explain movement in an electric field, hydration helps explain why nucleic acids can remain dissolved in water, and ultraviolet absorbance makes it possible to estimate total nucleic acid without knowing its sequence. None of these properties alone distinguishes a particular gene from the rest of the genome.

DNA normally contains deoxyribose and thymine, while RNA normally contains ribose and uracil. The additional hydroxyl group on the second carbon of ribose has important chemical consequences. In an RNA chain it can participate in intramolecular attack on the neighbouring phosphate, making the backbone particularly susceptible to base-promoted cleavage. The DNA backbone lacks this particular reactive group. DNA is therefore often chemically more robust than RNA, although neither molecule is indestructible. DNA can suffer depurination, oxidation and mechanical fragmentation; RNA can be protected by folding and association with proteins. Stability also depends on water activity, temperature, pH, the presence of metal ions and the action of enzymes. A statement about relative stability is a statement about specified conditions rather than a universal prediction about every molecule in a cell.

Double-stranded DNA consists of antiparallel strands whose sequences are related by complementary base pairing. Complementarity provides a route to recognising a sequence within a complex population of fragments. It also permits the duplex to reform when separated strands return to favourable conditions. RNA is often discussed as single-stranded, but extensive internal pairing is common: the anticodon stem of transfer RNA, ribosomal RNA structures and many regulatory RNA elements illustrate how a single chain can form locally double-stranded regions. Denaturation means disruption of ordered noncovalent structure; degradation means chemical breakage of the chain. They can occur together but need not. Heating a DNA duplex may separate its strands without destroying their phosphodiester backbones. Cleaving an RNA molecule into fragments destroys the original continuity even if some local paired structure survives. This distinction becomes essential when interpreting absorbance and electrophoresis.

The term genomic DNA usually refers to the DNA constituting the genome, whereas a plasmid is a separately maintained DNA replicon. In a typical laboratory teaching model involving nonpathogenic cells, the main chromosome and a plasmid may both be double-stranded DNA, yet differ enormously in contour length and topology. Nuclear, mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA likewise differ in organization and cellular location. A preparation called genomic DNA is not necessarily exclusively nuclear DNA; the exact material recovered depends on the cellular fractions included and the discrimination of the method. A claim about nuclear copy number requires particular attention to organelle contributions, whereas a general assessment of total cellular DNA need not exclude them.

Total RNA is not synonymous with messenger RNA. Ribosomal RNA often contributes most of the RNA mass in actively growing cells. Transfer RNAs and other noncoding RNAs contribute additional species, while messenger RNA may account for only a small fraction of the recovered material. Relative proportions depend on organism, cell type, developmental state and conditions. Consequently, a measurement of total RNA mass tells us little about the abundance of an individual transcript. A tube containing twice as much total RNA need not contain twice as much of the transcript under study, especially when two samples have markedly different ribosomal content. Enrichment and sequence-specific measurements are required when the scientific question concerns a selected RNA population or transcript.

Four properties of a preparation must be evaluated separately: quantity, purity, integrity and identity. Quantity is the mass or concentration of material recovered. Purity is the extent to which interfering or unwanted components have been excluded. Integrity refers to the continuity and structural state of the molecules relative to the intended purpose. Identity establishes that the measured material is the nucleic acid or specific sequence the investigator claims it to be. These properties are logically independent. A pure solution of small DNA fragments may be excellent for a short-sequence measurement but unsuitable for a question concerning an intact chromosome. An intact mixture of DNA and RNA can be unsuitable for a DNA-specific quantification based on ultraviolet absorbance. A sample may pass all general quality checks and still lack the sequence of interest.

Recovery is another distinct property: it compares the amount obtained with the amount present initially or with an independently defined input. In many biological specimens the initial target mass is not directly measurable without destroying the sample. Recovery can then be investigated using a known benign reference material, an added standard distinguishable from the natural target or a carefully controlled parallel comparison. Loss during extraction may arise because molecules remain trapped in tissue, adhere to unwanted surfaces, partition into a discarded fraction or become chemically damaged. Enrichment may improve purity at the cost of recovery. The best method is therefore not necessarily the one with the highest total yield, and comparisons must state the intended downstream measurement.

Historical evidence illustrates how isolation and interpretation developed together. Friedrich Miescher's nineteenth-century investigations established that nuclei contain a chemically distinctive phosphorus-rich substance. Later work on nucleic acid chemistry and heredity showed that the substance was not simply another nuclear protein. A crucial conceptual development was the separation of a biologically meaningful activity from other cellular components: chemical enrichment alone did not prove the nature of hereditary information. Modern molecular biology continues to rely on that distinction. An isolated fraction is a candidate for analysis, not automatically proof of the biological role attributed to it. Sequence detection, structural evidence and appropriate controls make such an inference stronger.

In the following sections the same benign teaching problem provides a unifying thread: comparing genomic DNA from plant tissue, a reference circular DNA preparation, and total RNA from matched tissue samples. The aim is to reason about which molecular property a method exploits, what a signal measures and what conclusions remain uncertain. Actual handling of hazardous chemical reagents or biological material belongs to an appropriately supervised laboratory. Here the focus is the conceptual and quantitative reasoning required to distinguish a reliable observation from a plausible but untested explanation.

Genomic DNA: release, protection and selective purification

The first barrier to genomic DNA recovery is physical compartmentalization. In a eukaryotic cell, nuclear DNA is associated with histones and other proteins, while organelle DNA occupies separate compartments. Membranes contain lipids and proteins that prevent free mixing between these compartments and the external medium. Plant cells also possess a wall with mechanically strong polysaccharide components. An effective preparation must make the relevant compartment accessible while limiting chemical and mechanical damage to its contents. The relative importance of each barrier differs between tissues: a soft cell population and a mature leaf cannot be assumed to have the same matrix. A general extraction strategy must therefore separate the concept of cell disruption from the particular technique selected to achieve it.

Membrane disruption is often explained using the amphipathic character of detergents. An amphipathic molecule contains regions that interact favourably with water and regions that associate with hydrophobic substances. By interfering with lipid packing and forming mixed assemblies with membrane components, suitable detergents help disperse membranes and release cellular contents. Detergents may also change protein structure and solubility. That is useful when the target is DNA rather than a functional membrane protein, but becomes problematic if detergent remains in the final preparation and interferes with an intended analytical assay. Not every detergent has the same effect on proteins or nucleic acids; the important exam principle is that releasing the target and making a clean final sample are separate goals.

Chromosomal DNA in a cell is not a free naked thread. In eukaryotic nuclei, histones associate with the negatively charged DNA backbone and contribute to chromatin compaction. Other proteins stabilize, regulate or modify chromosomal regions. Disrupting the nucleus is therefore insufficient to yield freely accessible DNA. Protein removal or dissociation is needed to reduce unwanted nucleoprotein complexes. Proteolytic activity can break proteins down into smaller fragments; other approaches alter solubility or partitioning so that proteins and nucleic acids occupy distinct fractions. The effectiveness of these operations depends on the chemical conditions and sample matrix. Residual protein can contribute to ultraviolet absorbance, alter electrophoretic migration, or inhibit enzyme reactions. However, a low measured protein-associated absorbance does not by itself prove that every DNA-binding protein has been removed.

Nucleases are enzymes that hydrolyse phosphodiester linkages in nucleic acids. Endonucleases cleave within a chain and exonucleases remove residues from an end. Some require divalent metal ions for catalytic activity; others differ in metal dependence. The disruption of cells releases endogenous nucleases alongside their substrates, creating a risk that did not exist to the same extent in the intact compartment. Chelating agents bind selected metal ions and can reduce the activity of metal-dependent nucleases. This is one reason a DNA-protective medium may include a chelating component. The same property explains why excessive carryover can be harmful in a later reaction requiring available magnesium. Protection during recovery must be balanced against compatibility with analysis. It would be an error to infer that chelation inhibits every nuclease equally.

Mechanical forces also affect long DNA molecules. A chromosome contains a very long polymer whose different regions can be subjected to different velocities when the surrounding liquid moves. The resulting stresses can break strands, particularly when the molecules are extended or entangled. A preparation intended to retain long DNA molecules should be distinguished from a preparation whose intended analysis only requires short fragments. In the first case, the distribution of fragment sizes is an essential performance characteristic; in the second, moderate fragmentation may have little effect on whether a short target region remains intact. The chemical identity of DNA is unchanged by moderate fragmentation, so an absorbance measurement may remain nearly constant despite a dramatic loss of high molecular weight material. A gel or other length-sensitive measurement is needed to see this difference.

Separation by differential solubility exploits the fact that molecules interact differently with their environment. The highly charged nucleic acid backbone is well hydrated in aqueous conditions. Many proteins, by contrast, become less soluble when their native folds and hydration shells are disturbed. An extraction involving aqueous and organic environments can therefore distribute proteins, lipids and nucleic acids differently between phases or interfaces. The distribution is not a perfect division into chemically pure categories: pH, salt composition, molecular size and interactions with other macromolecules influence partitioning. An interface can trap target material together with precipitated proteins. Collecting an apparently clear fraction too close to the interface may improve apparent yield while introducing contamination. Conversely, excluding the entire vicinity of the interface can reduce recovery. Exam questions about partitioning often test this trade-off rather than a memorized list of layers.

Precipitation offers a related but distinct way of collecting nucleic acids. Dissolved polymers are stabilized by their interactions with the solvent and ions. Changing the solvent environment and electrostatic interactions can reduce the favourability of remaining dissolved, leading to aggregation or precipitation. Counterions partially screen repulsion between neighbouring negative charges on DNA. Alcohol-rich mixtures reduce the ability of the solvent to stabilize those charges compared with water. The combination can favour recovery of nucleic acid in a condensed phase. Other substances may also precipitate, particularly when their concentrations are high. A recovered pellet is consequently not proof of chemical purity or even proof that it consists mainly of DNA. This distinction matters when comparing mass estimated spectroscopically with visual inspection of a precipitate.

Solid-phase purification is based on reversible interaction of nucleic acid with a material such as silica. Boom and colleagues reported in 1990 a nucleic-acid purification approach coupling a chaotropic environment with silica binding. A chaotropic agent perturbs the organized interactions that support macromolecular hydration and folding; the resulting environment can favour nucleic-acid association with the solid surface. Conditions favouring retention and conditions favouring release are deliberately different. The useful conceptual sequence is capture, exclusion of poorly retained material, and release of the desired material into a compatible aqueous phase. This is a separation based on conditional binding, not a membrane acting simply as a molecular sieve. The amount of solid surface and its binding capacity are finite, and competing components can change recovery. Residual substances from a binding or washing environment can also compromise downstream analysis even when DNA has been recovered.

Alternative supports can exploit differences in net charge. Anion-exchange materials carry groups that interact with negatively charged nucleic acids, and changes in ionic environment alter the competition between bound polymers and other ions. This shares the broad logic of conditional retention with silica but relies on a different material interaction. It is a useful conceptual connection to chromatography in Node 14.A.05. Charge-based capture is not automatically sequence-specific: two DNA molecules carrying similar backbone charges may both be retained. Selecting a particular sequence instead requires another form of discrimination, such as complementary hybridisation. Thus the choice of separation mechanism dictates what can be inferred about the composition of the recovered fraction.

Plant preparations present particular interpretation problems because cell walls and extracellular matrices contribute compounds that may accompany nucleic acids into aqueous fractions. Some polysaccharides can produce viscous mixtures or interfere with subsequent reactions; phenolic compounds may absorb light or undergo chemical changes that affect the sample. Pigments can change the apparent spectrum. A sample with a strong ultraviolet signal from such a matrix should not be interpreted using the pure-DNA conversion factor without checking the validity of that assumption. A target-selective fluorometric assay and an examination of fragment sizes can help distinguish apparent abundance from genuinely recoverable DNA. Comparing leaves of different developmental stages may require attention to changing tissue chemistry even when the same nominal mass of tissue is analysed.

The extraction of genomic DNA from two biological samples should not be compared using final concentration alone. A sample in a smaller final volume may have higher concentration despite a lower total recovered mass. For example, an illustrative preparation containing 80 nanograms per microlitre in 50 microlitres contains 4,000 nanograms, or 4 micrograms, of measured material. Another preparation containing 50 nanograms per microlitre in 100 microlitres contains 5 micrograms. The second is less concentrated but contains more total material. If the starting samples differ in mass or cell number, even total recovered mass is insufficient to compare the efficiency of the extraction. A defensible comparison relates quantity to a suitable input measure and separately assesses the quality of the measured material.

Consider two hypothetical genomic DNA gels. The first contains a prominent high molecular weight signal close to the sample well with relatively little signal at smaller sizes. The second contains a broad distribution extending much farther into the gel. If both preparations have similar target-selective mass estimates, the second is not necessarily less pure; its molecular length distribution differs. If the experimental aim is to inspect long contiguous regions, the first preparation may be preferable. If a short locus is being detected, both could be usable, provided the target and assay controls behave appropriately. Scientific evaluation depends on which feature of the preparation is essential for the biological inference, rather than on assigning an unconditional ranking to the two samples.

Plasmid DNA and the physical consequences of topology

A small circular DNA molecule differs physically from a chromosome even when both consist of the same nucleotide building blocks. In a covalently closed circular duplex, neither strand has a free end. The mutual winding of the strands therefore imposes a topological constraint: one strand cannot pass through the other without a transient break or strand-passage event. Twisting and coiling can redistribute stress while the linking number remains unchanged. A nick in one strand removes the constraint on rotation, allowing the molecule to relax. A double-strand break produces a linear molecule with two ends. These forms can have identical base sequences and nearly identical masses but different three-dimensional conformations. They provide a powerful illustration of why a molecular measurement may depend on more than sequence or mass.

The topology of a closed duplex is often described by the relationship Lk = Tw + Wr. Lk is the linking number, Tw describes twisting of the strands around the duplex axis, and Wr describes coiling of that axis in space. For a covalently closed molecule without strand passage, Lk is an integer and is conserved, although Tw and Wr can vary. A negatively supercoiled plasmid has a linking number below the relaxed reference value appropriate to its length and conditions. In an electrophoretic separation, compact supercoiled molecules often travel faster than relaxed open circles of the same molecular length under ordinary agarose conditions. The exact order of forms can depend on gel and chemical conditions. Therefore, a band position should not be converted into base pairs using a linear DNA ladder unless the molecule being measured is also linear or the method is explicitly calibrated for its topology.

Birnboim and Doly's 1979 study of alkaline extraction introduced an influential conceptual solution to the problem of distinguishing small circular DNA molecules from much larger chromosomal DNA. The approach exploits the different behaviour of molecules after disruption of duplex structure and return toward conditions favouring pairing. In the teaching model, covalently closed plasmid strands remain physically associated through their topology and can recover paired structure more readily than a very large chromosomal DNA population that has become entangled with cellular debris and denatured material. The distinction is conditional rather than an absolute property of every circular or linear molecule. Nicked plasmids, very large plasmids and fragmented chromosomes may behave differently. This is why an extraction enriched for plasmid DNA can contain chromosomal fragments or lose particular plasmid forms.

It is important not to interpret denaturation as selective chemical destruction of the chromosome. Denaturation disrupts base pairing and stacking, whereas the DNA backbone can remain covalently intact. In a selective plasmid preparation, the subsequent physical fate of large chromosome-derived material can differ from the fate of compact plasmid-derived material. Large aggregates may separate from the fraction containing recoverable small circular DNA. If chromosome-derived material has been mechanically fragmented, smaller fragments may remain in the recovered fraction and compromise selectivity. A high apparent DNA yield in such a case may reflect contamination rather than an improvement in plasmid recovery. This is an example of an upstream change in molecular size altering the effectiveness of a downstream separation mechanism.

Copy number provides an additional biological distinction. Some plasmids are represented by one or a few molecules per cell, while others occur at substantially higher numbers. The total recovered plasmid mass reflects not just copy number but plasmid length, cell input, integrity and method-dependent recovery. A small plasmid with many copies may contribute the same total mass as a larger plasmid with fewer copies. Since a double-stranded DNA molecule has mass approximately proportional to its number of base pairs, equal masses of different-length molecules do not imply equal molecule numbers. Conversely, two samples containing equal numbers of different plasmid molecules need not produce equal ultraviolet signals if their total masses differ. A question about copy number must use a molecular or sequence-specific reference rather than simply comparing mass concentration.

One can estimate the number of molecules in a pure double-stranded DNA preparation using the approximate average molecular mass of 660 grams per mole per base pair. For a hypothetical 5,000 base pair molecule, the approximate molar mass is 3.3 million grams per mole. A mass of 3.3 nanograms corresponds to 3.3 × 10⁻⁹ grams divided by 3.3 × 10⁶ grams per mole, or 10⁻¹⁵ moles. Multiplying by Avogadro's constant gives approximately 6.02 × 10⁸ molecules. This estimate assumes that the mass belongs entirely to molecules of the stated length and that a mean base-pair molar mass is sufficient. A mixture containing RNA, chromosomal DNA or plasmid multimers violates the assumption that all measured mass belongs to the specified molecular species. The arithmetic can be flawless and the biological answer still wrong if sample identity is not established.

A gel with three bands is not automatically evidence of three different plasmids. A single circular DNA sequence can occur as a compact supercoil, a relaxed nicked circle and a linear form. Multimers may add further bands. An informative comparison is the behaviour of a reference aliquot whose molecular form is known to be linear. In the hypothetical case of a plasmid with one defined cleavage site, complete cleavage at that site would convert monomeric circles into a single linear molecular length. Different bands in an untreated aliquot that correspond to conformational variants can therefore converge into one size class in the comparison. This is a conceptual interpretation of molecular forms, not proof that every sample with three bands has only one sequence. Sequence-specific detection or another independent identity measurement can further discriminate alternatives.

Another common source of ambiguity is residual RNA. Nucleic acid absorbance at 260 nanometres is shared by DNA and RNA; a plasmid-enriched fraction with RNA contamination can therefore appear to have an unusually high DNA concentration. Small RNA molecules and fragments may contribute a fast-moving region of a gel, while chromosomal DNA contamination may contribute a slow-moving or diffuse high molecular weight region. The appearance of either signal depends on staining and loading conditions. A DNA-selective measurement can reveal whether the apparent total nucleic acid mass is supported by a double-stranded DNA estimate. The best explanation of an anomalous band uses the expected molecular form, controls and independent analytical results, rather than treating every fast band as supercoiled plasmid or every slow band as chromosome.

The contrast between genomic and plasmid recovery thus depends on several related variables: the target's length, topology, abundance and association with other cellular components; the stability of those properties during processing; and the molecular property exploited by the separation. A method designed to retain long chromosomal material is not necessarily the best method for enriching small closed circles. Likewise, a fraction enriched in plasmid DNA does not establish that its sequence is the one expected. An examination problem may present the same mass or the same number of electrophoretic bands for two preparations and ask which is suitable for a given conclusion. The correct response begins by identifying what information is absent and what independent evidence would resolve the ambiguity.

RNA isolation: preserving a molecular snapshot

RNA isolation has a distinctive biological purpose. The DNA sequence of a cell is relatively persistent across many changes in physiological state, whereas the RNA population is continuously shaped by transcription, processing and degradation. A leaf undergoing a change in illumination may alter the abundance of selected transcripts without changing its inherited genome. An RNA analysis is therefore an attempt to recover evidence about a particular biological state at a particular time. Changes after collection can obscure that state. If degradation or continued biological activity occurs differently in two samples before they are stabilized, an observed difference in transcript abundance may reflect sample history rather than the original biological condition.

The term ribonuclease, or RNase, refers to enzymes that cleave RNA. RNases occur naturally in cells and may also enter a preparation through environmental contamination. Some members of this broad group are unusually stable under conditions that inactivate many other proteins. The well-studied RNase A, for example, does not require a divalent metal cofactor for its catalytic cleavage reaction. This contrasts with the many nucleases that use divalent metals and explains why a metal-chelating agent alone cannot be regarded as comprehensive protection for RNA. RNA protection requires attention to several independent mechanisms of damage: enzymatic hydrolysis, chemical hydrolysis, contamination and changes in sample state before isolation.

The 2′ hydroxyl of RNA provides a useful molecular explanation of base-promoted backbone cleavage. In favourable geometry and chemical conditions, the oxygen associated with this hydroxyl can act as a nucleophile and attack the adjacent phosphorus atom. The reaction breaks a phosphodiester linkage and may generate a cyclic phosphate intermediate and a new hydroxyl terminus. The important structural feature is the neighbouring hydroxyl that makes an intramolecular reaction possible. DNA lacks the corresponding 2′ hydroxyl, so this particular route of cleavage is unavailable to ordinary DNA. This does not imply that DNA withstands all harsh conditions or that RNA always degrades instantly; the relative rates depend on the chemical environment and molecular structure. A method whose selectivity depends on alkaline conditions appropriate for DNA cannot be assumed to preserve RNA equally well.

RNA may be associated with proteins, folded into secondary structures or contained in ribonucleoprotein particles. Disrupting the matrix and separating proteins are therefore essential aspects of RNA isolation. The chemical environment must simultaneously reduce the activity of endogenous degradative enzymes and make the target RNA available for recovery. Chaotropic environments help disrupt organized protein structures and interactions; this makes them useful for isolating nucleic acids under circumstances in which maintenance of native enzyme function is not desired. Nevertheless, the materials that help preserve the target during isolation can become contaminants if carried into an enzyme-dependent analytical reaction. A successful purification strategy changes the molecular environment across stages rather than keeping every initial component in the final preparation.

Chomczynski and Sacchi's 1987 report describes an influential method based on an acidic chaotropic and organic extraction system. It demonstrates how differential partitioning can help separate RNA from DNA and proteins within a complex mixture. The important conceptual observation is that RNA tends to be recovered in an aqueous fraction under the relevant acidic extraction conditions, whereas substantial DNA and protein are associated with other fractions. This separation is not an absolute chemical law independent of pH, composition or molecular state. DNA contamination may persist, and recovery of RNA species may vary. The role of the method in molecular biology is to show that chemical environment can create useful selectivity between molecules sharing many structural properties. Exact hazardous reagent handling is outside the scope of this conceptual chapter.

A solid-phase RNA purification scheme instead uses conditional retention on a supporting material. The basic logic resembles that described for DNA: a target-containing fraction is captured, undesired components are excluded, and the RNA is released into a compatible environment. The challenge is that DNA and RNA have similar polyanionic backbones and may both associate with the same support. A support that binds nucleic acid efficiently is not necessarily specific for RNA. Removal of unwanted DNA may require another source of discrimination, including the use of a DNA-selective nuclease in a suitable analytical context. Conversely, an RNA-selective nuclease may help diagnose RNA contamination of a DNA fraction. Whether the treatment has been effective is an experimental question, not something established solely by its inclusion in a workflow.

A common source of incorrect interpretation arises when residual genomic DNA contributes a signal to an assay intended to report RNA. Suppose a student analyses complementary DNA, or cDNA, generated from RNA by reverse transcription. An assay detecting the corresponding sequence may also detect genomic DNA if the target region is present there and the assay cannot distinguish the sources. A signal in a comparison lacking reverse transcription would then suggest DNA-derived signal or another unintended contribution rather than authentic evidence of the transcript. A negative result in that comparison supports, but does not by itself prove, the specificity of the main measurement. It must be interpreted together with the assay's detection limit and the behaviour of positive and negative controls. Reverse transcription and subsequent amplification are studied elsewhere in greater detail; their relevance here is the demand for molecular identity.

RNA populations differ not only in sequence but in length, structure and chemical features. For example, many mature eukaryotic messenger RNAs possess a polyadenylated 3′ end. A complementary oligo(dT) sequence can interact with this tail, providing a biochemical basis for enriching such molecules from total RNA. This enrichment excludes many unwanted abundant RNAs, yet it also excludes biologically important transcripts without suitable poly(A) tails. It can discriminate against fragmented transcripts that have lost the relevant portion. A sample enriched by poly(A) recognition is therefore not a universal representation of every RNA expressed in a cell. In contrast, depletion of selected abundant ribosomal RNAs leaves a different mixture of other RNA classes. The two strategies answer different questions and may yield different apparent transcript distributions even when the original sample is identical.

Length selection can impose a further bias. Some purification materials and separation conditions retain large and small RNA species with different efficiencies. A preparation suitable for examining a long messenger RNA may underrepresent transfer RNA or short regulatory RNAs. Conversely, an enriched small-RNA population is not a faithful representation of total cellular RNA. Students should distinguish intended selection, which defines the target population, from unwanted selection, which distorts an experiment without being recognized. If a study compares small RNA abundance between tissues, an RNA measurement dominated by ribosomal molecules provides little assurance that small-RNA recovery is comparable. An independently chosen reference or an appropriate control of the selected size range provides more relevant evidence.

Tissue heterogeneity is especially important for RNA measurements. A leaf contains cell types that differ in transcriptional activity and RNA content. A bulk preparation combines contributions from these populations in proportions determined by cell number, RNA abundance, extraction efficiency and tissue composition. An apparent change in the abundance of one transcript can arise because each cell changes expression or because the proportions of contributing cell types change. Extraction cannot, by itself, distinguish these biological interpretations. Normalizing to total RNA mass does not remove changes in cell composition. A study should specify the biological sampling unit and distinguish the technical reproducibility of a preparation from the validity of the biological comparison.

Consider an illustrative experiment in which two leaf samples have equal total RNA mass but different proportions of intact molecules. Both might give similar ultraviolet absorbance because cleavage does not immediately remove aromatic bases from solution. Yet the more fragmented sample can give weaker or biased evidence of long transcripts. A sequence near the end of a fragmented RNA may remain detectable while a distant portion has been lost from the same molecule. Consequently, two assays targeting different regions of the same transcript can disagree. This does not necessarily indicate alternative biological expression; unequal degradation is one plausible explanation. Integrity and regional coverage are central to distinguishing technical loss from biological change.

Preserving the RNA population is therefore more demanding than maximizing a single numerical yield. A reliable RNA preparation must represent the intended biological state, contain the desired classes of molecules, preserve the lengths required for the later measurement and avoid DNA or other substances that distort interpretation. A student faced with an examination case should ask whether a result is inconsistent with the expected quantity, molecular class, length distribution or sequence-specific signal. These questions convert a complicated description of reagents into a manageable analysis of cause and effect.

Ultraviolet absorbance: measurement, units and purity

When a beam of light passes through a solution, some photons may be absorbed by dissolved molecules. Nucleic acid bases contain electronic structures that interact strongly with ultraviolet radiation, especially near 260 nanometres. This optical property allows an instrument to estimate the amount of absorbing material without attaching a label to each molecule. The measurement is particularly convenient when a purified DNA or RNA preparation is available and when its concentration falls within the reliable range of the instrument. However, ultraviolet absorbance is a property shared by many substances, so the conversion from optical signal to nucleic acid concentration depends on assumptions about sample composition.

The fraction of light transmitted by a sample is T = I/I₀, where I₀ is the incident or reference light intensity and I is the transmitted intensity after the sample contribution has been isolated. Absorbance is defined as A = −log₁₀ T, equivalently log₁₀(I₀/I). It has no physical unit because it is the logarithm of a ratio. If half the incident intensity is transmitted, the absorbance is approximately 0.301. If one tenth is transmitted, the absorbance is 1. If one hundredth is transmitted, the absorbance is 2. An increase in absorbance from 1 to 2 therefore represents a tenfold reduction in transmitted fraction, not simply a twofold reduction. The logarithmic relationship is frequently tested because absorbance and transmittance describe the same observation on different scales.

The Beer–Lambert relation can be motivated by considering a thin slice of an absorbing solution. Under ideal conditions, each slice removes a fraction of the light proportional to the local intensity and the concentration of the absorber. As light traverses successive slices, the intensity decays multiplicatively. Taking a logarithm converts that multiplicative decrease into an additive quantity. In the conventional decadic form the result is A = εcl, where ε is the molar absorption coefficient at the selected wavelength, c is the molar concentration, and l is the optical path length. If c is expressed in moles per litre and l in centimetres, ε has units of litres per mole per centimetre. Absorbance remains dimensionless. The equation predicts that doubling either concentration or path length doubles absorbance, provided the absorption coefficient and the conditions remain unchanged.

The ideal law assumes that the detected light corresponds to the chosen wavelength, the sample is optically homogeneous and interactions do not substantially change the absorbing properties of its components across the measured concentration range. Scattering from suspended particles, inaccurate background subtraction, stray light and very high absorbance may cause departures. An instrument may also report an absorbance normalized to a one-centimetre path even when its actual optical path is shorter. Before applying a conversion factor, the reader must identify whether the data represent raw absorbance at a specified path or absorbance already corrected to a standard path. Applying a second path correction to normalized data produces an erroneous concentration despite correctly remembering the equation.

For ordinary textbook estimates, an A260 of 1 for a one-centimetre optical path is often associated with approximately 50 micrograms per millilitre of double-stranded DNA, 40 micrograms per millilitre of RNA, and 33 micrograms per millilitre of single-stranded DNA. These mass conversion factors are useful approximations for typical nucleic acid compositions, not exact universal constants. Short oligonucleotides can differ substantially in absorbance per mole according to sequence and neighbouring-base interactions. Defined short sequences are better quantified using their calculated or independently determined absorption coefficients. The conversion factor also changes with molecular conformation, because base stacking modifies optical absorption. The factors are most meaningful when the preparation is dominated by the stated nucleic acid class.

The units in these approximations are particularly convenient: one microgram per millilitre equals one nanogram per microlitre. Both numerator and denominator change by a factor of one thousand when converting between the two unit systems, leaving the numerical value unchanged. If a pure double-stranded DNA sample diluted fivefold has a one-centimetre-equivalent A260 of 0.24, the measured diluted concentration is 0.24 × 50 = 12 micrograms per millilitre. The original concentration is 12 × 5 = 60 micrograms per millilitre, or 60 nanograms per microlitre. If the original recovered volume is 80 microlitres, the measured mass is 4,800 nanograms, or 4.8 micrograms. Each multiplication answers a different question: the first converts optical signal into concentration, the second reverses dilution, and the third converts concentration into total mass.

A dilution factor is the ratio of final diluted volume to the original aliquot volume, not the ratio of added solvent to original volume. For example, a conceptual mixture consisting of one volume of sample and four volumes of solvent has a final volume of five relative units, making its dilution factor five. This distinction often matters in quantitative questions. If two serial dilutions are applied, their factors multiply because each dilution acts on the concentration produced by the preceding one. A fourfold dilution followed by a fivefold dilution produces an overall twentyfold dilution. The original concentration is recovered by multiplying the measured concentration by twenty, provided no material is lost and the solvent contributes no uncorrected background.

Path length requires similar care. Suppose the raw absorbance of a pure double-stranded DNA solution is 0.12 at an optical path of 0.2 centimetres. Under the Beer–Lambert model the corresponding one-centimetre absorbance is 0.12 divided by 0.2, or 0.60. The approximate concentration is then 30 micrograms per millilitre. Treating 0.12 as a normalized one-centimetre value would underestimate the concentration by a factor of five. If the instrument has already reported 0.60 as its normalized absorbance, dividing by 0.2 again would overestimate the concentration by the same factor. This example illustrates why the meaning of a reported number matters as much as its magnitude.

Purity assessment often uses absorbance ratios rather than only the 260-nanometre reading. Aromatic amino acid residues contribute to protein absorption near 280 nanometres, and many chemical contaminants contribute absorption in the shorter ultraviolet region near 230 nanometres. Ratios A260/A280 and A260/A230 therefore provide useful screening information. Typical expectations under suitable measurement conditions are approximately 1.8 for pure double-stranded DNA and approximately 2.0 for pure RNA for the first ratio, with values around 2.0–2.2 often expected for the second ratio in relatively clean nucleic acid preparations. These values are empirical guidelines. They do not define absolute chemical boundaries separating pure and impure samples.

The ratio A260/A280 can be influenced by pH and ionic environment even when the nucleic acid itself has not been contaminated by protein. Wilfinger, Mackey and Chomczynski published evidence in 1997 that changing the chemical environment used for spectrophotometric assessment could substantially alter measured RNA purity ratios. This finding is important because it separates a property of the measurement system from a property of the isolated material. If two laboratories report different ratios for equivalent RNA samples but use different reference media, it is not justified to conclude that one preparation necessarily contains more protein. A meaningful comparison uses appropriate and comparable reference conditions and examines the whole pattern of evidence.

A ratio is also mathematically unstable when the denominator is small. Suppose an illustrative dilute sample has A260 = 0.020 and A280 = 0.010, giving a ratio of 2.0. A small background error of 0.005 at 280 nanometres changes the denominator to 0.015, producing a ratio of approximately 1.33. The absolute error is small, yet the ratio changes dramatically. At higher absorbance values a similar absolute error would have a much smaller relative effect. This is why a seemingly ideal or abnormal ratio in a very dilute sample may carry little useful information. Such cases benefit from a more sensitive target-selective concentration measurement rather than increasingly elaborate interpretation of a noisy quotient.

Mixtures present an additional problem. If several independently absorbing components are present under ideal conditions, the total absorbance at a wavelength is approximately the sum of their individual contributions. DNA, RNA and free nucleotides can all contribute to A260, while proteins, phenolic compounds and other substances can contribute at overlapping wavelengths. A measurement at one wavelength supplies one numerical constraint but leaves multiple unknown concentrations. Even two ratios and an absolute absorbance do not automatically solve the composition of a mixture because the identities and absorption coefficients of the contaminants may be unknown. An apparently normal ratio can result from a mixture of components whose effects partially compensate. Spectral agreement is supportive evidence of purity, not proof of the absence of contaminants.

A schematic spectrum illustrates the difference between a diagnostic pattern and a single ratio. A nucleic acid dominated spectrum typically has substantial absorption near 260 nanometres. Elevated absorption around 230 nanometres can suggest carryover of certain chemical components; a relatively enhanced 280-nanometre signal can suggest protein or other absorbing material. A sloping baseline or signal in a region where the expected molecules absorb weakly can raise concern about scattering or background. These features are not unique signatures of individual contaminants. The analyst uses them to generate hypotheses and then chooses a comparison or independent assay capable of discriminating between those hypotheses.

Denaturation provides another example in which absorbance may change without a change in total nucleic acid mass. In a double-stranded DNA molecule the bases interact through stacking and hydrogen bonding. When ordered stacking is disrupted as the duplex melts, absorption at 260 nanometres commonly increases; this increase is called the hyperchromic effect. A melting curve plots absorbance against a changing condition such as temperature. The midpoint of an appropriate transition is often associated with a melting temperature under specified conditions. A higher absorbance after denaturation does not mean more DNA has been synthesized. Rather, the absorption coefficient has changed because the physical state of the bases differs. This example reinforces the distinction between concentration and optical response.

Suppose two aliquots of the same purified DNA are measured before and after an illustrative conformational transition. If A260 changes from 0.50 to 0.65 and both are measured in equivalent optical geometry, the fractional increase relative to the initial reading is (0.65 − 0.50)/0.50 = 0.30, or 30 percent. This percentage describes the optical change, not the percentage increase in DNA mass. The interpretation relies on the known equivalence of the two aliquots and the evidence that the signal change reflects molecular conformation rather than contamination or instrument error. Without these controls a rising absorbance would be an observation, not a unique diagnosis of denaturation.

Optical quantification is thus best understood as a model-based inference. It is powerful precisely because a simple physical relationship connects absorbance and concentration for a suitable sample. The examiner may change one assumption at a time: the chemical identity of the sample, the path length, the dilution factor, the background medium, the conformation or the concentration range. Predicting the effect of the changed assumption is more useful than memorizing a set of ratio thresholds. The next section contrasts this largely non-selective optical measurement with fluorescence measurements that can discriminate between molecular classes more effectively.

Fluorescence quantification and calibration

A fluorescent molecule absorbs energy from incident light and can subsequently emit a photon at a characteristic range of wavelengths. For many commonly used fluorescent labels, the emission occurs at longer wavelengths than the excitation because some energy is dissipated before radiative emission. A fluorescence instrument separates excitation from detected emission so that relatively small amounts of emitted light can be measured against an appropriate background. In nucleic acid analysis, dyes are useful when their fluorescence changes substantially upon association with DNA or RNA. Instead of estimating the total ultraviolet-absorbing material in the sample, the experiment estimates a dye-dependent response associated with a selected molecular class.

Selectivity and specificity should be distinguished carefully. A dye assay described as selective for double-stranded DNA produces a stronger or more useful response to double-stranded DNA than to other sample components under its validated conditions. It does not follow that the dye recognizes a specific gene sequence, nor that every other compound has zero effect. RNA-selective fluorescence likewise measures a class of molecules rather than individual transcripts. The binding preferences of a dye, its fluorescence efficiency, the chemical environment, the amount of reagent and the nature of the target influence the signal. An assay is calibrated for a particular purpose and concentration range. It cannot be made universally quantitative merely by obtaining a numerical display.

At low target concentrations within a validated range, a simple teaching model expresses the measured fluorescence signal as F = F₀ + kc, where F₀ is a background response, k is the sensitivity of the assay, and c is the concentration in the measurement mixture. The coefficient k has units of fluorescence signal per concentration unit and depends on the instrument, dye and measurement conditions. This equation is a local calibration model, not a universal law for every fluorescent system. At high concentration the binding reagent or detector may become limiting, or the sample may absorb excitation or emission light strongly enough to distort proportionality. Such departures produce curved or flattened calibration relationships. An unknown sample should therefore be evaluated within the validated range rather than estimated by arbitrarily extending a straight line.

A blank is essential because fluorescence may arise from the reagent, measurement vessel, optical background or components unrelated to the desired target. Known standards establish the relationship between concentration and signal after accounting for the blank. The most relevant standards contain the same type of target molecule and are measured using comparable conditions. A double-stranded DNA standard does not automatically calibrate an RNA dye response, and a standard containing intact DNA may not represent a preparation dominated by unusual molecular forms if the assay's response differs between them. A meaningful calibration includes the expected range of unknowns and permits examination of whether the signal remains proportional to concentration.

Consider a hypothetical calibration in which a blank gives 10 arbitrary fluorescence units and standards containing 5 and 15 nanograms per microlitre in the measurement mixture give 60 and 160 units, respectively. The slope estimated from the two standards is (160 − 60)/(15 − 5) = 10 signal units per nanogram per microlitre. An unknown gives 110 units. Subtracting the blank yields 100 signal units, and dividing by the slope gives 10 nanograms per microlitre in the measurement mixture. If the unknown preparation was diluted fourfold before this measurement, its original concentration is 40 nanograms per microlitre. This simple calculation assumes a linear relationship between the standards and the unknown and a background shared by the measurements.

The same example explains interpolation. The unknown signal of 110 lies between the 60 and 160 unit standards; the estimated concentration of 10 lies between their known concentrations. An unknown signal of 310 would lie well outside that interval. A mathematical extrapolation would give 30 nanograms per microlitre using the fitted line, but the result would not be supported by the same calibration evidence. The system might remain linear, or it might begin to saturate. A student should distinguish an arithmetic result from a validated measurement. The correct examination response often identifies the need for a measurement within the supported range rather than selecting the largest numerical estimate.

Fluorescence and absorbance can disagree for scientifically informative reasons. Suppose the absorbance-derived estimate of a nominal DNA preparation is 90 nanograms per microlitre, whereas a suitable double-stranded-DNA fluorescence assay estimates 35 nanograms per microlitre. The discrepancy suggests that the optical measurements are not measuring exactly the same molecular population or that one measurement is affected by an interfering condition. RNA contamination and free nucleotides are possible contributors to the ultraviolet estimate. But it would be premature to conclude that the difference of 55 nanograms per microlitre is exactly RNA mass: the conversion factor used in the absorbance calculation assumes DNA, and other compounds may contribute. The fluorescence response may also be suppressed by a matrix effect. A gel and an appropriately chosen control can help determine the most defensible explanation.

Matrix effects arise when constituents of the unknown influence the analytical response differently from the constituents of a standard. Fluorescence may be reduced through quenching or altered binding; absorbance may be enhanced by additional absorbers or scattering. An added reference amount, chosen to be distinguishable or independently interpretable, can provide a test of whether the sample matrix changes the expected response. If the expected incremental signal is much lower in the sample than in the reference medium, a reduced measurement response becomes a plausible contributor to the discrepancy. Such a test does not establish that the original target was recovered efficiently, because a reference added after isolation cannot account for target loss during earlier stages.

The distinction between analytical sensitivity and molecular integrity is equally important. A highly sensitive DNA fluorescence assay can detect small amounts of fragmented DNA, provided the fragments remain compatible with dye binding. It does not report how long the original molecules are, whether a particular region has been damaged or whether a complete circular molecule is present. An RNA dye assay may similarly detect material from a degraded sample. These measurements answer the quantity question more selectively than ultraviolet absorbance in many contexts, but they do not replace a length-sensitive method or a sequence-specific assay. Choosing the correct combination of measurements is a central skill in the analysis of nucleic acids.

Nucleic acid integrity: reading gels and electropherograms

A preparation may have the expected concentration and a satisfactory ultraviolet spectrum but still consist largely of broken molecules. Integrity is the property that addresses this possibility. For a polymer, integrity has at least two meanings. One is the retention of covalent continuity over the relevant length; the other is retention of the physical or chemical state needed for a particular measurement. A circular DNA molecule may lose its supercoiled state after a single nick even though nearly the whole sequence remains present. A messenger RNA may retain a short target region yet lose its full-length structure. The word intact therefore has to be interpreted in relation to the intended experiment.

Electrophoresis supplies information that neither absorbance nor bulk fluorescence directly provides. The phosphate backbone gives ordinary DNA and RNA molecules a negative charge in common electrophoretic environments. In an electric field they tend to migrate toward the positively charged electrode, the anode. A supporting gel creates an environment in which the motion of molecules depends on their interactions with a porous matrix. Smaller linear fragments usually traverse the matrix more readily than larger linear fragments under appropriately chosen conditions. This yields a spatial distribution that can be interpreted in terms of molecular length when suitable standards and assumptions are available. The broader physics of electrophoresis is developed in Node 14.A.06; here the emphasis is on what nucleic acid band patterns actually establish.

In a typical conceptual agarose gel, the sample begins near wells at one end of the matrix. A band is an accumulation of molecules whose mobilities are sufficiently similar that their signals overlap spatially. It is not necessarily a chemically uniform species. Two unrelated fragments with the same length can form one band, while one circular sequence can form several bands because of different topology. A smear represents signal distributed over a substantial migration range. Such a range may arise from a heterogeneous fragment-length population, from naturally diverse RNA species or from technical factors affecting separation. A band or smear is an observation about the distribution of a detectable signal rather than direct proof of molecular identity.

A molecular size standard, often called a ladder, contains defined molecular lengths. It provides an empirical relationship between migration and length for molecules behaving comparably to those in the standard. For linear double-stranded DNA, within a suitable region of a given gel, the migration distance often varies approximately linearly with the logarithm of fragment length. The relationship is not universal across all fragment sizes, matrix compositions or molecular conformations. It should be used within the region calibrated by the available standards. A highly compact plasmid molecule is not appropriately interpreted as a shorter linear DNA molecule simply because it travels a greater distance.

A quantitative example makes the logarithmic character of this relationship clear. Suppose an illustrative gel has a 1,000 base pair linear standard at a migration distance of 20 millimetres and a 100 base pair standard at 50 millimetres. The base-ten logarithms of their lengths are 3 and 2, respectively. In a locally linear calibration, an unknown migrating 35 millimetres lies halfway between these standards in log-length space, so the estimated logarithm is 2.5. Raising 10 to the power 2.5 gives approximately 316 base pairs. The answer is not 550 base pairs, the arithmetic midpoint of the two sizes. This reasoning assumes that the unknown behaves as a linear fragment and that the two standards bracket a region where the logarithmic approximation is appropriate.

A linear DNA fragment's length and mass are related, but a band intensity does not directly give molecule number. If the staining response is approximately proportional to DNA mass and neither signal is saturated, a band containing 1,000 base pair molecules can have the same intensity as a band containing 500 base pair molecules even if the second band contains approximately twice as many molecules. For example, equal masses of these two DNA lengths contain molecule numbers inversely proportional to length. If dye response depends on sequence, topology or other conditions, the mass inference is less secure. An examination question asking for equal copy numbers cannot assume that equal fluorescence intensity is sufficient without specifying the calibration and molecular forms.

The width of a band reflects both the physical distribution of molecules and the resolution of the measurement. A sharply defined band may correspond to molecules of similar size but does not guarantee sequence purity. A broader band may reflect a range of lengths or limited resolution. At excessive signal intensity, imaging saturation can conceal differences in band mass. Unequal sample loading can also produce misleading comparisons. A dense broad signal near a well may represent high molecular weight DNA, but it might also represent overloaded material or molecules whose movement is limited by association with other components. Interpretation improves when the sample is examined in the context of a ladder, a reference preparation and a measurement of how much target material was loaded.

Genomic DNA preparations are often evaluated by asking whether substantial high molecular weight material has been retained. A prominent slow-migrating signal with little lower-size material supports the presence of relatively long DNA within the resolving capability of the assay. A broad distribution extending into shorter sizes can suggest fragmentation. However, the largest molecules may be poorly resolved in an ordinary gel; a signal at the well is not a precise measurement of chromosome length. Specialized length-sensitive approaches can be appropriate when much larger molecules must be distinguished, but the examination principle is to identify the resolution limit of the assay before assigning a size. Reporting an exact length from material that has not been resolved is not justified.

Plasmid preparations require a different interpretation. An untreated reference circular DNA sample may display supercoiled and relaxed forms as well as linear molecules. A linear size ladder cannot independently identify the lengths of the circular bands. The number of observed forms is not necessarily equal to the number of different plasmid sequences. The comparison with a known linear form introduced earlier is therefore particularly informative. If the scientific aim is to compare the proportions of conformational forms, preservation of topology and an appropriate staining comparison matter. If the aim is to establish the size of a monomeric plasmid, a comparison in which the relevant material behaves as linear DNA is more useful. A gel must be read according to the molecular property the experiment was designed to reveal.

RNA integrity introduces additional considerations because RNA folds into complex structures. Intramolecular base pairing can produce differences in mobility unrelated to chain length. An electrophoretic measurement designed to reduce secondary structure can make size-based interpretation more informative. Denaturing conditions are intended to reduce unwanted noncovalent folding, not to cleave the polymer; preserving covalent continuity remains essential. This is a direct extension of the earlier distinction between denaturation and degradation. A folded but intact RNA and a degraded RNA may have different molecular properties, yet both can yield unusual mobility if the measurement fails to control secondary structure.

In many mammalian total RNA preparations, the major ribosomal RNAs are designated 28S and 18S. The S labels refer to sedimentation coefficients, not directly to chain lengths or exact numbers of bases. The larger ribosomal RNA often gives a stronger mass-associated signal in a suitable intact preparation because the two species occur in comparable molar amounts in ribosomes but differ in molecular mass. A frequently used historical visual guideline is an intensity ratio near 2:1 for the 28S and 18S species under appropriate conditions. This guideline must not be treated as a universally necessary criterion. It depends on the organism, the measurement and the sample population; many acceptable preparations do not conform to it.

Plant total RNA illustrates why organism identity matters. Besides cytosolic ribosomal RNA, chloroplasts and mitochondria can contribute their own ribosomal RNA species. Some RNA species undergo processing events that generate additional characteristic fragments. A plant RNA gel can therefore show bands or distributions that do not resemble a textbook mammalian 28S and 18S pattern. It would be a mistake to classify every extra band as degradation or to apply a mammalian visual rule without considering the sample. A suitable reference from the same type of material is more informative than a comparison with a biologically inappropriate standard. The correct inference begins by identifying the expected RNA population and the range over which the method can distinguish its components.

A fragmented total RNA population often displays a greater contribution from smaller molecules and a reduction in the distinctness of major ribosomal signals. This can be seen more quantitatively using a capillary or microfluidic electrophoresis system that records signal as a function of migration time or estimated size. The resulting electropherogram represents a distribution rather than a photograph of bands. Peaks indicate regions with increased signal, and the baseline and other regions of the trace can contain information about smaller fragments. The meaning of migration time depends on the instrument's calibration; it should not be converted to size by assuming that time and molecular length are directly proportional.

Schroeder and colleagues introduced the RNA integrity number in a 2006 study to make integrity assessment more standardized. The RIN is conventionally reported on a scale from 1, representing extensive degradation, to 10, representing high integrity for a suitable sample type. It is derived from features of an electrophoretic trace rather than being merely the quotient of two ribosomal RNA peak heights. This is a significant methodological advance because degradation can change several parts of the trace in ways that a single ratio may miss. However, the score remains an indirect assessment of a selected sample population under the conditions used by the algorithm. It does not directly measure the integrity of every transcript or guarantee the suitability of the RNA for every downstream experiment.

For example, two illustrative RNA samples may have RIN values of 8.5 and 5.0 and similar total RNA concentrations. A study requiring representation of long transcripts will generally have greater concern about the second sample. In contrast, a validated measurement restricted to very short target regions may remain informative with partially degraded RNA if the relevant regions survive and the effects of degradation are controlled. The numbers are not universal pass/fail thresholds. An experimental design must decide which molecular lengths and structures are needed for the scientific question. When different groups of samples have systematically different integrity, an apparent biological difference may instead reflect a technical bias in which molecules remain detectable.

Another important limitation of ribosomal RNA-based quality measures is the possibility of different degradation rates among RNA classes. A high-quality ribosomal profile provides useful evidence about bulk RNA integrity, but messenger RNA stability can depend on specific sequences, cellular localization and association with proteins. The profile cannot prove that every messenger RNA is full length. Conversely, a population intentionally enriched for small RNAs or depleted of ribosomal RNA may not be suitable for an integrity assessment designed around conventional total RNA. Applying the wrong quality metric may lead to rejecting a preparation precisely because the intended enrichment was successful. The most useful interpretation combines a general measurement with a metric suited to the particular RNA population.

A well-designed comparison therefore separates sample integrity from analytical detection. For a DNA experiment, the investigator might combine a target-class mass estimate with a length-sensitive profile and a sequence-specific observation. For an RNA experiment, an appropriate integrity profile can be combined with a measurement that distinguishes transcript-derived signal from DNA contamination. The combination is valuable because the measurements rely on different physical or biochemical properties. If all three support the intended interpretation, uncertainty is reduced. If they disagree, their differences can suggest where the assumptions have failed. The next section considers the property that provides sequence-level discrimination: complementary base pairing.

Hybridisation: converting complementarity into sequence evidence

The bulk properties of nucleic acids make it possible to recover and quantify them, but they do not establish whether a particular gene or transcript is present. A DNA fraction may contain millions of different sequence segments. If two fragments have the same length and similar base composition, ordinary electrophoresis and ultraviolet analysis may not distinguish them. To recognize a defined sequence, an experiment needs a molecular interaction that depends on the arrangement of bases. Complementary base pairing provides such an interaction. The deliberate use of complementary strands to form a recognizable duplex is called nucleic acid hybridisation.

A probe is a nucleic acid molecule selected to interact with a complementary target sequence. The probe may be DNA or RNA, and the target may likewise be DNA or RNA, depending on the assay. A DNA probe can hybridise with a complementary RNA transcript to form a DNA–RNA duplex; an RNA probe can also recognize suitable DNA or RNA targets. The target is the molecular sequence being sought, whereas the probe is the reagent used to reveal or capture it. Confusing these roles is particularly easy when both molecules are nucleic acids. A labelled probe generates a detectable signal when retained in association with its target, but that signal must be interpreted against background, specificity and accessibility controls.

Complementarity is directional because strands in an ordinary duplex run antiparallel. A target written in the 5′ to 3′ direction as 5′-AGTCC-3′ would align with 3′-TCAGG-5′. If the complementary strand is conventionally written from 5′ to 3′, its sequence is 5′-GGACT-3′. The operation of complementing each base and reversing the direction gives the reverse complement. Merely replacing A with T and G with C while retaining the same displayed 5′ to 3′ orientation is not generally the correct answer. This short example is deliberately too short to serve as a realistic unique detection probe in a complex genome; its purpose is to demonstrate strand polarity.

The stability of a complementary duplex arises from multiple interactions, including hydrogen bonding between paired bases and stacking interactions between neighbouring bases. The free-energy difference between associated and separated strands depends on sequence, temperature, ionic conditions and the concentrations of interacting molecules. A duplex is not held together by hydrogen bonds alone. Base stacking and the solvent environment play substantial roles, which helps explain why two sequences of equal length and identical total GC content can have different melting behaviour. The sequence arrangement, ends and local mismatch context can change duplex stability. An examination question should therefore state whether it invokes a simplified composition-based comparison or asks for an exact prediction.

Temperature influences the equilibrium between ordered paired states and separate strands. As thermal motion increases, the population of paired molecules can decline. The melting temperature is a condition-dependent midpoint of a defined transition, not a permanent intrinsic temperature attached to a gene. In a simple duplex system it is often described as the temperature at which half of the relevant duplex population is melted according to the specified definition. For a long DNA molecule containing regions of different stability, melting may occur over a broad range, and a single midpoint summarizes that behaviour. Ionic environment and strand concentration can change the equilibrium, so a melting temperature should always be associated with the conditions under which it was measured or predicted.

The negatively charged phosphate backbones of approaching strands repel one another. Counterions reduce the effective electrostatic repulsion, which often stabilizes a duplex relative to separated strands. Consequently, changing ionic conditions can alter the selectivity of hybridisation. A more strongly stabilizing environment may allow even imperfectly complementary strands to remain associated, whereas a less stabilizing environment may favour the retention of more strongly matched duplexes. This does not mean that ionic effects are identical for every nucleic acid structure or chemical system. The conceptual point is that hybridisation depends on the interaction of sequence with the surrounding environment, rather than on sequence alone.

Stringency describes how demanding the conditions of recognition are with respect to duplex stability. Under relatively high stringency, weakly paired molecules are less likely to remain associated, so a probe may distinguish a closely related target more effectively. Under lower stringency, imperfectly matched targets may remain detectable, which can be useful when the aim is to recognize a family of related sequences rather than one exact variant. The trade-off is between discrimination and the risk of losing a legitimate signal. In a simple conceptual comparison, increasing temperature or decreasing ionic stabilization tends to make recognition more demanding. Exact behaviour depends on the probe, target and assay, so stringency is best understood in terms of relative stability rather than a universal numerical recipe.

A mismatch interrupts the ideal pattern of base pairing and may disturb stacking interactions. Its effect depends on which bases are involved and where the mismatch lies. A mismatch near a duplex end may affect stability differently from an internal mismatch surrounded by neighbouring pairs. It is therefore incorrect to assign a fixed number of degrees of melting-temperature change to every mismatch regardless of sequence. If a probe recognizes both a fully matched sequence and a related one with a mismatch, a difference in retained signal may provide evidence of discrimination under the tested conditions. If both signals disappear when the conditions become more demanding, the result may indicate loss of sensitivity rather than successful identification of the target.

The presence of the target does not guarantee its accessibility. A sequence may be buried within a double-stranded region, folded into a stable intramolecular RNA structure or associated with other components. A probe must compete with interactions already present in the sample. A target RNA that folds into a hairpin can therefore produce a weaker hybridisation signal than an otherwise similar exposed sequence, even at equal concentration. Denaturation or other means of making the target accessible can change the response without changing the target mass. Conversely, degradation may remove the sequence entirely. These possibilities illustrate why a weak signal is not uniquely interpretable as low target abundance.

Hybridisation is a reversible association and can be described conceptually by mass action. Let P denote free probe, T free target and PT the associated duplex. For a simple one-to-one equilibrium the dissociation constant is Kd = [P][T]/[PT], with units of concentration when the conventional concentration expression is used. A smaller Kd indicates more favourable association under the stated conditions. If free probe is in large excess and remains approximately constant, the target fraction occupied is approximately [P]/(Kd + [P]). This follows by substituting [PT]/[T] = [P]/Kd into the definition of total target, [T] + [PT]. The model assumes a single accessible binding site, equilibrium, a known free probe concentration and no significant competing interactions. It is a useful reasoning model rather than a complete description of every membrane-based or solution assay.

Suppose an illustrative fully matched duplex has Kd = 2 nanomolar and a mismatched duplex has Kd = 20 nanomolar under a defined set of conditions. With a free probe concentration of 2 nanomolar, the predicted occupancies in the simplified model are 2/(2 + 2) = 0.50 and 2/(20 + 2), approximately 0.091. The fully matched target is thus more highly occupied. If the free probe concentration were very large relative to both dissociation constants, both occupancies would approach one, reducing the discrimination based on equilibrium occupancy alone. This example demonstrates why a high signal is not synonymous with high specificity. Real hybridisation measurements may involve kinetics, washing, surface interactions and nonideal responses that prevent direct application of this simplified numerical model.

The concentration of a target also affects the total number of duplexes formed. Two distinct target sequences may bind a probe with different affinities but occur at very different abundances. A highly abundant partially complementary target can contribute substantial signal even if each molecule binds less strongly. Conversely, a rare fully complementary target can remain difficult to detect. Analytical specificity is therefore a property of the complete assay and sample context, not simply the theoretical preference of the probe for one sequence. A relevant control should include plausible related molecules or an independently verified negative sample when discrimination between close alternatives matters.

A label can be associated with a probe in several ways. Direct labels produce a measurable optical or other signal; indirect labels permit subsequent recognition by a detection system. The important distinction is between the molecular interaction that identifies the target and the physical signal that reveals the interaction. A fluorescent signal may arise because a labelled probe is retained at a target location, whereas a colorimetric or other signal may arise from an additional detection reaction. These systems differ in sensitivity, dynamic range and background. The presence of a label does not make a probe inherently sequence-specific; its specificity still depends on recognition of the target and on the exclusion of alternative sources of signal.

Hybridisation can be used either for detection or for selective recovery. In a detection assay, the investigator asks whether a complementary target is present and, under validated conditions, how its signal compares with other samples. In an affinity capture model, complementary material attached to a support provides a selective interaction that can enrich molecules containing the target sequence. The two applications use the same recognition principle but differ in their intended outcome. Detecting a target on a membrane does not necessarily recover it for further use, and enriching a fraction through complementary binding does not establish that every recovered molecule is exactly the desired sequence. Recovery and identity remain separate properties even at this more selective level of analysis.

Sequence-specific detection: what a blot can establish

The development of molecular hybridisation methods made it possible to connect the position of a nucleic acid fragment in an electrophoretic separation with information about its sequence. In 1975, Edwin Southern described a method for detecting defined DNA sequences among DNA fragments that had been separated by gel electrophoresis. The intellectual advance was to combine two independent properties of a fragment: its physical mobility in a gel and its ability to associate with a complementary probe. This was particularly valuable because a genome-derived mixture can contain many fragments of similar length. A general stain reveals the distribution of detectable DNA, but a sequence-selective probe can reveal only a subset of that distribution.

In the conceptual Southern blot, separated DNA fragments are represented on a membrane in a spatial arrangement corresponding to the original separation. The target sequences are made accessible for complementary pairing, and a suitable probe provides a signal associated with fragments containing sufficiently complementary sequence. The transfer is useful because the membrane provides a relatively accessible surface on which target molecules can be retained and recognized. The presence of a band on the developed blot means that a detectable probe-associated signal occurred at the corresponding migration position. It is not equivalent to the total stain of the original lane, and it is not evidence that every molecule at that position contains the recognized sequence. Co-migrating molecules remain a possibility.

The molecular length represented by a Southern blot band ordinarily refers to the fragment carrying the complementary region, not to the length of the gene, probe or entire chromosome. For instance, an illustrative genome-derived fragment of 4 kilobases may contain a complementary region only a few hundred bases long. A probe can associate with that region while the electrophoretic location reflects the length of the complete fragment. If another sample carries a fragment of 6 kilobases that contains the same recognized sequence, both may give a positive signal but at different positions. A possible explanation is variation in the positions of fragment boundaries; another is variation in the length of a region lying between them. The blot itself establishes a difference in detected fragment size under the stated separation conditions, not a unique molecular cause.

An important analytical distinction concerns the number of signals. A probe that recognizes a sequence present at more than one location may give multiple bands if the recognized sequences occur in fragments of distinguishable lengths. A single band may represent one recognized fragment class, but it could also contain several similarly migrating fragments. A sequence-specific copy-number claim therefore needs information about how fragments were generated, whether probe binding is unique, whether signal response is in a quantitative range and whether appropriate comparisons exist. Multiple bands cannot automatically be translated into an equal number of copies per genome. Conversely, a single band is not necessarily proof of a single-copy gene.

The Northern blot extends the same broad separation-and-recognition logic to RNA. In a Northern measurement the original separation represents RNA molecules, often with the aim of reducing folding-related mobility differences so that length information is meaningful. A complementary probe can identify transcript-derived signals at positions corresponding to one or more RNA size classes. This makes it possible to compare both apparent transcript size and signal intensity in a way that a simple total RNA concentration measurement cannot. The term Northern is a conventional name inspired by Southern blotting; it does not designate the direction in which material moves through a membrane. The Western blot, by contrast, is ordinarily a protein detection method based on binding reagents such as antibodies rather than on nucleic acid base pairing.

Transcript size differences can be informative about biological processing. An illustrative Northern blot may show a 2 kilobase signal in one tissue and a 3 kilobase signal in another when both are measured with a probe recognizing a shared region. These observations are compatible with different transcript isoforms, changes in transcription initiation or termination, alternative processing or other mechanisms that affect RNA length. They do not identify the exact processing event without further information. A sample with degraded RNA can also contain shorter probe-positive fragments. To make a biological claim, the integrity of the RNA, the reproducibility of the band pattern and the recognition properties of the probe must be considered together.

Signal intensity in a blot is often used as evidence of relative abundance, but such interpretation has important assumptions. Equal amounts of total RNA loaded into two lanes do not guarantee equal numbers of cells represented, and the proportion of a particular transcript can change independently of total RNA mass. Transfer efficiency can differ between molecular sizes or between regions of a membrane. The detection reaction may be nonlinear, and strong signals may reach saturation. A sample with twice the target abundance may then produce less than twice the background-corrected measured intensity. A valid quantitative comparison needs a suitable reference, a response range in which the signal can be interpreted and an understanding of what biological quantity is being normalized.

For example, consider illustrative Northern signals of 120 and 180 arbitrary units from two lanes, with a background of 20 units for each. The background-corrected target signals are 100 and 160 units. If the respective validated loading-reference signals are 50 and 100 units after their own background corrections, the normalized target ratios are 2.0 and 1.6. Relative to the first sample, the second has a normalized signal of 1.6/2.0 = 0.80. Although its raw target band is brighter, the normalized result is lower. This calculation is meaningful only if the reference appropriately accounts for the loading or comparison quantity, both signals lie in their valid ranges and the background corrections are suitable. Normalization cannot repair a reference that changes for the same biological reason being investigated.

A dot blot or slot blot presents another conceptual comparison. Target-containing material is represented in spots or slots on a surface without the prior size-resolving separation used in Southern or Northern blotting. A complementary probe can reveal the presence of recognized sequences, and a calibrated response can sometimes support comparisons of signal abundance. However, the absence of an electrophoretic dimension means that the length of the molecule carrying the recognized sequence is not directly resolved. Two samples that produce equal dot signals might contain one intact long transcript and many shorter fragments bearing the same probe-recognized region. A student should choose a size-resolving method when the scientific question depends on molecular length as well as identity.

Hybridisation also provides a conceptual basis for selective isolation of a sequence. Rather than measuring total nucleic acid, an affinity capture system uses complementarity to enrich material containing a recognized region. A complementary strand retained on a suitable support can distinguish potential target molecules from those that interact less strongly. This logic is used in broad approaches to enrichment, including the poly(A)-recognition example discussed earlier. It is important to distinguish enrichment from perfect purification. Unrelated material can be retained through nonspecific interactions, and molecules containing a similar sequence may also associate. The recovered fraction must still be checked by a method capable of supporting the intended identity claim.

Amplification and sequencing provide different forms of sequence evidence. A targeted amplification measurement can indicate the presence of a region compatible with the recognition and amplification system, whereas sequencing can provide evidence about the arrangement of bases within the region actually read. Neither measurement alone establishes that the original molecule was intact over regions outside the observed sequence. A positive result for a short genomic locus does not prove that a complete chromosome survived extraction, and a detected cDNA region does not prove that a full-length transcript was recovered. These distinctions connect sequence detection to the chapter's earlier discussion of sample integrity. Detailed amplification and sequencing methods are assigned to related molecular biology and biotechnology chapters; the essential point here is that the measurement defines the scope of the conclusion.

A negative hybridisation or sequence-detection signal is more difficult to interpret than is sometimes assumed. It may indicate that the relevant sequence is absent, that it is present below the detection limit, that it is inaccessible, that it has been degraded, that it differs enough from the expected sequence to impair recognition, or that a measurement component has failed. A positive control tests whether the analytical system can produce the expected result for a known target. A negative control helps evaluate whether signal appears in the absence of the intended target. An independently assessed sample-quality measurement addresses another part of the explanation. Only by considering these observations together can a negative result become credible evidence of absence within a defined detection capability.

Quality controls and integrated experimental reasoning

A control is an observation designed to distinguish the intended explanation from plausible alternatives. A preparation can fail at many stages, and the final signal often reflects the combined influence of those stages. A reference DNA molecule measured directly in a compatible solution tests the analytical response but says nothing about the efficiency with which a biological matrix releases DNA. A reference present during a recovery comparison can provide information about loss during preparation, but it may not mimic the behaviour of native chromosomal DNA if it differs greatly in structure or association with other cellular constituents. Controls are therefore not interchangeable: each addresses a particular uncertainty.

A reagent or extraction blank conceptually represents the materials entering the preparation without the intended biological input. If a sequence-specific signal is present in that blank, there is reason to suspect introduced target-like material or another source of false signal. A measurement blank instead estimates the response of the measurement medium or reagent without added analyte. An acceptable measurement blank does not exclude contamination introduced during the earlier isolation process. Similarly, an extraction blank that behaves appropriately does not establish that the target-positive sample has been recovered efficiently. Examination problems often present one normal control and ask whether it validates an entirely different part of the experiment; the answer depends on precisely which stage the control represents.

A positive control is a known material expected to produce the signal of interest. Failure of that control weakens the interpretation of a negative unknown because the system has not demonstrated the ability to detect a relevant target. A negative control is a material that should not produce the target-specific signal. A positive signal from it raises concerns about specificity, background or contamination. Yet controls themselves must be relevant: a positive reference that is unusually concentrated may demonstrate that the system can detect an abundant target without demonstrating sensitivity to a rare one. A negative reference that is entirely unrelated may fail to challenge discrimination against closely similar sequences. Good experimental design matches controls to the alternative explanations that genuinely threaten the intended inference.

A comparison involving an added known reference amount can test recovery or matrix effects depending on where the reference enters the measurement. In an illustrative recovery study, a known distinguishable reference amount of 2 micrograms is introduced into a benign sample comparison before the evaluated isolation stage, and 1.4 micrograms of that reference is subsequently measured. The apparent recovery of the reference is 1.4/2 × 100 = 70 percent. This is evidence about the defined reference under the tested conditions. It does not establish that native genomic DNA or every RNA species is recovered with identical efficiency. If the reference is added only after isolation, the measured response addresses analytical compatibility rather than loss during extraction.

Replicate preparations from comparable material help estimate technical variability, whereas independent biological samples help assess biological variability. Suppose three measurements of one DNA preparation give 40, 41 and 39 nanograms per microlitre. These observations indicate reproducibility of the measurement for that preparation under the tested conditions. They do not establish that three different leaves contain identical amounts of DNA. Conversely, measurements from independent leaves may vary because of true biological differences as well as extraction and measurement variability. A study that measures a single preparation repeatedly should not treat those technical observations as independent biological replicates. The distinction is a foundation for later statistical reasoning.

Consider the following hypothetical analysis of three DNA preparations from comparable benign material. Sample A has a UV-derived estimate of 60 nanograms per microlitre, a double-stranded-DNA fluorescence estimate of 55 and a predominantly high molecular weight gel signal. Sample B has a UV-derived estimate of 110, a fluorescence estimate of 48 and additional fast-moving material. Sample C has a UV-derived estimate of 56, a fluorescence estimate of 53 and an extensive smear at smaller sizes. These observations raise different questions. Sample A has broadly concordant quantity and integrity evidence. Sample B has a substantial discrepancy between bulk and class-selective concentration estimates, which is consistent with extra absorbing material or a fluorescence response problem. Sample C has concordant estimates of target mass but different length evidence.

If the scientific aim requires long DNA molecules, sample A is the most promising of these illustrative cases, provided identity and assay controls also support its use. Sample B cannot be ranked first merely because its UV estimate is largest. Sample C might still be appropriate for a short-region measurement because fragmentation does not necessarily remove the short target. The comparison illustrates a general analytical rule: suitability is conditional on the biological question. The correct answer changes if the required property changes from full-length integrity to detection of a short sequence. It is therefore useful to formulate the intended conclusion before choosing which numerical quality indicator will guide the decision.

RNA comparisons require similar integrated reasoning. Imagine two independent groups of leaf samples with similar total RNA mass and similar UV purity ratios, but one group shows substantially more short-fragment signal in its electropherograms. A long-transcript measurement differs between the groups while a short-region measurement is similar. These observations are compatible with unequal RNA integrity and do not by themselves establish that the biological abundance of the transcript changed. An important alternative is biological variation in transcript length or processing, which would need appropriate sequence-specific and integrity evidence to distinguish. If the experimental groups differ systematically in their sample preservation history, the resulting association between group and apparent expression may be confounded.

Contamination can produce different analytical signatures depending on its molecular identity and the assay. Residual genomic DNA can contribute to total RNA absorbance, alter a gel pattern and provide a sequence signal in a comparison lacking reverse transcription. Residual RNA can contribute to UV measurements of DNA and affect the apparent mass assigned to a plasmid fraction. Other absorbing components can distort optical measurements without contributing a nucleic-acid-selective signal. Some substances primarily inhibit an enzyme-dependent measurement or quench fluorescence while having little impact on the expected purity ratios. A single screening number is therefore inadequate for identifying every form of contamination. The useful approach is to compare results whose assumptions fail in different ways.

The language of a scientific conclusion should reflect the limits of the evidence. A result described as consistent with DNA contamination identifies an explanation supported by the observations but not uniquely established. A statement that a defined target is detected under a validated measurement describes evidence of that region without asserting the integrity of the entire molecule. A statement that no target signal was detected above a known detection capability avoids the unwarranted claim that no molecule exists in the original specimen. This distinction is essential to Part C reasoning, where the best option is often the conclusion supported by the complete set of controls rather than the most definite-sounding statement.

The principles of nucleic acid recovery have parallels in the isolation of other biological macromolecules. A protein preparation, for example, may have a high mass estimate yet lack the activity needed for an enzyme study; a lipid fraction can contain multiple lipid classes with different chemical properties; a carbohydrate assay may respond differently to molecules of different composition. The shared analytical logic is to distinguish identity, quantity, purity, structural or functional integrity and recovery, then choose evidence suitable for each. The next chapter applies that logic to proteins, carbohydrates and lipids, whose chemical diversity creates additional challenges for selective separation and measurement.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Higher concentration always means higher recovery. Concentration is mass per volume. Confusion arises when final volumes differ; compare concentration multiplied by volume and normalize to input if appropriate.
  2. A clean ratio proves intact DNA. Ratios screen optical composition. Exams may give an excellent ratio and a smear; choose a length-sensitive test for integrity.
  3. Every A260 photon loss represents dsDNA. RNA, nucleotides and other absorbers contribute. A DNA conversion factor assumes that dsDNA dominates; compare selective fluorescence and controls.
  4. A260/A280 is a fixed chemical constant. The ratio depends on pH, ionic environment and measurement quality. Compare equivalent reference conditions rather than treating a guideline as a definition.
  5. A tiny absorbance gives an equally reliable ratio. Small denominator errors cause large ratio changes. Inspect the absolute signals and validated measurement range.
  6. Denaturation always cleaves the backbone. Noncovalent structure can be lost while covalent chains remain intact. A melting curve and fragmentation gel reveal different properties.
  7. Chelation blocks every RNase. RNase A is metal independent. An examiner may contrast nuclease cofactor requirements; infer only protection against relevant metal-dependent enzymes.
  8. Every plasmid band is a different plasmid. Equal-length circular molecules can have different topology and migration. Compare known linear forms and sequence evidence.
  9. A linear ladder sizes every circular DNA band. Topology changes mobility. A linear calibration is valid only for comparable linear molecules.
  10. More UV signal after melting means more DNA. Hyperchromicity changes absorption coefficient. In matched aliquots mass can remain constant.
  11. A high RIN proves every messenger RNA is full length. RIN summarizes a trace and does not test every transcript. Choose a metric suited to the relevant RNA class and length.
  12. All organisms require a 28S:18S ratio of two. This historical mammalian guideline is conditional. Plant and other organisms can have different RNA populations.
  13. Poly(A) selection recovers the entire transcriptome. Tail recognition enriches a subset; nonpolyadenylated RNAs and some fragments may be excluded. Distinguish target population from total RNA.
  14. A probe sequence is just the base-by-base complement. Strands pair antiparallel; when both sequences are written 5′ to 3′, use the reverse complement. The displayed direction is a common trap.
  15. A negative blot proves absolute absence. Detection depends on accessibility, complementarity, target abundance and controls. State absence only within the assay's supported detection capability.
  16. A Northern band position reports probe length. The separation precedes recognition; position represents the target-bearing RNA size class. Probe length is a separate property.
  17. An extraction blank and a positive control validate recovery. A blank checks unwanted signal; a positive control checks detection. A reference entering the evaluated extraction stage addresses recovery.
  18. Technical replicates are independent biological samples. Repeated measurements of one preparation assess measurement variation. For biological inference, independently sampled material is required.

Original examination practice

These 55 questions are original teaching questions, not previous-year examination questions. Numerical datasets are illustrative.

CSIR-NET Part B

B1. Which chemical feature principally gives a nucleic acid its negative charge near neutral pH?

  1. Ribose hydroxyls
  2. Phosphodiester backbone
  3. Watson–Crick hydrogen bonds
  4. Purine stacking
Answer and explanation

B. Phosphodiester backbone

Phosphate groups in the backbone carry negative charge under ordinary aqueous analytical conditions. Stacking and hydrogen bonding influence conformation, while sugar hydroxyls are not the primary source of the polymer's net negative charge.

B2. Which distinction between denaturation and degradation is correct?

  1. Degradation always increases molecule length
  2. Denaturation converts DNA into RNA
  3. Denaturation can disrupt base pairing without backbone cleavage
  4. Both necessarily cleave phosphodiester bonds
Answer and explanation

C. Denaturation can disrupt base pairing without backbone cleavage

Denaturation disrupts ordered noncovalent interactions; degradation cleaves covalent bonds. A duplex can melt with both strands intact. Neither process converts DNA into RNA, and cleavage generally reduces continuous chain length.

B3. Which feature explains RNA's greater susceptibility to base-promoted backbone cleavage?

  1. Thymine
  2. A neighbouring 2′ hydroxyl
  3. A 3′ phosphate on every residue
  4. A lack of all base pairing
Answer and explanation

B. A neighbouring 2′ hydroxyl

The ribose 2′ hydroxyl can participate in intramolecular attack on an adjacent phosphate. DNA lacks this feature. RNA can base-pair, and thymine or a terminal phosphate does not explain this distinction.

B4. Which term describes the continuity of molecules over a length relevant to an experiment?

  1. Integrity
  2. Concentration
  3. Selectivity
  4. Yield
Answer and explanation

A. Integrity

Integrity concerns structural continuity or relevant molecular state. Concentration measures amount per volume, yield is amount recovered, and selectivity is a method's discrimination among components.

B5. Which action of a chelator explains protection against some nucleases?

  1. Converting RNA to DNA
  2. Binding available divalent metal ions
  3. Generating complementary strands
  4. Hydrolysing all proteins
Answer and explanation

B. Binding available divalent metal ions

Some nucleases need metal ions; binding available ions may reduce their activity. This does not guarantee inhibition of metal-independent nucleases such as RNase A, and none of the other effects is the defining action of a chelator.

B6. A silica-based capture material is best understood as which type of separation?

  1. A sequence-specific antibody
  2. Irreversible covalent incorporation of DNA
  3. Conditional association and release
  4. Always a molecular size sieve
Answer and explanation

C. Conditional association and release

Silica purification relies on reversible retention under suitable chemical conditions and release under different conditions. It does not inherently select a gene or permanently incorporate target DNA.

B7. Why can a precipitation pellet be chemically impure?

  1. Only proteins are negatively charged
  2. DNA cannot precipitate
  3. All nucleic acids are insoluble in water
  4. Other substances may co-precipitate
Answer and explanation

D. Other substances may co-precipitate

Changes in solvation and charge screening can condense nucleic acids, but other materials may also precipitate. DNA is typically hydrated in aqueous media; a visible pellet does not identify its components.

B8. What is the effect of a single-strand nick on a covalently closed circular DNA duplex?

  1. It can permit relaxation of torsional constraint
  2. It creates two double-stranded ends
  3. It doubles the mass
  4. It changes every nucleotide
Answer and explanation

A. It can permit relaxation of torsional constraint

A nick permits relative rotation of strands and relaxation without requiring a double-strand break. The sequence and nearly all mass remain the same, unlike the other proposed effects.

B9. Which statement about closed circular DNA is valid in the absence of strand passage or backbone breakage?

  1. Wr is always zero
  2. Lk remains invariant while Tw and Wr may redistribute
  3. Lk changes whenever the shape changes
  4. Tw is always zero
Answer and explanation

B. Lk remains invariant while Tw and Wr may redistribute

Linking number is a topological invariant of an intact closed duplex; twist and writhe may trade off while their sum remains fixed. Neither component has to be zero.

B10. Which property underlies poly(A)-based RNA enrichment?

  1. Equal retention of all RNA classes
  2. Chromosome size
  3. Protein antigen recognition
  4. Complementarity to a suitable tail
Answer and explanation

D. Complementarity to a suitable tail

Complementary oligo(dT) can recognize polyadenylated RNA. This does not capture all RNA equally and is unrelated to antibody recognition or chromosomal length.

B11. Which statement about RNase A is correct?

  1. It degrades only DNA
  2. It necessarily requires Mg²⁺
  3. It is identical to all RNases
  4. Its activity does not require a divalent metal cofactor
Answer and explanation

D. Its activity does not require a divalent metal cofactor

RNase A is a classic metal-independent ribonuclease. A chelator therefore cannot be assumed to inactivate it. RNases are diverse, and RNase A is not a DNA-selective enzyme.

B12. Which measurement most directly screens bulk nucleic acid absorption?

  1. A260
  2. Signal at 700 nm alone
  3. A protein activity assay
  4. Molecular weight from an uncalibrated gel
Answer and explanation

A. A260

Nucleic acid bases absorb near 260 nm. The reading can include other absorbing substances, so it is not a unique DNA concentration or integrity measurement.

B13. The approximate A260 = 1 conversion for typical pure double-stranded DNA at a 1 cm path is:

  1. 40 mg/mL
  2. 1 ng/L
  3. 50 µg/mL
  4. 33 mol/L
Answer and explanation

C. 50 µg/mL

The usual mass factor is approximately 50 µg/mL per normalized absorbance unit. RNA and single-stranded DNA have different approximate factors, and the other options use inappropriate magnitudes or units.

B14. What does an A260/A280 ratio near 1.8 in a DNA sample establish?

  1. Every sequence is correct
  2. No inhibitor can be present
  3. The chromosome is intact
  4. It is compatible with relatively clean dsDNA under suitable conditions
Answer and explanation

D. It is compatible with relatively clean dsDNA under suitable conditions

A ratio near the usual guideline supports, but does not prove, purity. It does not directly identify sequence, fragment lengths or enzyme compatibility.

B15. What is an advantage of a target-class-selective fluorescence assay over bulk UV absorption?

  1. It always measures full-length DNA
  2. It always determines sequence
  3. It never requires calibration
  4. It can preferentially report a selected nucleic acid class
Answer and explanation

D. It can preferentially report a selected nucleic acid class

Preferential dye response can improve class selectivity and sensitivity. It does not establish exact sequence or polymer continuity and depends on appropriate calibration.

B16. In ordinary agarose separation, DNA migrates toward which electrode?

  1. The cathode, because DNA is neutral
  2. Both equally
  3. Neither electrode
  4. The positively charged anode
Answer and explanation

D. The positively charged anode

The negatively charged phosphate backbone drives DNA toward the positive electrode. Migration through a gel also depends on size, conformation and the separation conditions.

B17. Which statement about 28S and 18S ribosomal RNA bands is correct?

  1. They always correspond to 28 and 18 bases
  2. Their presence proves all mRNA molecules are intact
  3. Their labels are sedimentation coefficients
  4. Their intensity ratio is universal across organisms
Answer and explanation

C. Their labels are sedimentation coefficients

S refers to sedimentation coefficient, not nucleotide number. The relative intensities and expected bands vary with the sample, and rRNA integrity is an indirect indicator of other RNA classes.

B18. What does a Northern blot primarily detect?

  1. Protein through antibody recognition
  2. Only plasmid topology
  3. Total lipid
  4. RNA through complementary nucleic acid recognition
Answer and explanation

D. RNA through complementary nucleic acid recognition

Northern analysis combines separation of RNA with recognition of a complementary target. Western blots ordinarily detect proteins, while Southern blots detect DNA fragments.

B19. Which relationship is the usual definition of absorbance?

  1. log₁₀(I₀/I)
  2. I₀ × I
  3. I₀ − I
  4. I/I₀
Answer and explanation

A. log₁₀(I₀/I)

Absorbance is the negative base-ten logarithm of transmittance. A ratio of transmitted to incident light defines transmittance, not absorbance itself.

B20. Which control can reveal unintended target-like contamination introduced during isolation?

  1. Only the molecular ladder
  2. Only a reference measured after extraction
  3. Only a high positive standard
  4. Extraction blank
Answer and explanation

D. Extraction blank

An extraction blank represents the preparation without the intended input. A signal in it raises contamination or background concerns. The other controls address size, analytical detection or post-isolation response.

CSIR-NET Part C

C1. A pure dsDNA sample diluted fivefold gives normalized A260 = 0.30. What is the original concentration?

  1. 30 ng/µL
  2. 150 ng/µL
  3. 15 ng/µL
  4. 75 ng/µL
Answer and explanation

D. 75 ng/µL

The diluted concentration is 0.30 × 50 = 15 ng/µL; multiplying by five gives 75. The first choice omits dilution, and the other values do not follow the stated factor.

C2. An RNA preparation gives A260 = 0.40 after a tenfold dilution. Its recovered volume is 50 µL. Estimate recovered RNA mass assuming the usual conversion.

  1. 20 µg
  2. 8 µg
  3. 80 µg
  4. 0.8 µg
Answer and explanation

B. 8 µg

The original concentration is 0.40 × 40 × 10 = 160 ng/µL. Multiplying by 50 µL gives 8,000 ng, or 8 µg. The alternatives mishandle dilution, class factor or units.

C3. A dsDNA sample has raw A260 = 0.10 at a 0.25 cm optical path. There is no dilution. Estimate concentration.

  1. 50 ng/µL
  2. 200 ng/µL
  3. 20 ng/µL
  4. 5 ng/µL
Answer and explanation

C. 20 ng/µL

Normalized absorbance is 0.10/0.25 = 0.40; concentration is 0.40 × 50 = 20 ng/µL. Five ignores path correction; the larger choices apply incompatible factors.

C4. Two solutions have transmittance 0.1 and 0.01. What are their respective absorbances?

  1. 0.1 and 0.01
  2. 2 and 1
  3. 10 and 100
  4. 1 and 2
Answer and explanation

D. 1 and 2

The negative base-ten logarithms are 1 and 2. Transmittance values themselves and reciprocal transmittance are not absorbance; the reversed order is incorrect.

C5. Sample X has A260/A280 = 0.020/0.010. A residual background error adds 0.005 only to A280. What ratio results?

  1. 4.00
  2. 1.33
  3. 1.80
  4. 2.50
Answer and explanation

B. 1.33

The denominator becomes 0.015, giving 0.020/0.015 ≈ 1.33. This illustrates the instability of ratios at low absorbance. None of the other values uses the corrected denominator.

C6. A pure preparation of 6,000 bp dsDNA molecules has a total mass of 3.96 ng. Using 660 g/mol per bp, estimate molecule number.

  1. 6.02 × 10¹¹
  2. 6.02 × 10⁵
  3. 3.96 × 10⁹
  4. 6.02 × 10⁸
Answer and explanation

D. 6.02 × 10⁸

Molar mass is 6,000 × 660 = 3.96 × 10⁶ g/mol. The sample contains 10⁻¹⁵ mol, hence approximately 6.02 × 10⁸ molecules. The other results misconvert nanograms or ignore molar mass.

C7. Equal masses of pure linear 2 kb and 8 kb dsDNA are present. Which has more molecules?

  1. 2 kb, fourfold
  2. 2 kb, twofold
  3. Equal
  4. 8 kb, fourfold
Answer and explanation

A. 2 kb, fourfold

Molecular mass is approximately proportional to base-pair length. At equal mass, the shorter class contains 8/2 = four times as many molecules. Equal mass does not mean equal copy number.

C8. A sample has 90 ng/µL by UV and 35 ng/µL by a dsDNA-selective assay. Which conclusion is supported?

  1. Its DNA is certainly intact
  2. It contains exactly 55 ng/µL RNA
  3. Different response or interfering material requires investigation
  4. All fluorescence measurements are wrong
Answer and explanation

C. Different response or interfering material requires investigation

UV and fluorescence rely on different molecular responses. Extra absorbing material or fluorescence inhibition can explain the difference. Subtracting estimates cannot establish exact RNA mass or DNA integrity.

C9. A dye blank yields 20 units; 5 and 15 ng/µL standards yield 70 and 170 units. A sample gives 120 units in the calibrated mixture. Its concentration is:

  1. 20 ng/µL
  2. 10 ng/µL
  3. 100 ng/µL
  4. 12 ng/µL
Answer and explanation

B. 10 ng/µL

The slope is (170−70)/(15−5)=10 units per ng/µL. Corrected unknown signal is 120−20=100, giving 10 ng/µL. Twelve neglects blank subtraction; the other choices confuse signal and concentration.

C10. The unknown fluorescence signal is well above the highest validated standard. Which inference is best?

  1. The sample has no nucleic acid
  2. The curve must remain linear
  3. The blank should be set to zero after measurement
  4. A valid concentration requires evidence in the calibrated range
Answer and explanation

D. A valid concentration requires evidence in the calibrated range

An above-range signal is not evidence for a reliable extrapolation because binding or the detector can saturate. It does indicate a response, but neither a zero blank nor an assumption of linearity validates the value.

C11. In a locally log-linear gel calibration, 10 kb and 1 kb fragments migrate 10 and 30 mm. An unknown linear DNA fragment migrates 20 mm. Estimate size.

  1. 5.5 kb
  2. 2 kb
  3. 3.16 kb
  4. 9 kb
Answer and explanation

C. 3.16 kb

Halfway in migration corresponds to halfway between log₁₀(10)=1 and log₁₀(1)=0 when lengths are in kb. The estimate is 10^0.5 ≈ 3.16 kb; 5.5 is the arithmetic midpoint, not the logarithmic one.

C12. Two untreated plasmid bands migrate differently. A comparison in which each molecule is linearized gives one band. What is the most economical explanation?

  1. All DNA has degraded into nucleotides
  2. Two distinct genes must be present
  3. Different conformational forms of one molecular length may have been present
  4. The original bands were necessarily different lengths
Answer and explanation

C. Different conformational forms of one molecular length may have been present

Topology changes mobility; conversion to a comparable linear form can make equal-length variants converge. The result supports a topology explanation but does not alone prove sequence identity.

C13. Sample A and B have equal class-selective DNA mass. A is high molecular weight; B gives a broad low-size smear. Which is more promising for a long contiguous sequence analysis?

  1. Neither can contain DNA
  2. A, subject to other controls
  3. Both necessarily equal
  4. B because its molecules migrate faster
Answer and explanation

B. A, subject to other controls

A retains relatively long molecules, whereas B may be fragmented despite equal mass. For a short sequence, both might work; the intended length requirement determines the answer.

C14. A plant RNA gel has extra discrete bands relative to a mammalian reference. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. Organism-specific RNA populations and a matched reference should be considered
  2. A universal 28S:18S ratio is sufficient
  3. All extra bands prove degradation
  4. The RNA must be exclusively plasmid DNA
Answer and explanation

A. Organism-specific RNA populations and a matched reference should be considered

Plant organelles and RNA processing can contribute additional bands. A mammalian pattern alone is not an appropriate universal standard. Neither contamination nor degradation can be assigned solely from the extra bands.

C15. An RNA sample has a strong signal in an assay lacking reverse transcription. What does this suggest?

  1. Full-length RNA is proven
  2. The RNA purity ratio must be exactly 2.0
  3. An unintended DNA-derived or other signal must be considered
  4. The reference transcript is necessarily absent
Answer and explanation

C. An unintended DNA-derived or other signal must be considered

Without reverse transcription, a signal in a cDNA-dependent assay suggests another source such as genomic DNA or contamination. It does not establish RNA length, expression absence or a particular optical ratio.

C16. The target is 5′-ACGTA-3′. Which strand, written 5′ to 3′, is the reverse complement?

  1. ACGTA
  2. TGCAT
  3. TACGT
  4. ATGCA
Answer and explanation

C. TACGT

The aligned complement is 3′-TGCAT-5′, which becomes 5′-TACGT-3′ when rewritten in the conventional orientation. TGCAT has the correct paired bases in the wrong displayed direction.

C17. A matched duplex has Kd 1 nM and a mismatched duplex Kd 9 nM. At constant free probe 1 nM, compare occupancies in the simple one-site model.

  1. 0.50 and 0.10
  2. 0.10 and 0.50
  3. 1.00 and 0.90
  4. 0.90 and 0.10
Answer and explanation

A. 0.50 and 0.10

Using P/(Kd+P) gives 1/2 and 1/10. The smaller Kd yields higher occupancy at equal free probe. The other values use inconsistent denominators or reverse the stability relation.

C18. At very large free probe concentration compared with both Kd values, what happens to the occupancy difference in the ideal model?

  1. Both occupancies approach one
  2. Only the weaker duplex approaches one
  3. It increases without bound
  4. Both approach zero
Answer and explanation

A. Both occupancies approach one

When P is much larger than both Kd values, P/(Kd+P) approaches one for both. High occupancy does not guarantee discrimination; real assays may have additional kinetic effects.

C19. A matched probe and one-mismatch target both give signal at low stringency. Under more demanding conditions the mismatch signal falls while the match persists. What is supported?

  1. The target genome was destroyed
  2. Both duplexes necessarily have identical stability
  3. Recognition selectivity improved under the compared conditions
  4. The probe recognizes a protein
Answer and explanation

C. Recognition selectivity improved under the compared conditions

The retained matched signal alongside reduced mismatch signal supports improved discrimination. No inference about genome destruction or protein recognition follows, and unequal retention is consistent with different duplex stabilities.

C20. A 300-base probe detects a band at 4 kb on a Southern blot. What length does 4 kb represent?

  1. Always the entire gene length
  2. Apparent length of the recognized DNA fragment
  3. Whole chromosome length
  4. Probe length
Answer and explanation

B. Apparent length of the recognized DNA fragment

Electrophoretic position reflects the fragment containing the complementary region. The probe can recognize only part of that fragment; gene and chromosome lengths cannot be inferred from this band alone.

C21. Northern blot target signals are 90 and 150 units; shared background is 10. Loading-reference signals after correction are 40 and 100. What is the second-to-first normalized ratio?

  1. 0.70
  2. 1.75
  3. 1.67
  4. 2.00
Answer and explanation

A. 0.70

Corrected targets are 80 and 140; normalized signals are 80/40 = 2 and 140/100 = 1.4. The ratio is 1.4/2 = 0.70. Raw signal ratios do not account for the reference.

C22. A dot blot gives equal target signals from two samples. What information remains unavailable from the dot blot alone?

  1. Length of the molecules carrying the target region
  2. The identity of the labelled probe used
  3. Whether any signal was measured
  4. Whether the instrument had a blank
Answer and explanation

A. Length of the molecules carrying the target region

A dot blot lacks a size-resolving separation; equal signal can arise from intact or fragmented target-bearing material. Blank and probe identities are experimental design information rather than intrinsic missing size evidence.

C23. A pre-isolation reference of 4 µg gives 3 µg recovered. The same reference added after isolation gives its expected signal. What is the apparent pre-isolation reference recovery?

  1. 75%
  2. 25%
  3. 133%
  4. 100%
Answer and explanation

A. 75%

Three divided by four is 0.75, or 75 percent. The post-isolation result supports the analytical response for that reference but does not replace the observed pre-isolation recovery.

C24. A negative sample gives no target signal, but the positive reference also fails. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The target must be degraded
  2. Absence cannot be inferred because detection was not demonstrated
  3. The sample is pure
  4. The target is absent
Answer and explanation

B. Absence cannot be inferred because detection was not demonstrated

Failure of the positive control undermines interpretation of the negative result. No specific conclusion about purity, degradation or target absence follows until the measurement failure is resolved.

C25. Three readings of the same extract are nearly identical. What can this show?

  1. Technical repeatability for that extract
  2. All tissues have the same expression
  3. Three independent biological replicates
  4. The extraction recovery is necessarily 100%
Answer and explanation

A. Technical repeatability for that extract

Repeated measurements of one preparation assess technical repeatability, not population-level variability or extraction recovery. Independent biological inputs are needed for biological replication.

SET, assertion–reason and integrated revision

R1. Assertion: An intact DNA duplex can show a higher A260 after melting. Reason: disruption of base stacking can increase optical absorption without increasing mass.

  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Both true and reason explains assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Both true but unrelated
Answer and explanation

B. Both true and reason explains assertion

The hyperchromic effect arises from changes in base interactions and absorbance during melting. The reason explains how optical response can rise while the same DNA mass remains.

R2. Assertion: Every RNA sample with RIN below 7 is unusable. Reason: suitability depends on the target length and assay.

  1. Assertion false, reason true
  2. Both true and reason explains assertion
  3. Both false
  4. Both true but unrelated
Answer and explanation

A. Assertion false, reason true

RIN is an indirect and context-dependent metric. Short-target measurements may remain interpretable with partially fragmented RNA, so a universal cutoff is unjustified.

R3. Which statements are true? I. A260 can include free nucleotides. II. A selective dye necessarily identifies a gene. III. Electrophoretic size does not prove sequence identity.

  1. II and III only
  2. I, II and III
  3. I and III only
  4. I only
Answer and explanation

C. I and III only

I and III are true: UV absorption is shared, and fragments can co-migrate. II is false because molecular-class selectivity is not sequence-specific recognition.

R4. Which method can supply transcript size and complementary sequence evidence in the same conceptual observation?

  1. Total RNA fluorescence
  2. Bulk absorbance
  3. Northern blot
  4. A dot blot alone
Answer and explanation

C. Northern blot

Northern analysis associates probe signal with an electrophoretically resolved RNA size class. The bulk measurements do not provide sequence identity and a dot blot lacks size resolution.

R5. A solution contains 20 ng/µL in 200 µL. Another contains 60 ng/µL in 50 µL. Which has greater total mass?

  1. Second because concentration is higher
  2. First: 4 µg versus 3 µg
  3. Both: 3 µg
  4. Second: 12 µg versus 1 µg
Answer and explanation

B. First: 4 µg versus 3 µg

Concentration multiplied by volume gives 4,000 and 3,000 ng. The first sample has more mass despite lower concentration; concentration alone is not yield.

R6. The initial absorbance is 0.40 and after a conformational change it is 0.50. What is the fractional increase in absorbance?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.10
  3. 0.50
  4. 0.20
Answer and explanation

A. 0.25

The increase relative to the initial reading is (0.50−0.40)/0.40=0.25. The difference 0.10 is an absolute absorbance change and not the requested fraction.

R7. Assertion: A single Southern band proves one target copy per genome. Reason: multiple fragments of comparable mobility can co-migrate.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Assertion false, reason true
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Both true but unrelated
Answer and explanation

B. Assertion false, reason true

A single detected fragment class does not establish one copy because multiple similarly migrating fragments can contribute. The reason directly explains why the assertion is unsupported.

R8. Which is the best comparison to assess whether a sample matrix suppresses a calibrated dye response?

  1. Compare the response to an added known reference in sample and compatible reference medium
  2. Count the visible precipitate particles
  3. Assume A260/A280 determines dye response
  4. Measure only a molecular size ladder
Answer and explanation

A. Compare the response to an added known reference in sample and compatible reference medium

A reference increment in different media can reveal matrix-associated response changes. A precipitate, ladder or purity ratio cannot by itself quantify the dye response under the relevant matrix.

R9. Which association is correct?

  1. All three—DNA sequence hybridisation
  2. Southern—protein; Northern—DNA
  3. Southern—DNA; Northern—RNA; Western—protein
  4. Northern—lipid; Western—RNA
Answer and explanation

C. Southern—DNA; Northern—RNA; Western—protein

Southern and Northern use complementary nucleic acid recognition for DNA and RNA respectively. Western generally detects proteins using binding reagents such as antibodies.

R10. An extraction blank is positive and all unknown samples show faint target signals. Which report is appropriate?

  1. The target is absent in all unknowns
  2. Contamination or background compromises interpretation
  3. All unknowns are positive with certainty
  4. Only the brightest sample is automatically valid
Answer and explanation

B. Contamination or background compromises interpretation

A positive extraction blank indicates a possible introduced source of target-like signal. Faint unknown signals cannot be confidently attributed to the intended biological source until that explanation is assessed.

Revision, active recall and mastery

Isolation exploits molecular properties to recover the desired nucleic acid; analysis tests quantity, purity, integrity and identity separately. Genomic DNA demands attention to length, plasmid DNA to topology, and RNA to representation and degradation. Optical measurements require appropriate calibration and sample assumptions; gels supply mobility distributions, and hybridisation supplies complementary sequence evidence. Controls distinguish loss, interference, contamination and a genuine biological difference.
