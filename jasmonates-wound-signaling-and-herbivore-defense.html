1. The biological problem and the jasmonate family

A caterpillar feeding on a leaf creates a problem that cannot be solved by repairing the damaged cells alone. Some tissue has been removed permanently, surviving cells near the wound are exposed to further injury, and other leaves remain vulnerable. A plant cannot escape from the animal. Its defense must therefore be organized in space and time, using chemical signals to alter the activity of cells that may never have touched the herbivore. The jasmonates are particularly important regulators of this conversion of damage into physiological preparedness.

A plant hormone is a molecule produced by the organism that changes the behavior of responsive cells at concentrations that need not make a significant contribution to their nutrition. Jasmonates are lipid-derived regulators. The word jasmonate refers to a family rather than to a single chemically identical signal. Jasmonic acid, abbreviated JA, is a cyclopentanone-containing carboxylic acid; methyl jasmonate is its methyl ester; jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine is an amide formed between JA and the amino acid L-isoleucine. The precursor 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid, or OPDA, also has biological activities. An analytical method that reports total jasmonates without resolving these chemical forms therefore cannot automatically reveal the strength of the canonical receptor signal.

The connection to lipid metabolism is essential. Membranes contain fatty acids esterified to glycerol-based lipids. These molecules help determine membrane properties, but they also provide raw material for signaling. An oxylipin is a product of the oxidation of an unsaturated fatty acid. Many oxylipins are not jasmonates: an oxidized fatty acid can proceed toward volatile aldehydes, hydroxy fatty acids or other products depending on the enzymes available. Thus a damage-induced increase in lipid oxidation is not by itself evidence that JA signaling has been activated. The specific biochemical branch must be identified.

The separation between a precursor, an active ligand and a physiological response is particularly useful when studying this hormone. Consider a leaf in which JA production increases but the enzyme required to conjugate JA to isoleucine is defective. The leaf may contain substantial free JA, while the canonical COI1–JAZ signaling output is reduced. Conversely, a leaf that contains the active ligand but lacks functional COI1 may fail to activate target genes. These outcomes are not contradictory; they represent lesions at different levels in the causal chain. The same distinction underlies the interpretation of hormone-rescue experiments throughout plant physiology.

Jasmonate signaling also occurs during ordinary development. In Arabidopsis, appropriate hormone signaling contributes to male reproductive competence, whereas activated signaling in seedlings often restricts root elongation. The capacity of one chemical family to produce such different responses depends on the developmental identity of the responding cells, which transcription factors are present and which target genes can be expressed. A hormone does not carry a complete instruction specifying a universal phenotype. Rather, it changes regulatory conditions under which tissue-specific information is interpreted.

Historically, methyl jasmonate was identified in jasmine fragrance, and subsequent work on wound-induced proteinase inhibitors connected jasmonates to plant defense. The decisive modern advance was to explain the biochemical intermediate between hormone accumulation and gene expression. Genetics, hormone-conjugation studies, protein degradation experiments and structural biology established a receptor system in which a ligand promotes the association of an ubiquitin ligase component and a transcriptional repressor. This architecture explains why loss of a repressor can produce enhanced signaling whereas loss of a receptor can produce hormone resistance.

2. Formation of OPDA in the chloroplast

The pathway begins with α-linolenic acid, an eighteen-carbon fatty acid with three double bonds, conventionally written 18:3. The fatty acid is abundant in many chloroplast membrane glycerolipids. Mobilization of suitable fatty-acid substrates allows the biosynthetic pathway to respond rapidly to injury. It is tempting to assume that damaged membranes release all required substrate simply through mechanical rupture. In living cells, however, enzymes and the availability of lipid-derived substrate are important determinants of the response. A wound removes some cells but also triggers regulated metabolism in cells that remain viable.

The first committed sequence in the familiar octadecanoid route involves a 13-lipoxygenase, abbreviated 13-LOX. Lipoxygenases insert molecular oxygen into suitable polyunsaturated fatty acids. The position of oxygen addition matters: a 13-oxygenated product is not chemically equivalent to one oxygenated at carbon 9. In the canonical jasmonate pathway, α-linolenate is converted into 13-hydroperoxy-octadecatrienoic acid, usually shortened to 13-HPOT. The product still contains eighteen carbon atoms. Oxygenation changes its functional groups, not its carbon skeleton length.

The hydroperoxide is a branch point. Allene oxide synthase, or AOS, converts 13-HPOT into an unstable allene oxide. Because the intermediate can undergo competing nonenzymatic reactions, an efficient next step is required for directing carbon into the appropriate stereochemical product. Allene oxide cyclase, or AOC, catalyzes cyclization to OPDA. The five-membered ring of OPDA is retained in downstream jasmonates. LOX, AOS and AOC therefore perform distinct transformations: oxygen insertion, conversion to a reactive allene oxide and controlled ring formation. Merely listing the enzymes does not explain why they cannot substitute for one another.

The same initial lipid oxygenation may feed another pathway when hydroperoxide lyase cleaves a hydroperoxide to yield smaller volatile products. Green-leaf odors and jasmonate formation can thus share a biochemical starting point while differing in their downstream reactions and functions. A plant that releases a strong volatile odor after injury need not be accumulating a proportionally high level of JA-Ile. The branches compete for substrates in ways that depend on enzymes, tissue identity and environmental conditions. This is a concrete example of how metabolic flux differs from the abundance of an individual enzyme.

The chloroplast location of this part of the pathway is meaningful. It places the enzymes near an important pool of polyunsaturated lipids. The reactions also illustrate that chloroplasts are more than photosynthetic factories: they participate in synthesis of signaling compounds. A cell may therefore exhibit a chloroplast-dependent stress phenotype without the immediate cause being failure of photosynthetic electron transport. The distinction becomes especially important in experiments involving mutations that alter plastid membrane composition.

OPDA is a cyclopentenone oxylipin and a precursor of JA. Its electrophilic character contributes to signaling properties that need not be explained through the same COI1–JAZ ligand interaction as JA-Ile. A comparison of OPDA-treated seedlings and JA-Ile-treated seedlings can reveal overlapping and nonoverlapping transcriptional responses. However, external OPDA can also be metabolized into downstream jasmonates. To attribute an observed output specifically to OPDA, a researcher must control conversion to JA and examine the relevant receptor dependence. Precursor application alone does not distinguish direct precursor action from action of its metabolites.

3. Peroxisomal conversion and activation of jasmonic acid

OPDA formation does not complete the standard jasmonic acid biosynthetic route. The precursor must reach the peroxisome, a compartment equipped for oxidative fatty-acid metabolism. Movement between plastid and peroxisome is an obligatory aspect of the conventional pathway even though its detailed transport steps may vary among tissues. Transport-related defects can therefore reduce downstream hormone production despite intact chloroplast enzymes. Arabidopsis studies implicate transport components such as COMATOSE in precursor handling, but an examination answer should not assume that one transporter alone explains all interorganellar flux.

In the conventional OPR3-dependent pathway, OPDA reductase 3 reduces the cyclopentenone double bond of OPDA. Reduction changes the oxidation state of the ring system and produces an intermediate suitable for subsequent side-chain processing. The reaction is distinct from β-oxidation. The reduced compound retains its original eighteen carbons until shortening takes place. Acyl-CoA activation permits the side chain to enter a cycle of reactions that removes two carbon atoms per round as acetyl-CoA. Three rounds remove six carbons in total and convert an eighteen-carbon precursor skeleton into the twelve-carbon skeleton of JA. The count is a useful way to remember the sequence without treating individual carbon atoms as arbitrary labels.

β-Oxidation is named for oxidation involving the carbon β to the carboxyl group of an acyl chain. It comprises a sequence of transformations rather than a single bond cleavage. Acyl-CoA oxidase initiates oxidation; hydration and dehydrogenation steps prepare the β-ketoacyl intermediate; thiolase releases an acetyl-CoA unit and a shortened acyl-CoA. A related logic operates in other aspects of lipid metabolism, but in this case the biological product is a hormone precursor rather than simply fuel for the cell. The cyclopentanone-derived ring is not destroyed during the standard side-chain shortening sequence.

The conventional route is a sound starting model, not an assertion that every JA molecule in every plant requires exactly the same sequence of enzymes. Work in Arabidopsis has demonstrated an OPR3-independent route involving an alternative order of side-chain processing and reduction. The examination consequence is that an OPR3 mutation may not abolish all JA production under all conditions. A null phenotype has to be demonstrated, not inferred exclusively from the name of the enzyme. Similarly, plants can possess additional fatty-acid precursors that contribute to OPDA-related compounds.

JA subsequently becomes available for conjugation with L-isoleucine. JASMONATE RESISTANT 1, or JAR1, belongs to the GH3 family of acyl acid amido synthetases. ATP-dependent activation of the carboxyl group makes formation of the amide bond possible. The product is JA-Ile; the naturally active stereoisomer is particularly effective at promoting COI1–JAZ association. Students familiar with auxin conjugation sometimes assume that linking a hormone to an amino acid always inactivates it. JAR1 illustrates why such a rule fails: conjugation here produces a major receptor-active form. Whether a conjugation activates or removes a hormone depends on the chemistry and receptor specificity of the particular system.

A simple carbon calculation distinguishes two biochemical operations. JA has a twelve-carbon skeleton, whereas isoleucine has six carbons. Joining the molecules by amide formation gives a conjugate with eighteen carbons, but this does not regenerate OPDA. The additional carbons are supplied by a separate amino acid, and the bonding arrangement differs fundamentally. Carbon counting is informative only when combined with knowledge of molecular structure. An eighteen-carbon OPDA molecule and an eighteen-carbon JA-Ile molecule cannot be interchanged simply because their carbon counts are identical.

Staswick and Tiryaki's 2004 study connected the JAR1 gene to JA conjugation. The investigators compared mutant and wild-type plants and examined biochemical activity and complementation. Restoration of JAR1 function linked increased JA-Ile production to restored responsiveness. The significance of this combination is that genetics alone would locate JAR1 somewhere in the response network, whereas biochemical evidence identifies a catalytic activity. Neither result alone is as informative as the combined causal argument.

4. Hormone turnover, storage and signaling competence

A wound response must eventually subside if the plant is to resume ordinary growth. Hormone production is therefore only half of jasmonate homeostasis. The other half is conversion into less active forms, reversal of selected conjugates, export or redistribution and restoration of signaling repression. These processes can act simultaneously. Even when the measured amount of JA-Ile is rising, some newly produced JA-Ile may already be undergoing catabolism. Its accumulation reflects the difference between the rates of addition and removal.

Members of the CYP94 cytochrome P450 family contribute to oxidative modification of JA-Ile. Hydroxylation can produce 12-hydroxy-JA-Ile, and further oxidation can generate a carboxylated derivative. These conversions generally attenuate the canonical ligand activity, although biological activity of particular metabolites and contexts should not be dismissed categorically. Amidohydrolases including IAR3 and ILL6 can remove the amino acid from appropriate jasmonate conjugates. Other enzymes modify free JA or hydroxy-JA derivatives. The important general distinction is between changing the active ligand itself and changing the amount of precursor available to make it.

Methyl jasmonate, or MeJA, differs from JA by esterification of the carboxyl group. Its relatively volatile character allows it to participate in airborne communication in some experimental and ecological situations. MeJA can also be metabolized after entering a tissue. Thus an experiment in which MeJA treatment changes root growth does not prove that MeJA directly binds the canonical receptor without conversion. Methylation may change transport and availability rather than serving as a universal irreversible degradation reaction. Glycosylated or other conjugated jasmonate derivatives likewise cannot all be grouped into one readily mobilized storage pool.

For a small tissue volume, let C denote the concentration of active JA-Ile in nanomoles per gram fresh mass. Under a simplified model in which production is constant at rate P and loss is proportional to C with constant k, dC/dt = P − kC. P has units of nanomoles per gram per minute and k has units of inverse minutes. This equation ignores changes in tissue mass, spatial gradients and nonlinear feedback. Nevertheless, it provides useful intuition: a higher steady-state amount may result either from greater production or from slower removal, because at steady state C = P/k.

Suppose an illustrative tissue has P = 0.8 nmol g⁻¹ min⁻¹ and k = 0.2 min⁻¹. Its model steady-state concentration is 4 nmol g⁻¹. Halving k without changing production gives 8 nmol g⁻¹. The doubled concentration has not established that synthesis increased. If production stops and the loss remains first order, the half-life is ln(2)/k, or about 3.47 minutes under the original model. This is hypothetical arithmetic, not a measurement of a universal jasmonate half-life. Real plants alter synthesis, removal, transport and response sensitivity dynamically.

An appropriate experiment would separately measure free JA, JA-Ile and selected catabolites over time, using chemical standards and matched tissues. A single concentration measurement cannot distinguish production and turnover. Isotope-labeled precursor can help trace conversion, but label recovery depends on uptake, pool dilution and metabolism. A genetic lesion in a catabolic enzyme may raise hormone concentration while feedback adjusts biosynthetic activity. A convincing interpretation therefore combines the chemical profile with gene expression and a physiological response, rather than assuming concentration is an infallible proxy for signaling.

5. Perception and the COI1–JAZ switch

Plant cells do not respond to JA-Ile merely because it exists in the cytoplasm. The ligand must encounter competent signaling machinery. The best-established canonical system includes CORONATINE INSENSITIVE 1 (COI1), a member of the F-box protein family. An F-box protein helps identify a particular substrate for an SCF ubiquitin ligase complex. SCF takes its name from SKP1, CULLIN and the F-box protein. These proteins form a system that brings the substrate into proximity with ubiquitin-transfer machinery. In jasmonate signaling, the critical substrates are JASMONATE ZIM-DOMAIN proteins, abbreviated JAZ.

At low ligand availability, JAZ proteins bind transcription factors such as MYC2 and restrict their ability to drive target gene expression. In representative Arabidopsis contexts, JAZ can recruit corepressors, including through the adaptor NINJA and TOPLESS. This does not mean every JAZ protein must recruit identical partners in every cell. Their common regulatory logic is to maintain repression of selected transcriptional outputs when an appropriate activating signal is absent. A high abundance of MYC2 protein alone therefore need not result in strong expression of defense genes.

An increase in the effective concentration of JA-Ile promotes interaction between COI1 and a suitable JAZ degron, a region of the repressor involved in recognition by the degradation machinery. The ligand stabilizes a productive protein-protein association. A structural study by Sheard and colleagues in 2010 established how COI1 and a JAZ segment cooperate in ligand recognition, with an inositol phosphate contributing to the receptor complex. The important conclusion at NET level is that JA-Ile perception involves a co-receptor system: it is misleading to imagine a simple receptor that becomes a transcription factor after binding the ligand.

SCF-associated ubiquitination marks appropriate JAZ proteins for turnover by the 26S proteasome. The proteasome is the molecular machine that degrades the tagged protein; COI1 does not itself digest JAZ. MYC proteins are released from JAZ-dependent inhibition and can promote transcription of selected target genes. Some targets encode defense functions, others participate in hormone biosynthesis or feedback. MYC2, MYC3 and MYC4 are related transcriptional regulators in Arabidopsis; the output of a particular target gene also depends on promoter elements, other transcription factors and cell identity.

The direction of regulation is the major conceptual challenge. JA-Ile promotes removal of a negative regulator, so activation of the pathway involves a decrease in the abundance of a repressor. It is not necessary for the ligand to directly turn on RNA polymerase. Consider a stabilized JAZ repressor that can still bind MYC but cannot interact productively with COI1. Increasing hormone availability would not necessarily restore transcription because the inhibitory protein persists. Conversely, reducing JAZ function may increase some target outputs even when the endogenous ligand level is low. Because multiple JAZ proteins can overlap in function, a single loss-of-function mutation need not produce a dramatic constitutive phenotype.

The 2007 study by Thines and colleagues provided evidence for hormone-dependent association and degradation of JAZ. Its significance is the distinction between two models: hormone-mediated activation of an existing transcription factor versus hormone-mediated removal of a protein that restrains it. A hormone-dependent decrease in JAZ protein, blocked when the required ligase pathway is defective, is evidence for regulated proteolysis. Measuring JAZ messenger RNA would not by itself establish this mechanism. Transcript abundance and protein abundance can change in opposite directions because degradation and synthesis are independently regulated.

Feedback helps prevent unrestrained signaling. Among the genes induced during the JA response are genes encoding JAZ repressors and components of hormone metabolism. Initially, existing JAZ proteins can be degraded faster than new repressors accumulate. Later, as ligand concentration falls or the rate of synthesis changes, newly produced repressors help restore inhibition. This creates a time-dependent response even without a permanent change in the DNA sequence. A time course that shows an early decline in JAZ protein and a later increase in JAZ transcript is therefore mechanistically coherent rather than paradoxical.

6. From a local wound to a distant response

A wound interrupts cell walls and plasma membranes, changes extracellular chemical conditions and can release cellular contents. Surviving cells can detect these changes through multiple signaling mechanisms. A damage-associated signal need not itself be JA-Ile. Changes in ion movement, membrane voltage and cytosolic calcium can precede jasmonate production. In addition, compounds associated with herbivore feeding can modify the response relative to mechanical injury alone. The distinction between damage detection and jasmonate perception is fundamental: a molecule that initiates hormone synthesis is not necessarily the ligand recognized by COI1.

A distant leaf may increase jasmonate biosynthesis after damage to another leaf. At least three general mechanisms could account for this observation. First, the active hormone or a precursor may move from damaged tissue to receiving cells. Second, a rapidly propagating signal may trigger synthesis in the receiving tissue. Third, both routes may contribute, potentially with different importance at different times. Measuring increased JA-Ile in a distant leaf does not distinguish these alternatives. The crucial question is the origin of the molecules measured, not merely their eventual location.

Electrical changes and calcium signaling participate in rapid long-distance wound communication. Toyota and colleagues in 2018 used calcium imaging and Arabidopsis genetic evidence to connect glutamate released after damage with GLUTAMATE RECEPTOR-LIKE channels and calcium-associated defense signaling. The calcium response is not a nervous system in the animal sense. Plant cells are electrically excitable in particular contexts and can transmit information through their connected tissues, but do not possess neurons or synapses analogous to the vertebrate nervous system. A rapid increase in cytosolic calcium in a distal tissue can regulate enzymes and transcriptional responses there without requiring every molecule of JA-Ile to travel the same distance.

The speed of the signal helps discriminate hypotheses. A hormone accumulation detected after several minutes might be consistent with movement, new synthesis or both. If an electrical response reaches the distant tissue before the hormone rises, the electrical event is a candidate upstream component, not proof that it is sufficient. To test sufficiency, researchers can activate the proposed upstream process in an unwounded tissue and examine hormone production. To test necessity, they can disrupt a relevant component and ask whether the distal response is reduced while local damage remains comparable.

Grafting provides another form of causal separation. Imagine a graft in which the damaged source and undamaged receiver have independently controlled jasmonate biosynthetic capacities. If a biosynthesis-defective receiver can still mount the response when attached to a normal source, a mobile product or another route that bypasses the receiver's missing step becomes plausible. If the receiver must retain biosynthesis for a normal response, local synthesis in that receiving tissue is supported. However, graft junctions can affect transport and physiology; reciprocal grafts, matched genotypes, appropriate controls and chemical measurements are needed. A response at the receiving leaf does not identify a particular transported chemical without direct tracing.

Methyl jasmonate and other volatile compounds add another route for signaling among tissues and neighboring plants. An enclosed treatment may exaggerate airborne concentrations compared with open field conditions. In a greenhouse experiment, an untreated plant placed near a wounded plant may receive airborne compounds, altered humidity or changes in other aspects of the environment. Separation of these effects requires airflow controls and chemical analysis. The capacity of a volatile signal to affect another plant should not be described as purposeful warning or conscious communication. It is a physiological consequence of release and reception in an ecological context.

7. Herbivore defense and reproductive functions

Jasmonate-dependent defenses can act directly on the feeding animal or indirectly by changing interactions with other organisms. Direct defenses include proteins or metabolites that reduce food quality, interfere with digestion or discourage feeding. Indirect defenses can include the emission of volatile compounds that attract natural enemies of herbivores. Neither type is uniformly beneficial under all conditions. A volatile may attract a predator in one ecological setting and a herbivore in another. The actual consequence depends on the responding species and the surrounding community.

Proteinase inhibitors illustrate a molecular link between gene activation and physiological defense. Herbivorous insects digest dietary proteins with proteolytic enzymes in the gut. Certain plant inhibitors bind digestive proteases and reduce their catalytic availability. A larva may therefore obtain less usable amino acid from a given amount of leaf tissue, altering growth or feeding behavior. The plant inhibitor is not primarily intended to digest the insect. Rather, it interferes with the insect's own digestive processes. Some insects compensate by expressing alternative proteases, increasing enzyme production or adjusting feeding. Such compensation explains why the presence of a proteinase inhibitor does not guarantee complete resistance.

Specialized metabolites form a second set of outputs. Jasmonate signaling can regulate biosynthetic genes for compounds including particular alkaloids, terpenoids and glucosinolates. A metabolite is not a jasmonate merely because its production is regulated by the hormone. For example, a glucosinolate contains a nitrogen- and sulfur-bearing structure distinct from the lipid-derived JA backbone. MYC-dependent transcriptional regulation can connect the hormone pathway to biosynthetic genes without converting JA carbon directly into every defense product. This distinction separates regulatory signaling from metabolic substrate flow.

A plant with a defect in COI1 may consequently show both reduced defense gene induction and greater herbivore performance. Such a phenotype is informative but requires controls. The mutant may differ in leaf size, tissue age, water content or nutritional composition even before feeding. An insect growth assay should therefore compare plants at matched developmental stages and account for the available tissue. Measuring the amount of leaf consumed as well as insect mass helps distinguish a feeding preference effect from a post-ingestive effect. A smaller insect is not alone proof that it consumed less tissue.

Jasmonate signaling is also necessary for aspects of reproductive development in several experimental plants. In Arabidopsis, mutants impaired in JA production or perception can exhibit male fertility defects involving pollen function, anther opening and stamen development. These are coordinated processes with timing requirements. A hormone-deficient flower may be rescued by supplying a suitable jasmonate at the appropriate developmental window if its downstream machinery remains competent. A receptor-defective flower may not respond. The outcome demonstrates that jasmonates have roles beyond defense and that the same signaling machinery can influence distinct developmental targets.

Species differ in reproductive responses. It is not valid to infer from male sterility in Arabidopsis that every jasmonate-insensitive mutant in every flowering plant will be male sterile. Likewise, a developmental effect at one hormone concentration need not occur at another. Jasmonates can inhibit root elongation, influence leaf senescence and alter reproductive development, but these outputs arise from tissue-specific regulatory programs. A student should learn a representative experimentally supported example rather than memorizing an unconditional list of effects.

8. Growth, defense and hormonal interactions

Defense has opportunity costs. Producing defensive enzymes and metabolites requires carbon, nitrogen, sulfur and energy. Repairing tissue also consumes resources that might otherwise contribute to new growth or reproduction. Nevertheless, the growth–defense relationship cannot be reduced to a passive shortage of nutrients. Jasmonate signaling directly regulates developmental processes and intersects with hormone pathways that control cell expansion and division. Even under nutrient-rich conditions, signaling-dependent changes in growth can occur.

Gibberellin signaling releases growth repression by promoting the removal of DELLA proteins. Jasmonate and gibberellin pathways can interact through regulatory protein networks involving JAZ, DELLA and transcription factors. The resulting phenotype depends on tissue and experimental conditions. It is more accurate to say that the two pathways can influence shared growth-control networks than to assert that they always bind each other or that one invariably destroys the other. A perturbation of GA signaling might alter root growth without changing JA synthesis, while a different perturbation might affect both pathways through feedback.

Ethylene and jasmonates can cooperate in some defense contexts, particularly in responses to certain necrotrophic pathogens. EIN3/EIL1 and ERF-family transcriptional regulators help link the two signaling systems. The resulting output is not identical to the MYC-dominated herbivore-defense program. A plant may preferentially activate one subset of JA-responsive genes in one situation and another subset when ethylene signaling is also active. This branching helps explain why a transcript used as a marker for one defense pathway may not represent every JA-dependent response.

Salicylic acid, abbreviated SA, is important in defense against many pathogens that exploit living host tissue. Jasmonate-dependent responses are frequently important against chewing herbivores and many pathogens that kill host cells. These associations form a useful introductory framework, not a rigid classification rule. SA and JA can antagonize each other's transcriptional outputs in specific contexts, but outcomes depend on concentration, timing, pathogen lifestyle, developmental stage and additional hormones. A plant confronting simultaneous threats may express a mixed response; the presence of SA does not prove that all JA synthesis has stopped.

A factorial experiment helps distinguish interaction from separate effects. Imagine measuring a marker transcript in four groups: solvent control, JA treatment, SA treatment and JA plus SA. If the control has relative expression 1, JA has 10, SA has 1 and JA plus SA has 4, the combined treatment suppresses part of the JA-associated increase under these illustrative conditions. It does not prove that SA acts at the receptor, because suppression could occur at the promoter, transcription factor, ligand turnover or other levels. A chemical measurement of JA-Ile and a JAZ degradation assay would help localize the interaction.

A reasonable evolutionary interpretation is that an inducible defense allows the plant to avoid some costs of constitutive protection in environments where attack is intermittent. However, permanently low defense can be costly in an environment with frequent herbivory. The best allocation depends on expected attack, recovery, resource availability and reproductive schedule. Selection acts on performance across environments, not on the isolated objective of achieving maximum seedling length. Consequently, a line with longer roots on a sterile growth medium is not necessarily more fit under field conditions.

9. Experimental reasoning and quantitative interpretation

The most efficient way to solve many NET Part C problems is to divide the pathway into production, activation, perception, repressor turnover and physiological response. An upstream biosynthetic mutation often lowers the product of a reaction and may increase its substrate if other routes do not consume it. Supplying an appropriate downstream product can bypass the missing enzymatic step. In contrast, a mutation that prevents receptor function cannot generally be rescued by increasing the ligand concentration. A stable downstream repressor may likewise block activation despite abundant ligand. These are conditional predictions, not guarantees that hormone applied to any tissue reaches its molecular target.

For example, assume a hypothetical AOS-null plant has a severely reduced canonical OPDA-to-JA pathway, intact JAR1 and functional COI1. Direct application of bioactive JA-Ile should bypass both the missing upstream biosynthetic reaction and the need for conjugation, provided it reaches competent cells. Free JA would also be expected to help if the plant can convert enough of it into JA-Ile. Now consider a JAR1-defective line: direct JA-Ile is a more specific bypass than JA. A COI1-null line, by contrast, should remain defective in a strictly COI1-dependent marker response after either treatment. These predictions do not exclude independent activities of OPDA or other metabolites.

A separate class of questions examines the difference between messenger RNA and protein abundance. Imagine a reporter consisting of a fluorescent tag fused to JAZ. After adding JA-Ile, fluorescence declines in wild type but remains high in a COI1-defective line. One plausible explanation is receptor-dependent JAZ protein turnover. Yet fluorescence could also change because of altered expression, tag properties or general cell damage. Controls include a stable fluorescent protein expressed from the same promoter, an independent protein-abundance assay and a time course of the reporter transcript. A proteasome perturbation can provide additional evidence, though inhibitors may have broad effects on cellular physiology.

In an illustrative isotope experiment, a researcher feeds labeled α-linolenic acid and subsequently detects label in JA and JA-Ile. This is evidence that carbon from the precursor can enter the products. It is not evidence that all endogenous JA originated from that administered pool. A low labeling fraction may reflect limited uptake or dilution by unlabeled substrates. Distinguishing metabolic flux from a pool size requires knowledge of labeling over time and the enrichment of the precursor pool. In a transport experiment, labeling the wounded source tissue and measuring isotopologues in distant tissue is more informative than measuring total JA-Ile alone.

An additional quantitative problem uses a hypothetical first-order decline. If active ligand falls from 16 to 4 nmol per gram over 20 minutes after synthesis stops, it has decreased by a factor of four, corresponding to two half-lives. The estimated half-life is therefore ten minutes. The estimated first-order constant is ln(2)/10, or approximately 0.0693 min⁻¹. If synthesis continued during that period, the inferred constant would not necessarily represent the true catabolic rate. The wording that synthesis has stopped is therefore a scientific assumption required for the calculation, not incidental background.

Finally, consider an illustrative herbivory assay in which larvae gain 8 mg on control plants and 12 mg on a mutant, starting at equivalent masses. Their mean mass gain is 50 percent higher on the mutant because (12 − 8)/8 × 100 = 50. That difference alone cannot establish statistical significance; the number of independent plants, variation among larvae and treatment allocation are needed. Nor does the result locate the defective molecular step. Measuring JA-Ile, JAZ turnover and a suitable defense output in the same genotypes can identify whether impaired hormone metabolism or response is a plausible explanation.

Misconceptions

  1. All jasmonates are equally active ligands. JA-Ile is the major canonical ligand; JA, OPDA and MeJA have different biochemical fates.
  2. JA biosynthesis occurs exclusively in the chloroplast. OPDA formation is plastid-associated; canonical conversion involves the peroxisome.
  3. LOX shortens the carbon chain. 13-LOX oxygenates the fatty acid; β-oxidation later shortens the chain.
  4. AOS and AOC perform the same reaction. AOS makes an unstable allene oxide; AOC catalyzes cyclization.
  5. OPR3 performs all three β-oxidation rounds. OPR3 reduces the precursor; separate β-oxidation enzymes shorten the side chain.
  6. Conjugation always inactivates a hormone. JAR1-mediated conjugation activates a major jasmonate signaling form.
  7. COI1 is the protease that digests JAZ. COI1 selects substrates in the SCF ligase; the proteasome degrades tagged JAZ.
  8. JAZ activates MYC at rest. JAZ normally restrains MYC-dependent transcription.
  9. More JA-Ile always gives proportionally more target expression. Receptor competence, repression, saturation and feedback modify response.
  10. Increased JAZ mRNA proves stronger repression immediately. Existing JAZ protein can be degraded while new transcripts accumulate.
  11. All distal JA-Ile must have traveled from a wound. Rapid systemic signals can stimulate synthesis in distant tissue.
  12. Calcium waves prove plants have neurons. Plant ion signaling uses plant cellular and vascular systems, not animal neurons.
  13. Proteinase inhibitors digest herbivores. They reduce the activity of relevant digestive proteases.
  14. SA invariably blocks JA biosynthesis. Cross-talk is context dependent and may act at several regulatory levels.
  15. Jasmonates only function during stress. They also contribute to normal reproductive and developmental functions.

Original examination practice

  1. Part B — Which precursor has eighteen carbons and three double bonds?
    Answer and explanation

    α-Linolenic acid. It supplies the standard octadecanoid pathway; isoleucine supplies the amino acid in the final conjugate.

  2. Part B — What reaction does 13-LOX catalyze?
    Answer and explanation

    Fatty-acid oxygenation. The hydroperoxide remains an eighteen-carbon compound; chain shortening occurs later.

  3. Part B — Which enzyme forms OPDA by cyclization?
    Answer and explanation

    AOC. AOS produces the unstable allene oxide, whereas AOC controls the ring-forming reaction.

  4. Part B — In which organelle does canonical OPDA reduction and side-chain shortening occur?
    Answer and explanation

    Peroxisome. The chloroplast makes OPDA; the peroxisome carries out OPR3-dependent conversion and β-oxidation.

  5. Part B — What is the usual active amino-acid conjugate of JA?
    Answer and explanation

    JA-Ile. JAR1 attaches L-isoleucine to JA; methyl jasmonate is an ester, not this amide.

  6. Part B — What type of protein is COI1?
    Answer and explanation

    F-box protein. It contributes substrate recognition to the SCF ubiquitin ligase; it is not a DNA-binding MYC factor.

  7. Part B — What directly removes ubiquitinated JAZ?
    Answer and explanation

    26S proteasome. The SCF complex attaches ubiquitin; the proteasome performs degradation.

  8. Part B — What is the role of JAZ at low ligand abundance?
    Answer and explanation

    Transcriptional repression. JAZ restrains factors including MYC; it is not the active jasmonate biosynthetic enzyme.

  9. Part B — What enzyme family oxidizes JA-Ile during turnover?
    Answer and explanation

    CYP94 family. Its oxidative products contribute to ligand attenuation, not initial fatty-acid oxygenation.

  10. Part B — Why can a proteinase inhibitor reduce caterpillar growth?
    Answer and explanation

    It reduces digestive protease activity. Reduced digestion can lower amino-acid availability; effects vary among herbivores.

  11. Part C — A plant lacks AOS but retains JAR1 and COI1. Predict the effect of exogenous JA-Ile on a canonical marker.
    Answer and explanation

    Rescue is expected if the ligand reaches competent cells. JA-Ile bypasses the upstream synthesis block; a receptor lesion would not be bypassed in this manner.

  12. Part C — A mutant accumulates JA but shows low JA-Ile and responds to supplied JA-Ile. Which pathway step is implicated?
    Answer and explanation

    JA activation/conjugation. The downstream response is competent; the observed precursor and product profile suggests defective conversion, subject to turnover controls.

  13. Part C — JAZ reporter protein remains stable after JA-Ile in a COI1-null line. Explain.
    Answer and explanation

    Ligand-dependent recruitment of JAZ to SCF is disrupted. Hormone concentration does not replace the missing recognition component.

  14. Part C — A JAZ mutant retains MYC binding but loses recognition by COI1. Predict a canonical target response.
    Answer and explanation

    Reduced activation despite increased ligand. The persistent negative regulator continues restraining the transcription factor.

  15. Part C — In a simple first-order model, active ligand falls from 16 to 4 units in 20 minutes. What is its half-life?
    Answer and explanation

    10 minutes. A fourfold decline equals two successive halvings; the result assumes no continued synthesis.

  16. Part C — If JA-Ile concentration doubles after loss of a catabolic enzyme, does this prove synthesis doubled?
    Answer and explanation

    No. The pool can grow because removal slows even if production is unchanged.

  17. Part C — Distal calcium increases before local distal JA-Ile. Does this establish that JA-Ile moved from the wound?
    Answer and explanation

    No. The calcium signal may initiate synthesis in receiving cells; isotopic or genetic tests are required to locate the source.

  18. Part C — Relative marker expression is 1 in control, 10 with JA, 1 with SA and 4 with both. What can be concluded?
    Answer and explanation

    Partial suppression of the JA-associated expression under these conditions. The data do not identify whether suppression involves ligand metabolism, perception or transcription.

  19. Part C — Larvae gain 8 mg on wild type and 12 mg on a mutant. Calculate the relative increase.
    Answer and explanation

    50%. The increase is 4 mg divided by 8 mg, multiplied by 100; replication is needed for significance.

  20. Part C — A jar1 line responds to supplied JA-Ile but a coi1 line does not. What does this show?
    Answer and explanation

    Conjugation precedes COI1-dependent perception. Direct ligand bypasses a conjugation block but cannot replace the functional receptor.

Revision and recall

Jasmonate signaling connects plastid fatty-acid oxidation, peroxisomal metabolism, ligand conjugation, regulated proteolysis and tissue-specific transcription. Its regulation is as important as its activation.

  1. Trace α-linolenic acid to OPDA.
  2. Identify the compartment and carbon change in JA formation.
  3. Explain why JA-Ile is not simply a storage conjugate.
  4. Explain the low-ligand JAZ state.
  5. Describe SCF-mediated JAZ turnover.
  6. Distinguish hormone movement from distal synthesis.
  7. Predict rescue of an AOS mutant.
  8. Explain two possible mechanisms of ligand attenuation.
  9. Describe why proteinase inhibitors may not deter all insects.
  10. Explain context dependence of SA–JA interaction.
