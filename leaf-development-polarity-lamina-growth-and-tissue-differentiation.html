1. A small primordium becomes an organized organ

A mature foliage leaf presents several different biological achievements at once. Its blade spreads across a large surface, its base attaches firmly to the shoot, and its vascular tissue connects this surface to the rest of the plant. The exposed epidermis forms a protective interface while internal living cells are arranged to exchange gases and receive light. None of these arrangements is present in its mature form when the leaf first appears. A leaf begins as a small group of developing cells on the side of a shoot apical meristem. The central problem is to understand how an initially inconspicuous outgrowth becomes a coordinated organ rather than an arbitrary mass of growing cells.

A leaf primordium is the developing precursor of a leaf. The term does not imply that all of its cells have already acquired their final identities. A primordium may contain precursors of several tissues whose subsequent divisions, enlargement and differentiation occur over an extended period. A mature leaf is therefore not simply an enlarged version of its youngest primordium. Its shape and internal structure emerge from changes in cell behaviour, and these changes are spatially organized. Some groups of cells contribute primarily to the length of the leaf, others to its width or thickness, and still others build the specialized tissues within it.

Positioning a primordium on the shoot and patterning that primordium are related but distinct problems. Local auxin transport and response help specify the site of organ initiation at the peripheral zone of a shoot apical meristem. Once the primordium is established, cells must distinguish its different regions and grow appropriately within them. One can imagine a plant making primordia at approximately normal intervals but producing leaves with almost no blade. This would indicate that the mechanisms placing leaves on the shoot are more intact than the mechanisms making the blade. Conversely, irregular organ positioning need not automatically mean that the molecular identities of upper and lower leaf tissues are reversed.

A characteristic feature of many leaves is determinate growth. This means that the organ eventually ceases to enlarge substantially rather than continuously producing new regions like an indeterminate vegetative shoot meristem. Determinate does not mean that cells stop dividing as soon as the primordium is formed. The leaf requires a period of proliferative development before its tissues attain mature dimensions. Nor does determinate imply that every plant leaf grows for an identical period. In grasses, for example, active growth may remain localized near the leaf base long after the tip differentiates. Prolonged growth at a specific location is compatible with eventual maturation of the individual leaf.

The cell wall makes this developmental problem different from the construction of a freely rearranging animal cell population. Adjacent plant cells remain mechanically connected through walls and cannot normally exchange their positions by migration. When a plant tissue changes its geometry, it must do so through oriented divisions and coordinated changes in cell size and shape, together with controlled wall modification. A new cell wall partitions an existing cell, whereas enlargement increases the space occupied by the tissue. A mechanism that changes where divisions occur can therefore modify leaf anatomy without necessarily making the leaf larger immediately.

The concepts of specification and differentiation introduced earlier in developmental biology apply directly here. A cell can acquire a regional identity before it expresses the anatomical characteristics by which that identity will later be recognized. Thus an adaxial precursor need not yet resemble a mature palisade mesophyll cell. Specification refers to a developmental trajectory, while differentiation involves the acquisition of specialized properties. A leaf can be studied at early stages with molecular markers and later stages using sections and tissue anatomy. These complementary observations are essential when interpreting mutant phenotypes.

The transition from a meristem to a leaf

An incipient primordium is often called P0, while P1 denotes the youngest recognizable outgrowth and P2 a slightly older one. These labels describe relative position in a developmental sequence rather than an exact chronological age. A genotype that changes the interval between successive initiations may change the chronological age corresponding to each rank. Consequently, experimental comparisons should use developmental landmarks as well as elapsed time. An abnormality appearing in P2 but not P1 could reflect either a stage-specific requirement for a gene or a difference in developmental rates between samples. Repeated observations of identifiable primordia can help distinguish those alternatives.

A new leaf has to establish a boundary with the meristem without losing the connection to the shoot that will support its development. The growing organ must acquire a distinct program of gene expression, while its attachment region remains continuous with the shoot. In Arabidopsis, class I KNOX activity is characteristic of the meristem, and several members of this family are normally repressed during leaf development. ASYMMETRIC LEAVES1 participates in this developmental distinction. Byrne and colleagues (2000) connected the as1 phenotype to abnormal expression of meristem-related regulators. Subsequent work by Guo and colleagues (2008) supplied evidence that an AS1-containing complex directly regulates particular KNOX loci. This is a useful example of progressing from a mutant phenotype to a more specific regulatory explanation.

A hypothetical reporter experiment can test part of this mechanism. Compare a construct containing the normal regulatory region of the KNOX gene BREVIPEDICELLUS with a construct whose relevant AS1-complex binding sites have been mutated. If the altered construct is inappropriately expressed in developing leaves, the result supports the importance of those sequences for repression. However, promoter mutations may affect more than one interaction, and the expression result alone does not demonstrate direct binding. An independent binding assay or occupancy measurement can help establish that regulatory connection. The gene-specific result should not be generalized to every member of the KNOX family without evidence.

The regulation must remain effective as the primordium grows. If meristem-associated genes are temporarily repressed but become active immediately in descendant cells, the distinction would not remain stable. The action of regulatory complexes and associated chromatin mechanisms illustrates how a transcriptional state may be maintained without changing the underlying genome. It also distinguishes a developmental commitment from the immediate presence of mature tissue characteristics. Cells can be committed to participating in a leaf before they acquire the morphology of epidermal, ground or vascular tissues.

Cell layers and the early origin of leaf tissues

The relation between the shoot meristem and the young leaf can also be described in terms of histogenic layers. In the standard Arabidopsis shoot apex, the outer layer L1 is maintained mainly through divisions that preserve a continuous surface layer. L2 lies immediately below it, while L3 denotes the inner tissue. The descendants of these layers contribute to the developing organs. The outer layer normally supplies epidermis, and underlying cells supply internal tissues. This organization helps explain how the expanding leaf establishes a surface surrounding a volume of tissue without requiring cells from throughout the primordium to migrate to the surface.

These layer labels describe a position and a lineage relationship at the apex; they are not alternative names for the adaxial and abaxial domains. Both surfaces of the developing leaf have epidermal cells. A surface lineage may contribute to epidermis on the adaxial side and the abaxial side, depending on the region of the new organ. The internal tissue similarly includes cells at different positions along the thickness axis. A student who equates L1 with adaxial tissue and L2 with abaxial tissue would therefore make an incorrect prediction about a transverse section. The histogenic origin and the eventual positional identity of the cell must be described separately.

Division orientation has a role in maintaining the topology of a tissue. In a surface layer, an anticlinal division is oriented so that the new wall is approximately perpendicular to the local surface. It produces daughters that both remain in the surface layer. A periclinal division forms a wall approximately parallel to that surface and places daughters at different depths. Such a division can contribute to an increase in the number of tissue layers. These are geometrical descriptions of division planes and should not be interpreted as automatic assignments of fate. The newly formed daughter cells may still acquire distinct identities in response to their positions and regulatory states.

An examination problem could depict a young leaf in which the surface remains continuous but the internal region has fewer cell layers than normal. A plausible hypothesis is that internal formative divisions or subsequent growth were altered. This is different from a defect in the maintenance of the outer surface. It is not possible to determine the precise causal gene from such a section, but it is possible to decide which further observations are needed. Labeling dividing cells and comparing the orientation and frequency of divisions in equivalent regions can provide evidence about the source of the difference. An early section and a mature section together would distinguish a defect in layer formation from a later loss or compression of tissue.

Lineage tracing provides a way to determine the descendants of particular precursor cells. If a small group of early cells is permanently marked, their marked descendants reveal how that region contributes to the later organ. A lineage marker must remain detectable through cell division without changing the development of the cells being traced. Its distribution does not directly reveal when a cell became specified, because descendants of one progenitor may acquire different identities. By combining a lineage marker with a reporter of a developmental state, one can investigate how position and lineage contribute to differentiation. The distinction between the two kinds of marker is essential in a plant organ in which cells normally remain closely associated with their neighbors.

2. Three axes provide the positional framework

To understand a flattened organ, it is necessary to describe its geometry using more than the familiar terms top and bottom. The first axis is adaxial to abaxial. In a developing leaf, adaxial denotes the side oriented toward the shoot axis or meristem, and abaxial denotes the opposite side. In an ordinary horizontally displayed dorsiventral leaf, the adaxial side commonly becomes the upper surface. However, upper and lower in the everyday sense describe orientation relative to the observer or gravity, whereas adaxial and abaxial describe developmental relationships. A leaf may twist, hang vertically or become inverted without changing the positional identities established when it was young.

The proximal–distal axis runs from the attachment region toward the distal tip. The proximal part is closer to the point at which the leaf joins the shoot. A petiole, where present, is proximal to most of the blade. A sessile leaf does not lack a proximal–distal axis merely because it has no obvious stalk. The distinction remains meaningful in a grass leaf, where the basal sheath and the blade form differently organized parts of the same organ. Regional information along this axis helps determine the distribution of proliferating tissue and the positions of transitions between leaf parts.

The mediolateral axis describes the direction from the central region, commonly near the midvein, toward the margins. Expansion in this direction is fundamental to building a lamina, the expanded blade of a leaf. Consider a long but very narrow leaf. It may have achieved substantial proximal–distal extension while making relatively little lateral tissue. Conversely, a broad short leaf has a different balance of growth along these two axes. Length, width and thickness must therefore be considered separately when comparing developmental phenotypes.

These three axes are not three successive switches that activate in isolation. Regional identities interact as a young organ grows. The establishment of distinct adaxial and abaxial territories is linked to the ability of the primordium to expand sideways, and the duration of growth in different proximal–distal regions affects the final outline of the blade. A useful mental model is a three-dimensional volume of tissue in which each cell has coordinates relative to the rest of the organ. These coordinates are changing as cells divide and expand, but positional information can be maintained through regulatory interactions and communication between nearby cells.

Anatomical evidence allows the observer to recognize the axes after they have become established. In a typical dorsiventral eudicot leaf, elongate palisade mesophyll cells are prominent toward the adaxial surface, while the mesophyll closer to the abaxial surface commonly contains more intercellular space. Within a collateral vascular bundle, xylem usually lies on the adaxial side relative to phloem. Neither criterion should be treated as a universal anatomical law for every species, but together with developmental position they provide useful independent markers in a model leaf. A transverse section therefore carries information about both the thickness axis and the mediolateral organization of the organ.

Suppose two mutant leaves are equally narrow when observed from above. One is flat and contains both typical surfaces; the other is cylindrical and displays abaxial features around its circumference. An external measurement of width alone would make them appear equivalent. A transverse section reveals that they are fundamentally different. The flat leaf may have a defect in lateral proliferation, while the cylindrical leaf may have lost the spatial distinction necessary for normal lamina development. This is why the direction of change matters more than the generic description of a plant as having small leaves.

Initial positional asymmetry and experimental interpretation

The flank of the shoot apex is an asymmetrical environment even before the new organ forms a recognizable blade. The prospective adaxial side lies nearer the central region of the apex while the prospective abaxial side lies toward the periphery. Classical separation experiments were interpreted as evidence for an influence of the meristem on polarity. Modern regulatory models also consider spatial patterns already present around the shoot apex and interactions that maintain polarity after initiation. These explanations need not exclude one another: a pre-existing pattern may guide an initial distinction that is subsequently reinforced locally. The evidence does not justify assigning every aspect of leaf polarity to a single identified mobile substance.

Suppose that a fluorescent marker for an adaxial regulator is initially distributed across a broad region but later becomes restricted to one side. The signal may be lost from the opposite side through transcriptional repression, reduced RNA stability, altered protein turnover or a combination of processes. A single image of the fluorescent protein does not distinguish those mechanisms. Comparing expression from a transcriptional reporter with the distribution of a functional protein fusion, and examining a construct that differs in its microRNA recognition sequence, provides more informative tests. This distinction is important because a spatial protein pattern may emerge even if transcription itself was initially widespread.

Developmental mosaics provide a conceptual means of testing how far a polarity perturbation acts. Imagine a group of marked cells that ectopically expresses an abaxial-promoting regulator in an otherwise adaxial region. If neighboring unmarked cells retain their normal identities, the result is consistent with a predominantly cell-autonomous effect. If a change in regional marker expression extends beyond the marked cells, interactions between cells become important. Mechanical effects must also be considered: changing the dimensions of a clone can influence the shape of adjacent tissue without necessarily transferring a molecular signal. A change in shape outside the clone is not equivalent to a change in gene expression outside it. Molecular markers and anatomical observations therefore contribute different kinds of evidence.

3. How experimental evidence established the role of dorsiventrality

Classical developmental experiments asked whether a leaf develops its two surfaces entirely from information within its own cells or whether its relation to the shoot matters. Surgical separation of young leaf tissue from the apex provided evidence that this relationship is important. If a developing primordium is separated at an early enough stage, its shape and differentiation may be altered. Such an operation is more informative than removing a fully differentiated leaf because the early organ has not yet completed its developmental program. Nevertheless, the operation changes several things simultaneously: physical continuity, local chemical communication and mechanical conditions. It cannot on its own identify a specific molecular signal.

A genetic route to this problem became particularly informative in snapdragon, Antirrhinum majus. Waites and Hudson (1995) studied mutations at the PHANTASTICA locus and examined the relation between dorsiventral tissue identity and blade development. The important observation was not merely that some mutant leaves were small. Strongly affected leaves could become radial structures lacking normal adaxial differentiation. Other leaves displayed altered regional boundaries and unusual outgrowths. Such a combination of effects suggested that lateral growth was connected to the organization of different positional identities, rather than being only a consequence of the total rate of growth of the whole primordium.

Imagine examining a normal leaf section and a section of a strongly affected phantastica leaf at matched developmental stages. In the normal organ one would distinguish opposing tissue domains across the thickness of the blade. In the mutant, the ability to identify these opposing domains is compromised, and the characteristic blade geometry is also altered. The coexistence of these changes is significant because it connects a cellular pattern to an organ-level phenotype. It does not establish that PHANTASTICA is itself a mobile signal, that it is the only polarity regulator or that a single pathway accounts for every leaf shape.

Arabidopsis genetics supplied an important complementary test. The dominant phabulosa-1d mutant characterized by McConnell and Barton (1998) shows adaxial characteristics in positions where abaxial features would ordinarily develop. The direction of the identity transformation differs from that seen in strongly abaxialized phantastica leaves. Yet extensive conversion to either identity can disrupt normal blade formation. This comparison makes the causal argument stronger: if the blade merely required adaxial tissue in proportion to its amount, expanding that identity should improve blade growth. In fact, correct spatial relationships between the two identities are important.

The interpretation is especially powerful when a new blade-like outgrowth develops at an abnormal boundary between opposing tissue types. Such an outgrowth indicates that local positional arrangement can be relevant to growth even when the overall leaf is malformed. It motivates a boundary model of lamina formation, later refined by molecular studies that recognized a regulatory domain associated with lateral outgrowth. The boundary model is an explanation of a developmental requirement, not a claim that every cell at an interface will automatically form a new leaf.

The genetic evidence also illustrates the difference between a necessary function and a sufficient function. If disruption of a gene prevents the normal blade from forming, its activity may be required for that process in the tested genetic background. It does not follow that adding the gene product to any cell will produce a blade. A sufficient condition would require a separate experimental test in an appropriate context. Similarly, the absence of a phenotype in one mutant does not demonstrate that the gene has no function. Related genes may compensate, and a role may become apparent only in combinations of mutations.

Reading complementary gain- and loss-of-function evidence

The classical polarity phenotypes can be used to construct a logical comparison involving several independent perturbations. A severe reduction in an adaxial-promoting program might produce a leaf whose tissues resemble those of the normally abaxial region. In contrast, a reduction in an abaxial-promoting program might allow inappropriate adaxial characteristics. The predicted changes are directional. The two mutations do not simply reduce a quantity called polarity by the same amount; they change the relative distribution of the two regulatory states. A comparison that identifies both the presence and the spatial position of each marker is more informative than comparing their total abundance in an extract of the entire leaf.

Experiments on KANADI genes provide an independent line of evidence. Eshed and colleagues (2001) examined the consequences of altered KANADI activity in Arabidopsis lateral organs. Ectopic expression of KANADI in the developing primordium caused a strong transformation toward abaxial characteristics and compromised blade expansion. In combinations of mutations reducing KANADI function, inappropriate adaxial characteristics could appear. Together these observations support an antagonistic relationship between regional programs. They also demonstrate the value of complementary genetic perturbations: loss of an activity and expansion of the same activity may give distinct predictions about the direction of a fate change.

A critical limitation is that the organ may require several independent factors to maintain each regional state. Removing one factor from a normal domain may not immediately eliminate that domain if other regulators remain active. A strong effect of ectopic expression may similarly arise because the introduced activity overwhelms the normal balance of regulators. The comparison therefore does not establish that KANADI alone is a sufficient description of all abaxial fate or that a complete leaf could be constructed by distributing just two transcription factors across a primordium. The experiment provides evidence for an important component of a developmental program, not a complete reconstruction of the system.

The observation that a radialized organ is possible after either kind of fate transformation also guards against an easy diagnostic error. Imagine an unknown mutant with a cylindrical leaf and a collateral bundle reorganized into a concentric arrangement. The cylindrical shape alone gives no direction of the polarity transformation. The internal orientation of vascular components, when compared with the normal pattern, may help distinguish the possibilities. Molecular markers from the two opposing domains provide additional evidence. If the abnormal leaf contains adaxial characteristics throughout, it should not be diagnosed as abaxialized merely because it resembles the external shape of a phantastica mutant.

Cross-species comparisons can reveal conserved developmental principles even when the exact gene names differ. In maize, the ROLLED LEAF1 gene belongs to the HD-ZIP III regulatory class. Evidence for microRNA-mediated control of its expression supports the importance of spatial post-transcriptional regulation in a monocot as well as in eudicot models. The mature leaves of maize and Arabidopsis differ considerably in geometry and anatomy, so the comparison is not that identical mature forms are generated by identical genes. Instead, similar regulatory principles can be deployed within different developmental architectures. This distinction is important whenever an examination asks whether conservation of gene function implies conservation of organ morphology.

The different kinds of evidence can be placed in a causal sequence. An expression study establishes where a candidate factor is present. Genetic perturbation establishes whether changing its activity influences development. Comparison of marker distribution shows whether the perturbation changes a regional program. Rescue or complementary perturbations strengthen the link between factor activity and phenotype. Finally, anatomical and growth observations reveal how the regional change contributes to the mature shape. No single experiment necessarily completes every link in that sequence. The strength of the developmental explanation comes from the convergence of distinct observations.

4. Regulatory domains and the molecular basis of leaf polarity

Regional fate depends on regulation of gene expression. A transcription factor is a protein capable of influencing transcription of target genes through interaction with regulatory DNA and other proteins. The same genome is present in most developing leaf cells, yet different combinations of transcription factors can produce different patterns of gene expression. A polarity regulator does not give a cell a physical direction by pushing it toward one surface. Instead, it participates in the regulatory program characteristic of a position.

The class III homeodomain–leucine zipper proteins form an important group of adaxial-promoting regulators in Arabidopsis. PHABULOSA (PHB), PHAVOLUTA (PHV) and REVOLUTA (REV) are representative members. A homeodomain is a DNA-binding region and a leucine zipper participates in protein association. This molecular organization helps explain why the proteins can regulate transcription. Their developmental action depends not only on the amount of gene expression but also on where functional protein accumulates. A gene with expression in a restricted tissue region can therefore produce a spatial pattern of regulatory activity.

KANADI genes participate in the opposing, abaxial developmental program. Their products are transcription factors of the GARP family. The essential examination distinction is that the HD-ZIP III and KANADI regulatory activities promote contrasting regional identities and antagonize inappropriate expression of the opposing program. This arrangement helps maintain distinct domains even as the primordium grows. An organ in which KANADI activity is expanded into normally adaxial territories may lose adaxial characteristics, while failure of abaxial-promoting activity can allow adaxial traits to occur outside their usual territory. Actual phenotypes depend on the alleles, genetic redundancy and developmental stage.

MicroRNAs add a second level of control. A microRNA is a short regulatory RNA that can guide an Argonaute-containing complex to a complementary target RNA. In Arabidopsis and other model plants, miR165 and miR166 can restrict the accumulation of particular HD-ZIP III transcripts through post-transcriptional regulation. These small RNAs are associated with the abaxial side during leaf development, limiting the adaxial-promoting program there. In this way a regional pattern of regulatory RNA contributes to the pattern of transcription factor activity. Transcription of a target gene and accumulation of its protein are therefore not interchangeable measurements.

A dominant gain-of-function allele can arise if a mutation disrupts recognition of a regulatory RNA target without abolishing the transcription factor's biological activity. The target may now persist where it would normally be suppressed. This is a mechanistically different situation from deleting the coding sequence of the transcription factor. For examination problems, it is crucial to distinguish a mutation that removes function from one that permits function in an inappropriate region. In the latter case, the phenotype may involve expansion of a cell identity rather than loss of that identity. A target-resistant transgene may therefore have a different effect from expression of the normal coding region under the same promoter.

The ASYMMETRIC LEAVES1 (AS1) and ASYMMETRIC LEAVES2 (AS2) system helps stabilize the leaf developmental program. AS1 is related to the PHANTASTICA class of MYB transcription factors, whereas AS2 belongs to the LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES domain family. Together their activities contribute to repression of inappropriate meristem-related class I KNOX expression in developing Arabidopsis leaves and to appropriate polarity. The significance of these examples is that maintaining leaf identity and establishing regional identities must be coordinated. If cells in a developing leaf retain or reacquire characteristics associated with an indeterminate meristem, their pattern of growth may change substantially.

The network is not adequately described as a single gene commanding a second gene in a linear chain. It contains several partly overlapping regulatory systems, and different perturbations may affect several of them at once. The YABBY family and particular auxin response factors also participate in regional differentiation and lamina development. For a standard examination, it is more useful to understand the logic of opposed positional programs and their spatial restriction than to memorize a long list of every interacting gene. The molecular details that matter are the direction of the regulatory effect, the territory in which it occurs, and the phenotype predicted when that territory expands or disappears.

Genetic tests of polarity and transcriptional identity

Because PHB, PHV and REV belong to a related transcription factor family, the consequences of deleting one member may be less severe than simultaneously reducing several. Such a result is evidence for overlapping genetic functions, although it does not imply that the proteins are identical or expressed in the same domain. A standard genetic interpretation compares the single and combined mutants and examines which developmental traits change. A mild single-mutant phenotype cannot by itself be used to conclude that the corresponding regulator has no important role.

A microRNA-resistant transcript provides a contrasting perturbation. Because the target recognition region can occur in a protein-coding sequence, mutations may affect either RNA recognition or the resulting protein. A useful test compares constructs encoding the same protein but differing in synonymous nucleotides that influence target recognition. Under the same promoter, accumulation of the resistant product outside its usual domain would support a post-transcriptional restriction mechanism. It would not establish that transcription has increased in the affected cells. A separate transcriptional reporter or RNA measurement can help distinguish these alternatives.

The AS1 and AS2 system has more than one regulatory output. In a mutant with inappropriate KNOX expression and a disturbed polarity pattern, reducing one ectopically expressed KNOX gene might rescue some features of leaf geometry but leave other traits abnormal. Such a partial rescue would be consistent with several regulatory outputs contributing to the original phenotype. It would not demonstrate that the remaining abnormalities are unrelated to the AS1–AS2 system. Genetic suppression is especially informative when the phenotype is decomposed into multiple measurements, such as regional marker expression, length, width, vein organization and progression of differentiation.

Epistasis is often taught through simplified linear pathways, but leaf development involves spatially interacting programs. In a linear model, a downstream mutation may mask the effect of an upstream mutation. In a developing blade, a double mutant may instead show a new configuration of regional identities. The interpretation of epistasis must be tied to the trait being measured and the underlying pathway assumption. A regulator can be epistatic for one feature of leaf shape without being downstream of another regulator for every developmental process.

A functional copy introduced into a mutant can help establish whether the affected gene contributes to the phenotype. Rescue under the native regulatory pattern is especially informative because it restores activity in approximately its normal spatial and temporal setting. Strong ectopic expression may instead bypass a defect. If it restores leaf width without restoring the normal distribution of tissue identities, it demonstrates an effect on width but does not demonstrate restoration of the original polarity mechanism. A rigorous rescue experiment includes an appropriate construct control, comparison with the untransformed mutant and separate assessment of different anatomical traits.

Regulatory RNA and spatially restricted responses

The action of miR165 and miR166 is a particularly helpful example of the distinction between gene transcription and final gene activity. A target transcript is initially produced by transcription of DNA. A small RNA acting through an Argonaute-containing complex can recognize a complementary target sequence and reduce accumulation of the target RNA or its translated product. Therefore, the same transcriptional input can yield different functional protein outputs in cells where the small RNA activity differs. This gives a developing organ an additional way of creating a sharply restricted regulatory domain without requiring every component of the transcriptional machinery to be restricted to the same territory.

Kidner and Martienssen (2004) connected spatially restricted small RNA action to leaf polarity in Arabidopsis. The important implication is that the distribution of a small regulatory RNA is part of the developmental pattern, not merely a uniform background mechanism for reducing transcript abundance. A change in small RNA accumulation or action may allow the target regulator to function where it ordinarily would not. Since target proteins participate in regional identity, the resulting phenotype can extend to the geometry and anatomy of the entire organ. This links molecular RNA regulation to a visible developmental outcome.

The interpretation of a reporter requires attention to what is actually being reported. A transcriptional reporter driven by a target gene promoter but lacking its normal RNA recognition site can report the activity of the promoter. A reporter containing the target sequence can additionally be subject to post-transcriptional restriction. If the first is expressed on both sides of a primordium while the second accumulates preferentially on one side, the difference is consistent with regulation acting after transcription. Controls are needed because different constructs may also differ in expression level, stability or protein localization. An appropriately designed comparison can reveal why the transcriptional and functional domains are not always identical.

A similar caution applies to interpreting a high concentration of a regulator. The normal distribution of a factor may be as important as its total amount. An organ containing the same total amount of an adaxial-promoting factor as a wild-type leaf could still develop abnormally if that factor were distributed evenly rather than concentrated in its normal territory. Conversely, increasing the activity in the normal domain may not reproduce the consequences of introducing it into the opposing domain. The experiment must distinguish quantitative changes within a domain from changes in the boundaries or location of that domain.

One can formulate a simple conditional prediction. Suppose a small RNA normally restricts an adaxial-promoting target on the abaxial side. Reducing the small RNA's ability to recognize that target would tend to expand target activity if other regulatory processes remain unchanged. Reducing production of the target protein itself would instead tend to decrease the activity. The two perturbations can therefore produce contrasting regional effects even though both concern the same regulatory interaction. This reasoning applies broadly to developmental systems involving repression. The expected phenotype must be traced from the molecular change through the spatial expression pattern to the resulting tissue identities.

Not all regulatory responses are strictly binary. Cells near the interface may contain different levels of factors or may express additional regulators associated with the intermediate domain. The three-domain model of early leaf development is useful precisely because it does not require treating every cell as an exclusive member of just one of two homogeneous blocks. The pattern is organized into functional territories that may differ in regulatory state, growth behavior and developmental competence. A schematic representation with sharply defined colors is an aid to reasoning, not a literal map of every cell at every developmental stage.

5. From opposing domains to lateral outgrowth

The existence of adaxial and abaxial identities does not by itself explain the production of many cells across the width of a blade. A leaf needs a mechanism that converts regional organization into a spatial pattern of growth. The interface between opposing territories provides a way to establish a third regulatory region, associated with mediolateral expansion. In Arabidopsis, WUSCHEL-related homeobox factors WOX1 and PRESSED FLOWER (PRS, also known as WOX3) participate in blade outgrowth and differentiation of the middle domain. Studies of their combined genetic loss and expression patterns have helped distinguish this growth-promoting domain from either surface identity considered alone.

The term middle domain is a developmental description. It refers to a region of the developing primordium in which particular regulatory activities help coordinate outgrowth between the adaxial and abaxial territories. It does not mean that every anatomically central cell in a mature leaf expresses WOX1 or that all mesophyll cells are descendants of a single narrow strip of tissue. A developmental expression domain is defined by its location and regulatory state at the relevant time. Its descendants may later differentiate into more than one kind of cell, and the spatial pattern can change.

In a simple transverse model, the adaxial territory lies on one side of the young primordium and the abaxial territory lies on the other. Lateral growth on the two sides of the interface progressively increases width. For the organ to stay flat, growth must be coordinated across its depth as well as along its margins. If one tissue layer expands faster than a connected layer, it can impose bending stresses. Thus a change in leaf shape could be caused either by changes in the identities of regions that normally grow or by altered growth rates among tissues whose identities remain recognizable.

A comparison of a severe polarity mutant with a mutant impaired specifically in lateral outgrowth clarifies the difference. In the first, loss of regional patterning can remove the organization that normally permits blade growth. In the second, the opposing domains may still be recognizable, but the mechanisms supporting width expansion are compromised. A narrow blade is not sufficient to decide which process has failed. One needs a combination of regional molecular markers, transverse anatomy and measurements of growth. The WOX1 and PRS example illustrates why loss of one gene may produce a modest phenotype whereas combined disruption can reveal a more important shared activity.

The visible margin of the mature blade is the edge where its surfaces meet, but the mechanisms producing the margin act much earlier. Local growth around the developing margin can create teeth or lobes as some positions expand more strongly than nearby positions. Conversely, a smooth margin need not imply absence of developmental patterning. It can arise from fairly uniform growth around the edge or from a different arrangement of growth-promoting and growth-restricting activities. The shape of the mature outline is a record of integrated growth across time, not simply a photograph of the initial pattern of gene expression.

Auxin can participate in these spatial differences in growth. A local hormone response depends on hormone distribution and the ability of cells to respond, which includes the presence of relevant receptors and transcriptional regulators. A reporter for auxin response should consequently be interpreted as an output of the signaling system rather than a direct measurement of hormone concentration. The distinction is especially important when comparing mutant leaves: the same auxin concentration could produce different reporter signals if the signaling response has changed.

Local patterning of the developing margin

The production of a serrated margin requires spatial differences in growth around the edge of the leaf. If a blade simply increased in scale with an unchanged outline, it would not acquire a new repeated series of teeth. Local auxin transport and the boundary-associated factor CUP-SHAPED COTYLEDON2 (CUC2) participate in margin development in Arabidopsis. Bilsborough and colleagues (2011) presented a model in which PIN1-dependent auxin focusing interacts with CUC2 expression. In that model, CUC2 promotes conditions for localized auxin activity maxima, while auxin restricts CUC2 expression at the outgrowth sites. The result is a spatial distinction between protruding regions and intervening boundary regions.

A useful way to interpret this model is to follow a short segment of margin. A localized response maximum can favor growth near the emerging tip, while the adjacent region retains a distinct regulatory state. As the margin becomes longer, additional sites may arise. The final number of teeth depends on both the establishment of outgrowth sites and their later development. A mature leaf with fewer visible teeth may have formed fewer initiation sites, or initially similar sites may have failed to expand. A time series comparing early local response patterns with later outline changes addresses this distinction. A reporter for auxin response is not itself a direct quantitative assay of auxin concentration.

The difference between a simple serrated leaf and a compound leaf is also related to the degree and timing of local separation. A region that grows relatively slowly between two outgrowths can produce a notch, but the resulting organ may remain one continuous blade. In a compound leaf, leaflet primordia develop as distinct subdivisions of the original leaf. Similar classes of regulators can participate in both contexts without making the two structures identical. Boundary function is a developmental relationship between neighboring regions rather than a complete physical barrier to molecular transport.

Changes in the two margins of a blade can be compared by measuring the positions of outgrowths relative to the midvein and the base-to-tip axis. If the leaves differ substantially in size, a distance measured in millimeters may not be the most informative measure of position. Expressing a distance as a fraction of leaf length allows comparison of relative placement. Differences in relative position and in absolute dimensions then provide separate clues about pattern formation and growth. The same reasoning applies to the spacing of internal vein branches.

6. Growth by division and enlargement

The young leaf expands through proliferation and enlargement of its cells. Cell proliferation increases the number of cells; cell enlargement increases the volume or projected area occupied by individual cells. These processes are related but not identical. A division creates two daughter cells within the volume occupied by the original cell, so the division itself does not automatically double tissue size. The descendants must also grow, taking up water, synthesizing material and modifying their walls. This explains why the rate of cell-cycle progression alone is not a sufficient description of leaf growth.

Different regions and tissue layers do not necessarily progress through division and differentiation in synchrony. In an expanding Arabidopsis leaf, proliferative activity commonly becomes restricted toward the base as distal regions mature. This broad pattern can be described using the concept of a cell-cycle arrest front, provided one remembers that the front is an idealized description of a changing spatial distribution rather than a sharply moving anatomical wall. Donnelly and colleagues (1999) used a cyclin-associated reporter to investigate the spatial and temporal distribution of cell cycling. Their observations support the important principle that proliferative activity can differ between adjacent tissue types.

A reporter showing active expression of a cell-cycle-associated gene provides a way to detect cells in a particular stage or regulatory state. The absence of signal in a tissue at one observation time does not necessarily mean that none of its cells ever divides. Some cells may be in another phase of the cycle, and the reporter may not be equally expressed in all lineages. Comparing several developmental stages and using additional evidence, such as repeated observations of cell division or cell-number changes, makes the inference more robust. This methodological distinction avoids treating a static picture as a complete history of a tissue.

Water uptake contributes substantially to the enlargement of plant cells. Solutes influence water potential, and the difference between the water potential of a cell and its surroundings can drive water movement. Turgor pressure develops as the cell contents exert force against the wall. For persistent enlargement, however, the wall must yield or be remodeled sufficiently to allow an increase in dimensions. An increase in water content that merely stretches the wall elastically may be reversed when the water is lost. Irreversible developmental growth is different from reversible swelling or shrinkage.

Growth may be anisotropic, meaning that it proceeds more strongly in one direction than another. The orientation of cellulose microfibrils and the mechanical properties of walls influence the directions in which cells can expand under turgor. A developing blade can become wider through changes in both the number and shape of cells arranged across the mediolateral axis. It may also become longer without an equivalent increase in width. A mutation affecting wall architecture or division orientation can therefore change leaf proportions even if it does not alter regional fate markers.

The energetic requirements of leaf development arise from active cellular processes rather than from a special leaf-specific thermodynamic equation. DNA replication, protein synthesis, wall polymer production, transport and maintenance of ion gradients require metabolic resources. Developing tissue may receive imported assimilates from other parts of the plant, particularly before its own photosynthetic capacity is well established. Environmental stress may decrease the resources available for growth or change water relations. Small leaves in a stressed plant cannot automatically be interpreted as evidence for a genetic lesion in the polarity pathway.

Growth measurements can separate some of these possibilities. When projected blade area is measured along with mean projected epidermal cell area, an approximate epidermal cell count can be estimated by dividing the former by the latter. This approximation assumes representative sampling and a single specified surface. It ignores differences among cell types unless the sampling scheme accounts for them. A leaf with fewer but larger cells could have the same projected area as a leaf with many smaller cells. Final size therefore does not uniquely identify the underlying developmental process. Measurements at several ages can help distinguish a slower growth rate from earlier cessation of growth.

Deriving simple quantitative relationships for growth

Let A represent the projected area of a developing blade. The absolute growth rate is the change in area per unit time, which may be measured in square millimeters per day. For comparisons between leaves of different initial size, the relative growth rate is often more useful. Its instantaneous form is (1/A)(dA/dt). Since this is the derivative of ln A with respect to time, integration over an interval gives the average relative rate as ln(A2/A1)/(t2 − t1). The units are inverse time. This quantity represents an average even when the instantaneous rate changes during the observation interval. Using it to extrapolate beyond the measured interval assumes similar growth conditions and may fail near maturation.

For a family of approximately similar outlines, the projected area can be represented as A = kLW, where L is length, W is maximum width and k is a dimensionless shape factor. Taking logarithms gives ln A = ln k + ln L + ln W. If k remains constant, the relative area growth rate equals the sum of the relative length and width growth rates. The relative rate of change in the ratio L/W equals the longitudinal rate minus the width rate. This is useful in separating a change in shape from a uniform change in overall size. If k changes because the margin becomes deeply lobed or the outline changes substantially, an additional term for the rate of change in k is required. Direct projected-area measurements then become particularly important.

A similar balance applies to a specified epidermal surface. If N cells cover it and their mean projected area is a, the total surface area can be approximated as N multiplied by a. The logarithmic relationship illustrates why reduced cell number and increased mean cell area can offset one another. Such an observation is often described as compensation, but it does not in itself prove that cells directly sense the number of their neighbors. Cell-type composition, spatial sampling and altered developmental timing can all affect the measurements. Comparison of the same defined surface and developmental region is essential. In a highly curved leaf, a projection onto a plane also differs from the true surface area.

A simple physical model of irreversible plant cell growth is the Lockhart relationship. If turgor pressure P exceeds a wall yield threshold Y, relative irreversible expansion is approximated by φ(P − Y), where φ is the extensibility coefficient. If pressure is measured in megapascals and time in hours, φ has units of inverse megapascals per hour. At or below the threshold, the idealized model predicts no irreversible expansion from this mechanism. This relationship explains why two cells with identical turgor may grow at different rates: their walls may differ in extensibility or in the pressure required for yielding.

The simple equation is not a complete model of leaf shape. Actual growth depends on water availability, time-dependent wall properties and constraints imposed by surrounding tissues. Direction-dependent mechanical properties contribute to different rates of expansion along different axes. A pressure measurement in one cell does not directly predict the curvature of an entire lamina. Nevertheless, the equation gives a useful mechanistic distinction between pressure and the capacity of a wall to permit irreversible growth. A mutant with impaired wall yielding can grow slowly even when turgor is not unusually low.

Timing cell proliferation and the transition to expansion

The number of cells contributing to a developing region depends on both the rate of cell division and the length of time during which those cells continue to divide. These quantities need not change together. A leaf with relatively few cells may have slow division during a normal proliferative period, or it may have an approximately normal division rate but cease dividing prematurely. A measurement of final cell number cannot distinguish these possibilities. Observing successive stages is therefore essential when trying to identify the regulatory role of a growth-associated gene.

A simplified model can illustrate the distinction. If a population of equivalent cells undergoes repeated divisions and each daughter remains proliferative, its cell number doubles after each complete round. After n ideal synchronous rounds, the number is N0 multiplied by 2 to the power n. Actual leaf cells are not all synchronized, and some exit the cell cycle while others continue dividing. The model is useful for recognizing why a modest change in the number of effective division rounds can strongly influence the final population. It should not be used to claim that the entire mature epidermis underwent exactly the same number of divisions. Differentiating lineages, such as the stomatal lineage, have their own division patterns.

One mechanism contributing to the regulation of leaf growth in Arabidopsis involves GROWTH REGULATING FACTOR transcription factors and GRF-INTERACTING FACTOR proteins. ANGUSTIFOLIA3, also called GIF1, is a representative coactivator associated with cell proliferation. A transcriptional coactivator contributes to the regulation of transcription through interactions with other regulatory proteins; it need not itself recognize a specific DNA sequence in the same way as a DNA-binding transcription factor. The interaction of GRFs with GIFs provides an example of a regulatory module that influences the proliferative capacity of developing tissue.

Many GRF transcripts are regulated by miR396, whose activity helps restrict proliferation-associated gene expression as development proceeds. CIN-like TCP transcription factors participate in the progression toward differentiation and restriction of excessive proliferation; several are themselves regulated by miR319. The useful general principle is that growth-promoting and differentiation-promoting activities can be coordinated through regulatory networks that alter the duration and distribution of proliferative competence. These relationships should not be memorized as an absolute rule for every member of the GRF or TCP family. Individual members can have distinct roles, and the organ-level phenotype depends on which activity has changed and where.

A developmental time course provides an appropriate way to test such a module. Suppose a mutant has a similar initial leaf area to the wild type and initially a similar rate of cell-number increase. At later stages the mutant ceases adding cells while the wild type continues to do so. These observations support an alteration in the duration of proliferation rather than a simple reduction of the initial division rate. If mutant cells subsequently become larger than wild-type cells, partial compensation may occur. The term compensation describes the increase in cell size accompanying a decrease in cell number, but the extent of compensation must be measured. It may restore only part of the area difference, and it is not a necessary consequence of every mutation reducing proliferation.

Studies of leaf-size mutants, including work on ANGUSTIFOLIA3 and fugu mutants, demonstrate that several combinations of cell-number and expansion phenotypes are possible. Such studies support a view of leaf-size control in which proliferation and postmitotic enlargement are coordinated but not rigidly interchangeable. A mutant with normal final leaf area can have an abnormal cell number and cell size. Conversely, a small leaf can show larger cells if those cells fail to compensate fully for reduced number. A comparison of area alone may therefore conceal a substantial change in the developmental program.

Endoreduplication is another relevant cellular phenomenon. In some differentiating plant cells, DNA replication occurs without an ordinary mitotic division, resulting in increased nuclear DNA content. Such a cycle is distinct from proliferation because it does not produce two separate daughter cells. Increased ploidy is associated with cell enlargement in some tissues, but the relationship is not a universal simple proportionality. A measurement of DNA content can reveal changes in the distribution of nuclear states; it cannot on its own determine the size of every cell or prove that all additional DNA replication directly causes enlargement. The significance for leaf development is that the transition from cell division to differentiation can involve changes in the mode of the cell cycle.

An experiment measuring both nuclear DNA content and cell size should control for the identity of the sampled cells. If the mutant contains a different proportion of pavement cells and guard cells, bulk measurements can change merely because different cell types contribute to the sample. The investigator might examine nuclear DNA content in a specified tissue and independently measure cell geometry. These complementary measurements can distinguish a change in the cellular population from a change within one defined lineage. This reasoning also explains why conclusions about the whole leaf should not be drawn uncritically from measurements of only one epidermal region.

7. Simple leaves, compound leaves and developmental timing

Simple and compound leaves illustrate how a broadly similar developmental starting point can produce different final architectures. A simple leaf has a single continuous blade, although the margin may be deeply lobed. A compound leaf has a blade divided into distinct leaflets, which are parts of one leaf rather than separately initiated leaves of the main shoot. The difference is easiest to understand by identifying the proximal attachment and the axillary bud of the complete leaf. A typical leaflet does not have an independent axillary bud of the main shoot at its base.

A developing compound leaf can maintain competence for localized organ-like outgrowths for a longer period than a typical simple leaf. During this period, particular positions along the developing leaf produce leaflet primordia, while adjacent boundary regions remain relatively restricted in growth. Auxin distribution and local boundary regulation are relevant to this process. The resulting leaflets belong to the developmental field of the original leaf, even though a mature leaflet may resemble a complete simple leaf in outline and internal differentiation.

Class I KNOX genes provide a useful comparison between leaf architectures. In simple Arabidopsis leaves, class I KNOX activity characteristic of the shoot meristem is normally restricted away from the developing leaf. In some species with compound leaves, such as tomato, KNOX-related activities contribute to the prolonged morphogenetic potential of the leaf. A prolonged period during which new lateral structures can arise provides a developmental explanation for greater complexity of outline. The comparison should not be converted into a universal rule that every compound leaf requires the same KNOX gene. Evolution has recruited different regulators in different plant groups.

The same distinction applies to serrations. An increase in the number or depth of marginal outgrowths does not automatically turn a simple leaf into a compound leaf. The degree of separation and the developmental organization of each outgrowth matter. A mutant may exhibit deep lobes because growth between lobes is restricted, or because the lobes themselves expand more. A comparison based only on the largest distance across the blade misses these alternatives. Time-resolved observations of the margin can help reveal whether extra outgrowths are initiated or whether initially similar outgrowths develop differently.

Leaf diversity also emphasizes the limits of a single model plant. Many grasses have long narrow leaves with a sheathing base and a distinct growth zone. Their geometry need not signify the same mechanism as a narrow-leaf mutation in Arabidopsis. The appropriate comparison asks whether the observed structure is abnormal relative to the developmental organization of that species. Conservation of regulators and of broad positional principles is compatible with considerable diversity of form. An exam problem that presents a normal grass leaf should not be answered by simply applying a mutant eudicot phenotype to it.

Comparative development of the maize leaf

The proximal–distal axis can be studied especially clearly in grasses. A maize leaf has a proximal sheath that surrounds the stem and a distal blade that projects away from it. At the junction are the ligule and auricles, specialized structures associated with the boundary between blade and sheath. The ligule is a fringe-like outgrowth on the adaxial side; the auricles contribute to the architecture of the junction and the angle of the blade. These structures provide useful anatomical landmarks for distinguishing regional identity from simple differences in longitudinal growth.

A mutation that changes the position of the sheath–blade boundary is different from a mutation that reduces the length of both regions without altering their identities. In the first case, tissue characteristics normally found in the proximal region may extend into an inappropriate distal position. In the second, the overall dimensions change while the regional pattern remains recognizable. The phenotype should be examined in terms of the distribution of tissue identities, not merely the length of the blade. This is the same type of reasoning used to distinguish altered adaxial–abaxial identity from altered lateral growth in Arabidopsis.

Classical maize mutants involving ectopic expression of KNOX genes demonstrate that meristem-associated regulators can influence proximal–distal leaf patterning. The appearance of sheath-like or ligule-associated characteristics within the blade is evidence that a region can acquire developmental features inappropriate to its position. The result illustrates the principle of homeotic or regional identity transformation rather than merely growth stimulation. The exact phenotype depends on the pattern and timing of ectopic activity. The normal developmental functions and expression patterns of KNOX genes in different species should be considered before making broad comparisons with compound-leaf development.

The grass leaf also illustrates a different spatial arrangement of growth. Cells near the base of a developing blade can continue proliferating and expanding while more distal regions differentiate. A sample taken from the base and another from the tip of the same leaf may therefore represent different developmental states, even though they were collected at the same chronological time. This arrangement is particularly helpful for understanding how position within an organ can be related to maturation. Nevertheless, a base-to-tip series is not equivalent to observing the entire history of an individual cell. Growth changes the relative positions of cells, and some developmental events cannot be inferred solely from a static spatial series.

The difference between the elongated grass blade and a broad eudicot leaf does not imply that grasses lack mediolateral or adaxial–abaxial organization. Each organ must establish distinct regions along all three axes. The balance of growth and the specialized features associated with those axes differ. Recognizing this fact prevents the common mistake of treating the proportions of a normal grass leaf as if they were evidence of a failure to produce a lamina. A narrow shape is a phenotype only relative to an appropriate reference. Comparative developmental biology requires identifying homologous developmental problems while recognizing differences in the solutions used by different plants.

Other forms, such as unifacial or strongly modified leaves, reinforce this caution. A naturally cylindrical organ can have a developmental history distinct from that of a radialized mutant in a species that normally makes flat leaves. The external shape alone does not establish the expression domains of polarity regulators. The comparison should use developmental evidence, anatomical markers and the normal organization of related species rather than assuming that a single structural category has one universal molecular origin.

8. Epidermis and the differentiation of a stomatal lineage

The surface of the developing leaf becomes an epidermis. This tissue is important both because it forms a protective boundary and because it contains specialized cell types. Ordinary pavement cells occupy much of the surface; trichomes are epidermal outgrowths; stomata are pores bordered by specialized guard cells. A stomatal pore is not an empty gap caused by a random failure of epidermal growth. It belongs to a differentiated structure with a regulated developmental history.

In Arabidopsis, a representative stomatal lineage starts when a competent epidermal precursor enters a specialized division program. An asymmetric division produces daughters of different size and developmental potential. The smaller daughter, the meristemoid, can undergo further asymmetric divisions before acquiring guard mother cell identity. The guard mother cell subsequently divides symmetrically to produce the pair of guard cells. Not every neighboring larger daughter follows the same fate; it may become a pavement cell or participate in a subsequent lineage division under appropriate positional conditions.

Three basic helix–loop–helix transcription factors illustrate the succession of regulatory states: SPEECHLESS (SPCH), MUTE and FAMA. SPCH is associated with entry into and proliferation within the early stomatal lineage. MUTE promotes the transition from meristemoid to guard mother cell. FAMA is important for final guard-cell differentiation and restriction of additional proliferation. Loss of early lineage entry and failure of terminal differentiation therefore predict different outcomes. In the first, the population of lineage precursors is reduced; in the second, precursor-like cells can remain without completing the mature structure.

Stomata are typically separated by at least one non-stomatal epidermal cell in the model Arabidopsis pattern. Local signaling helps regulate the behaviour of neighboring precursor cells and thereby contributes to spacing. A failure of this spatial inhibition can produce clustered stomata. This is a tissue-patterning phenotype rather than necessarily a loss of guard-cell identity. A plant can have an approximately normal number of stomata with an abnormal spatial distribution, or an abnormal number with reasonably preserved spacing. Measurements of density and nearest-neighbor arrangement address different questions.

Stomatal density, defined as the number of stomata per unit area, is influenced by epidermal expansion as well as lineage entry. Imagine that the same number of stomata is distributed over twice the surface area after growth. Density falls by half without any reduction in the absolute number of stomata. The stomatal index, which expresses the proportion of stomata among stomata and other counted epidermal cells, can provide complementary information. Neither statistic alone gives a complete account of lineage history. Sampling at comparable leaf regions and developmental stages is needed for a meaningful interpretation.

The differentiation of the epidermis also illustrates how dorsiventral pattern interacts with cell-type differentiation without being identical to it. Stomata may occur on both leaf surfaces, and their relative numbers depend on species and developmental conditions. The mere presence of stomata on one surface is not sufficient to identify that surface as abaxial. Similarly, the presence of trichomes on a particular side can be developmentally informative in a defined species and age class but is not a universal indicator of polarity. A robust interpretation uses positional context and multiple anatomical or molecular observations.

Recognizing distinct stomatal lineage arrests

An early lineage division can be asymmetric both geometrically and developmentally. The daughter cells differ in size and subsequent developmental potential, but a change in division orientation alone does not demonstrate that their regulatory identities have switched. Conversely, the regulatory state may change before a distinct anatomical difference is evident. Morphological evidence and stage-specific expression markers should therefore be used together.

In a conceptual mutant comparison, one epidermis contains very few asymmetric lineage divisions, whereas another contains many small dividing precursor-like cells but very few mature stomata. A count of mature stomata might give similar results in both mutants, yet the developmental interpretations are different. The first is consistent with a defect in early entry or proliferation; the second with persistence of an earlier lineage state or failure of differentiation. The SPCH–MUTE–FAMA sequence supplies hypotheses about these stages, but the phenotype should not be used to assign a particular mutation without additional genetic evidence.

Local signaling helps prevent inappropriate adjacent stomatal differentiation. A defect in spacing can produce clusters of otherwise differentiated stomata, whereas a defect in terminal guard-cell differentiation can reduce the number of recognizable stomata without generating clusters. These outcomes address different developmental processes. To compare samples, count each guard-cell pair as a single stomatal complex and apply the same definition of other epidermal cells throughout. Sample equivalent regions because the epidermal pattern may vary across the same leaf. Density, index and spacing are complementary observations, not substitutes for one another.

9. Mesophyll and the construction of functional internal tissue

Inside the epidermis, many foliage leaves develop mesophyll, the photosynthetically important ground tissue. In a common eudicot dorsiventral arrangement, palisade mesophyll cells are elongated and densely arranged toward the adaxial surface, whereas spongy mesophyll cells are less regularly arranged with prominent spaces, often toward the abaxial surface. These features arise gradually during differentiation as cells enlarge, change shape, develop chloroplasts and establish tissue-specific contacts and spaces. The mature anatomy reflects both positional identities and later growth.

Intercellular air spaces are a structural feature of the internal leaf tissue rather than necessarily a sign of injury or dead cells. Spaces arise as the geometry and contacts of developing cells change. They contribute to internal gas exchange between the epidermal pores and photosynthetic surfaces. Their formation must be compatible with the mechanical integrity of the tissue and its vascular supply. A leaf with extensive unregulated tissue separation would not necessarily gain a functional advantage from having more air space.

The arrangement of mesophyll cells shows why anatomical differentiation should be interpreted in developmental context. A young primordium is much more densely cellular and less obviously specialized than a mature blade. In an early developmental section, the absence of mature palisade morphology does not show that the adaxial program is missing. The relevant test would compare tissue identities at equivalent stages or use molecular markers that are informative earlier. A delay in chloroplast differentiation could alter the color of a leaf without changing its polarity; a polarity defect could alter the regional organization of mesophyll without preventing chloroplasts from forming in every affected cell.

The familiar dorsiventral leaf is not the only viable anatomical organization. Some leaves have relatively similar mesophyll organization on their two faces, a condition often described as isobilateral. The surfaces of a vertical leaf may encounter a different pattern of illumination from the surfaces of a horizontal leaf. Comparative anatomy therefore requires attention to species, leaf orientation and developmental context. The examination principle is that anatomical differences between the two surfaces are useful evidence of polarity when the reference pattern for the plant is known, but their absence in an unfamiliar normal leaf is not proof that developmental polarity was never established.

The internal tissue must expand in coordination with the epidermis. If mesophyll growth outpaces expansion of the surface, mechanical constraints can deform the leaf. Conversely, expansion of the surface without appropriate internal differentiation could produce a large but poorly functioning blade. A coherent explanation of leaf development must therefore connect the formation of specialized tissues to the mechanics of the whole organ rather than treating each tissue as an independent layer assembled after the others have stopped growing.

Coordinated differentiation of the internal and surface tissues

An internal mesophyll tissue becomes functional through several changes that occur over a shared period of development. Cells increase in size, acquire characteristic shapes and develop chloroplasts; spaces between cells become established as their contacts and geometry change. Chloroplast differentiation contributes to the ability of mesophyll cells to carry out photosynthesis, but the biochemical reactions of photosynthesis are distinct from the developmental processes that establish the tissue. The presence of chloroplasts indicates a differentiated physiological capacity, not the entire history of regional specification.

The difference between a palisade cell and a spongy mesophyll cell is not simply the presence or absence of chloroplasts. Both may contain chloroplasts, while their geometry, arrangement and relationship to intercellular spaces differ. The arrangement helps produce a tissue that combines light interception with internal diffusion of gases. A leaf can possess apparently normal chloroplasts but show abnormal tissue organization if cell enlargement or separation is altered. Conversely, the internal geometry can be approximately normal even when a defect in chloroplast differentiation reduces pigmentation. These comparisons show why microscopy and pigment measurements answer different questions.

As tissue differentiates, cells can change the extent to which they remain in contact with adjacent cells. Intercellular spaces may emerge as the developing tissue grows and contacts are remodeled. Their arrangement is not equivalent to a simple reduction in the number of cells. A tissue with the same number of cells can have a different porosity if the sizes, shapes and spatial arrangement of those cells change. Porosity can be described as the fraction of a defined volume occupied by air spaces. A two-dimensional section provides information about area occupied by spaces, but a complete three-dimensional arrangement cannot always be inferred from one section. Repeated representative sections or suitable three-dimensional observations provide stronger evidence.

The differentiation of the mesophyll and the epidermis is functionally related. Stomatal pores provide a connection between the atmosphere and internal spaces, while the organization of those spaces affects access to internal photosynthetic tissue. Lundgren and colleagues (2019) investigated the relationship between functional stomata and mesophyll porosity. Their findings support the conclusion that the two developing structures are coordinated rather than entirely independent. The result does not mean that a simple numerical increase in stomata will always produce a corresponding proportional increase in internal airspace. Developmental conditions, tissue growth and the functioning of the pores all matter.

A conceptual comparison might involve three genotypes. One has fewer stomata but normal guard-cell differentiation in the structures that form. A second has a normal number of structures but impaired pore function. A third shows ordinary stomatal characteristics but abnormal mesophyll organization. Measuring mesophyll porosity together with epidermal pattern and pore function would distinguish several routes to impaired coordination. If reduced porosity were associated specifically with impaired pore function, this would motivate a hypothesis about the influence of functional gas exchange on internal development. Such an inference would need controls for leaf age, growth and environmental conditions; it would not establish that gas exchange is the only developmental signal.

This example also shows why a physical property can be part of a developmental phenotype without being an independent developmental gene pathway. Changes in the surface may alter the environment experienced by internal tissue, and changes in internal tissue can influence the functioning of the surface. The resulting organization emerges from interactions among cells with different developmental roles. The teaching objective is to recognize the need for coordinated differentiation across layers, not to enumerate every physiological process that occurs in the mature leaf.

10. Vascular differentiation and continuity with the shoot

An expanding blade requires a connection to the transport system of the parent shoot. In a typical leaf, major veins connect to vascular tissue through the proximal attachment, and finer veins distribute transport capacity through the blade. A vascular strand contains xylem and phloem that differ in differentiation and transport functions. Before these tissues become mature, elongated precursor cells form a developing procambial strand. The pattern of these precursor strands provides evidence about where veins are being specified, independently of the later visibility of lignified xylem.

A developmental problem arises because the pattern of veins changes as the leaf expands. The first major strand forms in relation to the developing organ and the existing shoot vasculature. Later strands develop in the growing blade and connect to existing vascular routes. In many eudicots, minor veins form a network with branches and loops. A model for the initiation of new vascular routes must explain both the restricted location of each strand and its continuity with the developing network. A picture showing only the finished veins cannot distinguish whether a strand was specified early or formed in response to later changes in tissue geometry.

Auxin transport provides an important part of the explanation. PIN efflux carriers can become asymmetrically localized in cell membranes, influencing the direction of intercellular auxin transport. The canalization hypothesis proposes that initially broad auxin movement can become progressively concentrated along routes, with feedback between transport and the establishment of transport capacity. Such restricted paths can be associated with the acquisition of vascular precursor identity. The model connects a dynamic regulatory process with the emergence of narrow, continuous cell files. It does not imply that mature xylem tubes existed before the transport pattern was established.

Scarpella and colleagues (2006) investigated the relation between PIN1-associated polarity patterns and developing veins in Arabidopsis. The correspondence of directional transport-associated patterns with emerging procambial routes provides evidence for a role of auxin transport in vein positioning. Experimental interference with auxin transport and examination of developing tissues can further test that relationship. It is important, however, to distinguish a protein localization pattern from a direct measurement of the flux of auxin molecules. Localization supplies information about transport capacity and directionality; actual net transport depends on additional factors. More recent syntheses also indicate that a model based solely on polar transport is insufficient to explain every aspect of vein formation.

Vascular differentiation has its own adaxial–abaxial organization. In an ordinary collateral bundle of a dorsiventral leaf, xylem lies closer to the adaxial side, with phloem toward the abaxial side. Some mutants affecting polarity also alter this relationship. An altered bundle with vascular tissue arranged around a central component can thus provide independent evidence that a polarity defect affects internal as well as surface tissues. It is still necessary to compare with the usual bundle structure of the species and organ being studied. A concentric bundle may be a normal configuration in a different botanical context.

The pattern and density of veins should not be conflated. A leaf can retain a similar total length of visible vein per unit area yet differ in connectivity if loops fail to form. Conversely, identical vein topology can yield different density measurements when blade area changes. The biological significance of connections is that they create routes by which the blade remains integrated with the shoot, while finer network architecture affects the distribution of transport capacity within the organ. Detailed transport physiology belongs to a separate study of plant function; here the emphasis is how the arrangement is established during development.

Testing the formation and continuity of vascular strands

A fluorescent protein fusion to a transport-associated protein can reveal membrane localization patterns in the developing blade. The fusion must retain relevant biological function, otherwise its localization or the resulting development may be altered by the reporter itself. A rescue experiment can help establish that the reporter remains functional. Comparing its distribution with a separate vascular precursor marker in the same stage of leaf development supplies information about temporal order. If restricted transport-associated patterns precede recognizable precursor strands, the observation supports the proposed order but does not alone prove that the former causes the latter.

A transport inhibitor such as NPA provides a conceptual perturbation. If treatment changes vein arrangement, the result is consistent with a role for auxin transport. However, treatment applied before leaf initiation may also alter earlier organ development and consequently affect veins indirectly. Comparing treatments introduced at different stages, using a solvent control and measuring overall blade dimensions helps distinguish a change in vascular specification from a secondary consequence of altered leaf geometry. A phenotype caused by a broadly acting inhibitor should not be treated as proof of a direct molecular interaction at a particular gene.

The finished vascular system can also be analyzed in terms of its connections. Junctions and terminal points can be represented as graph nodes and the vein segments connecting them as edges. This abstraction describes the connectivity of a pattern rather than the actual movement of water or assimilates. A network with loops provides alternative paths between some locations; a tree-like network does not. Two leaves may have the same total vein length but differ in their distribution of loops and free endings. Thus vein density, defined as length per unit area, cannot fully describe the developmental architecture of the vascular network.

Suppose a narrow leaf has half the area and half the total vein length of a normal leaf. The two leaves have identical vein densities, yet the narrow leaf may still have fewer connections or fewer orders of minor veins. Conversely, higher density could arise if the blade expanded less after vascular strands were specified. Measuring early precursor patterns, final connectivity and blade expansion separately helps reveal the developmental source of these differences.

11. Maturation, heteroblasty and the next developmental transition

The completion of blade expansion does not occur in every cell at the same time. A young leaf can contain regions that continue to enlarge together with more differentiated regions, and a mature blade may retain some developmental plasticity. For ordinary developmental comparisons, maturation refers to the attainment of relatively stable tissue architecture and organ dimensions, not to the onset of senescence. A mature green leaf is physiologically active. Its eventual aging and loss of function involve additional processes distinct from its formation.

The successive leaves produced by a shoot are not necessarily identical even when they are genetically equivalent. Heteroblasty describes changes in the characteristics of organs produced at different stages of shoot development. In Arabidopsis, juvenile and adult vegetative leaves can be distinguished by features including their shape and the occurrence of abaxial trichomes. The developmental stage of the shoot when a leaf is specified is relevant to its eventual morphology. This is different from the fact that a single leaf grows larger as it ages: heteroblasty concerns differences among organs initiated at different phases.

A decline in the abundance of miR156 and related miR157 during vegetative development permits increased activity of target SPL transcription factors. These regulators help promote adult vegetative characteristics. Prolonging the juvenile regulatory state can delay the appearance of such traits in later-formed leaves, while reducing juvenile-promoting activity can lead to earlier expression of adult features. This is a useful example of temporal positional information: the developmental age of the shoot alters the program expressed in a new lateral organ. It is not simply a reversal of adaxial and abaxial identity because an adult leaf develops a new feature on its abaxial surface.

Environmental effects can also modify the form of leaves. Heterophylly is often used in discussing production of different leaf forms under contrasting environmental conditions, for example in some amphibious plants. The terms heteroblasty and heterophylly can overlap in broader botanical usage, so the evidence in an examination vignette should guide the interpretation. Comparing leaves from the same genotype at a defined developmental phase but different environments addresses plastic responses. Comparing successive leaves under controlled environmental conditions addresses age-dependent change more directly. An interaction between age and environment is also possible.

These phase-dependent differences lead naturally to the next developmental question: how a vegetative shoot becomes competent to initiate reproductive development. Adult vegetative identity and reproductive identity are distinct; a plant can express adult leaf characteristics without immediately producing flowers. The regulatory programs that influence the timing of reproductive transition are studied separately. Leaf morphogenesis provides the foundation for that study because leaves are developing organs with their own temporal identities and also participate in systemic communication with the shoot apex.

Integrating developmental measurements

A coherent experiment begins by specifying which level of organization is affected. A malformed leaf could reflect changed organ initiation, regional fate, growth geometry or tissue differentiation. The same gene may influence more than one level, but the phenotype alone cannot determine which abnormality occurred first. External morphology, tissue-level markers and a time series provide three complementary kinds of evidence. A mature blade of reduced area could have started with a small primordium, expanded more slowly or stopped expanding earlier. These histories are distinguishable only through developmental observations.

A genetic rescue should be evaluated with respect to each measured trait. Restoration of normal marker expression does not prove that organ geometry has recovered; restoration of blade area does not prove that normal tissue identities have been established. These distinctions have practical relevance to crop improvement as well. Leaf geometry affects the presentation of photosynthetic tissue to the environment, but larger leaves may also change the resource requirements of the shoot or the overlap between successive leaves. Increased leaf size alone is not evidence of increased productivity. Performance must be measured under the relevant conditions.

The principles of leaf morphogenesis therefore link regional gene expression, local communication and the mechanics of growing tissues. The leaf is a particularly instructive organ because its cell walls preserve much of the positional relationship between neighboring cells as the organ changes in size. Developmental changes can be analyzed as a changing spatial pattern rather than a sequence of unrelated anatomical features. This approach prepares the student to interpret later changes in shoot identity and reproductive development.

Testing temporal identity and environmental plasticity

The observation that successive leaves differ in morphology raises a question about the source of temporal information. One possibility is that the differences reflect the increasing age of the shoot, another that the environment changes as the plant grows, and a third that the positions of the leaves expose them to different conditions. These alternatives can be investigated by growing comparable plants under controlled conditions and comparing leaves according to their order of initiation. The phenotype must also be assessed at comparable stages of leaf maturation, because the outline of a young leaf can differ from that of the same leaf after expansion.

The appearance of trichomes on the abaxial surface of Arabidopsis rosette leaves was studied as a marker of vegetative phase change by Telfer and colleagues (1997). A trichome marker is convenient because it can be scored without requiring a complete anatomical analysis. Nevertheless, a mutation that directly affects trichome development can change the marker without changing the overall developmental age of the shoot. A robust assessment therefore combines more than one phase-associated trait, such as leaf shape and the pattern of marginal serrations, with molecular information about the age pathway. A phenotype is most informative when its relationship to the underlying developmental process has been established.

The miR156–SPL relationship provides a mechanistic explanation of age-dependent change. High miR156 activity in juvenile development restricts expression of target SPL transcription factors, while declining activity permits adult-promoting functions to become more prominent. A transgene maintaining miR156 at a high level would therefore be expected to prolong juvenile features under suitable conditions. The prediction concerns the developmental state of leaves produced by the shoot, not simply the amount of time an individual leaf takes to finish enlarging. A plant can make mature juvenile-type leaves if it remains in the juvenile vegetative phase while those leaves form.

Environmental information can influence the timing of the transition as well. Carbon status and light conditions can affect developmental growth and age-associated regulatory programs. It would be incorrect to interpret a delay in the appearance of an adult trait as proof that an age regulator is genetically defective when the plants have been grown under different conditions. A useful experimental design compares genotypes within the same environment and each genotype across environments. This allows an investigator to distinguish a general environmental effect from a genotype-specific response and to recognize possible interactions between the two.

The final comparison separates developmental phase, competence and the actual transition to reproduction. The appearance of adult vegetative traits can be associated with increasing competence to respond to flowering cues, but competence is not identical to commitment to make a flower. A competent shoot may remain vegetative in noninductive conditions. Conversely, a perturbation affecting the expression of a particular leaf phase marker does not necessarily have the same effect on every aspect of flowering. This distinction prepares the ground for studying the developmental timing of flowering without conflating the maturation of an individual leaf with the developmental identity of the entire shoot.

Misconceptions and exam traps

Adaxial always means upper relative to gravity
It denotes the side toward the shoot axis during development. Confusion arises because most horizontal leaves present that face upward; an inverted leaf is an effective test case.
A small leaf must have fewer cells
Reduced cell size, number or both can reduce area. Organ size is a product of cell number and cell size; an exam dataset can test which variable has changed.
A leaf lacks polarity if it lacks a petiole
Proximal–distal organization does not require a petiole. Petiole is an anatomical feature, not the definition of the axis; identify proximal and distal positions on a grass blade.
All narrow leaves are abaxialized
A narrow leaf may preserve both domains but have limited blade growth. Width is a final geometric output, not a fate marker; compare transverse sections and regional reporters.
A radial leaf always has abaxial identity
Severe adaxialization may also produce radialization. Opposite identity transformations can both remove the normal interface; infer direction from markers rather than outline.
KANADI promotes adaxial identity
KANADI participates in abaxial specification. Opposing regulatory families are easily interchanged; predict ectopic KANADI activity in adaxial territory.
HD-ZIP III knockout and resistant target are equivalent
Loss of function and escape from post-transcriptional repression can have opposite effects. Both perturbations involve the same target, but their effects on protein accumulation differ; compare the two predicted expression domains.
A marker is a direct hormone concentration measurement
A response marker reports signaling output. Signal output depends on responsiveness as well as concentration; distinguish reporter output from a direct chemical assay.
Every compound leaf requires the same KNOX gene
Compound leaf mechanisms vary across species. An example from tomato is not a universal rule; compare distinct compound-leaf developmental programs.
Leaflets are individual leaves of the main shoot
They are subdivisions of one leaf. Leaflets resemble leaves in shape; use an axillary bud and the full organ attachment to identify them.
Mature palisade anatomy must be present at initiation
Early fate specification precedes mature anatomy. Early specification is molecular and positional; compare stage-matched precursor markers with later anatomy.
Fewer stomata per area proves fewer stomata formed
Surface expansion may lower density without reducing number. Density has area in its denominator; calculate index and density separately when area changes.
All cycling cells are detected by a single phase marker
Reporter state depends on phase and expression. Cell-cycle reporters may be phase-specific; use time-course observations rather than infer permanent arrest from a snapshot.
A vein has formed when PIN polarity appears
Transport-associated patterns precede mature tissue differentiation. PIN localization reports transport-associated capacity, not a mature vessel or direct flux; compare early and late markers.
Adult vegetative leaves prove the shoot is flowering
Vegetative phase change is distinct from reproductive transition. Adult vegetative identity is not reproductive commitment; distinguish developmental competence from actual transition.

Correct answer: D. The early rates are comparable and the plateau differs; larger final cells are consistent with compensation.

  • A: The data explicitly show similar early increase.
  • B: Larger cells do not support complete absence of growth-driving pressure.
  • C: The data concern proliferation inside individual leaves.

31. A cyclin reporter is absent in a distal field at one time but present in basal tissue. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Every distal cell has been permanently determined since initiation.
  2. Every basal cell will divide synchronously at the next minute.
  3. The measured cell-cycle-associated activity differs spatially at that time; further observations are needed for complete division histories.
  4. The reporter directly measures auxin concentration.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. A phase- or state-dependent reporter supplies spatial evidence, not a complete lineage history.

  • A: One observation does not establish timing or permanence of every cell's commitment.
  • B: Signal does not establish synchronous division.
  • D: A cyclin reporter is not an auxin concentration sensor.

32. In a sampled region there are 30 stomata and 120 other epidermal cells in 0.15 mm². After expansion the area is 0.30 mm² with the same counts. What changes?

  1. Density remains 200 mm⁻² while index halves.
  2. Density falls from 200 to 100 mm⁻² while index stays 20%.
  3. Index increases from 20% to 40%.
  4. Both density and index halve.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. Density depends on area; index depends on the proportion 30/(30+120), which is unchanged.

  • A: The area doubles, so density halves.
  • C: The counts determining index do not change.
  • D: Only density depends on the expanded area.

33. A mutant epidermis accumulates meristemoid-like cells but very few guard mother cells. Which candidate perturbation fits best?

  1. Complete SPCH loss before entry into the lineage.
  2. Enhanced FAMA-mediated final differentiation alone.
  3. Loss of leaf xylem after maturation.
  4. Impaired MUTE-dependent transition.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. MUTE participates in the meristemoid-to-guard-mother-cell transition; the observed arrest is compatible with its impairment.

  • A: An early entry defect should reduce the precursor population.
  • B: Enhanced final differentiation does not explain a block before guard mother cells form.
  • C: The phenotype concerns an epidermal lineage transition.

34. In a spacing mutant, individual stomata differentiate normally but frequently contact one another. Which conclusion is best?

  1. Spatial regulation of lineage entry or placement can fail separately from guard-cell identity.
  2. FAMA-dependent identity is necessarily absent from all cells.
  3. The mutant has necessarily reversed every leaf polarity marker.
  4. Stomatal index must be exactly unchanged.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Normal individual differentiation with abnormal adjacency distinguishes tissue patterning from final identity.

  • B: The observed stomata differentiate normally.
  • C: Clustering is not proof of a whole-organ polarity reversal.
  • D: The index could also change, but it was not supplied.

35. A transport reporter becomes restricted to narrow cell files before procambial identity markers appear. What is the strongest inference?

  1. The reporter proves direct measurement of auxin flux.
  2. Mature vessels must already exist before the reporter changes.
  3. Temporal order proves that no other regulators contribute.
  4. The timing supports a possible transport-associated patterning role; a perturbation is needed for causal evidence.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. Temporal precedence supports a developmental model but not causation by itself.

  • A: Localization is not a direct flux measurement.
  • B: Procambial markers precede mature vascular differentiation.
  • C: A shared upstream cause or additional pathways remain possible.

36. Hypothetical leaf A has 120 mm total vein length in 30 mm²; B has 80 mm in 20 mm². B has no closed loops in the sampled area, whereas A has several. Which conclusion is warranted?

  1. B has twice the vein density of A.
  2. Equal density proves equal connectivity.
  3. Both have density 4 mm⁻¹, but their vascular topology differs.
  4. B necessarily has greater gas exchange.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. Both ratios equal 4 mm of vein per mm²; loop differences independently describe connectivity.

  • A: The calculated densities are identical.
  • B: Topology is not uniquely determined by total length per area.
  • D: Gas exchange cannot be determined from these measurements alone.

37. A transport inhibitor changes vein topology and reduces blade area when applied before leaf initiation. Which added comparison best distinguishes a direct vein-patterning contribution from earlier organ effects?

  1. Apply treatment after initiation at a defined stage and use a matched solvent control plus growth measurements.
  2. Compare only mature leaf color without controls.
  3. Increase concentration until no primordia survive.
  4. Compare treated seedlings with unrelated species at different ages.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Stage-specific treatment and appropriate controls help separate direct vascular patterning effects from consequences of earlier developmental disruption.

  • B: Color cannot distinguish the proposed routes.
  • C: Severe loss of organs prevents specific interpretation.
  • D: Species and age confound the comparison.

38. A maize mutant forms ligule-like tissue within the distal blade. Which axis is most directly implicated by this ectopic regional identity?

  1. Only adaxial–abaxial.
  2. The longitudinal axis of the primary root.
  3. Phyllotactic divergence angle alone.
  4. Proximal–distal.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. A normally proximal boundary feature has appeared at a distal leaf position.

  • A: The observed defect is along the leaf length, although other axes may also be affected.
  • B: The root is not the affected organ.
  • C: Positioning of successive leaves is not established by this within-leaf phenotype.

39. A marked clone of cells expressing an abaxial-promoting regulator causes the blade to bend, but regional marker changes are confined to the clone. What does this show?

  1. Mechanical influence outside the clone is possible even though the measured identity effect remains local.
  2. A diffusible fate signal necessarily converted every neighboring cell.
  3. All effects are proven cell-autonomous, including the bending.
  4. The perturbation had no developmental consequence.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Connected tissues can bend mechanically without changing identity markers outside the clone.

  • B: The marker evidence does not show fate conversion outside it.
  • C: An organ-level mechanical effect may extend beyond the clone.
  • D: Both identity and shape changes were observed.

40. A transgene restores leaf width in a polarity mutant, but adaxial and abaxial markers remain abnormally distributed. Which conclusion is most appropriate?

  1. The original developmental mechanism is fully restored.
  2. The transgene must be the wild-type allele of the original mutated gene.
  3. Width is rescued, but normal regional patterning has not been demonstrated.
  4. The markers are guaranteed to be biologically irrelevant.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. Rescue must be evaluated trait by trait; a growth output can recover without recovery of polarity.

  • A: Marker defects remain.
  • B: Bypass suppression is possible and the data do not identify the original lesion.
  • D: The result does not establish that the markers are irrelevant.

41. A promoter reporter is expressed throughout a young leaf, while a functional protein fusion accumulates only adaxially. Which additional comparison is most directly informative?

  1. Measure only the number of roots.
  2. Compare a target-resistant protein fusion with a normal target-site fusion under the same promoter.
  3. Compare mature blade color in unrelated species.
  4. Delete the promoter from both constructs and interpret their lack of fluorescence as evidence for miRNA activity.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. The comparison tests whether post-transcriptional restriction contributes to the difference.

  • A: Root number does not test the spatial target restriction.
  • C: The species comparison introduces many unrelated variables.
  • D: Without promoter activity neither construct tests the specific recognition mechanism.

42. A leaf initially forms six evenly spaced auxin-response maxima along the margin in both genotypes. At maturity only three of the six protrusions are visible in the mutant. What is the first interpretation?

  1. The mutant necessarily failed to specify all six initial sites.
  2. The mutant is necessarily compound-leaved.
  3. Later development of some outgrowths is altered; early maximum formation was not absent.
  4. The reporter proves exact auxin concentrations were equal at every site.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. The early positions were observed in both samples, so later growth or persistence is implicated.

  • A: It contradicts the stated early observations.
  • B: Tooth number does not establish compound architecture.
  • D: A response reporter is not a concentration assay.

43. A mutant with fewer stomata also has low mesophyll porosity. Which experiment best tests whether functional pore activity, rather than count alone, matters?

  1. Measure only total chlorophyll in mature roots.
  2. Compare matched leaves with differing pore function but comparable stomatal numbers, alongside wild type.
  3. Treat all samples as identical if their projected areas match.
  4. Infer direct signaling from the correlation without further evidence.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. Holding stomatal count approximately comparable helps distinguish the contribution of pore function.

  • A: The measurement does not address the leaf structures in question.
  • C: Area alone does not control pore function or tissue organization.
  • D: Correlation does not identify the causal route.

44. Plants grown under identical conditions form abaxial trichomes starting at leaf 8 in wild type and leaf 13 in a high-miR156 line. Which interpretation is supported?

  1. The transgene has necessarily eliminated adaxial identity.
  2. Leaf 13 must be smaller than every earlier leaf.
  3. The transgene is consistent with prolonged juvenile vegetative identity.
  4. The line is proven incapable of flowering under any conditions.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: C. The phase-associated marker appears later in the high-miR156 line; additional phase traits strengthen the inference.

  • A: Abaxial trichome timing is not evidence of loss of the adaxial domain.
  • B: Leaf dimensions were not given.
  • D: Vegetative phase marker timing is not an absolute flowering-capacity test.

45. A leaf has area 20 mm² at day 2, 40 at day 4 and 60 at day 6. Which interval has greater average relative area growth?

  1. Days 2–4, because ln(2)/2 > ln(1.5)/2.
  2. Days 4–6, because the final area is greater.
  3. Both intervals have identical relative rate because each adds 20 mm².
  4. Days 4–6, because 60/40 = 2.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. Relative rate is logarithmic; the same absolute gain is a larger proportion of the smaller starting area.

  • B: Final area alone is not the relative rate.
  • C: The absolute rates match, but relative rates differ.
  • D: 60/40 equals 1.5, not 2.

46. Using the hypothetical area trajectories in Figure 11, compare growth from day 2 to day 6. What conclusion is supported?

  1. Wild type has a higher relative area growth rate; the graph alone does not resolve division versus enlargement.
  2. Both lines have equal relative growth because the initial area is equal.
  3. The mutant has a higher relative area growth rate because its final area is smaller.
  4. The mutant must be completely abaxialized because its area is lower.

Correct answer: A. Wild type: ln(16/4)/4 = 0.347 day⁻¹. Mutant: ln(9/4)/4 = 0.203 day⁻¹. The difference is 0.144 day⁻¹; cell count and size measurements are needed to identify its basis.

  • B: Identical starting areas do not establish equal proportional change.
  • C: Its proportional increase is smaller: 9/4 rather than 16/4.
  • D: Area measurements cannot establish regional identity; marker evidence is required.

Integrated formats

47. Assertion: A leaf with no recognizable blade can retain proximal–distal growth. Reason: Blade widening and longitudinal extension involve distinguishable developmental axes.

  1. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  2. The assertion is true, but the reason is false.
  3. The assertion is false, but the reason is true.
  4. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. Growth along the proximal–distal axis can persist despite severe mediolateral impairment, so the reason directly accounts for the assertion.

  • A: The causal distinction is precisely why elongation may persist.
  • B: The axes are distinguishable, so the reason is not false.
  • C: A bladeless or severely narrow phenotype can remain elongated.

49. Three statements about leaf polarity are proposed. I: Adaxial identity is developmentally related to the shoot axis. II: A severe polarity defect can affect vascular arrangement. III: Stomatal presence alone universally defines the abaxial surface. Which are correct?

  1. I and III only.
  2. I and II only.
  3. II and III only.
  4. I, II and III.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: B. I defines the developmental relationship, II reflects coordinated tissue identity and III is false because stomatal distribution varies.

  • A: III is incorrect and II is true.
  • C: III is incorrect and I is true.

50. A hypothetical diagram shows an epidermal cell with an unequal division, followed by a small cell that later becomes a larger guard mother cell. Which ordered events best match it?

  1. Lineage entry/asymmetric division → meristemoid → guard mother cell → symmetric guard-cell division.
  2. Guard mother cell → meiosis → pavement cell → root hair.
  3. Symmetric guard-cell division → meristemoid → zygote → stomata.
  4. Mesophyll expansion → procambium → guard mother cell → xylem.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: A. This sequence reflects the Arabidopsis stomatal lineage from entry to guard-cell differentiation.

  • B: Meiosis and root hairs are unrelated to the depicted lineage.
  • C: The proposed order reverses the developmental sequence and introduces a zygote.
  • D: These are different tissue lineages rather than stages of stomatal development.

51. Which numerical statement follows from A = kLW for a constant shape factor? Length increases 50% and width increases 20%.

  1. Area increases 70%.
  2. Area increases 30%.
  3. Area increases 2%.
  4. Area increases 80%.
Answer and reasoning

Correct answer: D. The new area ratio is 1.50 × 1.20 = 1.80, or an 80% increase.

  • A: Adding 50 and 20 ignores the multiplicative cross-term.
  • B: Subtracting percentages does not give the area ratio.
  • C: No such area change follows from the supplied factors.

52. Assertion: The polar transport model of vein formation is useful but does not completely explain every vascular pattern. Reason: Transport-associated localization and a mature vein pattern are distinct kinds of evidence, and other processes may contribute.

  1. Both are true, but the reason has no connection to the assertion.

  3. Both are true, and the reason supports the assertion.
  4. The assertion is false, but the reason is true.

Revision and active recall
