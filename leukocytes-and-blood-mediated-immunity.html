1. Blood as a mobile defence system

A small breach in an epithelial barrier presents a problem that oxygen transport alone cannot solve. Microorganisms entering the connective tissue are initially encountered by cells and soluble substances already present there. The number and distribution of these local defenders are limited, however, and a growing microbial population may quickly exceed their capacity. A vascular network solves this geographical problem. It transports protective cells and molecules throughout the body and permits their selective delivery wherever the need arises. Blood-mediated immunity therefore depends upon circulation as much as upon recognition. A white blood cell that can kill a microorganism but cannot reach it may be of little protective value in an infected tissue.

The cellular constituents of blood are called formed elements. Unlike mature mammalian erythrocytes, circulating leukocytes retain nuclei and the machinery required for responsive changes in behaviour. They can alter the expression of surface receptors, rearrange their cytoskeleton, secrete signalling molecules, and in suitable circumstances travel across vessel walls. Platelets, although they participate in vascular inflammation, are cell fragments rather than leukocytes. The functions of blood cells are coordinated with plasma proteins. An antibody can bind a microbial surface without ingesting it, whereas a neutrophil can capture that coated microbe and destroy it. Complement proteins provide another connection between a soluble recognition reaction and cellular effector activity.

The term innate immunity refers to protective mechanisms based largely on inherited recognition systems, including receptors that respond to conserved microbial or injury-associated features. Adaptive immunity uses antigen-specific lymphocyte populations whose receptors vary among clones. These are not two independent armies. Soluble products of adaptive lymphocytes can recruit innate effector mechanisms, and signals made by innate cells shape adaptive responses. In this chapter the focus is on what blood delivers, how those components leave the vascular compartment and how the resulting responses contribute to tissue homeostasis. Detailed receptor rearrangements, antigen presentation and complement enzyme cascades belong to Unit 7.

Classical observations of engulfment by mobile cells, associated with Élie Metchnikoff's work, demonstrated that ingestion of foreign material could be part of an active cellular defence. Later work on the bactericidal properties of serum established that soluble factors were also important. These observations are compatible rather than mutually exclusive. They direct attention to the physical location of each component: a plasma protein can reach an exposed surface by diffusion and convection, whereas a circulating cell must arrest, deform and cross a barrier before acting in an extravascular site. A productive defence requires coordination of these different routes.

Historical observations and the physiological meaning of immune protection

The distinction between cellular and humoral defence was not obvious at the beginning of experimental immunology. Investigators could observe cells gathering around foreign material, but accumulation alone did not establish whether the cells protected the animal or were merely responding to damage that was already occurring. Likewise, serum from an exposed animal could show protective activity in a cell-free preparation, but the existence of this activity did not prove that all defence in living tissues was accomplished by soluble substances. The apparently conflicting observations reflected experiments performed in different compartments of the organism.

Metchnikoff's studies of mobile cells in transparent animal larvae made cell movement and enclosure of foreign material visible. His interpretation was that engulfment was not solely a nutritional process, but could be employed to protect a multicellular organism. The observation that cells converged on a foreign object supported an active response to intrusion. To establish that this response protected the animal from living microorganisms, additional observations concerning their capture, fate and the consequences for the host were needed. The 1908 Nobel Prize awarded jointly to Metchnikoff and Paul Ehrlich recognised contributions to immunity associated with cellular and humoral ideas. The historical record is useful because it shows how seemingly different lines of evidence became complementary.

A conceptual reconstruction of the question uses two kinds of preparations. In one, viable cells encounter a target in a fluid containing suitable nutrients. In the other, a target encounters a cell-free serum preparation. If the target is eliminated in the first condition, cellular activity is plausible; if it is damaged in the second, soluble activity is plausible. If both act, a combination may be more effective than either alone. A negative result in one preparation may also occur because a required partner is missing. For example, a phagocyte may recognise a surface inefficiently when an opsonin is absent, while a soluble antibody may attach to a surface without directly destroying the target.

The strongest interpretation of this reconstruction depends on controls. A cell-free fluid might contain factors released before cell removal; a cellular preparation might retain serum proteins carried over during collection. A target might lose viability without either immune contribution under unsuitable environmental conditions. An appropriate reference containing the same target in a matched medium is therefore important. The aim is not to reproduce a particular historic protocol, but to understand how experimental separation of cells and soluble components tests a causal claim about blood.

Opsonins offered an especially important conceptual connection. If a soluble substance changes the surface of a particle so that a cell captures it more efficiently, a humoral factor and a cellular response are not rival explanations. Instead they act at successive stages. Modern knowledge of antibodies and complement fragments provides molecular examples. The consequences are physiological as well as biochemical: an antibody may have travelled from its site of production through blood, while the phagocyte may have travelled from marrow into blood and then entered a tissue. Their convergence depends upon the organisation of the whole animal.

The unit on animal physiology therefore approaches immunity by examining distribution, availability, transport, regulation and biological outcomes. Unit 4 explains antigen receptor diversity and molecular pathways in greater detail. The same observation can be interpreted at both levels without teaching the same material twice: an IgG-coated target illustrates receptor recognition in molecular immunology and cooperation of blood-borne molecules with mobile cells in physiology. The distinction is the scientific question being asked, rather than a boundary between unrelated subjects.

2. Recognising circulating leukocytes

A stained peripheral blood film reveals several distinct populations rather than a single white cell type. Granulocytes are classically identified by the appearance of prominent cytoplasmic granules and characteristic nuclear shapes. They include neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils. Monocytes and lymphocytes are classed as agranulocytes, a historical morphological term that does not mean that they lack lysosomes, secretory vesicles or all visible granules. The distinction is useful for recognising cells in a routine stain but is not a complete account of lineage, receptor repertoire or function.

A typical mature neutrophil has a nucleus divided into several connected lobes and fine cytoplasmic granules. An eosinophil often has two major nuclear lobes and conspicuous reddish-orange granules. A basophil has strongly staining granules that may obscure the nuclear outline. Monocytes tend to be larger than small lymphocytes and may display a folded or kidney-shaped nucleus with abundant grey-blue cytoplasm. A small resting lymphocyte often has a dense round nucleus surrounded by a narrow rim of cytoplasm. These are tendencies, not infallible identifiers: activation, maturation, preparation and disease can change appearances. Routine morphology cannot reliably separate a resting B cell from a resting T cell.

A useful adult human teaching range for circulating leukocytes is approximately 4,000–11,000 per microlitre, with neutrophils frequently making up the largest fraction and lymphocytes the second largest fraction. Reference intervals depend on age, population and measurement method. Percentages cannot be interpreted as universal properties of animals. Some vertebrates show a different balance between granulocytes and lymphocytes, and birds possess heterophils that occupy a broadly comparable defensive role to mammalian neutrophils. The important comparative principle is the existence of mobile defenders with complementary recognition and effector strategies, not the exact human percentage.

A blood film reveals morphology but not the total inventory of leukocytes in the organism. Many immune cells spend much of their functional lives outside the sampled circulation. Some travel through lymphatic vessels and lymphoid tissues; others remain associated with organs for long periods. A low representation in blood may coexist with substantial activity in tissue. Conversely, an increased blood concentration need not imply increased production: reduced tissue exit, redistribution from vessel-associated pools or a change in plasma volume may produce a similar observation. This distinction is the basis of quantitative interpretation later in the chapter.

Blood-film classification and its limitations

The ability to classify a cell in a stained preparation depends on the way different cellular substances interact with dyes. Blood films are usually prepared as thin layers so that individual cells can be observed without extensive overlap. In commonly used Romanowsky-type stains, acidic and basic dye components interact differently with cellular proteins and nucleic acids. Nuclear chromatin usually appears purple to blue. Granules rich in certain basic proteins bind acidic eosin and appear reddish, whereas granules rich in strongly acidic substances may bind basic dyes and appear dark. The words eosinophil and basophil describe staining affinities, not a preference for acidic or alkaline extracellular fluids.

The identification of an eosinophil therefore draws upon several independent features: coarse eosinophilic granules, a frequently bilobed nucleus and a characteristic amount of cytoplasm. If the stain is poorly prepared, one feature may be less reliable. A basophil's dark granules can make the nuclear outline difficult to observe. A neutrophil's granules are finer and its nucleus is frequently segmented, but immature neutrophils may have a curved band-shaped nucleus rather than clearly separated lobes. The term band neutrophil refers to maturation morphology, not to a separate long-lived leukocyte lineage.

Imagine two blood films in which the same number of leukocytes are counted. One contains many band forms and the other contains almost entirely mature segmented cells. The total counts alone cannot describe that difference in composition. The observation of more immature granulocytic forms can be compatible with increased marrow release during a strong inflammatory demand, but a smear is not an independent proof of the cause. The pattern can be interpreted only in the context of the sample and the reliability of classification. Granulocyte morphology provides information about the sampled cells' state, while a time series of counts gives information about population change.

A conventional human differential often uses approximate ranges such as neutrophils 50–70%, lymphocytes 20–40%, monocytes 2–8%, eosinophils 1–4% and basophils below roughly 1%. These illustrative ranges overlap differently across laboratories and should not be added as if every class simultaneously occupied its highest value. In any actual classified sample the proportions sum to one. An increase in one proportion necessarily entails a reduction in the combined proportions of the remaining classes, even if their individual absolute concentrations do not change. This mathematical constraint is essential when interpreting relative changes in a mixed cell population.

A common examination question contrasts a smear with a method using antibodies to identify cellular surface markers. The smear may reveal a population of small lymphocytes, but the marker-based method can distinguish cells expressing different lineage-associated molecules. For example, CD3 is associated with T lymphocytes, and CD19 with many B lymphocytes; CD56 is often used with other markers to help identify human NK cells. No single marker should be treated as an infallible classifier under all physiological conditions. The teaching principle is that morphology and receptor expression answer different questions. A count based on one method need not describe the same population as a count based on another.

A further complication is that cell size in a stained film is not identical to the dimensions of an actively migrating cell in living tissue. The preparation flattens cells, and migration changes shape. It is reasonable to describe monocytes as often larger than small lymphocytes in routine films, but misleading to infer an exact pore size required for transit from these images. A living cell deforms, reorganises its cytoplasm and interacts with a deformable barrier. This distinction between a static image and a dynamic process prepares the learner for the mechanism of leukocyte extravasation.

Comparative physiology: what is conserved across animals?

Blood cell classification in humans is a useful foundation, but animal physiology also asks how protective functions are organised in other circulatory systems. Vertebrates share many broad immune functions while differing in the appearance and relative abundance of circulating cells. A bird's heterophil is functionally analogous in important respects to a mammalian neutrophil, including rapid responses and ingestion of microbial material, but its granule morphology and repertoire of antimicrobial mechanisms are not identical. A human reference interval or staining description cannot be carried over unchanged to a bird, fish or amphibian.

In many invertebrates circulating haemocytes participate in recognition, ingestion and other protective responses. In animals with an open circulatory system the circulating fluid is haemolymph, which interacts more directly with tissues than vertebrate blood confined to a vascular network. A mobile defensive cell in haemolymph does not face exactly the same endothelial recruitment problem as a neutrophil in a mammalian postcapillary venule. The functional comparison is nevertheless valuable: in either arrangement, defensive cells must reach the challenge and bring appropriate effector mechanisms to bear. It would be incorrect to assume that all invertebrate haemocytes use the mammalian selectin-integrin cascade or that they possess conventional vertebrate antibodies.

One useful example is a large foreign body that cannot be engulfed by a single cell. Some invertebrate haemocyte responses involve encapsulation of foreign material by multiple cells. In mammalian physiology, extracellular granulocyte secretion against large targets illustrates a different solution to a related physical constraint. These are comparisons of the challenge and broad strategy, not an assertion that the mechanisms are evolutionarily or molecularly identical. They reveal why target size is a general consideration when interpreting cellular defence.

The comparative approach also helps students distinguish homology from analogy. Cells with similar functions need not have identical ancestry or molecular machinery, and related cells may have acquired different specialisations. A question that describes phagocytic activity in an unfamiliar animal can be answered from the observation that particles are enclosed and processed, but assigning the cell a specific mammalian name requires additional evidence. Similarly, the ability of a circulating fluid to damage microorganisms does not prove the presence of vertebrate immunoglobulin. Functional evidence supports a functional claim; molecular identity requires molecular evidence.

3. Neutrophils: rapidly deployable phagocytes

Neutrophils are especially important in defence against many extracellular bacteria and fungi. They develop in bone marrow, where a substantial reserve of mature cells can be available for release. Their circulating lifetime and tissue survival vary with physiological state; it is misleading to assign a single fixed duration to every neutrophil. Inflammation changes both availability and survival. Their deployment is rapid because many proteins needed for ingestion, digestion and secretion are already stored or can be activated without waiting for long transcriptional programmes.

The lobulated nucleus is a useful morphological identifier and is associated with the architecture of a deformable mobile cell. Movement through endothelial junctions nevertheless requires active regulation of the actin cytoskeleton and adhesion molecules. A nucleus with lobes does not independently cause directed migration. The cell first needs to receive directional signals and establish a front and rear. Actin assembly pushes its leading edge forward; adhesion and contraction allow the remainder of the cell to follow. These same principles help explain the formation of a phagocytic cup around a particle.

Neutrophil granules contain different sets of enzymes and antimicrobial proteins. Primary or azurophilic granules include myeloperoxidase and proteases; other granule populations contribute antimicrobial products and mobilisable membrane proteins. Release towards an enclosed phagosome directs harmful chemistry at the ingested material. Release outside the cell can affect extracellular microorganisms, but may also injure extracellular matrix and endothelium. This spatial distinction is physiologically important because the chemistry that destroys a microbe is not intrinsically harmless to the host.

Some activated neutrophils release extracellular networks containing chromatin and associated proteins, termed neutrophil extracellular traps. These structures may immobilise microorganisms and concentrate antimicrobial substances but do not substitute for phagocytosis in every situation. Excessive extracellular activity may contribute to vascular obstruction and tissue injury. A physiological account must therefore distinguish an antimicrobial action from a net beneficial outcome. A response can be effective against a microbe yet harmful when prolonged, misplaced or disproportionately intense.

Neutrophils are often conspicuous early in acute inflammation. This is a general pattern, not a definition of acute inflammation or a guarantee that every infection begins with neutrophil predominance. The cell types arriving at a site reflect the local mediator pattern, vascular adhesion machinery and properties of the initiating stimulus. Sterile tissue damage may recruit neutrophils too, because signals arising from disrupted host cells can activate inflammatory pathways without a living pathogen.

Population reserves, release and redistribution

A neutrophil population in blood can change over minutes, hours or days for different reasons. Production of new cells requires progenitor proliferation and maturation, while the release of mature cells already stored in marrow can occur without waiting for an entire differentiation programme. Movement between freely sampled blood and vessel-associated pools can alter a count even more rapidly. These mechanisms operate together during many physiological responses, but their different time scales provide clues to the origin of observed changes.

Consider the concentration of neutrophils during a short episode of physical stress. Catecholamine-associated redistribution can contribute to a rapid rise in the number of cells sampled from circulation. Such a change does not require a corresponding burst of cell division in mature neutrophils. If a question states that the rise is rapid and transient and shows no evidence of new production, redistribution is a stronger immediate explanation than proliferation of differentiated circulating cells. Over a longer period, altered marrow output and recruitment can make important contributions. One must not generalise the short-term mechanism to all elevations of neutrophils.

Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, abbreviated G-CSF, influences neutrophil production and mobilisation. Its effects are relevant to a blood-mediated response because tissue demand must be matched to supply. Progenitor responses cannot instantly replace every recruited cell, but they can alter the capacity for later recruitment. The marrow also contains storage populations that can help meet acute needs. The two routes resemble replenishing a stocked warehouse versus increasing factory output, with the limitation that living cells are continually maturing, ageing and responding to signals rather than being inert packages.

A balance of retention and mobilisation signals determines whether developing and mature cells remain in marrow-associated niches or appear in blood. CXCR4-associated retention and CXCR2-associated mobilisation provide a useful conceptual contrast for neutrophil trafficking, although the relative contributions depend on cell maturation and context. Altering the response to one signal may redistribute cells rather than directly altering their ability to kill microbes. This explains why a regulator of cell location is physiologically significant even when it is not an antimicrobial molecule.

Circulating neutrophils do not form a perfectly well-mixed homogeneous compartment. The freely flowing population and transiently vessel-associated populations exchange cells. Vascular beds differ in geometry, flow and the deformability demanded of cells. A decrease in measured blood neutrophils may therefore reflect movement into tissue or temporary sequestration rather than immediate cell destruction. For an examination question, a population that disappears from one sampled compartment has not necessarily disappeared from the body.

A conceptual mass balance can make this precise. Define B as the number in the sampled circulating pool, R as the rate of release into that pool, M as the net movement into it from vessel-associated or other returning populations, and E as the rate of exit or removal. Then dB/dt = R + M − E. Every rate has units of cells per unit time. M may be positive or negative according to the net direction of transfer. This relationship is conservation of number within a defined compartment, not a complete physiological model. It omits the dynamics of the other pools, changes in blood volume and possible differences between measured and average concentrations.

If B remains constant, the equation gives R + M = E. This is a dynamic steady state: individual cells may enter and leave even when population size is unchanged. A steady concentration likewise does not prove steady absolute number if blood volume changes. A useful prediction follows: if tissue exit is reduced while release and other exchanges initially remain unchanged, B tends to increase. If release is reduced while exit continues, B tends to decrease. In living systems feedback may subsequently change multiple rates, so the initial prediction and long-term result must be distinguished.

4. Eosinophils, basophils and tissue mast cells

Eosinophils are granulocytes with proteins concentrated in their distinctive granules. Their functions include responses to helminths and participation in allergic inflammation. A multicellular parasite can be far too large to fit into a single phagosome. Recognition of material at the parasite surface and release of granule contents towards it provide a different geometrical solution to the problem of defence. Products such as major basic protein and eosinophil peroxidase can participate in extracellular damage. They may also harm nearby host cells, especially when eosinophil-rich inflammation is sustained.

Interleukin-5 is particularly associated with eosinophil differentiation, survival and activation. Chemokine signals help direct these cells to selected tissues. An increase in eosinophils in a blood sample may accompany a response to particular parasites or allergic conditions, but does not establish either cause by itself. Some parasite infections elicit little eosinophilia, and eosinophils may accumulate in tissue without a proportional rise in circulating number. The physiological inference should begin with the role of the cell and then incorporate the location and timing of measurement.

Basophils are a rare circulating granulocyte population. Their granules contain histamine and they can participate in responses involving immunoglobulin E, or IgE. When an appropriate multivalent antigen cross-links receptor-bound IgE, signalling can lead to mediator release. Histamine acts on nearby vascular cells, contributing to changes in vessel diameter and permeability. Other mediators recruit or regulate additional leukocytes. The low abundance of basophils does not mean their signalling influence is negligible: secreted molecules can act on many target cells.

Mast cells share important effector features with basophils, including histamine storage and high-affinity IgE receptors, but are not simply the tissue name for basophils. Mast cells are distinct populations that mature in tissues and commonly occupy positions near vessels or epithelial interfaces. Their location permits local detection and mediator release before large numbers of circulating leukocytes arrive. Comparing the two populations illustrates why functional overlap does not establish identity. Blood basophil abundance is not a direct estimate of the tissue mast-cell population.

Geometry and specificity of granulocyte effector responses

A central problem in immune defence is matching the effector to the size and location of the target. A small extracellular particle may be internalised by a professional phagocyte. A much larger organism cannot be enclosed within the same dimensions of membrane. A host cell containing a replicating virus presents yet another problem: ingestion of every intact host cell would not provide a sufficiently selective initial defence. Eosinophil degranulation and lymphocyte cytotoxicity illustrate solutions that differ from classic bacterial phagocytosis. The contrast is about biological context rather than a rigid rule that one leukocyte recognises exactly one type of organism.

Extracellular degranulation exposes nearby structures to concentrated substances. An eosinophil interacting with a helminth surface can release granule-associated proteins near that interface. The proximity of release is important because diffusion dilutes a soluble product as it moves away from its source. However, the interface does not form an impermeable phagosome, and host epithelium may be exposed too. Thus the same effector products may contribute to parasite-associated defence and to injury in an eosinophil-rich allergic response. The net result depends on localisation, amount of release and duration.

IgE-dependent mediator release provides a different example of a cell coupling external recognition to intracellular secretion. Antigen-binding portions of IgE can engage a suitable antigen while the Fc portion associates with a high-affinity IgE receptor. When a multivalent antigen brings receptor-bound antibodies into an appropriate arrangement, the resulting receptor signalling can trigger secretion. Receptor occupancy alone does not necessarily produce the same activation as productive cross-linking. If a teaching experiment applies a monovalent ligand that binds without effectively cross-linking, a weaker degranulation response than with a multivalent ligand would be compatible with this distinction.

Basophils and mast cells are both relevant to histamine-mediated responses, but the timing of their contribution depends on where the cells are before a stimulus appears. A mast cell positioned in connective tissue near a microvessel can influence the vessel locally without first undergoing recruitment. A basophil originating in blood must either act within the vascular compartment or be brought into an appropriate tissue. It is the combination of location, receptor engagement and mediator release that determines effect, rather than the presence of granules alone.

An instructive comparison is between a substance that increases endothelial permeability and a chemokine that supplies a directional recruitment signal. Increased permeability can help soluble proteins and fluid leave the circulation. It does not by itself specify the direction in which an individual leukocyte should migrate. A chemokine can guide responsive cells through a gradient or activate their adhesiveness at an endothelial surface, but is not interchangeable with histamine. The physiological response depends on these distinct signals working in concert. A blockade of one route can leave other parts of the response intact.

5. Monocytes, macrophages and tissue residence

Circulating monocytes can leave the bloodstream and contribute to macrophage populations or other monocyte-derived cells in tissues, particularly during inflammation. Their folded nucleus, broad cytoplasm and phagocytic capacity distinguish them from small resting lymphocytes in many blood films. Their functions include ingestion of material, secretion of mediators and participation in repair. A macrophage is often larger and more extensively adapted to its local environment than a circulating monocyte.

It is important not to interpret a traditional monocyte-to-macrophage diagram as the origin of every resident macrophage. Many tissue-resident macrophage populations are established during development and can maintain themselves locally, while other populations receive substantial input from circulating monocytes. The balance depends on organ, developmental history and inflammatory circumstances. The correct physiological distinction is between the sampled circulating pool and cells residing in a tissue, not a universal linear replacement rule.

At an injured site macrophages can recognise microbes and damaged host material, release recruitment signals and later ingest apoptotic cells. The same broad category can thus contribute to initiating and resolving inflammation. It is not useful to classify every macrophage permanently into just two opposing types: local combinations of cues influence diverse functions. A cell that removes apoptotic neutrophils without spilling their contents may support resolution rather than continued recruitment. This change in the local environment can occur while substantial numbers of blood leukocytes remain elsewhere.

Monocyte concentration reflects marrow output, circulation, tissue entry and loss from the blood pool. The appearance of macrophages in tissue cannot by itself determine which source supplied them. Experimental lineage tracing addresses origin, whereas a single blood film primarily addresses present morphology. The distinction between ancestry and current state is important in interpreting an experiment in which a tissue population expands after injury.

Sentinel location, local maintenance and evidence of cell origin

A macrophage residing near a tissue interface can detect a problem earlier than a circulating cell that must first be recruited. Such a cell may encounter microbial products, particles from damaged cells or metabolic debris. Phagocytosis is not restricted to pathogens: routine clearance of material contributes to normal tissue maintenance. This creates a need to coordinate ingestion with inflammatory signalling. Removal of an apoptotic host cell under noninflammatory conditions is not equivalent to recognition of an invasive microbial population.

In the spleen, macrophage-associated clearance contributes to removal of aged or altered erythrocytes, linking immune-cell physiology to the erythrocyte life cycle in the preceding node. In the liver, resident macrophages associated with sinusoids are positioned to encounter material delivered in blood. In the lung, alveolar macrophages occupy an interface where particles and microorganisms can arrive with inhaled air. These examples illustrate the importance of cellular address. They do not imply that every macrophage in these organs derives from the same progenitor source or responds identically to every stimulus.

Classical cell-classification schemes encouraged a simple model in which marrow-derived blood monocytes continually replenished macrophages throughout the body. The model is useful for understanding recruited inflammatory cells but does not explain all tissue populations. Studies reviewed by Ginhoux and Guilliams have shown the importance of embryonically seeded resident populations in mammalian systems. A student should understand why the evidence changes the model rather than merely memorising the revised terminology.

Suppose an experimental lineage label is introduced at a defined developmental stage into a population of precursors, and its descendants inherit the label. If labelled resident macrophages persist into adulthood even when the circulating monocyte population is largely unlabelled, continuous replacement solely by those adult monocytes would be difficult to reconcile with the result. Local persistence or self-renewal would be plausible. However, label specificity, the timing of induction and the possibility of incomplete marking must be considered. Conversely, the appearance of newly labelled circulating monocyte descendants in damaged tissue can support a contribution of recruited cells under those conditions without proving that every macrophage in the healthy tissue follows that route.

This evidence illustrates a distinction between identity and ancestry. A cell can acquire a macrophage-like morphology and function from more than one developmental origin. Counting cells expressing a macrophage-associated marker after injury can demonstrate a change in a present population; it cannot by itself establish the origin of the new cells. Lineage evidence, longitudinal observations and controls that distinguish recruited from resident populations are more informative. For NET-level reasoning the emphasis is on the logical inference, not the technical details of generating a particular animal line.

6. Lymphocytes and the soluble–cellular partnership

Lymphocytes comprise B cells, T cells and natural killer cells among the major populations examined in elementary physiology. Their common morphological designation does not mean that they carry out the same recognition mechanism. B and T lymphocytes have clonally distributed antigen receptors and participate in adaptive immunity. Natural killer cells use a different repertoire of activating and inhibitory receptors and are innate lymphoid effectors. Small resting members of these groups can look very similar in an ordinary blood film.

B cells may differentiate after appropriate activation into antibody-secreting plasma cells. Many plasma cells are located in tissues such as marrow rather than freely circulating. Antibodies released into extracellular fluid can reach the blood, circulate and gain access to other sites according to vascular permeability and local conditions. Thus the presence of soluble antibody in plasma does not require the simultaneous presence of the producing cell in the same sampled tube. This spatial separation explains why humoral defence can persist after an activating encounter has ended.

Helper T cells coordinate aspects of immune activity through contact-dependent signals and cytokines. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes recognise appropriate antigen-derived peptide presented by major histocompatibility complex molecules and can induce the death of selected target cells. Natural killer cells can also kill selected abnormal cells but integrate activating and inhibitory signals differently. Neither cytotoxic mechanism is equivalent to engulfing an entire target cell and digesting it. Perforin and granzymes are representative cytotoxic effector molecules; the clearance of resulting apoptotic material may then involve phagocytes.

Lymphocyte movement is not restricted to overt inflammation. Recirculation through blood, secondary lymphoid organs and lymph allows cells to encounter antigen and other immune cells at organised sites. Specific address systems on vessels and corresponding cell receptors influence where entry occurs. Blood is consequently a route linking geographically separated compartments rather than the sole site of lymphocyte action. A circulating cell count measures one compartment of this dynamic system.

Movement between blood and lymphoid compartments

The blood sample is especially misleading as a picture of the entire lymphocyte population because many lymphocytes are continually distributed between tissues. A naive lymphocyte is one that has not yet undergone the relevant antigen-driven activation programme. Its movement into appropriate lymphoid tissue increases the probability that it will encounter antigen presented in a suitable context. Lymph nodes receive material from drained tissues and lymphocytes from circulating populations. This spatial organisation concentrates encounters that would be inefficient if every possible antigen-specific cell had to search every peripheral tissue independently.

Specialised high endothelial venules in many lymph nodes support lymphocyte entry from blood. Their endothelial surface provides adhesive and signalling cues suited to this movement. Lymphocytes may subsequently leave through lymphatic channels and return to the blood. This ordinary recirculation differs in purpose from the rapid recruitment of large numbers of neutrophils to an injured venule, although the two processes use related principles of cell adhesion and directional movement. Selective expression of receptors and their ligands helps determine which populations enter particular compartments.

An important consequence is that lymphocyte abundance in blood may fall while a lymphoid organ contains many lymphocytes, or vice versa. If a perturbation prevents exit from a lymph node, for example, blood lymphocytes can be reduced without requiring immediate destruction of the missing cells. If a perturbation prevents entry, blood populations may remain available while the efficiency of encounters in the node falls. To identify a trafficking mechanism experimentally, one needs observations of at least two compartments or direct measures of entry and exit.

Antibodies have a different mode of distribution from the cells that produce them. A soluble protein carried by plasma can reach a wide vascular network without undergoing selectin-dependent rolling. Its entry into tissue depends on properties of the vascular barrier, molecular size, binding and fluid movement. It is therefore possible to detect antibody activity in a sample containing few B lymphocytes. The persistence of that activity depends on secretion, degradation and distribution, rather than on a count of B cells in the sample alone.

Natural killer cells illustrate why classification by lymphocyte morphology does not determine whether an adaptive antigen receptor is present. Their target recognition uses a balance of signals rather than the conventional clonally rearranged receptors of B and T cells. A target cell that alters the normal inhibitory signals received by an NK cell may become more susceptible to attack, especially when activating signals are also present. This is not a universal rule that any cell lacking a particular marker must be killed. The necessary net signal depends on the interacting cells and context. Unit 4 examines those receptor systems in detail; the physiological point here is the contribution of a mobile cytotoxic population to surveillance.

7. Absolute counts, fractions and compartment dynamics

A differential leukocyte count gives the fraction of observed leukocytes assigned to each class. Let W be the total leukocyte concentration in cells per microlitre and f the fraction belonging to a chosen class. The absolute concentration is A = Wf. If a percentage p is supplied, f = p/100. Both W and A have units of cells per microlitre; f has no units. Multiplication rather than subtraction is required because the percentage is a part-to-whole ratio, not an absolute concentration. The expression assumes the total and differential measurements refer to the same blood sample and that the differential is representative.

Consider an illustrative sample with W = 8,000 cells per microlitre and neutrophils making up 60 percent. The absolute neutrophil concentration is 4,800 cells per microlitre. A second sample contains W = 5,000 and 80 percent neutrophils, giving 4,000 cells per microlitre. The percentage increased while the absolute concentration fell. The observation is possible because the concentrations of other leukocytes fell more strongly. A statement that the neutrophils increased is therefore ambiguous unless the variable being compared is specified.

A further distinction separates concentration from total number. If a simplified vascular compartment contains N circulating cells in a blood volume V, the average concentration is N/V. If N remains fixed while V decreases, concentration rises. Real dehydration and fluid shifts may also alter cell trafficking, so this calculation isolates just the dilution effect. It cannot establish the cause of a real measured count. One microlitre equals one cubic millimetre, and a concentration of 5,000 cells per microlitre equals 5 × 10⁹ cells per litre. Errors in this conversion often arise from treating a microlitre as a millilitre.

The number of cells in a circulating compartment changes when marrow release or return from other compartments exceeds tissue recruitment, removal and exit. This mass-balance idea explains why a high count can coexist with impaired tissue defence. If cells enter the bloodstream normally but cannot adhere to vessel walls, they may accumulate upstream of the blocked step. Increased abundance then reflects failed delivery rather than increased antimicrobial efficiency. An exam problem that reports blood and tissue counts separately can distinguish this situation from deficient marrow output, provided other rates are controlled.

Manual differentials have sampling uncertainty. If one basophil is encountered among one hundred classified leukocytes, the reported fraction is one percent, but that estimate has substantial uncertainty because rare events are represented by very few observations. Counting more cells improves precision under representative sampling; it does not remove systematic misclassification. Automated classification can improve throughput, while microscopy provides direct information about morphology. Neither method can directly establish that an identified cell is functionally competent.

Worked reasoning about blood volume and sampling

Suppose a simplified individual has five litres of blood and a uniformly distributed circulating leukocyte concentration of 7 × 10⁹ cells per litre. Multiplying volume and concentration gives 35 × 10⁹ cells in the defined circulating compartment, or 3.5 × 10¹⁰ cells. If sixty percent are neutrophils, the corresponding circulating neutrophil number is 2.1 × 10¹⁰. This estimate is not an estimate of the total body neutrophil population, because marrow stores and cells in other compartments are excluded. The calculation also assumes that the sample represents the circulating compartment and that the blood volume estimate is appropriate.

Now suppose that after an acute fluid shift the blood volume is 4.5 litres and, in a deliberately simplified model, the circulating number remains 35 × 10⁹. The concentration becomes approximately 7.78 × 10⁹ per litre, or an increase of about eleven percent. No extra cells are required for this increase. The distinction matters in experiments using concentration as a proxy for cell production. Measuring a second variable related to volume change, or estimating the total compartment size, may help separate dilution from population effects. Haematocrit can offer contextual evidence of concentration changes, but altered red-cell mass or other physiological factors can also change it.

The differential can be analysed as a composition problem. Let the absolute concentrations of two classes be 6 and 4 in arbitrary equal units. Their fractions are 60% and 40%. If the first class stays at 6 while the second falls to 2, the new fractions are 75% and 25%. The first class did not increase at all, yet its relative fraction increased by fifteen percentage points. Calling the change a fifteen percent increase would also be mathematically ambiguous: relative to the initial fraction, 75/60 − 1 equals a 25% increase. Examination questions often deliberately contrast percentage points with percent change.

If a manual differential counts two hundred cells, and twenty are monocytes, the observed fraction is 0.10. For a simple model of independent random sampling, the approximate standard error of a fraction is the square root of f(1 − f)/n, where n is the number of observations. With f = 0.10 and n = 200, the estimated standard error is about 0.021, or 2.1 percentage points. The model is only approximate because cells may not be sampled independently or uniformly and classification can be uncertain. It helps explain why a small count of rare cells has limited precision, not prescribe a laboratory diagnostic method.

The same sampling principle applies to reading a flow-cytometry plot. A selected gate may include only a subset of viable singlet leukocytes, and the fraction reported within that gate refers to its denominator. A cell population comprising thirty percent of a lymphocyte gate is not necessarily thirty percent of all white cells. If lymphocytes are twenty percent of leukocytes, a subgroup comprising thirty percent of those lymphocytes represents six percent of all leukocytes, assuming nested and representative populations. Students should always identify the denominator before converting percentages into absolute concentrations.

8. From flowing blood to tissue: the adhesion cascade

Leukocytes entering an inflamed tissue must solve a mechanical problem. Blood flow transports them past endothelial surfaces, and firm interaction with the wall cannot occur efficiently if contact is too brief. In many systemic tissues recruitment occurs mainly through postcapillary venules. Vascular geometry, red-cell movement and local haemodynamics help bring leukocytes toward the vessel boundary. This proximity is called margination. It provides an opportunity for adhesive interactions but is not itself synonymous with rolling or firm arrest.

Inflammatory signals such as tumour necrosis factor and interleukin-1 change endothelial behaviour, including the expression of adhesion molecules. Selectins can form transient contacts with appropriate carbohydrate-bearing ligands on the opposing cell. Under flow these interactions repeatedly form and break, slowing the leukocyte into rolling motion. A rolling cell can sample local signals at the endothelial surface. Selectin-mediated rolling is therefore functionally upstream of stable adhesion in the classical cascade, though some vascular beds use variants of this sequence.

Chemokines presented at the vascular surface bind suitable leukocyte receptors, triggering intracellular changes that increase integrin adhesive function. This inside-out signalling permits integrins such as LFA-1 and Mac-1 to engage endothelial counter-receptors including ICAM-1 more effectively. The rolling cell can arrest despite the mechanical forces of flow. VCAM-1 and VLA-4 provide another important adhesive pairing in relevant leukocyte populations. The distinction between selectin bonds and activated integrin interactions is not simply their chemical class; it concerns their distinct roles in coordinating contact with stable arrest.

After arrest, leukocytes can crawl on the endothelial surface toward sites suitable for exit. Transendothelial migration, or diapedesis, often occurs between endothelial cells, with junctional proteins including PECAM-1 contributing in relevant settings. Some cells instead cross through the body of an endothelial cell. Passage across the endothelium is followed by movement through basement membrane and perivascular structures before the cell reaches interstitial tissue. These barriers explain why stopping a cell at the wall and delivering it to the tissue are separable experimental outcomes.

A defect in beta-2 integrins can impair firm adhesion and tissue recruitment even when rolling remains possible. The resulting tendency toward elevated circulating neutrophils and reduced accumulation at infected sites is a classic reasoning example of leukocyte adhesion deficiency. Other defects can affect selectin ligand formation or activation of integrins. The location of the functional block matters more than a general label of impaired immunity. A flow-based observation that distinguishes transient contacts from stable arrest gives more information than counting the cells at the end of the entire recruitment pathway.

Why flow and the endothelium matter

The endothelial lining is not a passive barrier. Its surface molecules, junctions and responses to local signals determine where circulating cells can stop and cross. Under normal conditions most circulating neutrophils do not arrest indiscriminately on every vessel they encounter. When an adjacent tissue releases an inflammatory signal, endothelial activation can alter this local selectivity. This localisation prevents the general circulation from becoming a continuous site of unrestricted leukocyte adhesion.

Rolling involves repeated formation and release of bonds while fluid motion tends to displace the cell. A permanently adhesive first contact would risk arrest wherever a weak interaction happened to occur. A transient interaction allows the cell to slow while continuing to sample the endothelial surface. An appropriate chemokine signal can then change leukocyte adhesion function. This is a useful example of biological information being integrated with mechanical opportunity: the endothelial signal matters most if the cell remains in contact long enough to perceive it.

P-selectin can be mobilised rapidly from endothelial storage compartments, whereas other changes in adhesion molecule expression can require new gene expression. The different time scales make early and later phases of recruitment distinguishable. E-selectin and P-selectin may both contribute to rolling in relevant vascular contexts. On the leukocyte side, proper carbohydrate modifications on ligands are important for selectin recognition. A protein can be present at the surface yet bind poorly if a required glycan is missing. Thus the quantity of an adhesion molecule is not always equivalent to its function.

Integrins are heterodimeric receptors with alpha and beta subunits. Their capacity for ligand binding can change with receptor conformation, localisation and clustering. Inside-out signalling describes the process in which an intracellular signal modifies the external adhesive capability of a receptor. Once ligand binding occurs, outside-in signalling can further influence cytoskeletal behaviour. These changes permit dynamic arrest and movement rather than requiring a cell to remain maximally adhesive at all times. A mutation that leaves the receptor detectable but prevents its activation may produce a functional phenotype resembling receptor deficiency.

LFA-1 contains a beta-2 subunit shared with related leukocyte integrins. Its engagement with ICAM-family ligands contributes to arrest and related interactions, while Mac-1 has additional roles including crawling and recognition of certain complement-coated particles. The overlap explains why defects in shared subunits can affect more than one measured activity. Nevertheless, if a question provides separate evidence of normal uptake and impaired firm adhesion, the defect should be interpreted at the level supported by the data rather than assuming all possible functions are abnormal.

Not all leukocyte recruitment follows the same arrangement. In some specialised beds, notably parts of the pulmonary microcirculation, cell deformation and other adhesive mechanisms can contribute in ways that differ from the classical postcapillary venule model. Certain lymphocyte interactions and some monocyte routes involve different integrin–ligand combinations. The classical cascade is a powerful conceptual model because it identifies separable operations, but its implementation is not universal in every organ or cell type. An examination statement containing 'all leukocytes' or 'every tissue' should be evaluated carefully when a mechanism is context dependent.

Once a leukocyte has crossed the endothelial layer, the extracellular environment imposes another movement problem. It must find paths through interstitial structures while following appropriate signals. Crawling on the vascular surface, crossing the barrier and migrating within tissue can use overlapping cytoskeletal machinery but are different measurable events. A perturbation that changes junctional passage may leave initial arrest unaffected. A perturbation that compromises directional sensing may allow crossing yet reduce localisation at the final source of inflammation. Tracking individual cells at consecutive stages is therefore more informative than a final total alone.

9. Directional migration in interstitial tissue

Chemotaxis is a directed component of cell movement in response to a spatial chemical gradient. It differs from chemokinesis, a change in motility that does not require net directional bias. A neutrophil may move quickly in a uniform concentration of an activating substance and yet show little movement towards a selected side. Conversely a slower cell that persistently moves up a gradient may reach a source more effectively than a faster wandering cell.

Chemoattractants include CXCL8 in human inflammatory settings, complement-derived C5a and certain microbial formylated peptides. These signals act through suitable receptors and influence cell polarity. A moving cell forms a leading edge where actin polymerisation favours protrusion and a trailing region where contraction helps retract the cell. Directional sensing and the organisation of movement are coupled but distinct: a cell could detect unequal receptor stimulation while failing to convert that information into effective motion if its cytoskeleton is impaired.

To quantify directional bias in a simplified two-dimensional observation, project the displacement between the start and finish onto the gradient axis. Divide that signed displacement by the total distance travelled. A cell travelling 100 micrometres in total and ending 40 micrometres farther towards the source has an index of 0.40. A cell ending 20 micrometres in the opposite direction after the same travel has an index of −0.20. The index is dimensionless, and under consistent measurement its magnitude cannot exceed one. It measures directional efficiency rather than velocity, which requires a time interval.

An experiment comparing a gradient with a uniform concentration should match exposure conditions as far as possible, measure motility as well as direction and include a control without attractant. Otherwise the appearance of more cells at the far side of a chamber could result from increased random movement or differential survival rather than directional sensing. In actual tissue the gradient may be shaped by secretion, diffusion, degradation, extracellular binding and flow. There is no requirement that a concentration gradient remain spatially simple.

From spatial information to measurable migration

A concentration gradient is a spatial change in concentration. In a one-dimensional teaching example, the local gradient can be approximated by the concentration difference between two positions divided by their separation. If the concentration of an attractant is 4 nanomolar at one position and 8 nanomolar at a second position 100 micrometres away, the average gradient along the chosen axis is 0.04 nanomolar per micrometre. The sign depends on the direction chosen as positive. This calculation describes the external distribution of the substance, not the cell's sensitivity to it.

For a small moving cell, the concentration difference across its body may be much smaller than the difference across a tissue region. A gradient of 0.04 nanomolar per micrometre would give an approximate difference of 0.4 nanomolar across a ten-micrometre distance if the gradient were linear. Receptor occupancy at opposite regions of the cell can consequently differ only slightly. A cell must turn small differences in external information into an organised change in cytoskeletal behaviour. It does not require separate organs for sensing and movement; local and cell-wide signalling can coordinate protrusion and contraction within a single cell.

The response depends on the absolute concentration as well as on the spatial difference. In a simplified receptor binding model, fractional occupancy at equilibrium is C/(C + Kd), where C is free ligand concentration and Kd is a dissociation constant with the same concentration units. The formula assumes a single class of independent binding sites and simple equilibrium. It is not a complete model of a migrating leukocyte, but it illustrates why the same absolute change in concentration need not give the same difference in fractional occupancy at every baseline concentration.

For example, take a hypothetical receptor with Kd = 10 nanomolar. At 5 nanomolar its equilibrium occupancy is 5/15, or about 0.33; at 6 nanomolar it is 6/16, or about 0.375. The difference is about 0.042. At 100 and 101 nanomolar the occupancies are 100/110 and 101/111, a difference of only about 0.00082. The ligand difference is one nanomolar in both comparisons, but the simple occupancy difference is far smaller at the high baseline because the sites are approaching saturation. This example explains why strong uniform receptor stimulation cannot be equated with a strong directional signal.

Living leukocytes are not equilibrium binding detectors. They can adapt to previous exposure, change receptor distribution, internalise receptors and respond to several attractants simultaneously. A gradient may also change while cells move. The simple occupancy calculation therefore gives a limiting principle rather than a prediction of actual migration speed or direction. When an exam asks how a uniform high ligand concentration might affect gradient sensing, it is appropriate to discuss receptor saturation as one possible mechanism, but additional cellular regulation can be important.

The measurement of movement also requires clarity about what is being measured. In a time-lapse record of a cell, speed is total path length divided by elapsed time. Net displacement is the straight-line separation of the initial and final positions, and the projected displacement is its component along a specified axis. Directional efficiency along a gradient can be compared by dividing the projected displacement by the total path length. A stationary cell has no meaningful value for a path-normalised index because its denominator is zero. Assigning it an arbitrary value of zero may be convenient for a particular analysis but changes the definition and must be reported.

In a hypothetical experiment, three equally viable populations are observed for five minutes. A control cell travels 25 micrometres with zero projected displacement; a uniformly stimulated cell travels 100 micrometres with 5 micrometres projected displacement; a gradient-exposed cell travels 75 micrometres with 60 micrometres projected displacement. Their speeds are 5, 20 and 15 micrometres per minute, respectively. Their projected efficiencies are 0, 0.05 and 0.80. The uniformly stimulated cell is fastest, but the gradient-exposed cell shows the most effective directed movement. A conclusion based only on speed would miss the property of interest.

Multiple cells and repeated experiments would be required to establish a robust population difference. One trajectory can be unusually straight or tortuous by chance, and a sample may include stationary, damaged or differently activated cells. Measuring distributions of speed and direction is more informative than presenting a representative path alone. The logic applies to both neutrophil and other leukocyte migration. It also connects to cell biology: a cytoskeletal perturbation may reduce speed and directional movement, while a defect in signal recognition may primarily reduce bias in otherwise mobile cells.

10. Particle capture and intracellular destruction

Phagocytosis begins with recognition and attachment of a suitably sized target. Receptors can recognise microbial surface structures directly or bind host molecules already attached to a target. An opsonin is a molecule that enhances the recognition and uptake of a particle by a phagocyte. IgG and C3b-derived fragments provide important examples. An opsonin may increase the probability of attachment without directly damaging the particle. The coating and the receptor that recognises it must both be available for the intended bridge to function.

When receptor engagement initiates ingestion, the membrane extends around the target under the control of cytoskeletal machinery. Completion of enclosure produces a phagosome. Attachment at the outer cell surface, internalisation and destruction must be distinguished. A microscopy image showing many bacteria associated with a cell does not prove that all of them are inside, and a fluorescent signal from an internalised bacterium does not establish that the bacterium is dead. Separate assays are required when these stages are being compared.

Phagosomal membranes and contents are modified by interactions with intracellular vesicles. In macrophages, progressive maturation generally leads towards an acidic degradative compartment with lysosomal components. Neutrophil phagosomes receive granule contents and undergo dynamic ion and pH changes; they should not be assumed to acidify in exactly the same way at every stage as macrophage phagosomes. The common functional outcome is localisation of antimicrobial chemistry near the captured particle. Proteases, antimicrobial peptides and other granule or lysosomal components contribute even when oxidative killing is impaired.

The phagocyte NADPH oxidase transfers electrons from cytosolic NADPH to oxygen on the opposite side of a membrane, producing superoxide. This increase in oxygen consumption is termed the respiratory burst, and is distinct from mitochondrial ATP production. Superoxide can undergo dismutation to produce hydrogen peroxide and oxygen. Myeloperoxidase in neutrophil granules can use hydrogen peroxide and chloride to generate hypochlorous acid. The reactions depend upon compartmentalisation and redox substrates. A defect in the oxidase removes a major source of reactive oxygen products; a defect in myeloperoxidase alters one downstream reaction without necessarily removing superoxide formation.

Chronic granulomatous disease illustrates the separation between ingestion and effective killing. A phagocyte with a deficient oxidase can still attach to and engulf material, but oxidative antimicrobial defence is impaired. An oxidant-sensitive fluorescence assay can report a defect in burst-associated chemistry under controlled stimulation. It does not alone measure viable microorganisms or prove that every other killing system is absent. An interpretation combining internalisation, oxidative activity and survival of captured microbes provides stronger evidence than any one readout.

Oxidative chemistry as a compartmental process

The phagosome is not simply a static bag. Its boundary separates an aqueous lumen containing the target from the cytoplasm of the cell. Receptors and membrane trafficking establish the compartment, while transport proteins and enzymes alter its chemistry. An enzyme on the membrane can receive a substrate from one side and release a product on the other. NADPH oxidase provides a useful illustration: electrons obtained from cytosolic NADPH are passed across the membrane to oxygen in the lumen or extracellular side, depending on the location of the active complex.

A simplified half-reaction for NADPH oxidation is NADPH → NADP⁺ + H⁺ + 2e⁻. Oxygen accepts one electron to form superoxide, O₂ + e⁻ → O₂•⁻. Combining two oxygen reduction events with the NADPH oxidation gives NADPH + 2O₂ → NADP⁺ + H⁺ + 2O₂•⁻. These equations describe electron accounting rather than the full structural mechanism of oxidase assembly. They explain why the production of superoxide involves oxygen consumption without requiring that oxygen reduction occur through the mitochondrial respiratory chain.

Superoxide can undergo dismutation according to 2O₂•⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O₂ + O₂. Myeloperoxidase can use peroxide in a reaction with chloride and a proton to form hypochlorous acid and water: H₂O₂ + Cl⁻ + H⁺ → HOCl + H₂O. These reactions illustrate the difference between primary oxygen reduction and downstream modification of the products. They do not imply that every superoxide molecule generated in a phagosome ultimately produces hypochlorous acid. Competing chemical reactions, local pH and substrate availability influence the mixture of reactive products.

The movement of charge during oxidase activity creates a physiological need for compensating ion movements. If electrons were continuously moved into a sealed compartment without compensation, the resulting electrical gradient would oppose further transfer. Ion channels and other transport processes help regulate the developing electrochemical conditions. Proton consumption and movement, granule fusion and other factors influence pH. As recent reviews of the neutrophil phagosome emphasise, its early chemical environment is not adequately described by importing a simplified macrophage lysosome model unchanged.

This complexity explains why a defect in one oxidative enzyme does not eliminate every antimicrobial mechanism. An oxidase-deficient cell may still deliver granule proteins, and a cell with reduced myeloperoxidase activity may still generate superoxide and peroxide. Conversely, normal generation of reactive products does not guarantee normal ingestion or appropriate spatial delivery. A cell that releases an active oxidase product outside the phagosome may produce a strong extracellular readout while failing to deliver equivalent chemistry to the target.

Nonoxidative killing includes the effects of antimicrobial peptides and proteases, and the chemical conditions of the compartment can influence these molecules. The contributions of different mechanisms depend on the type of phagocyte and the susceptibility of the organism. It is therefore incorrect to rank oxidative and nonoxidative killing as universally dominant and dispensable, respectively. The appropriate comparison asks how the systems cooperate under a defined condition and how an experimental perturbation affects the final outcome.

11. Inflammation, vascular change and resolution

Acute inflammation begins when local surveillance systems detect infection or tissue damage. Resident cells and soluble recognition systems initiate the response before large numbers of newly recruited leukocytes arrive. Cytokines influence endothelial adhesion and systemic responses, chemokines help guide cellular movement, and lipid or amine mediators can change vascular behaviour. These mediators are not interchangeable simply because they participate in the same broad response.

Vasodilation increases local blood flow and contributes to redness and warmth. Increased permeability permits more fluid and selected plasma proteins to enter the extracellular space, producing swelling when drainage cannot match accumulation. Pain can arise from chemical sensitisation of sensory endings and mechanical consequences of swelling. Reduced function can be a consequence of pain or tissue injury. The classical signs describe physiological consequences, not a test that uniquely identifies a pathogen. An internal inflammatory site may lack externally visible redness while still showing substantial vascular change.

Inflammatory signals also affect marrow release and the behaviour of circulating cells. In systemic responses, cytokine signalling can influence acute-phase protein production by the liver. C-reactive protein is one example of a soluble recognition protein whose abundance can change during inflammation. Its concentration reflects a response rather than identifying the initiating stimulus by itself. A marker of inflammation must not be treated as a direct count of invading organisms.

Successful inflammation includes termination. Once the stimulus is removed, recruitment signals decline and existing effector cells may undergo apoptosis. Macrophages can ingest apoptotic cells, a process often termed efferocytosis. Containment of intracellular contents and changes in mediator production support repair. Failure to clear debris or continued detection of microbial and injury signals can prolong recruitment. In that circumstance antimicrobial mechanisms may become a source of tissue damage. Physiological immunity therefore includes both activation and control.

Local vascular responses and systemic coordination

Local inflammation can be understood as a sequence of changes in the exchange between blood and tissue. A previously quiescent microvascular bed delivers oxygen and nutrients with controlled movement of water and solutes. Following a stimulus, local changes in vascular tone and barrier properties alter both the amount and the composition of the material delivered. More blood flow can increase local warmth; increased permeability can allow proteins normally restricted more strongly to plasma to enter interstitial fluid. Recruitment changes the cellular component of that exchange. The three processes are linked but one should not infer the other automatically.

Imagine that an experimental intervention increases vessel diameter but does not change permeability or endothelial adhesion molecule function. Flow and delivery may change, yet the conditions that allow leukocytes to arrest and proteins to escape are not necessarily altered to the same extent. Conversely, a change in endothelial junctions may increase escape of fluid and protein without producing an equivalent change in the upstream diameter of a vessel. An inflamed area can exhibit all these processes together because the mediators overlap in time, not because they are physically identical.

Protein movement into tissue affects fluid balance. Albumin and other plasma proteins normally contribute to differences in oncotic pressure between vascular and extravascular compartments. Increased permeability can allow more protein to leave the circulation and change local water movement. Lymphatic drainage returns excess fluid and proteins to the circulation, but swelling develops if net accumulation exceeds removal. The full description of fluid exchange involves the endothelial glycocalyx and local vascular properties and is reserved for the later blood-volume and cardiovascular chapters. It is enough here to recognise that vascular permeability and drainage both influence inflammatory oedema.

Histamine is a rapidly acting mediator whose effects can occur before changes requiring substantial new protein synthesis. Cytokines such as TNF and IL-1 can alter endothelial activation, including adhesive properties, and can contribute to systemic signalling. Chemokines have more directly directional roles in recruitment. Prostaglandins and other lipid mediators influence aspects of vascular response and sensory sensitivity. These general roles are not exclusive; a single mediator may act in multiple processes, and the same observable effect may have several contributors. A mechanistic question should distinguish the cell that releases a mediator from the cell or tissue that responds to it.

A signal produced at a small wound can influence distant organs if it reaches the circulation or triggers a secondary signal. Interleukin-6 is an important mediator of changes in liver acute-phase protein production. C-reactive protein can increase as part of such a systemic response. Its value as a measured variable derives from the connection between local inflammation and liver synthesis, not from the idea that it is produced by every leukocyte at the injured site. Some acute-phase proteins increase and others decrease, reflecting a changed pattern of hepatic output rather than a universal increase in plasma protein concentration.

An increase in inflammatory mediators can also affect temperature regulation and marrow supply. During fever the thermoregulatory system operates around an altered regulated temperature, whereas nonregulated overheating has a different physiological basis. The detailed mechanism belongs in the thermoregulation chapter. For this node the relevant connection is that immune mediators circulating through blood can alter functions of organs far away from the original stimulus. Blood serves as a signal transport system in addition to its role in transporting cells and antimicrobial proteins.

The systemic consequences of inflammation are not always beneficial. If vascular permeability increases widely, fluid distribution and effective circulation may be compromised; if inflammatory activation is extensive, excessive mediator production may disrupt normal organ function. The usefulness of a local response therefore depends partly on confinement. A physiological response that improves delivery of immune components at a limited site can become damaging if activated throughout the vasculature. This is a general example of why the location and intensity of a feedback response matter as much as its molecular identity.

Resolution is more than exhaustion of a response. The local environment must stop generating signals that attract new cells, and existing cellular products and tissue debris must be cleared. Neutrophil apoptosis, subsequent efferocytosis and changes in macrophage activities can support this transition. A perturbation that blocks new recruitment may reduce further tissue accumulation yet leave a large burden of dying cells and damaged material behind. A perturbation that improves clearance may change the tissue state even without directly blocking the early vascular response. These distinctions are important when reasoning about the temporal phases of inflammation.

A simple population model describes the number of neutrophils in tissue, T, by dT/dt = J − L, where J is entry per unit time and L is net removal per unit time. If both equal 500 cells per hour, the tissue number is steady even though cells are entering and leaving. If entry declines to 100 while removal remains 500, the initial rate of decline is 400 cells per hour. Conversely if removal fails, cell numbers may remain elevated even after entry has decreased substantially. The model does not specify the mechanism of loss: apoptosis followed by clearance, exit and other processes can all contribute. It demonstrates why cessation of recruitment and completion of resolution are not identical events.

Timing also matters when interpreting a mixed tissue infiltrate. Neutrophils are often prominent early in acute responses and monocyte-derived cells may become more evident later, but there is no fixed clock that applies to every organ or stimulus. An increase in macrophage-associated cells may indicate recruitment, local expansion or a mixture of both. Tissue snapshots are most informative when compared across appropriate time points and with independent evidence about origin and state. The sequence is a useful teaching pattern rather than a universal diagnostic schedule.

12. Antibodies and complement in circulating blood

Antibodies recognise molecular features called antigens through their antigen-binding regions. The remaining portions of an antibody can engage other immune molecules and cells, depending upon isotype and context. Neutralisation occurs when antibody binding interferes with a harmful function, such as attachment of a microbial product to a target receptor. Opsonisation instead makes a target easier for phagocytes to recognise. These are distinct outcomes, although the same antibody population can contribute to more than one protective effect.

IgG coating of an extracellular particle can promote ingestion through appropriate Fc gamma receptors. An antibody fragment retaining antigen binding but lacking the Fc region may still neutralise a toxin, yet lose this particular route of phagocyte recruitment. Such a comparison separates recognition from effector coupling. IgM is often efficient at initiating classical complement activation when bound appropriately to antigen; its efficiency in this role does not imply that it interacts with phagocyte Fc gamma receptors like IgG. The exact class and arrangement of antibody on a target determine the consequences of binding.

Complement consists of soluble and membrane-associated components that cooperate in a regulated amplification system. Classical initiation can follow recognition of suitable antibody-coated targets; lectin initiation recognises particular surface carbohydrates; alternative activation and amplification can proceed without specific antibody. All three routes can promote cleavage of C3, generating fragments with distinct functions. C3b and its processed products assist opsonisation, whereas C5a is a particularly potent inflammatory recruitment signal. The terminal C5b–9 membrane attack complex can damage susceptible membranes. These outcomes explain why complement deficiency at different stages leads to different functional consequences.

Complement is not uniformly bactericidal against all organisms, and its host targets must be protected by regulatory proteins. Surface-associated regulators such as CD55 and CD59 restrain different stages of activation or terminal pore formation. Without adequate regulation, the same amplification that benefits defence can injure host membranes. Complement and antibodies should consequently be understood as part of a coordinated, spatially regulated system. Unit 7 develops the molecular details of convertases and regulation; here their relevance is that soluble recognition, inflammatory recruitment and phagocyte capture can occur through one connected response.

Serum protection, clearance and the logic of complementation

The distinction between serum and plasma is relevant when assessing humoral activity. Plasma is the fluid obtained when clotting is prevented during preparation, whereas serum is the fluid remaining after clot formation and removal. Some soluble immune activities can be studied in serum, but sample preparation can alter the activity of proteins involved in complement or coagulation. An experiment comparing these fractions should not assume that their composition is identical except for one arbitrarily chosen protein.

A classic conceptual experiment compares microbial survival in a serum-containing condition with survival after conditions that inactivate heat-sensitive complement activity. If killing is reduced in the second condition, a heat-sensitive factor is implicated, but heating could affect multiple components and cannot by itself prove the identity of the responsible factor. Restoration by adding a source of functional complement under suitable controlled conditions strengthens the proposed connection. Antibody binding can be assessed separately to determine whether recognition remains intact while downstream effector activity is lost.

This reasoning parallels genetic complementation logic: reintroducing the missing functional contribution can distinguish a defect in recognition from a defect in execution. It should not be assumed that every preparation can be restored by a single component; multiple defects or inhibitors can change the result. A controlled comparison of appropriate serum fractions, antibody binding, target survival and an independent complement readout supplies more specific evidence than a simple observation that a sample is bactericidal.

The ability of complement to label particles can also support their removal from blood without requiring direct membrane lysis. A phagocyte with appropriate receptors can recognise deposited complement fragments and ingest the coated material. Erythrocytes in humans can participate in transport of some complement-coated immune complexes through complement receptors, helping deliver them to sites of clearance. This is a useful bridge between the preceding erythrocyte chapter and the current topic, but it does not convert erythrocytes into professional phagocytes. The red cell provides a transport surface, while macrophage-associated processes contribute to removal.

An antibody can agglutinate suitable particles when its binding sites cross-link targets into larger assemblies. Agglutination is different from membrane lysis and from neutralisation, although it may facilitate other processes. This distinction becomes especially important in blood group physiology, where antibodies recognising erythrocyte surface antigens can promote clumping and, under appropriate conditions, haemolysis. The next blood-group chapter develops this mechanism in its specific context. Here it illustrates that humoral recognition can protect the host or cause injury depending on the identity and location of the recognised surface.

The complement system also contributes to the physiological removal of damaged material. Because complement activation can amplify, regulators limit where and for how long that activity occurs. CD55 accelerates decay of certain enzyme complexes involved in activation; CD59 interferes with terminal pore assembly. A defect in one regulator is therefore not equivalent to a general absence of complement. In particular, loss of host protection can increase inappropriate injury even though circulating complement proteins are present. This distinction between deficient activation and deficient regulation is a recurrent pattern across physiology.

Cooperation of vascular defence with clearance and haemostasis

Blood-mediated immunity is especially important when protective action must occur within the circulating compartment itself. A particle introduced into blood can be transported rapidly to other regions. Soluble recognition factors can interact with it while it circulates, and phagocytes positioned in filtering organs can help remove it. The result is different from a local tissue response in which cells first leave a postcapillary venule and travel through extracellular tissue towards a source. Location determines which sequence of events is appropriate.

The liver and spleen provide useful examples of sites where blood encounters specialised cellular environments. In the liver, sinusoidal blood flow places circulating material in contact with resident macrophages associated with the vascular interface. In the spleen, organised regions support interactions involving blood-borne material, phagocytes and lymphocytes. These organs are not interchangeable filters: their anatomical organisation and cell populations differ. The general point is that clearance involves an encounter between flowing material and appropriately positioned recognition or effector systems. A conventional peripheral blood count does not directly measure this encounter rate.

A simple transport model clarifies the distinction between delivery and removal. Suppose a vascular bed receives a fluid flow Q, expressed in millilitres per minute, with an incoming particle concentration C in particles per millilitre. The number of particles delivered per minute is QC. If a fraction E is removed in one passage, the removal rate attributable to that passage is QCE. All three variables have distinct meanings: flow determines delivery, concentration determines how many particles travel with a volume of blood, and the dimensionless extraction fraction describes the fraction removed under specified conditions.

For an illustrative flow of 20 millilitres per minute and an inlet concentration of 100 particles per millilitre, delivery is 2,000 particles per minute. If the extraction fraction is 0.25, removal is 500 particles per minute. A doubling of flow would double delivery at constant inlet concentration, but it would not necessarily double removal in a real organ because extraction can change with residence time, receptor availability or saturation of removal capacity. The numerical example describes a one-pass idealisation and should not be read as a measured organ clearance rate. Its value is to show that transport and cellular efficiency can influence the same observed removal outcome.

An examination problem may describe an increased particle concentration downstream of an organ despite a normal number of resident macrophages. This could result from reduced recognition, altered blood flow, saturation of uptake capacity or a change in the target surface. The downstream observation alone cannot discriminate among those mechanisms. Measurements of inlet and outlet concentration and flow can establish a net extraction rate under appropriate assumptions. Functional assays can then examine whether recognition and ingestion have changed. Counting resident cells addresses yet another question, that of availability.

The protective response to vascular injury also interacts with haemostasis. Injury may expose surfaces and release mediators that influence both leukocyte recruitment and the behaviour of platelets. Platelets can communicate with leukocytes and endothelial cells, and inflammatory conditions may influence coagulation. This interaction does not mean that a leukocyte is a platelet or that every inflammatory response is a clotting response. The immediate goal of haemostasis is to limit blood loss following vascular damage, while immune responses recognise and remove threats and damaged material. In an injured tissue these objectives can operate simultaneously.

Consider the potential trade-off if cellular and soluble defence are activated indiscriminately within an intact vessel. Adhesive cells, secreted granule products and extensive inflammatory signalling can impair normal vascular function. Excessive clot-associated activity may obstruct flow and thereby compromise the transport of oxygen and nutrients. Conversely, failure to recruit protective cells and failure to contain a breach can each impair recovery in different ways. Healthy physiology depends upon restricting the site, strength and duration of these responses. This chapter has followed how leukocytes and plasma immune components are delivered and controlled; the next node considers the distinct sequence by which platelets help form an initial haemostatic plug.

13. Experiments that distinguish cell abundance from function

A teaching experiment can compare neutrophils from three otherwise matched preparations: a reference population, a population lacking effective firm adhesion and a population with deficient oxidative activity. Under flow, quantify rolling cells and stably adherent cells, then assess tissue-side migration separately. In a different assay, supply an equal number of particles to cells and distinguish internalised material from surface-associated material. Finally compare an oxidative readout and microbial survival. These observations isolate different steps and avoid confusing delivery with destruction.

In an illustrative dataset, reference cells show 80 rolling events, 60 arrests and 45 successful crossings per fixed observation period; adhesion-deficient cells show 78 rolling events, 5 arrests and 2 crossings. Oxidase-deficient cells show 79 rolling events, 58 arrests and 43 crossings. In an independent particle-uptake assay all three preparations internalise similar numbers of particles, whereas oxidative fluorescence is very low only in the third population. The first abnormal preparation has a defect downstream of rolling, and the second has a defect in oxidative effector function rather than recruitment. These are hypothetical teaching numbers, not data from a published study.

Controls are essential to this inference. If fewer viable cells are supplied to one chamber, a reduction in arrests could follow simply from fewer available cells. If the endothelial layer differs between groups, adhesion may vary for reasons unrelated to leukocyte function. A positive oxidant-sensitive signal requires an appropriate dye and stimulation, and the assay should account for baseline fluorescence. Multiple biological preparations are needed to generalise beyond a single sample. The logic of controlled comparison is a core examination skill because different mechanisms frequently generate the same gross phenotype.

A second experiment can use identical particles with or without IgG coating, and compare uptake in cells with functional or blocked Fc gamma receptors. Enhanced uptake that disappears specifically after receptor blockade supports the proposed coupling mechanism. It does not exclude direct recognition of the uncoated particle by other receptors. Including an uncoated-particle condition and a control treatment for blockade helps interpret the change. A separate complement-containing condition can investigate whether coating through C3-derived fragments supplies an alternative recognition route.

The unifying lesson is that blood-mediated defence is a sequence of conditional capabilities. A healthy count does not guarantee delivery, attachment does not guarantee ingestion, ingestion does not guarantee killing, and killing does not guarantee resolution. When a question describes a perturbation, locate the earliest affected step and then predict which upstream observations may remain normal and which downstream observations should change.

Integrating blood data, functional tests and mechanistic predictions

The value of blood physiology for problem solving becomes particularly clear when several apparently contradictory measurements are supplied together. Consider three illustrative populations with the same total leukocyte count. One has a low fraction of neutrophils and high lymphocyte fraction, another has normal fractions but weak phagocyte oxidative activity, and a third has increased neutrophil abundance with impaired tissue recruitment. The first difference concerns composition, the second function within a cell and the third transfer between compartments. A test of only total concentration would fail to distinguish all three.

An independent evaluation of recruitment can use observation of cells interacting with an endothelial surface under flow. First count the number of supplied viable cells crossing a reference region in a specified interval. Then count how many make sustained rolling contacts, how many arrest and how many cross. The ratios must be interpreted with a clear denominator. If 100 cells are available and 80 roll, capture occurs in 80 percent of the input under the defined conditions. If 60 of those 80 arrest, the conditional arrest fraction is 75 percent among rollers, but 60 percent among the initial input. These figures answer different questions about the same experiment.

Suppose that a treatment reduces the number of rolling cells from 80 to 20 while the fraction of rollers that arrest remains 75 percent. Total arrests decline from 60 to 15. The treatment has reduced the overall number of arrested cells, yet the conditional ability of a rolling cell to arrest appears unchanged. A conclusion that integrins were necessarily inhibited because the total arrest count fell would be unsupported. The earliest detectable defect lies in obtaining rolling contacts. By contrast, if rolling stays at 80 and arrest falls from 60 to 15, the conditional arrest fraction declines from 75 to 18.75 percent, supporting a defect at or before stable arrest after capture.

The same conditional reasoning applies to phagocytosis. In a simplified sequential model, let a be the fraction of offered particles that attach, b the fraction of attached particles that are internalised and c the fraction of internalised particles that lose viability during a defined period. The proportion of all offered particles rendered nonviable by this route is abc, assuming the stages are nested and nonviable particles are counted within that route. If a = 0.8, b = 0.75 and c = 0.5, the fraction is 0.30. An intervention doubling c from 0.5 to 1 would raise that fraction to 0.60 if other stages remained unchanged.

This product rule is useful for understanding why a large improvement in one stage may have little effect if another is severely limiting. Suppose attachment is only 0.1 while the other stages are already near maximal. Even perfect intracellular killing could eliminate only a small fraction of the initially offered particles by the specified route. Increasing attachment might be more important in that case. The model excludes extracellular killing, repeated capture attempts, microbial growth and competition between particles. These omissions must be remembered when interpreting the simplified result as a biological prediction rather than a complete description of an infection.

A well-designed comparison of uptake and killing also needs to account for the initial number of viable particles. If two preparations start with different numbers, their final viable counts cannot be compared as if the inputs were equal. Fractional survival relative to the appropriate initial population may be more useful. Likewise, a smaller final number of viable organisms could reflect slower proliferation rather than immediate killing. A time-matched target-only control helps distinguish intrinsic changes in the target population from effects associated with the immune cells. Multiple readouts and time points strengthen the interpretation.

An antibody-mediated uptake experiment illustrates the value of a factorial design. Vary two conditions independently: whether particles are coated with a suitable antibody and whether cells have functional Fc receptors. If uptake increases with coating only when those receptors are functional, the pattern suggests that the two conditions cooperate. If coating also improves uptake when Fc recognition is absent, another route may contribute, such as complement-dependent opsonisation if functional complement is present. The correct interpretation depends on the conditions actually included. An experiment performed without complement does not establish what would happen in intact blood.

Consider another illustrative set of observations: uncoated particles produce 15 units of uptake in reference cells and 14 units after Fc receptor blockade; coated particles produce 55 units in reference cells and 17 units after blockade. The large coating-associated increment is diminished after blockade. A difference between 55 and 17 cannot simply be attributed to toxicity without checking the uncoated baseline, which remains similar. Nevertheless, a viability and treatment control is required before concluding that receptor function alone caused the change. In a data interpretation question, the strongest claim is normally that the increment is associated with functional Fc-mediated recognition under the specified conditions, not that every form of uptake depends on it.

A complement-dependent lysis experiment can similarly be designed so that antibody binding, complement deposition and target membrane integrity are measured separately. If antibody binding is present but no C3-derived deposition occurs, an early complement activation problem is plausible. If deposition occurs but terminal lysis fails in a susceptible target, later events or protective surface regulation deserve consideration. If a phagocyte is added, deposition may still enhance uptake despite failure of lysis. The physiological outcomes of complement are therefore branches, not one compulsory linear readout. A deficiency that removes one branch does not necessarily eliminate all protection by the system.

The choice of biological target is central to experimental interpretation. A membrane attack mechanism that is effective against a susceptible microorganism may be ineffective against a target with an outer structure that restricts access or with protective mechanisms. A phagocyte that efficiently ingests one particle may not efficiently ingest another with a different surface. Experimental findings should not be extended to every organism without evidence. This is why exam questions often specify an extracellular bacterium, a helminth, a virus-infected host cell or an inert opsonised bead. Each places different constraints on the effector response.

Finally, one should distinguish a causal intervention from a correlation. If high neutrophil numbers accompany a high inflammatory marker, both could have been induced by a common upstream stimulus. It does not follow that neutrophil abundance directly produced the entire marker response. An intervention that selectively changes recruitment, compared with a matched reference, can provide stronger mechanistic evidence than a single association. Even an intervention may alter several variables at once, so the interpretation should acknowledge specificity and possible secondary effects. The purpose of reasoning from blood is not to attach a disease label to a count, but to identify which physiological mechanisms are compatible with the observed data.

Choosing evidence for an integrated physiological explanation

The final step in interpreting a blood-mediated immune response is to decide which observations would discriminate competing explanations. Consider a model in which a soluble coating is proposed to improve the clearance of particles from blood. A reduction in the concentration of circulating particles could be compatible with the proposal, but several other processes could generate the same observation. Particles may redistribute into tissue, lose detectable fluorescence, adhere to a surface without being internalised or be eliminated through a different route. To support increased phagocytic removal, one needs evidence linking coating to recognition, internalisation and the fate of the particle.

This is why a physiological investigation often benefits from measuring a process at several points rather than one final endpoint. Blood concentration can establish that material is disappearing from the sampled compartment. Tissue imaging can identify where it accumulates. A location-sensitive uptake measurement can distinguish enclosure from attachment. A separate measure of target viability or degradation can establish the fate of the captured material. Each observation narrows a different class of alternative explanations. The strongest inference arises when the measurements agree with the predicted sequence and when relevant controls exclude changes unrelated to the proposed mechanism.

A similar approach applies to inflammation. An increased number of cells near a damaged vessel may result from increased entry, slower removal or a change in local retention. Measurements taken before and after a defined perturbation can reveal temporal order, but temporal order alone is not proof of causation. A selective perturbation of an adhesion interaction, accompanied by evidence that cell viability and blood delivery remain comparable, supports a more specific causal interpretation than an uncontrolled comparison of different injured tissues. The investigation can then test whether the recruitment change alters target control or tissue repair.

These principles prepare students for questions that do not explicitly name a mechanism. Start by locating each observation within the system: marrow supply, sampled circulation, endothelial interaction, extravascular movement, capture, killing or clearance. Identify the measured denominator, distinguish time-dependent rates from accumulated numbers and use the earliest altered step to form a hypothesis. Then ask what additional evidence might refute that hypothesis. The result is a transferable reasoning method that connects cell biology, immunology and vascular physiology without relying on memorised associations alone.

Original Part B practice

1. Which circulating granulocyte commonly has a multilobed nucleus? A. Neutrophil · B. Monocyte · C. Small lymphocyte · D. Erythrocyte

Answer: A. Neutrophil.

Its segmented nucleus and fine granules support identification. A monocyte typically has a folded nucleus and lymphocytes often have a dense round nucleus.

A mature mammalian erythrocyte lacks a nucleus and is not a leukocyte.

2. Which cell class is associated with granule-mediated attack on large helminths? A. Platelet · B. Eosinophil · C. Erythrocyte · D. Resting B lymphocyte

Answer: B. Eosinophil.

A large parasite is not readily enclosed in a phagosome, so extracellular granule release can contribute to defence.

Platelets are primarily haemostatic; red cells transport gases; resting B cells do not execute this granule-mediated response.

3. Which statement about mast cells and basophils is correct? A. Yes; both are circulating neutrophils · B. Yes; all mast cells are mature basophils · C. No; distinct populations share some effector features · D. No; basophils are erythrocyte fragments

Answer: C. No.

Mast cells mature in tissue and basophils circulate; they share histamine and IgE-associated functions but are distinct populations.

The shared granules and IgE association do not establish identical development. Neither population is an erythrocyte fragment.

4. Which molecule mediates rolling in the classical recruitment cascade? A. MHC molecules · B. Actin alone · C. Antibodies alone · D. Selectins

Answer: D. Selectins.

Transient selectin–ligand contacts support rolling; activated integrins primarily support arrest.

Actin supports movement but is not the extracellular rolling ligand system; MHC and antibody are not the defining rolling interaction.

5. What is the role of PECAM-1 in the classical cascade? A. Supports junctional passage · B. Synthesises antibodies · C. Cleaves C3 · D. Reduces oxygen to superoxide

Answer: A. It contributes to transendothelial migration.

PECAM-1 participates at endothelial junctions; it is not the principal initiator of selectin-dependent rolling.

Antibody secretion, complement cleavage and oxidase chemistry occur through different molecular machinery.

6. What does an opsonin do? A. Generates ATP directly · B. Enhances target recognition and uptake · C. Prevents all leukocyte movement · D. Always ruptures microbial membranes

Answer: B. Enhances recognition and uptake of a target by phagocytes.

Opsonisation depends on receptors for the coating, and is not necessarily direct microbial killing.

Opsonisation is neither an energy reaction nor necessarily lysis. It facilitates uptake when suitable receptors are available.

7. Which complement fragment is a potent chemoattractant? A. Haemoglobin · B. Fibrinogen · C. C5a · D. Albumin

Answer: C. C5a.

C5a promotes inflammatory recruitment, while C3b and its processed fragments mainly provide opsonic functions.

The other molecules are not complement-derived chemoattractants.

8. Which enzyme generates superoxide during the respiratory burst? A. Myeloperoxidase · B. Lysosomal protease · C. ATP synthase · D. Phagocyte NADPH oxidase

Answer: D. Phagocyte NADPH oxidase.

The enzyme transfers electrons to oxygen; myeloperoxidase acts on peroxide downstream.

Proteases digest substrates and ATP synthase makes ATP; MPO uses downstream peroxide rather than carrying out the primary electron transfer.

9. Which cell produces antibodies after B-cell differentiation? A. Plasma cell · B. Eosinophil · C. Neutrophil · D. Platelet

Answer: A. Plasma cell.

Plasma cells secrete antibodies, while cytotoxic T and NK cells induce death of selected targets.

The other formed elements do not carry out the specialised antibody-secretion programme of plasma cells.

10. What is efferocytosis? A. Formation of a platelet plug · B. Engulfment of apoptotic cells · C. Migration down a pressure gradient · D. IgE receptor cross-linking

Answer: B. Engulfment of apoptotic cells.

It helps clear dying cells and can support resolution without uncontrolled release of contents.

The alternatives represent haemostasis, physical movement and activation, respectively, rather than clearance of apoptotic material.

11. Why is a band neutrophil distinguished from a segmented neutrophil? A. It lacks DNA · B. It is a distinct adaptive lymphocyte · C. It has less segmented nuclear morphology · D. It is a platelet precursor

Answer: C. It has a less segmented nuclear morphology associated with maturation.

A band cell is not a distinct granulocyte lineage; the observation concerns maturation.

Both band and segmented forms belong to the neutrophil maturation sequence and possess nuclei.

12. What does G-CSF regulate in this chapter's context? A. Erythrocyte oxygen binding · B. Fibrin cross-linking · C. Direct IgG neutralisation · D. Neutrophil production and mobilisation

Answer: D. Neutrophil production and mobilisation.

It changes availability of cells; it is not an opsonin or directly a bactericidal granule protein.

G-CSF regulates granulocyte availability, not these biochemical effector processes.

13. Which signal is especially associated with eosinophil development and survival? A. IL-5 · B. C3b · C. Albumin · D. Haem

Answer: A. IL-5.

It supports eosinophil biology, while endothelial selectins support adhesion rather than lineage-specific growth.

C3b helps opsonisation; albumin and haem have different physiological roles.

14. Why is ordinary morphology insufficient to classify all resting lymphocyte subsets? A. All lymphocytes lack receptors · B. Many resting lymphocyte subsets look alike · C. All lymphocytes are granulocytes · D. Only neutrophils have DNA

Answer: B. Many B, T and NK cells have overlapping appearances.

Surface marker and functional information can distinguish populations that a blood film cannot.

Morphological similarity does not mean lack of receptors or absence of DNA.

15. What is margination? A. Phagosome fusion · B. Terminal complement attack · C. Distribution of leukocytes near a vessel wall · D. Secretion of IgG

Answer: C. Movement or distribution of leukocytes near the vascular wall.

It increases opportunity for contact but is not equivalent to rolling or transmigration.

These other processes occur after or independently of the spatial positioning of blood leukocytes.

16. Which endothelial site commonly supports recruitment during systemic acute inflammation? A. Aortic valve · B. Alveolar air space · C. Marrow nucleus · D. Postcapillary venules

Answer: D. Postcapillary venules.

The classical model applies in many tissues; specialised vascular beds may differ.

In many systemic tissues the microvascular venular segment supports the classical recruitment sequence.

17. Which receptor class undergoes inside-out activation during firm adhesion? A. Integrins · B. Haemoglobin · C. Soluble IgM alone · D. Myeloperoxidase

Answer: A. Integrins.

Intracellular signals change external adhesive function, unlike the basic transient selectin interactions.

Inside-out signalling modifies an adhesion receptor's binding properties, not a soluble oxygen carrier or a granule enzyme.

18. What is the principal difference between serum and plasma preparation? A. Serum contains all intact clotting factors at unchanged concentrations · B. Serum follows clotting; plasma is obtained without clotting · C. Plasma contains no proteins · D. Serum consists only of erythrocytes

Answer: B. Serum follows clot formation; plasma is obtained with clotting prevented.

This distinction affects composition and possible functional assays.

Serum and plasma are both fluid fractions; clot formation alters components such as fibrinogen.

19. Which complement regulator interferes with terminal pore assembly? A. CD3 · B. CD19 · C. CD59 · D. Haemoglobin

Answer: C. CD59.

CD55 instead helps regulate convertase stability; neither is a microbial antigen receptor.

CD3 and CD19 are useful lineage-associated markers, while haemoglobin transports gases.

20. Which antibody region engages Fc gamma receptors? A. Antigen epitope alone · B. Erythrocyte spectrin · C. Complement C5a · D. Fc region of suitable IgG

Answer: D. Fc region of suitable IgG.

Antigen binding occurs through the antigen-binding regions; the Fc portion supplies a distinct effector connection.

Fc receptors bind appropriate antibody Fc regions rather than antigen epitopes or unrelated structural proteins.

Original Part C practice

1. A blood count changes from 8,000 cells/µL with 60% neutrophils to 5,000 with 80%. Did absolute neutrophils rise?

Answer: No: 4,800 to 4,000 cells/µL.

Multiply each total by its fraction. Relative enrichment is compatible with an absolute fall.

2. Rolling remains 78 versus 80 in a reference, but arrests fall from 60 to 5. Which stage should be investigated first?

Answer: Chemokine-dependent integrin activation or firm adhesion.

Initial contacts remain nearly normal while stable arrest is specifically impaired. A primary defect in selectin-dependent capture is less supported.

3. Cells migrate and ingest normally but show little superoxide production. What is the most direct candidate defect?

Answer: NADPH oxidase function.

The data isolate a defect in oxidative effector chemistry rather than vascular delivery or uptake.

4. A fragment of IgG binds a toxin but lacks Fc. Which action could be retained, and which is lost?

Answer: Neutralisation can persist; Fc gamma receptor-mediated uptake is lost.

Antigen binding and Fc-mediated recruitment are distinct. This experiment separates them.

5. Cell A travels 100 µm and advances 60 µm up a gradient. Cell B travels 150 µm and advances 30 µm. Compare directional efficiency.

Answer: A: 0.60; B: 0.20.

Divide projected displacement by total path length. B travels farther but makes less efficient progress in the intended direction.

6. A sample has high blood neutrophils and few neutrophils in an infected tissue, with normal marrow release. Give a plausible mechanistic explanation.

Answer: Impaired vascular recruitment.

Failure to exit the circulating pool can increase blood abundance while diminishing local defence. Other trafficking rates must be controlled.

7. Why include a uniform concentration of chemoattractant in a migration experiment?

Answer: To separate chemokinesis from chemotaxis.

Increased motility alone can occur without directionality; a gradient-specific bias supports chemotaxis.

8. IgG coating increases uptake, and Fc receptor blockade removes the increment without abolishing all uptake. What follows?

Answer: The increment depends on Fc-mediated recognition; other uptake mechanisms remain possible.

Residual uptake is compatible with direct recognition or other receptors; the result does not establish a single exclusive pathway.

9. An oxidase-deficient cell contains fluorescent bacteria. Is this evidence of viable bacteria?

Answer: No.

Fluorescent material may persist after killing. Viability must be assessed separately from association and uptake.

10. Two samples contain the same total circulating leukocyte number, but one has 20% less blood volume. What is the idealised concentration change?

Answer: 25% increase.

With constant N, concentration is N/V; 1/0.8 = 1.25. Real redistribution may modify this isolated dilution prediction.

11. A rise in circulating neutrophils occurs within minutes without detectable immature forms. Which mechanism is consistent with this time course?

Answer: Redistribution from vessel-associated pools.

New progenitor proliferation and full maturation take longer; rapid redistribution is plausible, although other rapid release mechanisms may contribute.

12. A leukocyte receptor is detected by an antibody, but its ligand binding does not increase after chemokine exposure. What type of defect is suggested?

Answer: Deficient integrin activation or coupling, rather than necessarily receptor absence.

Expression and functional affinity are distinct. The observed receptor is present but its regulated response is impaired.

13. Labelled embryonic macrophages persist in adult tissue while adult monocytes are unlabelled. Which simple model is challenged?

Answer: Continuous replacement exclusively by adult blood monocytes.

Persistence is compatible with resident maintenance. Labelling efficiency and specificity need controls.

14. The total leukocyte concentration is 9 × 10⁹/L, 30% lymphocytes, and 40% of lymphocytes belong to a B-cell gate. Estimate absolute B-cell concentration.

Answer: 1.08 × 10⁹/L.

Multiply 9 by 0.30 and 0.40. The second denominator is the lymphocyte gate, not all leukocytes.

15. A blood volume declines from 5 L to 4.5 L at fixed cell number. What is the approximate percentage increase in concentration?

Answer: 11.1%.

The ratio is 5/4.5 = 1.111; the 10% decrease in denominator gives an approximately 11% increase.

16. A lineage marker count is unchanged but serum antibody neutralisation declines. Why is the cell count insufficient?

Answer: Secretion, antibody specificity or distribution may change without the counted population changing.

A population's abundance and its functional output are separate variables.

17. Chemokine treatment increases movement speed equally in all directions. Is this sufficient evidence of chemotaxis?

Answer: No; it supports chemokinesis.

Directional bias relative to a gradient must be measured independently.

18. Reference serum kills susceptible particles, heated serum does not, and functional complement restores killing. What is supported?

Answer: A heat-sensitive complement contribution to killing.

Restoration strengthens the inference, but controls are needed for other heat-sensitive changes and target susceptibility.

19. Loss of CD59 increases injury to a host cell without reducing antibody binding. Where is the likely defect?

Answer: Terminal complement regulation.

The loss of protection permits inappropriate membrane attack downstream of target recognition.

20. A mutation prevents electron transfer by the phagocyte oxidase but leaves granule delivery intact. Which outcomes may differ?

Answer: Superoxide falls while some nonoxidative defence persists.

The oxidase and granule-derived antimicrobial components are not the same effector system.

Mixed NET and State SET practice

1. Assertion: A rising neutrophil percentage always means rising absolute neutrophil concentration. Reason: The percentage is a ratio of neutrophils to total leukocytes.

Answer: Assertion false; reason true.

The denominator can decrease sufficiently to produce a larger proportion despite an unchanged or smaller numerator. Compare absolute concentrations using Wf.

2. Assertion: An antibody fragment can retain antigen binding after removal of the Fc portion. Reason: The antigen-binding and Fc-mediated effector functions are structurally distinguishable.

Answer: Both true; reason explains assertion.

An antigen-binding fragment may neutralise a toxin but cannot use the removed Fc region to engage Fc receptors.

3. Select every true statement: (i) mast cells and basophils are distinct; (ii) eosinophilic granules imply the cell prefers acidic blood; (iii) all agranulocytes lack lysosomes; (iv) an ordinary blood film cannot reliably distinguish every lymphocyte subset.

Answer: (i) and (iv).

The staining designation reflects dye interactions, not blood pH preference; agranulocytes retain intracellular organelles.

4. Which is the best explanation for normal rolling and markedly reduced arrest? A. Failure of antigen recognition by B cells. B. Defective stable adhesion after capture. C. Loss of erythrocyte haem. D. Increased antibody neutralisation.

Answer: B.

The first step observed is preserved and the earliest reduced step is arrest. The other choices do not directly explain that measured stage.

5. In a sequential model 60% of particles attach, half of attached particles enter the cells, and two thirds of internalised particles are killed. What proportion of initial particles is killed through this route?

Answer: 20%.

0.60 × 0.50 × 2/3 = 0.20, assuming nested stages and no parallel killing routes.

6. A chamber receives 120 viable cells; 90 roll and 45 arrest. State the arrest fraction relative to input and relative to rolling cells.

Answer: 37.5% of input; 50% of rollers.

45/120 = 0.375, while 45/90 = 0.50. The difference reflects the chosen denominator.

7. A cell travels 90 µm in 6 minutes and ends 18 µm opposite the attractant source. Find speed and signed directional efficiency.

Answer: 15 µm/min; −0.20.

Speed uses total path length; projected displacement is negative in the defined gradient direction.

8. A simplified tissue receives 700 cells per hour and loses 900 per hour. Predict its initial net population change.

Answer: Decrease by 200 cells per hour.

Entry minus removal is −200 cells/hour. Feedback may subsequently alter either rate.

9. Which observation best distinguishes failed uptake from failed killing? A. Total leukocyte count. B. One image of attached particles. C. Separate internalisation and viability measurements. D. Nuclear segmentation.

Answer: C.

Only the paired functional measurements separate the stage of enclosure from target survival; the other observations do not test both steps.

10. In a hypothetical receptor model Kd is 10 nM. At 10 nM ligand, what is fractional occupancy? At 30 nM?

Answer: 0.50 and 0.75.

Use C/(C+Kd). The simplified equilibrium model is not a full prediction of cellular migration.

11. A complement-coated susceptible target is not lysed but is efficiently captured by phagocytes. Is this contradictory?

Answer: No.

Opsonisation and terminal membrane damage are distinct outputs. Deposition can enhance recognition even if terminal attack is impaired.

12. Choose the most accurate inference from a simultaneous increase in CRP and blood neutrophils: A. Neutrophils necessarily produce all CRP. B. A shared inflammatory signal may affect liver production and leukocyte dynamics. C. Tissue recruitment is necessarily normal. D. The pathogen is identified.

Answer: B.

Liver acute-phase responses and leukocyte distribution can respond to related signals. Correlation does not establish the other claims.

Misconceptions

  1. Agranulocytes contain no granules. The term concerns routine morphology; lysosomes and secretory organelles may be present. Why the error occurs: the name is interpreted literally. Examination trap: a question equates no conspicuous granules with inability to digest material. Reason from organelle function, not naming.
  2. All macrophages originate from recent blood monocytes. Some resident populations originate during development and maintain themselves locally. Why: simplified lineage trees omit developmental origins. Trap: a labelled adult monocyte is assumed to account for every resident cell. Ask whether lineage tracing actually establishes replacement.
  3. Basophils become mast cells. They are distinct populations with overlapping functions. Why: both release histamine and can respond through IgE. Trap: functional resemblance is used as evidence of identity. Compare developmental route and tissue location.
  4. A high leukocyte count proves strong defence. Recruitment or functional defects can coexist with increased blood counts. Why: abundance is easier to measure than function. Trap: a high blood neutrophil count is treated as proof of delivery. Check tissue recruitment and killing independently.
  5. Differential percentage equals absolute count. Multiply total concentration by fractional abundance. Why: percentages are familiar and easy to compare. Trap: a rising percentage is paired with a falling total. Calculate the product for each sample before interpreting.
  6. Rolling is firm adhesion. Transient selectin contacts differ from integrin-mediated arrest. Why: both are forms of adhesion under flow. Trap: selectins and integrins are exchanged in a mechanism question. Locate the step where transient contact becomes stable arrest.
  7. Diapedesis is passive leakage. It involves active cell and barrier interactions. Why: extravasation is confused with fluid leakage. Trap: vessel permeability alone is assumed sufficient for cellular movement. Follow receptor-dependent migration and the barriers crossed.
  8. Chemotaxis is simply rapid movement. Directionality must be measured separately from speed. Why: fast movement appears effective. Trap: the longest track is called the strongest chemotactic response. Calculate projected movement in relation to the gradient.
  9. All bacteria associated with a cell have been ingested. Distinguish surface attachment from internalisation. Why: surface and internal fluorescence may overlap. Trap: associated particles are counted as enclosed particles. Require a measurement that distinguishes their locations.
  10. Ingestion proves killing. Measure survival independently. Why: digestion is assumed to follow enclosure automatically. Trap: normal uptake is used to reject a killing defect. Test microbial viability separately.
  11. Respiratory burst is mitochondrial ATP generation. Phagocyte oxidase uses oxygen to generate reactive products. Why: the word respiratory is associated with mitochondria. Trap: oxygen consumption is taken as proof of ATP generation. Identify the oxygen-reducing enzyme and product.
  12. MPO and oxidase perform the same reaction. MPO uses peroxide downstream of oxidase-associated chemistry. Why: both are linked to oxidative defence. Trap: absence of MPO is claimed to abolish primary superoxide production. Trace the reaction order.
  13. Complement always requires antibody. Lectin and alternative routes do not require antigen-specific antibody. Why: the classical pathway is introduced first. Trap: antibody absence is said to eliminate all complement activation. Distinguish initiation routes and downstream convergence.
  14. C3b and C5a have identical functions. C3b is an opsonic fragment; C5a strongly promotes recruitment. Why: both are complement cleavage products. Trap: a recruitment readout is assigned to C3b and opsonisation to C5a. Identify the primary physiological function of each fragment.
  15. Inflammation necessarily means infection. Sterile damage can activate inflammatory pathways. Why: inflammation is often introduced through microbial infection. Trap: a sterile wound is assumed incapable of attracting neutrophils. Consider damage-associated signals and local vascular activation.

Mastery checklist

  • Identify each major circulating leukocyte from morphology and explain its representative function.
  • Calculate absolute counts and interpret compositional changes without assigning unsupported causes.
  • Predict blood and tissue changes after a defect in release, adhesion or directional migration.
  • Explain ingestion, compartment maturation, NOX2-dependent chemistry and granule-mediated defence.
  • Distinguish antibody neutralisation, opsonisation, complement-mediated recruitment and terminal attack.
  • Explain local vascular changes, systemic signalling and resolution as related but distinct operations.

Equations for rapid recall

Absolute concentration = total concentration × fraction. Compartment concentration = cell number / volume. Directional efficiency = signed projected displacement / total path length. Population change = entry rate − removal rate. In the simple binding model, fractional receptor occupancy = C/(C + Kd). For a nested sequence of events, multiply conditional fractions. State units, assumptions and denominators before applying any of these relationships.

How to revise for analytical questions

Begin with location, identify the measured quantity, then trace the relevant causal sequence. Predict the earliest altered observation and distinguish it from downstream effects. Repeat worked examples using different hypothetical inputs and explain why a count or single readout cannot establish a mechanism on its own.
