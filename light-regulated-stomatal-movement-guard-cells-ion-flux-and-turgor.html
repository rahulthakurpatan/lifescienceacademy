1. The carbon–water exchange problem

A green leaf is a remarkably exposed organ. Light arrives at its surface, but the carbon needed to manufacture carbohydrate arrives as carbon dioxide from the surrounding atmosphere. The waxy cuticle that limits uncontrolled water loss also restricts diffusion of gases. A leaf therefore needs passages connecting the atmosphere to its internal air spaces. Stomata provide these passages. Their aperture can change repeatedly within the lifetime of the leaf, allowing a plant to regulate gas exchange without producing a new epidermis each time the environment changes. This adjustment is one of the most accessible examples of how environmental information can alter the transport properties of a living cell and then alter the performance of an entire organ.

The term stoma refers to an individual pore and is often used in the broader sense of the pore and its associated guard cells. The plural is stomata. Two guard cells generally border the pore of a stomatal complex. Air diffusing through the pore can reach a substomatal cavity and then the intercellular air spaces of the mesophyll. Carbon dioxide eventually crosses aqueous and cellular barriers before reaching the chloroplasts of photosynthetic cells. Water follows a different net route: liquid water delivered through the vascular system replenishes evaporating surfaces, and water vapour moves toward the atmosphere. A single opening therefore serves two linked yet physiologically different fluxes.

The need to take up carbon dioxide and retain water creates a trade-off. Opening a pore can lower one resistance to carbon dioxide diffusion, but it also facilitates diffusion of water vapour when the leaf interior is more humid than the outside air. If water delivery is adequate and the mesophyll can use additional carbon dioxide, opening can be advantageous. If the supply of liquid water is restricted, increasing vapour loss may threaten cell hydration and photosynthetic capacity. Stomatal regulation is thus not simply an on–off switch associated with sunrise and sunset. It is the changing outcome of several environmental signals acting upon transport and mechanics.

Consider two plants placed under identical light but supplied with different amounts of water. Both may receive enough photons to sustain photosynthesis, yet the water-limited plant may maintain narrower stomatal pores. The difference cannot be explained by light capture alone. It arises because the guard cells integrate information about the environment and the state of surrounding tissues. A plant can reduce water loss by narrowing a pore even when the biochemical machinery of the Calvin cycle is capable of fixing additional carbon. This distinction is important when interpreting a decline in net assimilation: it may reflect limited carbon dioxide delivery, impaired photosynthetic metabolism, or both.

Guard cells accomplish movement without skeletal muscle, contraction of an actomyosin apparatus comparable to animal muscle, or the creation of a new pore. They regulate the amounts and distributions of dissolved substances, alter their water content and change pressure against their walls. The mechanical properties of those walls convert changes in cell pressure into a change in the shape of the opening. In this way, a photon absorbed by a receptor can initiate a chain of events ending in a measurable displacement of a cell boundary.

Three explanatory scales will recur in this chapter. The molecular scale concerns receptors, protein phosphorylation, pumps and channels. The cellular scale concerns solute concentration, membrane potential, water potential and pressure. The leaf scale concerns aperture, conductance, assimilation and transpiration. These scales are related, but measurements from one cannot automatically be treated as measurements of another. A protein phosphorylation signal does not itself report how widely the pore opens, and an increase in leaf temperature is not a direct measurement of a channel current.

2. Stomatal anatomy and the mechanics of movement

A typical epidermal preparation from a broad-leaved flowering plant reveals two curved guard cells arranged around an elongated central pore. Their ends remain connected, and their shared mechanical geometry means that changes in shape can widen or narrow the pore. Guard cells are surrounded by pavement cells and, depending upon the species, subsidiary cells with specialized functions and shapes. The term subsidiary cell denotes a recognizable neighboring cell that contributes to the stomatal complex; it should not be applied indiscriminately to every epidermal cell.

A cell wall prevents a hydrated plant cell from expanding freely like a soap bubble. The expanding protoplast presses against the wall, which develops tensile stress. Pressure within the cell relative to its surroundings is called turgor pressure. Guard cells increase in volume as they take up water, but their walls resist deformation differently in different directions. The combination of wall thickness, cellulose arrangement, geometry and attachment to neighboring cells directs the resulting displacement. In many kidney-shaped stomata, increased turgor causes the cells to bow away from each other and increases pore width.

A common textbook drawing represents the wall facing the pore as thicker than the outer wall. This is helpful for introducing differential wall mechanics, but thickness alone is not a complete mechanical explanation. The orientation of cellulose microfibrils determines which directions resist extension, while the geometry of the poles and the surrounding epidermal tissue influences the path of deformation. Two cells with the same turgor can therefore produce different apertures if their walls have different mechanical properties. Conversely, a defect in wall organization may impair movement despite a normal molecular signal and normal solute uptake.

In many grasses, the guard cells have enlarged ends and a relatively narrow middle region, giving them a dumbbell-like outline. Specialized subsidiary cells flank the guard cells. A redistribution of solutes between these cells can favor changes in the pressure difference across their shared walls. The combination of geometry and pressure exchange contributes to rapid movement in representative grasses. One should not infer that every plant with dumbbell-shaped stomata has identical kinetics under all conditions; the supply of water, transport proteins and environmental conditions remain important.

The pressure of the epidermal cells around a stoma can oppose opening. If a neighboring cell presses inward, the same guard-cell turgor can produce a smaller aperture than when the neighboring cell is less pressurized. This explains why stomata must be understood as mechanical systems embedded in living tissue rather than as isolated elastic objects. Experiments comparing pressure and aperture in intact and manipulated tissue have supported the importance of neighboring-cell mechanics. Franks and Farquhar examined the relationship between stomatal form, pressure and gas-exchange dynamics in contrasting species, illustrating why anatomy is functionally consequential.

A stoma usually changes its width on a timescale shorter than developmental changes in stomatal density. Density describes how many stomata occupy a specified leaf area, whereas aperture describes the dimensions of an individual opening. Density can respond to developmental conditions in newly formed leaves, but an already mature leaf can regulate its momentary gas exchange primarily through aperture. If a mutant has fewer stomata yet each remaining stoma opens normally, reduced leaf conductance need not indicate a defect in the blue-light signaling pathway.

The guard-cell vacuole occupies a large fraction of the swollen cell volume. Its membrane, the tonoplast, is a distinct transport barrier from the plasma membrane. Transport into the cytoplasm is consequently not equivalent to transport into the vacuole. Solutes entering through the outer membrane can be stored or redistributed across the tonoplast, affecting how much water the entire cell contains while maintaining cytosolic conditions compatible with metabolism. During closure the vacuole changes volume and organization. Neither the vacuole nor the cell wall acts as a light receptor simply because it participates in movement.

3. Water potential, electrochemical gradients and turgor

To understand the direction of water movement, it is useful to separate the chemical effect of solutes from the mechanical effect of pressure. Water moves spontaneously toward lower water potential when a permeable path connects the two regions. At the cellular scale, neglecting gravitational and matric terms where appropriate, water potential is approximated as the sum of solute potential and pressure potential: Ψw = Ψs + Ψp. The solute component is negative relative to pure water at the same temperature and pressure, because adding dissolved particles lowers water activity. The pressure component is usually positive in a turgid living plant cell.

Suppose a guard cell initially has a solute potential of −0.8 MPa and a pressure potential of +0.5 MPa. Its water potential is −0.3 MPa. If the surrounding apoplast has a water potential of −0.3 MPa, the two compartments are at equilibrium with respect to net water movement. Now suppose the guard cell accumulates solutes, initially lowering its solute potential to −1.0 MPa without an immediate change in volume. Its water potential becomes −0.5 MPa. Water can now enter from the apoplast. As the cell swells, rising pressure and dilution of solutes tend to raise its water potential. The final volume depends upon both the wall mechanics and the remaining solute content.

The dilute ideal-solution approximation expresses solute potential as Ψs = −iCRT. Here i represents the effective number of dissolved particles per formula unit, C is molar concentration, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. With R = 0.008314 L MPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and a total ideal osmolar concentration of 0.10 mol L⁻¹ at 298 K, the solute potential is approximately −0.248 MPa. If concentration refers to the total particles rather than undissociated salt formula units, no additional multiplication by a dissociation factor is appropriate. Real guard-cell solutions contain interacting solutes and therefore depart from an ideal solution; the equation is a teaching approximation, not a direct measurement of intracellular osmotic conditions.

A cell may gain osmotic particles without importing every particle from the external medium. Hydrolysis of a polymer such as starch can yield a larger number of soluble molecules from stored material. Conversion of carbon compounds into organic anions can also alter the cell's osmotic composition. Nevertheless, changes in concentration must be distinguished from changes in amount. A cell can contain more potassium after opening while having a smaller change in concentration than expected because its water volume has also increased. Measurements of concentration alone cannot establish the net amount transported unless volume is known or controlled.

Ions experience both a chemical gradient and an electrical gradient. A positively charged potassium ion is attracted toward a region of lower electrical potential. If a cell has a negatively charged interior relative to the outside, the electrical gradient favors potassium entry, but a high intracellular potassium concentration favors its outward diffusion. The direction of spontaneous movement depends on the sum of these tendencies. With membrane potential defined as inside minus outside, the equilibrium potential of a monovalent potassium ion is EK = (RT/F) ln([K⁺]out/[K⁺]in), where F is the Faraday constant. At 25 °C the expression is approximately 59 mV multiplied by the base-ten logarithm of the outside-to-inside concentration ratio.

For an illustrative guard cell with external potassium activity approximated as 10 mM and internal activity approximated as 100 mM, EK is about −59 mV. At a membrane potential of −120 mV, inward movement is energetically favored when potassium channels are open; at −20 mV, outward movement is favored. These numbers are hypothetical. In real cells, channel gating, activity coefficients, intracellular compartmentation and other permeant ions alter the observed current. The equilibrium potential determines the direction of a passive electrochemical tendency, not whether the necessary channel is actually open.

A plasma-membrane proton pump uses the energy from ATP hydrolysis to export positively charged hydrogen ions. The immediate electrical tendency is for the interior to become more negative, a change called hyperpolarization. The extracellular solution also becomes more acidic if it is not strongly buffered. Proton extrusion is primary active transport because the transporter couples ATP utilization directly to transport against an electrochemical gradient. In contrast, potassium movement through an open channel is passive with respect to its own electrochemical gradient. The energy spent by the pump establishes the condition that can favor ion entry through the channel.

Water entry is not itself powered by ATP hydrolysis. Water moves along the water-potential difference through available membrane routes, including aquaporins, while metabolism supplies energy for establishing and maintaining the ion distribution. This separation explains how a change in activity of a single pump can initiate several downstream processes without that pump carrying potassium or water. It also explains why shutting down ATP production can disrupt osmoregulation even when water molecules remain freely able to cross a membrane.

The small electrical imbalance responsible for membrane voltage must not be confused with bulk charge accumulation. The membrane separates charges and behaves partly like a capacitor. Only a small proportion of the total dissolved ions is required to produce a substantial membrane voltage. Sustained potassium accumulation, however, requires compensating negative charge, often supplied by inorganic anions and metabolically produced organic anions. Without such balancing processes, a growing electrical opposition would limit further cation entry. The principle of approximate bulk electroneutrality applies while a voltage is maintained across the membrane.

4. From blue photons to the guard-cell proton pump

Light can influence the stomata through more than one route. In the blue-light-specific response, phototropins perceive photons and initiate a signaling pathway in guard cells. Their LOV domains bind flavin mononucleotide and participate in light-dependent conformational changes that regulate a protein kinase domain. The detailed photochemistry and receptor architecture belong to the preceding phototropin chapter; the central question here is how the same type of photoreceptor selects a particular physiological output in a guard cell.

Arabidopsis possesses two major phototropins, phot1 and phot2. Both can contribute to blue-light-induced opening, and their relative roles depend on illumination and context. Loss of one receptor does not necessarily eliminate the response because the second receptor can contribute. Experiments comparing phototropin mutants helped demonstrate that these receptors participate in blue-light regulation of stomatal aperture. A double mutant provides a more stringent test of the requirement for the receptor class than a single mutant, but it is still necessary to control for baseline aperture and other physiological changes.

A crucial intermediate is BLUE LIGHT SIGNALING 1, or BLUS1, a protein kinase associated with the guard-cell response. Phototropins phosphorylate BLUS1 in response to blue illumination. BLUS1 helps provide specificity: loss of the protein impairs the stomatal response even though phototropins also control processes elsewhere in the plant, such as directional shoot growth and movement of chloroplasts. The same class of receptor can therefore be connected to different downstream proteins in different tissues, rather than requiring a new photoreceptor for every output.

Evidence from Takemiya and colleagues in 2013 connected BLUS1 to the pathway between phototropin activation and proton-pump activation. Their genetic and biochemical results allowed investigators to distinguish a lesion in the relay from a lesion in primary light perception. A guard cell that still activates phototropin but fails to stimulate the pump after illumination demonstrates that activation of the receptor is not sufficient to guarantee the final response. The finding is particularly useful in examinations about pathway ordering because upstream activation and downstream failure are measured separately.

Another implicated protein is BHP, a Raf-like kinase identified through studies of blue-light-induced proton-pump phosphorylation. BHP contributes to the signaling process, and type 1 protein phosphatase activity has also been implicated. A simplified pathway diagram should not suggest that each protein directly phosphorylates the next one merely because they are placed in a sequence. Genetic requirement, physical association and direct enzymatic substrate relationship are different experimental claims. The downstream regulation between early kinases and the pump includes steps whose detailed connections have required continued investigation.

The plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase is the effector responsible for proton export. Its regulatory carboxyl-terminal region normally constrains enzyme activity. Phosphorylation of a conserved threonine near the end of the protein permits binding of a 14-3-3 regulatory protein, stabilizing an activated state. The resulting increase in proton transport links the phosphorylation network to an electrical and chemical gradient. Kinoshita and Shimazaki demonstrated the relationship between blue illumination, phosphorylation of the regulatory tail, 14-3-3 association and increased pump activity in experiments published in 1999.

Protein phosphorylation transfers a phosphate group to a target protein; it does not mean that the phosphate itself supplies the sustained energy used for proton export. Regulatory phosphorylation can alter protein conformation or binding properties. Once activated, the H⁺-ATPase repeatedly hydrolyses ATP to perform transport cycles. A cell could exhibit phosphorylation of the regulatory site but fail to maintain substantial proton extrusion if ATP availability were inadequate. Conversely, an artificial stimulus that stabilizes the activated pump can sometimes bypass defective upstream signaling, provided that downstream transport and water supply remain functional.

Blue light is therefore information at the beginning of this pathway and metabolic energy is needed at the effector stage. The distinction prevents a common confusion between phototropin signaling and chloroplast photophosphorylation. Phototropins do not produce a stream of photosynthetic electrons to reduce NADP⁺, and the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase does not synthesize ATP by harvesting a thylakoid proton gradient in this process. The two systems involve different membranes, proteins and directions of energy coupling.

A useful experiment compares guard cells maintained under red background illumination with those receiving the same red background plus a weak blue component. A response to the added blue component, especially when it appears at irradiances that produce relatively little additional mesophyll assimilation, provides evidence that blue light acts as a specific signal. It does not by itself prove the identity of the receptor or establish complete independence from the metabolic state of the leaf. Genetic tests and biochemical measurements are needed to connect the spectral response to particular pathway components.

5. Ion entry, counterions and the opening response

The proton pump does not itself transport the major osmotically active cation. Its electrogenic activity creates a membrane voltage that can favor the entry of potassium ions through other proteins. An ion channel is a selective aqueous pathway through a membrane, capable of switching between states that permit or restrict permeation. The channel does not have to hydrolyze ATP to drive each ion through the pore. Its selectivity and gating determine which ion species can cross and under what conditions. Inward-rectifying potassium channels in the guard-cell plasma membrane are particularly important when the membrane is strongly negative.

The term inward rectification describes a relationship between current and membrane potential, not a permanent prohibition against every movement in the opposite direction. In the physiological opening pathway, hyperpolarization increases the activity of relevant inward channels and favors potassium accumulation. KAT1 and KAT2 are useful Arabidopsis examples. A genetic defect in one potassium channel may produce a modest phenotype because other channels can contribute to the total transport capacity. By contrast, blocking all relevant inward potassium conductance would remove an important route for using the voltage generated by the pump. Whether aperture is completely abolished would depend on alternative osmotic solutes and experimental conditions.

Imagine a guard cell that has just activated its plasma-membrane proton pump. Hydrogen ions leave the cytoplasm and membrane voltage becomes more negative. As potassium begins entering, the positive charge carried by potassium tends to oppose further hyperpolarization. Pump activity, inward current and other currents jointly determine the actual voltage. There is no requirement that the membrane potential remain at the most negative value attained immediately after pump activation. A voltage trace during opening may therefore show a rapid transient followed by a partially recovered negative potential while potassium accumulation continues. Interpreting such a trace requires consideration of the net current rather than an assumption that a single transporter fixes the voltage.

An ion that can enter a cell thermodynamically is not necessarily entering at an appreciable rate. The membrane lipid bilayer provides a large barrier to passage of charged solutes. Channels, transporters and their availability therefore determine kinetic access to the gradients established by the pump. For example, in an illustrative preparation maintained at a membrane potential more negative than EK, a potassium-selective channel blocker reduces inward current without needing to reverse the direction of its electrochemical gradient. The absence of current in this case is evidence about the conducting pathway, not evidence that the potassium gradient vanished.

The movement of chloride must be considered separately from potassium because chloride carries negative charge. A negative intracellular voltage tends to repel a freely permeating chloride ion. It is consequently incorrect to explain the entry of both potassium and chloride solely by attraction to a negatively charged cell interior. Uptake of anions can depend on their concentration gradients and on coupled transport with protons. The proton gradient generated by the H⁺-ATPase can provide energy for secondary active uptake. Chloride and nitrate are examples of inorganic anions whose contribution depends upon availability in the surrounding medium and the transport systems expressed by the cell.

A guard cell can also produce malate, an organic anion. At near-neutral cytosolic pH malate is substantially negatively charged, making it useful as a counterion for potassium accumulation. Its synthesis is connected with metabolism of carbon skeletons. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase can catalyze the addition of bicarbonate to phosphoenolpyruvate, forming oxaloacetate, which can then be reduced to malate by malate dehydrogenase. The pathway illustrates how metabolic transformations can contribute to an osmotic response while maintaining charge balance. Its importance does not imply that malate is always the only organic solute accumulating in every species and treatment.

A simplified charge calculation shows why the identity of counterions matters. Suppose, for illustration, a cell accumulates 20 femtomoles of K⁺ while chloride is the only compensating anion. Approximately 20 femtomoles of Cl⁻ would balance that positive charge. If divalent malate were the only compensating anion in this simplified model, approximately 10 femtomoles of malate²⁻ would balance the same amount of potassium. The total number of osmotically active particles would differ between the two scenarios. In a real guard cell, changes in hydrogen ion binding, other solutes and compartmentation must also be considered. The exercise is meant to connect valence and osmotic particle number, not to predict an exact aperture from two ion amounts.

The distinction between charge balance and osmotic balance becomes especially important when comparing neutral sugars and salts. Sucrose contributes osmotic particles but does not supply an equivalent amount of negative charge to balance potassium. A cell may accumulate sucrose as part of its osmotic adjustment while still requiring appropriate counterions for retained cations. Conversely, metabolically converting malate into a neutral product without changing other ions could create a charge-balance problem. Cellular metabolism and membrane transport therefore cannot be treated as independent lists of events.

Potassium and anions are often redistributed into the vacuole during opening. As the vacuole expands, the cell can accumulate considerable solute and water without exposing every cytosolic enzyme to equally large concentration changes. Tonoplast transporters regulate this distribution using voltage and proton gradients across the tonoplast. Vacuolar proton pumps and ion exchangers are distinct from the plasma-membrane proton ATPase at the beginning of the blue-light opening pathway. A drug that changes vacuolar acidification can therefore influence aperture even if the plasma membrane still hyperpolarizes normally. In a mechanistic experiment, recording plasma membrane voltage alone would not resolve the state of the vacuole.

Solute accumulation lowers the water potential of the guard cell before the additional water enters. The resulting flow raises turgor until pressure, dilution and environmental supply establish a new balance. The mechanical system converts the swelling into a change in pore area. Thus the electrical signal is translated into an osmotic change, and the osmotic change is translated into movement. A defect at any step can reduce aperture, but the same final phenotype does not show which step is defective. The causal chain is useful because it allows the investigator to design measurements of distinct intermediates instead of relying only on the visible endpoint.

6. Metabolic support: starch, sugars and guard-cell chloroplasts

Guard cells generally possess chloroplasts in flowering plants, unlike many surrounding pavement cells. Their chloroplasts contain photosynthetic machinery and can participate in the metabolic processes that support stomatal responses. However, their metabolism should not be regarded as identical to that of the larger photosynthetic mesophyll compartment. Guard cells have the specialized task of repeatedly adjusting osmotic content, transporting ions and maintaining membrane gradients. A change in carbon metabolism may therefore influence pore opening through energy supply, synthesis of solutes or signaling rather than through the production of exportable carbohydrate alone.

Stored starch is particularly instructive. A starch polymer consists of many glucose units, but a polymer molecule counts as a single dissolved particle if it is dissolved at all, and starch granules are not freely dissolved osmolytes. Breakdown of the polymer can increase the number of smaller soluble molecules available for metabolism. Some products can supply glycolytic intermediates, while others can contribute to soluble sugars or organic acids. Thus mobilizing a pre-existing store is one means of supporting an early response to illumination without having to wait for the same quantity of carbon to be fixed anew.

In Arabidopsis guard cells, enzymes including the starch-degrading activities associated with BAM1 and AMY3 have been linked to light-responsive starch mobilization. Mutants with defective mobilization can display impaired opening kinetics under appropriate conditions. This evidence is more informative than a simple observation that starch content decreases during illumination, because a correlation between starch loss and opening would not establish whether the loss is necessary for the measured response. Even a genetic requirement for rapid opening, however, does not show that every osmotically active molecule originated from starch or that the same pathway dominates in all plant species.

The fate of a glucose unit liberated from storage depends on metabolic conditions. If it is metabolized through glycolysis, ATP and carbon intermediates may be generated. If carbohydrate accumulates as a soluble sugar, it can contribute to the osmotic potential. If an intermediate provides carbon for malate synthesis, it can contribute indirectly to the counterion pool. The routes compete for carbon and are coordinated with transport. A simple statement that starch changes into sugar and sugar opens stomata does not explain which solutes balance ionic charge, where ATP is produced or how metabolism is regulated.

Historically, explanations of stomatal movement shifted from emphasis on transformations of carbohydrate to a more integrated understanding that includes potassium transport, organic acid metabolism, membrane voltage and active proton pumping. The older carbohydrate-based observations were not meaningless: they identified metabolic changes occurring in the same specialized cells that changed shape. The limitation was treating a single observed metabolic change as a complete explanation. Modern models incorporate both metabolic and electrical processes because neither alone accounts for all experimental perturbations.

Guard cells can receive sugars from neighboring tissues and the apoplast. This allows their energetic state to depend partly on processes occurring elsewhere in the leaf. At different times of day, the relative contributions of potassium salts, malate and sugars to osmotic content can change. These changes should not be converted into an absolute rule that potassium controls only morning movement while sucrose controls only afternoon movement. The pattern depends on species, growth conditions, carbon availability and measurement time. The appropriate general principle is that the osmotic system can use more than one solute strategy.

ATP required by the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase can be supplied by cellular energy metabolism. Respiratory metabolism remains relevant to illuminated guard cells; illumination does not turn mitochondria off. Photosynthetic processes in guard-cell chloroplasts can also affect energy and reductant availability, depending on the species and conditions. A blue-light pulse is not chemically converted into ATP by the phototropin molecule. It changes the activity of a regulatory pathway that determines how cellular ATP will be used. A metabolic inhibitor may consequently suppress opening even when the photoreceptor remains competent to absorb light.

The interpretation of experiments using chloroplast inhibitors requires particular care. An inhibitor of photosynthetic electron transport may change mesophyll assimilation, intercellular carbon dioxide, guard-cell energy status and redox signals simultaneously. A reduced response after such treatment does not prove that the guard-cell chloroplast alone is the site of the relevant effect. Comparing isolated epidermis with intact leaves, testing different illumination conditions, and measuring intermediate biochemical responses can help separate these possibilities. Each preparation also introduces its own limitations: isolated guard cells no longer experience all of the physiological communication present in a whole leaf.

The same reasoning applies to measurements of sugars. An increase in guard-cell sugar content during illumination could reflect photosynthetic synthesis, uptake from another cell, starch breakdown or a combination of these processes. Distinguishing them requires knowledge of fluxes, not just the size of the metabolite pool at one time. A comparison of starch content, soluble carbohydrate content and opening kinetics at multiple times can identify relationships that a single endpoint cannot. Where a perturbation changes all three measurements, the direction of causation still requires a suitable control or rescue experiment.

These connections explain why the electrical and metabolic components of stomatal opening should be taught together. The pump establishes the driving forces for membrane transport, organic metabolism supplies energy and some osmolytes, and vacuolar storage converts net solute accumulation into a change in water content. A functioning guard cell is not an electronic circuit alone and not a passive osmometer alone; it is a living metabolic and mechanical system that uses both principles.

7. Blue-light-specific and photosynthesis-associated responses

A plant leaf exposed to red light can show increased stomatal conductance even though the canonical guard-cell phototropin pathway is activated especially by blue wavelengths. This observation is not a contradiction. Photosynthesis in the mesophyll consumes carbon dioxide, reducing its concentration in the intercellular air spaces when consumption rises faster than diffusion from the atmosphere. Guard cells respond to carbon dioxide-related information, so changes associated with photosynthetic activity can encourage opening. Red light can also influence guard-cell chloroplast metabolism and other photoreceptor signaling. The term red-light response therefore describes a physiological response to an illumination treatment, not a claim that a single receptor explains every component.

To distinguish these responses experimentally, it is helpful to choose a red background that supports measurable photosynthesis and establish a baseline conductance. A small blue-light increment is then superimposed while other conditions remain as similar as possible. If the additional opening is much greater than would be expected from its incremental effect on mesophyll assimilation, a specific signaling component is suggested. One must still ask whether the two treatments alter total photon flux, leaf temperature or carbon dioxide concentration. Matching only the power of two light sources does not automatically match photon number, because photon energy depends on wavelength.

The energy of one photon is hc/λ. Using the same incident energy flux at 450 and 660 nm gives different photon fluxes because photons at the shorter wavelength carry more energy individually. For physiological illumination experiments, photosynthetic photon flux density is often expressed in micromoles of photons per square metre per second. This measure counts photons over a defined wavelength range rather than weighting each by its energy. A spectral comparison may also need information about absorption by the leaf; equal incident photon flux does not guarantee equal absorbed photon flux. These considerations are especially relevant when the experiment is designed to discriminate a spectral receptor response from an energy-dependent metabolic response.

The blue-light-specific response can be sensitive to relatively low blue irradiance against a red-light background, while larger red-light treatments often produce responses associated with photosynthetic carbon metabolism. These descriptions are tendencies and not universal saturation points. A numerical threshold measured in one species, growth condition or experimental apparatus cannot simply be transplanted to another. If an examination graph shows saturation of the added-blue response while photosynthesis continues to increase with red irradiance, the sensible inference is that at least two distinguishable response components are present, not that photosynthesis has ceased.

Changes in intercellular carbon dioxide, conventionally represented by Ci, are central to the interpretation of an intact-leaf gas exchange experiment. Suppose an investigator increases red irradiance and observes a rise in assimilation followed by a rise in conductance. The sequence is compatible with enhanced carbon consumption contributing to an opening signal. But both assimilation and conductance may be responding to other effects of light; temporal association alone is not enough. If a second experiment holds Ci approximately constant using controlled atmospheric carbon dioxide, changes in conductance can be compared under more discriminating conditions. Such manipulation is not identical to holding external carbon dioxide constant, because the concentration within the leaf depends on both supply and consumption.

A 2021 study examining BLUS1 signaling and intercellular carbon dioxide emphasized the importance of combining receptor-associated signaling with carbon dioxide-related conditions in the experimental context studied. This is a useful corrective to diagrams in which blue light is shown as an unconditional command to open. A model that predicts an aperture must account for the prevailing state of the guard cell and the leaf. A biochemically active pathway may be necessary for one component of opening but still insufficient for a large net movement under a condition that strongly favors closure.

In a comparative experiment, wild-type plants and phot1 phot2 double mutants can be tested under red background illumination with and without the same weak blue increment. The primary comparison is the difference caused by the increment within each genotype. If the mutant loses most of the blue increment but still exhibits a red-associated increase, the two responses are at least partly separable. Differences in basal aperture or stomatal density must be measured before attributing a lower absolute conductance to the signaling lesion. A genetically complemented line provides an additional control for attributing the phenotype to the disrupted receptors.

An epidermal strip provides a different experimental window. It removes much of the mesophyll and can expose stomatal responses more directly to controlled light, buffer composition and external ions. A blue-light response in this preparation supports the ability of the epidermal complex to respond without intact mesophyll. Nevertheless, isolation can change carbon dioxide levels, hydration, metabolic supply and mechanical constraints. A response in an isolated epidermis does not establish that the same pathway is the sole determinant of conductance in an intact plant.

The term action spectrum refers to the magnitude of a biological response as a function of wavelength under stated conditions. It differs from the absorption spectrum of a purified pigment or receptor, which measures light absorption. A close match between spectra can suggest the participation of a chromophore, but overlapping absorption of different pigments and indirect metabolic responses complicate the inference. Genetic evidence from phototropin-deficient plants and measurements of early pathway activation therefore provide stronger mechanistic discrimination than an action spectrum alone.

Red and blue inputs can occur simultaneously in sunlight. A naturally illuminated leaf does not first execute a complete red pathway and then a separate complete blue pathway. Instead, the guard cells and mesophyll integrate simultaneous signals while changing the ionic, metabolic and hydraulic state of the tissue. Separating the pathways in an experimental diagram is valuable for understanding their contributions, but physiological behavior results from their interaction. A comparison of monochromatic treatments is a way to dissect the system rather than a complete description of what happens under a natural spectrum.

8. Closing the pore: anion release, depolarization and water loss

Opening and closure are reversible at the level of aperture but are not simply the same list of molecular events read in reverse order. The opening response depends substantially on proton extrusion and accumulation of osmolytes. Active closure often recruits pathways that inhibit opening machinery and activate ion release. This allows a guard cell to reduce its solute content even when environmental conditions still favor some degree of photosynthetic activity. A physiological signal promoting closure can therefore compete with a signal promoting opening.

Consider a light-to-dark transition in a well-watered C3 plant. Activation of the blue-light-dependent proton pump decreases as the activating signal is removed and regulatory phosphorylation is reversed. Photosynthetic consumption of carbon dioxide declines, and intercellular carbon dioxide may rise. These changes reduce opening-associated drives, but closure still requires a means for losing osmotic solutes. Anion channels, outward potassium channels and tonoplast transport provide routes through which the guard cell can reduce its osmotic content. The detailed contributions depend upon the cell's initial state and the darkness response of the particular species.

Loss of negatively charged anions through the plasma membrane tends to make the interior less negative. This change is termed depolarization. Once membrane potential becomes sufficiently positive relative to the potassium equilibrium potential and the relevant outward-rectifying potassium channels are activated, potassium can leave the cell. In Arabidopsis, GORK provides a representative outward potassium-channel activity. Its name identifies a transport component, not a light receptor. Anion release and potassium release thus cooperate to remove osmotic particles and maintain near electroneutrality.

The direction of anion movement cannot be inferred merely from the label depolarization. It depends on the ion's electrochemical gradient and the transport pathway involved. During typical active closure, anions stored in the guard cell can leave through available efflux routes, and the departure of negative charge contributes to depolarization. This depolarization then helps favor potassium efflux. A useful examination strategy is to ask separately which ion leaves, what charge leaves with it, and what that does to the voltage defined as inside relative to outside.

Slow anion channels and rapid anion channels are distinguished by electrophysiological properties, including their characteristic activation kinetics. SLAC1 is associated with a major slow anion-channel activity in Arabidopsis guard cells, and ALMT12, also called QUAC1, is associated with an important rapid anion conductance. The names of the channel classes do not mean that an entire stomatal closing event has a fixed duration equal to the opening time of one channel. The observed kinetics of the pore depend on many transport processes, vacuolar solute availability, water exchange and mechanical properties.

Genetic evidence that SLAC1 is required for normal S-type anion channel function was reported by Vahisalu and colleagues in 2008. Such studies combined mutations affecting stomatal signaling with measurements of channel activity. An intact plant with a closure defect could otherwise have a lesion in receptor signaling, ion transport, water transport or anatomy. Recording an impaired S-type current while other electrical functions remain measurable helps localize the defect more specifically. A mutation that disrupts anion efflux can also diminish the voltage change that would normally recruit outward potassium conductance.

During sustained closure, osmotic solutes must become available from internal stores. A guard cell with a large vacuole cannot lose all of its stored osmotic content solely through transport across the plasma membrane unless solutes can also move from the vacuolar lumen to the cytosol. The tonoplast thus participates in the process. Organic solutes may also be metabolized, incorporated into other compounds or transported, depending on the conditions. The total water-potential change is governed by all effective osmotic components and by pressure, not exclusively by the loss of one ion species.

As solutes are lost, the guard-cell solute potential becomes less negative at a given volume. If its water potential becomes higher than that of the surroundings, net water movement out of the cell is favored. The resulting fall in volume and turgor changes the configuration of the guard-cell pair and narrows the pore. The process has a mechanical limit: once the opening is nearly closed, further solute loss cannot produce a negative pore width. A guard cell can still undergo additional internal changes after visible closure, so an aperture measurement at zero does not establish that all transport has ceased.

A plant can close stomata without the destruction of guard cells. The ability to reopen when conditions improve demonstrates why closure is ordinarily a regulated physiological movement rather than cellular death. An experiment in which guard cells fail to reopen after a chemical treatment may therefore require a viability control. If the treatment damaged membranes irreversibly, the observed closure would not be useful evidence about the normal signaling pathway. A reversible response and an appropriate control treatment make the mechanistic inference stronger.
