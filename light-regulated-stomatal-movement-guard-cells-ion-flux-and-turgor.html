1. The carbon–water exchange problem

A green leaf is a remarkably exposed organ. Light arrives at its surface, but the carbon needed to manufacture carbohydrate arrives as carbon dioxide from the surrounding atmosphere. The waxy cuticle that limits uncontrolled water loss also restricts diffusion of gases. A leaf therefore needs passages connecting the atmosphere to its internal air spaces. Stomata provide these passages. Their aperture can change repeatedly within the lifetime of the leaf, allowing a plant to regulate gas exchange without producing a new epidermis each time the environment changes. This adjustment is one of the most accessible examples of how environmental information can alter the transport properties of a living cell and then alter the performance of an entire organ.

The term stoma refers to an individual pore and is often used in the broader sense of the pore and its associated guard cells. The plural is stomata. Two guard cells generally border the pore of a stomatal complex. Air diffusing through the pore can reach a substomatal cavity and then the intercellular air spaces of the mesophyll. Carbon dioxide eventually crosses aqueous and cellular barriers before reaching the chloroplasts of photosynthetic cells. Water follows a different net route: liquid water delivered through the vascular system replenishes evaporating surfaces, and water vapour moves toward the atmosphere. A single opening therefore serves two linked yet physiologically different fluxes.

The need to take up carbon dioxide and retain water creates a trade-off. Opening a pore can lower one resistance to carbon dioxide diffusion, but it also facilitates diffusion of water vapour when the leaf interior is more humid than the outside air. If water delivery is adequate and the mesophyll can use additional carbon dioxide, opening can be advantageous. If the supply of liquid water is restricted, increasing vapour loss may threaten cell hydration and photosynthetic capacity. Stomatal regulation is thus not simply an on–off switch associated with sunrise and sunset. It is the changing outcome of several environmental signals acting upon transport and mechanics.

Consider two plants placed under identical light but supplied with different amounts of water. Both may receive enough photons to sustain photosynthesis, yet the water-limited plant may maintain narrower stomatal pores. The difference cannot be explained by light capture alone. It arises because the guard cells integrate information about the environment and the state of surrounding tissues. A plant can reduce water loss by narrowing a pore even when the biochemical machinery of the Calvin cycle is capable of fixing additional carbon. This distinction is important when interpreting a decline in net assimilation: it may reflect limited carbon dioxide delivery, impaired photosynthetic metabolism, or both.

Guard cells accomplish movement without skeletal muscle, contraction of an actomyosin apparatus comparable to animal muscle, or the creation of a new pore. They regulate the amounts and distributions of dissolved substances, alter their water content and change pressure against their walls. The mechanical properties of those walls convert changes in cell pressure into a change in the shape of the opening. In this way, a photon absorbed by a receptor can initiate a chain of events ending in a measurable displacement of a cell boundary.

Three explanatory scales will recur in this chapter. The molecular scale concerns receptors, protein phosphorylation, pumps and channels. The cellular scale concerns solute concentration, membrane potential, water potential and pressure. The leaf scale concerns aperture, conductance, assimilation and transpiration. These scales are related, but measurements from one cannot automatically be treated as measurements of another. A protein phosphorylation signal does not itself report how widely the pore opens, and an increase in leaf temperature is not a direct measurement of a channel current.

2. Stomatal anatomy and the mechanics of movement

A typical epidermal preparation from a broad-leaved flowering plant reveals two curved guard cells arranged around an elongated central pore. Their ends remain connected, and their shared mechanical geometry means that changes in shape can widen or narrow the pore. Guard cells are surrounded by pavement cells and, depending upon the species, subsidiary cells with specialized functions and shapes. The term subsidiary cell denotes a recognizable neighboring cell that contributes to the stomatal complex; it should not be applied indiscriminately to every epidermal cell.

A cell wall prevents a hydrated plant cell from expanding freely like a soap bubble. The expanding protoplast presses against the wall, which develops tensile stress. Pressure within the cell relative to its surroundings is called turgor pressure. Guard cells increase in volume as they take up water, but their walls resist deformation differently in different directions. The combination of wall thickness, cellulose arrangement, geometry and attachment to neighboring cells directs the resulting displacement. In many kidney-shaped stomata, increased turgor causes the cells to bow away from each other and increases pore width.

A common textbook drawing represents the wall facing the pore as thicker than the outer wall. This is helpful for introducing differential wall mechanics, but thickness alone is not a complete mechanical explanation. The orientation of cellulose microfibrils determines which directions resist extension, while the geometry of the poles and the surrounding epidermal tissue influences the path of deformation. Two cells with the same turgor can therefore produce different apertures if their walls have different mechanical properties. Conversely, a defect in wall organization may impair movement despite a normal molecular signal and normal solute uptake.

In many grasses, the guard cells have enlarged ends and a relatively narrow middle region, giving them a dumbbell-like outline. Specialized subsidiary cells flank the guard cells. A redistribution of solutes between these cells can favor changes in the pressure difference across their shared walls. The combination of geometry and pressure exchange contributes to rapid movement in representative grasses. One should not infer that every plant with dumbbell-shaped stomata has identical kinetics under all conditions; the supply of water, transport proteins and environmental conditions remain important.

The pressure of the epidermal cells around a stoma can oppose opening. If a neighboring cell presses inward, the same guard-cell turgor can produce a smaller aperture than when the neighboring cell is less pressurized. This explains why stomata must be understood as mechanical systems embedded in living tissue rather than as isolated elastic objects. Experiments comparing pressure and aperture in intact and manipulated tissue have supported the importance of neighboring-cell mechanics. Franks and Farquhar examined the relationship between stomatal form, pressure and gas-exchange dynamics in contrasting species, illustrating why anatomy is functionally consequential.

A stoma usually changes its width on a timescale shorter than developmental changes in stomatal density. Density describes how many stomata occupy a specified leaf area, whereas aperture describes the dimensions of an individual opening. Density can respond to developmental conditions in newly formed leaves, but an already mature leaf can regulate its momentary gas exchange primarily through aperture. If a mutant has fewer stomata yet each remaining stoma opens normally, reduced leaf conductance need not indicate a defect in the blue-light signaling pathway.

The guard-cell vacuole occupies a large fraction of the swollen cell volume. Its membrane, the tonoplast, is a distinct transport barrier from the plasma membrane. Transport into the cytoplasm is consequently not equivalent to transport into the vacuole. Solutes entering through the outer membrane can be stored or redistributed across the tonoplast, affecting how much water the entire cell contains while maintaining cytosolic conditions compatible with metabolism. During closure the vacuole changes volume and organization. Neither the vacuole nor the cell wall acts as a light receptor simply because it participates in movement.

3. Water potential, electrochemical gradients and turgor

To understand the direction of water movement, it is useful to separate the chemical effect of solutes from the mechanical effect of pressure. Water moves spontaneously toward lower water potential when a permeable path connects the two regions. At the cellular scale, neglecting gravitational and matric terms where appropriate, water potential is approximated as the sum of solute potential and pressure potential: Ψw = Ψs + Ψp. The solute component is negative relative to pure water at the same temperature and pressure, because adding dissolved particles lowers water activity. The pressure component is usually positive in a turgid living plant cell.

Suppose a guard cell initially has a solute potential of −0.8 MPa and a pressure potential of +0.5 MPa. Its water potential is −0.3 MPa. If the surrounding apoplast has a water potential of −0.3 MPa, the two compartments are at equilibrium with respect to net water movement. Now suppose the guard cell accumulates solutes, initially lowering its solute potential to −1.0 MPa without an immediate change in volume. Its water potential becomes −0.5 MPa. Water can now enter from the apoplast. As the cell swells, rising pressure and dilution of solutes tend to raise its water potential. The final volume depends upon both the wall mechanics and the remaining solute content.

The dilute ideal-solution approximation expresses solute potential as Ψs = −iCRT. Here i represents the effective number of dissolved particles per formula unit, C is molar concentration, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. With R = 0.008314 L MPa mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and a total ideal osmolar concentration of 0.10 mol L⁻¹ at 298 K, the solute potential is approximately −0.248 MPa. If concentration refers to the total particles rather than undissociated salt formula units, no additional multiplication by a dissociation factor is appropriate. Real guard-cell solutions contain interacting solutes and therefore depart from an ideal solution; the equation is a teaching approximation, not a direct measurement of intracellular osmotic conditions.

A cell may gain osmotic particles without importing every particle from the external medium. Hydrolysis of a polymer such as starch can yield a larger number of soluble molecules from stored material. Conversion of carbon compounds into organic anions can also alter the cell's osmotic composition. Nevertheless, changes in concentration must be distinguished from changes in amount. A cell can contain more potassium after opening while having a smaller change in concentration than expected because its water volume has also increased. Measurements of concentration alone cannot establish the net amount transported unless volume is known or controlled.

Ions experience both a chemical gradient and an electrical gradient. A positively charged potassium ion is attracted toward a region of lower electrical potential. If a cell has a negatively charged interior relative to the outside, the electrical gradient favors potassium entry, but a high intracellular potassium concentration favors its outward diffusion. The direction of spontaneous movement depends on the sum of these tendencies. With membrane potential defined as inside minus outside, the equilibrium potential of a monovalent potassium ion is EK = (RT/F) ln([K⁺]out/[K⁺]in), where F is the Faraday constant. At 25 °C the expression is approximately 59 mV multiplied by the base-ten logarithm of the outside-to-inside concentration ratio.

For an illustrative guard cell with external potassium activity approximated as 10 mM and internal activity approximated as 100 mM, EK is about −59 mV. At a membrane potential of −120 mV, inward movement is energetically favored when potassium channels are open; at −20 mV, outward movement is favored. These numbers are hypothetical. In real cells, channel gating, activity coefficients, intracellular compartmentation and other permeant ions alter the observed current. The equilibrium potential determines the direction of a passive electrochemical tendency, not whether the necessary channel is actually open.

A plasma-membrane proton pump uses the energy from ATP hydrolysis to export positively charged hydrogen ions. The immediate electrical tendency is for the interior to become more negative, a change called hyperpolarization. The extracellular solution also becomes more acidic if it is not strongly buffered. Proton extrusion is primary active transport because the transporter couples ATP utilization directly to transport against an electrochemical gradient. In contrast, potassium movement through an open channel is passive with respect to its own electrochemical gradient. The energy spent by the pump establishes the condition that can favor ion entry through the channel.

Water entry is not itself powered by ATP hydrolysis. Water moves along the water-potential difference through available membrane routes, including aquaporins, while metabolism supplies energy for establishing and maintaining the ion distribution. This separation explains how a change in activity of a single pump can initiate several downstream processes without that pump carrying potassium or water. It also explains why shutting down ATP production can disrupt osmoregulation even when water molecules remain freely able to cross a membrane.

The small electrical imbalance responsible for membrane voltage must not be confused with bulk charge accumulation. The membrane separates charges and behaves partly like a capacitor. Only a small proportion of the total dissolved ions is required to produce a substantial membrane voltage. Sustained potassium accumulation, however, requires compensating negative charge, often supplied by inorganic anions and metabolically produced organic anions. Without such balancing processes, a growing electrical opposition would limit further cation entry. The principle of approximate bulk electroneutrality applies while a voltage is maintained across the membrane.

4. From blue photons to the guard-cell proton pump

Light can influence the stomata through more than one route. In the blue-light-specific response, phototropins perceive photons and initiate a signaling pathway in guard cells. Their LOV domains bind flavin mononucleotide and participate in light-dependent conformational changes that regulate a protein kinase domain. The detailed photochemistry and receptor architecture belong to the preceding phototropin chapter; the central question here is how the same type of photoreceptor selects a particular physiological output in a guard cell.

Arabidopsis possesses two major phototropins, phot1 and phot2. Both can contribute to blue-light-induced opening, and their relative roles depend on illumination and context. Loss of one receptor does not necessarily eliminate the response because the second receptor can contribute. Experiments comparing phototropin mutants helped demonstrate that these receptors participate in blue-light regulation of stomatal aperture. A double mutant provides a more stringent test of the requirement for the receptor class than a single mutant, but it is still necessary to control for baseline aperture and other physiological changes.

A crucial intermediate is BLUE LIGHT SIGNALING 1, or BLUS1, a protein kinase associated with the guard-cell response. Phototropins phosphorylate BLUS1 in response to blue illumination. BLUS1 helps provide specificity: loss of the protein impairs the stomatal response even though phototropins also control processes elsewhere in the plant, such as directional shoot growth and movement of chloroplasts. The same class of receptor can therefore be connected to different downstream proteins in different tissues, rather than requiring a new photoreceptor for every output.

Evidence from Takemiya and colleagues in 2013 connected BLUS1 to the pathway between phototropin activation and proton-pump activation. Their genetic and biochemical results allowed investigators to distinguish a lesion in the relay from a lesion in primary light perception. A guard cell that still activates phototropin but fails to stimulate the pump after illumination demonstrates that activation of the receptor is not sufficient to guarantee the final response. The finding is particularly useful in examinations about pathway ordering because upstream activation and downstream failure are measured separately.

Another implicated protein is BHP, a Raf-like kinase identified through studies of blue-light-induced proton-pump phosphorylation. BHP contributes to the signaling process, and type 1 protein phosphatase activity has also been implicated. A simplified pathway diagram should not suggest that each protein directly phosphorylates the next one merely because they are placed in a sequence. Genetic requirement, physical association and direct enzymatic substrate relationship are different experimental claims. The downstream regulation between early kinases and the pump includes steps whose detailed connections have required continued investigation.

The plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase is the effector responsible for proton export. Its regulatory carboxyl-terminal region normally constrains enzyme activity. Phosphorylation of a conserved threonine near the end of the protein permits binding of a 14-3-3 regulatory protein, stabilizing an activated state. The resulting increase in proton transport links the phosphorylation network to an electrical and chemical gradient. Kinoshita and Shimazaki demonstrated the relationship between blue illumination, phosphorylation of the regulatory tail, 14-3-3 association and increased pump activity in experiments published in 1999.

Protein phosphorylation transfers a phosphate group to a target protein; it does not mean that the phosphate itself supplies the sustained energy used for proton export. Regulatory phosphorylation can alter protein conformation or binding properties. Once activated, the H⁺-ATPase repeatedly hydrolyses ATP to perform transport cycles. A cell could exhibit phosphorylation of the regulatory site but fail to maintain substantial proton extrusion if ATP availability were inadequate. Conversely, an artificial stimulus that stabilizes the activated pump can sometimes bypass defective upstream signaling, provided that downstream transport and water supply remain functional.

Blue light is therefore information at the beginning of this pathway and metabolic energy is needed at the effector stage. The distinction prevents a common confusion between phototropin signaling and chloroplast photophosphorylation. Phototropins do not produce a stream of photosynthetic electrons to reduce NADP⁺, and the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase does not synthesize ATP by harvesting a thylakoid proton gradient in this process. The two systems involve different membranes, proteins and directions of energy coupling.

A useful experiment compares guard cells maintained under red background illumination with those receiving the same red background plus a weak blue component. A response to the added blue component, especially when it appears at irradiances that produce relatively little additional mesophyll assimilation, provides evidence that blue light acts as a specific signal. It does not by itself prove the identity of the receptor or establish complete independence from the metabolic state of the leaf. Genetic tests and biochemical measurements are needed to connect the spectral response to particular pathway components.

5. Ion entry, counterions and the opening response

The proton pump does not itself transport the major osmotically active cation. Its electrogenic activity creates a membrane voltage that can favor the entry of potassium ions through other proteins. An ion channel is a selective aqueous pathway through a membrane, capable of switching between states that permit or restrict permeation. The channel does not have to hydrolyze ATP to drive each ion through the pore. Its selectivity and gating determine which ion species can cross and under what conditions. Inward-rectifying potassium channels in the guard-cell plasma membrane are particularly important when the membrane is strongly negative.

The term inward rectification describes a relationship between current and membrane potential, not a permanent prohibition against every movement in the opposite direction. In the physiological opening pathway, hyperpolarization increases the activity of relevant inward channels and favors potassium accumulation. KAT1 and KAT2 are useful Arabidopsis examples. A genetic defect in one potassium channel may produce a modest phenotype because other channels can contribute to the total transport capacity. By contrast, blocking all relevant inward potassium conductance would remove an important route for using the voltage generated by the pump. Whether aperture is completely abolished would depend on alternative osmotic solutes and experimental conditions.

Imagine a guard cell that has just activated its plasma-membrane proton pump. Hydrogen ions leave the cytoplasm and membrane voltage becomes more negative. As potassium begins entering, the positive charge carried by potassium tends to oppose further hyperpolarization. Pump activity, inward current and other currents jointly determine the actual voltage. There is no requirement that the membrane potential remain at the most negative value attained immediately after pump activation. A voltage trace during opening may therefore show a rapid transient followed by a partially recovered negative potential while potassium accumulation continues. Interpreting such a trace requires consideration of the net current rather than an assumption that a single transporter fixes the voltage.

An ion that can enter a cell thermodynamically is not necessarily entering at an appreciable rate. The membrane lipid bilayer provides a large barrier to passage of charged solutes. Channels, transporters and their availability therefore determine kinetic access to the gradients established by the pump. For example, in an illustrative preparation maintained at a membrane potential more negative than EK, a potassium-selective channel blocker reduces inward current without needing to reverse the direction of its electrochemical gradient. The absence of current in this case is evidence about the conducting pathway, not evidence that the potassium gradient vanished.

The movement of chloride must be considered separately from potassium because chloride carries negative charge. A negative intracellular voltage tends to repel a freely permeating chloride ion. It is consequently incorrect to explain the entry of both potassium and chloride solely by attraction to a negatively charged cell interior. Uptake of anions can depend on their concentration gradients and on coupled transport with protons. The proton gradient generated by the H⁺-ATPase can provide energy for secondary active uptake. Chloride and nitrate are examples of inorganic anions whose contribution depends upon availability in the surrounding medium and the transport systems expressed by the cell.

A guard cell can also produce malate, an organic anion. At near-neutral cytosolic pH malate is substantially negatively charged, making it useful as a counterion for potassium accumulation. Its synthesis is connected with metabolism of carbon skeletons. Phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase can catalyze the addition of bicarbonate to phosphoenolpyruvate, forming oxaloacetate, which can then be reduced to malate by malate dehydrogenase. The pathway illustrates how metabolic transformations can contribute to an osmotic response while maintaining charge balance. Its importance does not imply that malate is always the only organic solute accumulating in every species and treatment.

A simplified charge calculation shows why the identity of counterions matters. Suppose, for illustration, a cell accumulates 20 femtomoles of K⁺ while chloride is the only compensating anion. Approximately 20 femtomoles of Cl⁻ would balance that positive charge. If divalent malate were the only compensating anion in this simplified model, approximately 10 femtomoles of malate²⁻ would balance the same amount of potassium. The total number of osmotically active particles would differ between the two scenarios. In a real guard cell, changes in hydrogen ion binding, other solutes and compartmentation must also be considered. The exercise is meant to connect valence and osmotic particle number, not to predict an exact aperture from two ion amounts.

The distinction between charge balance and osmotic balance becomes especially important when comparing neutral sugars and salts. Sucrose contributes osmotic particles but does not supply an equivalent amount of negative charge to balance potassium. A cell may accumulate sucrose as part of its osmotic adjustment while still requiring appropriate counterions for retained cations. Conversely, metabolically converting malate into a neutral product without changing other ions could create a charge-balance problem. Cellular metabolism and membrane transport therefore cannot be treated as independent lists of events.

Potassium and anions are often redistributed into the vacuole during opening. As the vacuole expands, the cell can accumulate considerable solute and water without exposing every cytosolic enzyme to equally large concentration changes. Tonoplast transporters regulate this distribution using voltage and proton gradients across the tonoplast. Vacuolar proton pumps and ion exchangers are distinct from the plasma-membrane proton ATPase at the beginning of the blue-light opening pathway. A drug that changes vacuolar acidification can therefore influence aperture even if the plasma membrane still hyperpolarizes normally. In a mechanistic experiment, recording plasma membrane voltage alone would not resolve the state of the vacuole.

Solute accumulation lowers the water potential of the guard cell before the additional water enters. The resulting flow raises turgor until pressure, dilution and environmental supply establish a new balance. The mechanical system converts the swelling into a change in pore area. Thus the electrical signal is translated into an osmotic change, and the osmotic change is translated into movement. A defect at any step can reduce aperture, but the same final phenotype does not show which step is defective. The causal chain is useful because it allows the investigator to design measurements of distinct intermediates instead of relying only on the visible endpoint.

6. Metabolic support: starch, sugars and guard-cell chloroplasts

Guard cells generally possess chloroplasts in flowering plants, unlike many surrounding pavement cells. Their chloroplasts contain photosynthetic machinery and can participate in the metabolic processes that support stomatal responses. However, their metabolism should not be regarded as identical to that of the larger photosynthetic mesophyll compartment. Guard cells have the specialized task of repeatedly adjusting osmotic content, transporting ions and maintaining membrane gradients. A change in carbon metabolism may therefore influence pore opening through energy supply, synthesis of solutes or signaling rather than through the production of exportable carbohydrate alone.

Stored starch is particularly instructive. A starch polymer consists of many glucose units, but a polymer molecule counts as a single dissolved particle if it is dissolved at all, and starch granules are not freely dissolved osmolytes. Breakdown of the polymer can increase the number of smaller soluble molecules available for metabolism. Some products can supply glycolytic intermediates, while others can contribute to soluble sugars or organic acids. Thus mobilizing a pre-existing store is one means of supporting an early response to illumination without having to wait for the same quantity of carbon to be fixed anew.

In Arabidopsis guard cells, enzymes including the starch-degrading activities associated with BAM1 and AMY3 have been linked to light-responsive starch mobilization. Mutants with defective mobilization can display impaired opening kinetics under appropriate conditions. This evidence is more informative than a simple observation that starch content decreases during illumination, because a correlation between starch loss and opening would not establish whether the loss is necessary for the measured response. Even a genetic requirement for rapid opening, however, does not show that every osmotically active molecule originated from starch or that the same pathway dominates in all plant species.

The fate of a glucose unit liberated from storage depends on metabolic conditions. If it is metabolized through glycolysis, ATP and carbon intermediates may be generated. If carbohydrate accumulates as a soluble sugar, it can contribute to the osmotic potential. If an intermediate provides carbon for malate synthesis, it can contribute indirectly to the counterion pool. The routes compete for carbon and are coordinated with transport. A simple statement that starch changes into sugar and sugar opens stomata does not explain which solutes balance ionic charge, where ATP is produced or how metabolism is regulated.

Historically, explanations of stomatal movement shifted from emphasis on transformations of carbohydrate to a more integrated understanding that includes potassium transport, organic acid metabolism, membrane voltage and active proton pumping. The older carbohydrate-based observations were not meaningless: they identified metabolic changes occurring in the same specialized cells that changed shape. The limitation was treating a single observed metabolic change as a complete explanation. Modern models incorporate both metabolic and electrical processes because neither alone accounts for all experimental perturbations.

Guard cells can receive sugars from neighboring tissues and the apoplast. This allows their energetic state to depend partly on processes occurring elsewhere in the leaf. At different times of day, the relative contributions of potassium salts, malate and sugars to osmotic content can change. These changes should not be converted into an absolute rule that potassium controls only morning movement while sucrose controls only afternoon movement. The pattern depends on species, growth conditions, carbon availability and measurement time. The appropriate general principle is that the osmotic system can use more than one solute strategy.

ATP required by the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase can be supplied by cellular energy metabolism. Respiratory metabolism remains relevant to illuminated guard cells; illumination does not turn mitochondria off. Photosynthetic processes in guard-cell chloroplasts can also affect energy and reductant availability, depending on the species and conditions. A blue-light pulse is not chemically converted into ATP by the phototropin molecule. It changes the activity of a regulatory pathway that determines how cellular ATP will be used. A metabolic inhibitor may consequently suppress opening even when the photoreceptor remains competent to absorb light.

The interpretation of experiments using chloroplast inhibitors requires particular care. An inhibitor of photosynthetic electron transport may change mesophyll assimilation, intercellular carbon dioxide, guard-cell energy status and redox signals simultaneously. A reduced response after such treatment does not prove that the guard-cell chloroplast alone is the site of the relevant effect. Comparing isolated epidermis with intact leaves, testing different illumination conditions, and measuring intermediate biochemical responses can help separate these possibilities. Each preparation also introduces its own limitations: isolated guard cells no longer experience all of the physiological communication present in a whole leaf.

The same reasoning applies to measurements of sugars. An increase in guard-cell sugar content during illumination could reflect photosynthetic synthesis, uptake from another cell, starch breakdown or a combination of these processes. Distinguishing them requires knowledge of fluxes, not just the size of the metabolite pool at one time. A comparison of starch content, soluble carbohydrate content and opening kinetics at multiple times can identify relationships that a single endpoint cannot. Where a perturbation changes all three measurements, the direction of causation still requires a suitable control or rescue experiment.

These connections explain why the electrical and metabolic components of stomatal opening should be taught together. The pump establishes the driving forces for membrane transport, organic metabolism supplies energy and some osmolytes, and vacuolar storage converts net solute accumulation into a change in water content. A functioning guard cell is not an electronic circuit alone and not a passive osmometer alone; it is a living metabolic and mechanical system that uses both principles.

7. Blue-light-specific and photosynthesis-associated responses

A plant leaf exposed to red light can show increased stomatal conductance even though the canonical guard-cell phototropin pathway is activated especially by blue wavelengths. This observation is not a contradiction. Photosynthesis in the mesophyll consumes carbon dioxide, reducing its concentration in the intercellular air spaces when consumption rises faster than diffusion from the atmosphere. Guard cells respond to carbon dioxide-related information, so changes associated with photosynthetic activity can encourage opening. Red light can also influence guard-cell chloroplast metabolism and other photoreceptor signaling. The term red-light response therefore describes a physiological response to an illumination treatment, not a claim that a single receptor explains every component.

To distinguish these responses experimentally, it is helpful to choose a red background that supports measurable photosynthesis and establish a baseline conductance. A small blue-light increment is then superimposed while other conditions remain as similar as possible. If the additional opening is much greater than would be expected from its incremental effect on mesophyll assimilation, a specific signaling component is suggested. One must still ask whether the two treatments alter total photon flux, leaf temperature or carbon dioxide concentration. Matching only the power of two light sources does not automatically match photon number, because photon energy depends on wavelength.

The energy of one photon is hc/λ. Using the same incident energy flux at 450 and 660 nm gives different photon fluxes because photons at the shorter wavelength carry more energy individually. For physiological illumination experiments, photosynthetic photon flux density is often expressed in micromoles of photons per square metre per second. This measure counts photons over a defined wavelength range rather than weighting each by its energy. A spectral comparison may also need information about absorption by the leaf; equal incident photon flux does not guarantee equal absorbed photon flux. These considerations are especially relevant when the experiment is designed to discriminate a spectral receptor response from an energy-dependent metabolic response.

The blue-light-specific response can be sensitive to relatively low blue irradiance against a red-light background, while larger red-light treatments often produce responses associated with photosynthetic carbon metabolism. These descriptions are tendencies and not universal saturation points. A numerical threshold measured in one species, growth condition or experimental apparatus cannot simply be transplanted to another. If an examination graph shows saturation of the added-blue response while photosynthesis continues to increase with red irradiance, the sensible inference is that at least two distinguishable response components are present, not that photosynthesis has ceased.

Changes in intercellular carbon dioxide, conventionally represented by Ci, are central to the interpretation of an intact-leaf gas exchange experiment. Suppose an investigator increases red irradiance and observes a rise in assimilation followed by a rise in conductance. The sequence is compatible with enhanced carbon consumption contributing to an opening signal. But both assimilation and conductance may be responding to other effects of light; temporal association alone is not enough. If a second experiment holds Ci approximately constant using controlled atmospheric carbon dioxide, changes in conductance can be compared under more discriminating conditions. Such manipulation is not identical to holding external carbon dioxide constant, because the concentration within the leaf depends on both supply and consumption.

A 2021 study examining BLUS1 signaling and intercellular carbon dioxide emphasized the importance of combining receptor-associated signaling with carbon dioxide-related conditions in the experimental context studied. This is a useful corrective to diagrams in which blue light is shown as an unconditional command to open. A model that predicts an aperture must account for the prevailing state of the guard cell and the leaf. A biochemically active pathway may be necessary for one component of opening but still insufficient for a large net movement under a condition that strongly favors closure.

In a comparative experiment, wild-type plants and phot1 phot2 double mutants can be tested under red background illumination with and without the same weak blue increment. The primary comparison is the difference caused by the increment within each genotype. If the mutant loses most of the blue increment but still exhibits a red-associated increase, the two responses are at least partly separable. Differences in basal aperture or stomatal density must be measured before attributing a lower absolute conductance to the signaling lesion. A genetically complemented line provides an additional control for attributing the phenotype to the disrupted receptors.

An epidermal strip provides a different experimental window. It removes much of the mesophyll and can expose stomatal responses more directly to controlled light, buffer composition and external ions. A blue-light response in this preparation supports the ability of the epidermal complex to respond without intact mesophyll. Nevertheless, isolation can change carbon dioxide levels, hydration, metabolic supply and mechanical constraints. A response in an isolated epidermis does not establish that the same pathway is the sole determinant of conductance in an intact plant.

The term action spectrum refers to the magnitude of a biological response as a function of wavelength under stated conditions. It differs from the absorption spectrum of a purified pigment or receptor, which measures light absorption. A close match between spectra can suggest the participation of a chromophore, but overlapping absorption of different pigments and indirect metabolic responses complicate the inference. Genetic evidence from phototropin-deficient plants and measurements of early pathway activation therefore provide stronger mechanistic discrimination than an action spectrum alone.

Red and blue inputs can occur simultaneously in sunlight. A naturally illuminated leaf does not first execute a complete red pathway and then a separate complete blue pathway. Instead, the guard cells and mesophyll integrate simultaneous signals while changing the ionic, metabolic and hydraulic state of the tissue. Separating the pathways in an experimental diagram is valuable for understanding their contributions, but physiological behavior results from their interaction. A comparison of monochromatic treatments is a way to dissect the system rather than a complete description of what happens under a natural spectrum.

8. Closing the pore: anion release, depolarization and water loss

Opening and closure are reversible at the level of aperture but are not simply the same list of molecular events read in reverse order. The opening response depends substantially on proton extrusion and accumulation of osmolytes. Active closure often recruits pathways that inhibit opening machinery and activate ion release. This allows a guard cell to reduce its solute content even when environmental conditions still favor some degree of photosynthetic activity. A physiological signal promoting closure can therefore compete with a signal promoting opening.

Consider a light-to-dark transition in a well-watered C3 plant. Activation of the blue-light-dependent proton pump decreases as the activating signal is removed and regulatory phosphorylation is reversed. Photosynthetic consumption of carbon dioxide declines, and intercellular carbon dioxide may rise. These changes reduce opening-associated drives, but closure still requires a means for losing osmotic solutes. Anion channels, outward potassium channels and tonoplast transport provide routes through which the guard cell can reduce its osmotic content. The detailed contributions depend upon the cell's initial state and the darkness response of the particular species.

Loss of negatively charged anions through the plasma membrane tends to make the interior less negative. This change is termed depolarization. Once membrane potential becomes sufficiently positive relative to the potassium equilibrium potential and the relevant outward-rectifying potassium channels are activated, potassium can leave the cell. In Arabidopsis, GORK provides a representative outward potassium-channel activity. Its name identifies a transport component, not a light receptor. Anion release and potassium release thus cooperate to remove osmotic particles and maintain near electroneutrality.

The direction of anion movement cannot be inferred merely from the label depolarization. It depends on the ion's electrochemical gradient and the transport pathway involved. During typical active closure, anions stored in the guard cell can leave through available efflux routes, and the departure of negative charge contributes to depolarization. This depolarization then helps favor potassium efflux. A useful examination strategy is to ask separately which ion leaves, what charge leaves with it, and what that does to the voltage defined as inside relative to outside.

Slow anion channels and rapid anion channels are distinguished by electrophysiological properties, including their characteristic activation kinetics. SLAC1 is associated with a major slow anion-channel activity in Arabidopsis guard cells, and ALMT12, also called QUAC1, is associated with an important rapid anion conductance. The names of the channel classes do not mean that an entire stomatal closing event has a fixed duration equal to the opening time of one channel. The observed kinetics of the pore depend on many transport processes, vacuolar solute availability, water exchange and mechanical properties.

Genetic evidence that SLAC1 is required for normal S-type anion channel function was reported by Vahisalu and colleagues in 2008. Such studies combined mutations affecting stomatal signaling with measurements of channel activity. An intact plant with a closure defect could otherwise have a lesion in receptor signaling, ion transport, water transport or anatomy. Recording an impaired S-type current while other electrical functions remain measurable helps localize the defect more specifically. A mutation that disrupts anion efflux can also diminish the voltage change that would normally recruit outward potassium conductance.

During sustained closure, osmotic solutes must become available from internal stores. A guard cell with a large vacuole cannot lose all of its stored osmotic content solely through transport across the plasma membrane unless solutes can also move from the vacuolar lumen to the cytosol. The tonoplast thus participates in the process. Organic solutes may also be metabolized, incorporated into other compounds or transported, depending on the conditions. The total water-potential change is governed by all effective osmotic components and by pressure, not exclusively by the loss of one ion species.

As solutes are lost, the guard-cell solute potential becomes less negative at a given volume. If its water potential becomes higher than that of the surroundings, net water movement out of the cell is favored. The resulting fall in volume and turgor changes the configuration of the guard-cell pair and narrows the pore. The process has a mechanical limit: once the opening is nearly closed, further solute loss cannot produce a negative pore width. A guard cell can still undergo additional internal changes after visible closure, so an aperture measurement at zero does not establish that all transport has ceased.

A plant can close stomata without the destruction of guard cells. The ability to reopen when conditions improve demonstrates why closure is ordinarily a regulated physiological movement rather than cellular death. An experiment in which guard cells fail to reopen after a chemical treatment may therefore require a viability control. If the treatment damaged membranes irreversibly, the observed closure would not be useful evidence about the normal signaling pathway. A reversible response and an appropriate control treatment make the mechanistic inference stronger.

9. ABA signaling and the competition between opening and closure

Abscisic acid, abbreviated ABA, is a plant hormone with important functions during dehydration and other physiological challenges. The hormone is not the material that physically plugs a stomatal pore. Its influence is mediated through regulatory proteins that change the transport state of guard cells. Because a stoma can open or close within an already differentiated leaf, a pathway that modifies existing membrane proteins provides an especially effective means of controlling its movement.

A central ABA perception mechanism involves the PYR/PYL/RCAR family of receptors and a group of protein phosphatases belonging to clade A of the PP2C family. In the absence of a strong ABA signal, these phosphatases restrain relevant SnRK2 kinases. When ABA binds to its receptors, the receptor–hormone complex inhibits the phosphatases, thereby releasing inhibition of the kinases. OST1, also known as SnRK2.6, is a representative kinase important for guard-cell responses. The double negative relationship is significant: inhibiting an inhibitor allows a downstream activity to increase.

OST1 can promote anion-channel activity through phosphorylation of SLAC1 and other regulatory interactions. Increased anion efflux favors depolarization and can recruit outward potassium transport. ABA also influences the opening side of the balance by suppressing processes that normally favor solute accumulation. In particular, inhibition of the blue-light-activated plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase reduces the driving force for sustained inward potassium transport. Thus ABA need not reverse every individual biochemical step in the phototropin pathway. It changes the relative activities of transport systems whose combined output determines net solute retention or loss.

The inhibition of PP2Cs by ABA-bound receptors is often misunderstood as destruction of the phosphatases. A receptor complex can inhibit an enzyme without degrading it. This distinction matters when predicting the reversibility of the response. If the hormone concentration falls and the inhibitory complex dissociates, available phosphatases can once again influence kinase activity. An experimental measurement showing unchanged abundance of a PP2C protein after treatment is therefore compatible with a large change in its functional activity.

Consider a loss-of-function mutation affecting OST1. A suitable experiment might compare the wild type and mutant under identical illumination and initially similar apertures before adding ABA. The expected result is an impaired ABA-induced decrease in aperture in the mutant, although parallel pathways may leave some residual response. If no ABA response is observed, the result alone cannot show whether ABA binding to receptors is absent or whether signaling after receptor activation has failed. Measuring receptor-dependent PP2C inhibition or testing the ability to activate a downstream ion channel can help distinguish these possibilities.

Calcium is another important component of guard-cell signaling. Changes in the cytosolic free calcium concentration can regulate ion channels, kinases and other proteins. Calcium-dependent protein kinases can contribute to activation of anion channels in appropriate conditions. Reactive oxygen species, including hydrogen peroxide, participate in signaling events associated with ABA; controlled production of these molecules can affect calcium-related responses. The same chemical species can cause cellular damage when present at excessive concentrations, so a signal-generating process must be distinguished from uncontrolled oxidative stress.

It is tempting to represent ABA signaling as one universal chain in which hormone binding is always followed by the same calcium transient and every channel is activated in precisely the same order. Such a representation is too rigid. Calcium-dependent and calcium-independent processes can contribute to the response, and their relative importance can change with stimulus strength, developmental context and experimental preparation. An observed calcium increase may be sufficient to modify some transport activities without being the only route by which ABA influences closure. The essential NET-level model is a network converging on ion transport, not an obligatory single-file sequence of all known messengers.

A calcium chelator provides an instructive perturbation. If buffering cytosolic calcium reduces the closing response but does not abolish it, the remaining closure need not be attributed to experimental error. It may indicate calcium-independent signaling or incompletely buffered local changes. Controls must establish that the chelator reached the intended compartment and that guard cells remained viable. Conversely, if artificially elevating calcium produces closure, the observation shows that the manipulated condition can activate closing machinery; it does not establish that every natural ABA response requires exactly the same calcium signal.

ROS measurements must also be interpreted in relation to the biological readout. An increase in fluorescence from a redox-sensitive dye after ABA treatment might indicate a change in the intracellular oxidant environment. However, dye loading, illumination, cellular compartmentation and specificity can influence the signal. Genetic or pharmacological perturbation of ROS production can add evidence, provided that off-target effects are considered. An examination question asking whether an observed fluorescence increase proves direct phosphorylation of SLAC1 has an unambiguous answer: it does not, because the measurements concern different molecular events.

The contrast between opening and closure can be organized around the membrane voltage. Blue-light-dependent pump activation favors a more negative membrane potential and promotes inward potassium conductance. ABA-associated anion efflux favors a less negative potential and can recruit outward potassium conductance. These are typical causal tendencies, not fixed voltages for all species. A cell experiencing both stimuli has simultaneous currents; the net voltage and fluxes depend on the strength and timing of each component. The pore can remain partly open at a dynamic steady state without either signaling pathway being completely inactive.

Suppose a constitutively activated proton pump is expressed in guard cells. A first prediction is that the opening machinery may remain active under conditions that normally reduce pump stimulation. A second, separate prediction concerns ABA closure: anion and potassium efflux may still occur if the closure pathway remains capable of overcoming the opening-associated current and solute uptake. Whether the mutant actually fails to close depends on the quantitative balance between these processes. An answer that declares closure physically impossible whenever any proton pump is active ignores the simultaneous operation of multiple transporters.

The full hormonal physiology of ABA includes biosynthesis, turnover, transport and responses in non-stomatal tissues. Those processes belong to the plant hormone chapter. Here the required connection is from receptor perception to the transport and mechanical events that counter light-induced opening. Understanding this connection enables students to predict how a leaf can receive strong light yet maintain a restricted aperture during dehydration, and why direct signaling in guard cells is more informative than memorizing a universal statement that light opens stomata and ABA closes them.

10. Carbon dioxide as a regulatory input

Carbon dioxide occupies two roles in stomatal physiology. It is a substrate for photosynthetic assimilation, and its availability also provides information that influences stomatal aperture. In an intact leaf the intercellular carbon dioxide concentration depends on the rate at which carbon dioxide arrives through the pore and the rate at which it is consumed or released by metabolism. A guard cell can therefore receive information about the balance between supply and demand rather than simply about the concentration in the surrounding atmosphere.

At a given external concentration, an increase in photosynthetic demand can lower Ci before the pore has had time to adjust. This change can contribute to opening, increasing the capacity for diffusion into the leaf. If the external concentration rises sufficiently, Ci may increase even while assimilation increases, and many C3 plants reduce their stomatal conductance under elevated carbon dioxide. The response is not a decision made by the chloroplast in isolation. Regulatory systems in the guard cells detect changes associated with carbon dioxide and bicarbonate, transmitting the information toward transport machinery.

Carbonic anhydrases accelerate the reversible interconversion of dissolved carbon dioxide and bicarbonate. This reaction links dissolved gas concentration to the availability of bicarbonate, while the equilibrium is influenced by pH. Carbonic anhydrase itself is not an ion channel that opens the pore. Evidence from Arabidopsis has implicated carbonic anhydrases and guard-cell signaling proteins, including the kinase HT1 and MAP kinases MPK4 and MPK12, in the response to carbon dioxide. At examination depth, the relevant principle is that carbon dioxide-related sensing regulates anion-channel activity through signaling pathways that overlap with, but are not identical to, ABA signaling.

Recent mechanistic studies support inhibition of HT1 through carbon dioxide/bicarbonate-dependent interactions involving MPK4 or MPK12. The downstream consequences favor closing under elevated carbon dioxide. A detailed molecular inventory of every reported intermediate is not required to understand the core mechanism. More important is the logic of the genetic perturbation: disrupting a negative regulator of closing-associated signaling can have a different phenotype from disrupting a positive regulator, and the functional consequence cannot be inferred from the word kinase alone.

Anion-channel activation is a point of convergence for several closing signals. A mutation in a shared downstream transport component may impair responses to both ABA and elevated carbon dioxide. This does not prove that ABA and carbon dioxide are detected by the same upstream receptor. If two pathways converge on a common effector, eliminating that effector affects both outputs even though the inputs remain mechanistically distinct. Conversely, a mutation specifically affecting carbon dioxide perception might leave a substantial ABA response intact. Testing more than one stimulus is therefore an effective approach to identifying the level at which a mutant is defective.

Consider an illustrative experiment in which external carbon dioxide is increased while photosynthetic photon flux, temperature and humidity are held constant. Net assimilation increases from its initial value, whereas stomatal conductance falls after a delay. There is no contradiction because the concentration gradient for inward carbon dioxide diffusion has changed. An increased gradient can permit a higher flux despite a decrease in conductance. Inferring carbon dioxide flux from conductance alone would omit an essential part of the physical relationship.

The distinction between external concentration and Ci also prevents circular reasoning. A narrower stoma tends to restrict carbon dioxide entry, which can lower Ci if demand remains high. But a low Ci can favor opening, creating negative feedback that tends to oppose the initial restriction. In a dehydrating leaf, ABA and hydraulic signals can nevertheless dominate and maintain lower conductance. The direction of the carbon dioxide-related feedback does not determine the final aperture independently of the other inputs.

A further distinction is between Ci and the carbon dioxide concentration at the site of Rubisco in chloroplasts. Carbon dioxide must cross additional barriers between intercellular air spaces and chloroplast stroma. Mesophyll conductance represents part of this further limitation. Therefore an estimate of Ci is not a direct measurement of chloroplast carbon dioxide concentration. For a question about the regulation of stomatal movement, Ci is often an informative variable, but for a complete quantitative analysis of photosynthetic limitation additional resistances and biochemical capacity would be required.

In a controlled study of spectral effects, an investigator may compare a red-light treatment that lowers Ci with a blue increment whose primary effect is activation of guard-cell signaling. To evaluate interaction between these inputs, a factorial design can vary blue illumination and carbon dioxide independently against a constant red background. A large added-blue response at one carbon dioxide level but a smaller response at another would demonstrate context dependence. It would not necessarily indicate failure of phototropin activation in the second condition. Direct measurement of the biochemical pathway would be needed before making that claim.

11. Humidity, temperature, drought and circadian modulation

The driving force for water vapour loss depends on the difference in vapour pressure between the leaf interior and the atmosphere. The vapour-pressure deficit, often abbreviated VPD, is a useful way of describing this difference. At a fixed temperature, lower relative humidity corresponds to a greater deficit. Because the saturation vapour pressure of water increases with temperature, two environments with the same relative humidity can impose different evaporative demands. A warm leaf in moderately humid air may therefore experience substantial demand for water even if a simple humidity percentage does not appear extreme.

In many leaves, the intercellular air spaces are approximately saturated with water vapour near the temperature of the evaporating surfaces. The leaf-to-air vapour pressure difference is then approximated by the saturation vapour pressure at leaf temperature minus the actual vapour pressure of the ambient air. If leaf and air temperatures differ, using air temperature alone to compute both terms is inaccurate. This matters in illumination experiments because changing the spectral composition or intensity of light can alter leaf temperature, which can change evaporative demand without any alteration of ambient relative humidity.

An immediate increase in evaporative demand can change the hydraulic state of the leaf before substantial biochemical regulation has occurred. Guard cells and surrounding epidermal cells may lose water at different rates. Since neighboring cells can exert a mechanical opposition to opening, a rapid decrease in epidermal turgor can in some circumstances cause a transient increase in aperture, even though the longer-term response reduces conductance. Such a transient is sometimes termed a wrong-way response because its direction is opposite to the eventual protective adjustment. It should not be mistaken for evidence that the plant has no mechanism for responding to water deficit.

The sustained response to higher VPD often includes changes in guard-cell ion transport and in ABA-related signaling in flowering plants. Hydraulic changes and hormonal signaling can interact: a fall in tissue turgor may influence ABA synthesis, and the resulting hormone signal can alter guard-cell transport. The response varies across plant lineages and conditions, so it is inappropriate to insist that every stomatal response to VPD in every vascular plant requires the same level of ABA production. Comparisons among species can reveal different relative contributions of direct hydraulic effects and active signaling.

Drought describes an insufficiency of available water over a physiological timescale and need not be equivalent to a sudden increase in atmospheric demand. A plant growing in dry soil may experience reduced root water uptake even when atmospheric humidity is high. Conversely, a well-watered plant can temporarily encounter high VPD. Both situations can affect leaf water potential and stomatal behavior, but their initial perturbations differ. A well-designed experiment varies root water supply and atmospheric humidity separately rather than treating them as interchangeable.

Temperature also has multiple consequences. A warmer leaf may experience higher evaporative demand, but transpiration itself can cool a leaf through evaporation. Temperature can modify metabolic rates, membrane transport and signaling. In suitable conditions, some plants increase conductance during heating, helping to support evaporative cooling; under water limitation or very high VPD, other signals may instead promote closure. A statement that heat always closes stomata or always opens stomata neglects the environmental context and the competing effects of cooling and water conservation.

When an infrared camera records leaf temperature, the result reflects an energy balance rather than aperture directly. A leaf absorbs radiation, loses or gains sensible heat, exchanges longwave radiation, and loses latent heat through evaporation. Lower transpiration may produce a warmer leaf under otherwise comparable conditions. But variations in leaf angle, illumination, boundary-layer conductance and temperature of surrounding air can also change the thermal signal. Thermal screening of mutants is therefore useful for finding candidates, followed by more direct measurements of conductance and aperture.

Stomatal responses additionally depend on time of day. An endogenous oscillator can regulate physiological sensitivity and produce rhythms under suitable constant conditions. Light at two different circadian phases can therefore evoke different responses even when the immediate light stimulus is identical. The difference may arise from rhythmic regulation of ion transport, metabolism, sensitivity to hormones or other aspects of the guard-cell and leaf state. The next chapter treats the molecular plant circadian clock; for stomatal reasoning, the important point is to distinguish a direct response to illumination from a response modulated by internal phase.

A direct light response should disappear or change when light is removed, whereas an endogenous rhythm can persist for a time under constant conditions. To distinguish the two, plants may first be entrained by regular day–night cycles and then observed under constant illumination and temperature. Repeated rhythmic variation in conductance under constant conditions supports an endogenous contribution. A single difference between morning and evening in a greenhouse does not provide equivalent evidence because irradiance, temperature and humidity may also have changed. Gas-exchange experiments comparing mutants should be conducted at controlled times or explicitly analyze phase.

The familiar daytime opening pattern of many C3 plants is not universal. CAM plants often open stomata during the night, when evaporative demand may be lower, and accumulate carbon dioxide-derived organic acids for subsequent use. During much of the following day they may maintain reduced conductance while internal carbon dioxide is released for photosynthetic assimilation. The timing and strength of these phases vary among obligate and facultative CAM plants, and some display appreciable daytime opening under suitable conditions. The exception illustrates why light is one input to guard-cell regulation rather than a universal binary instruction.

C4 plants also provide a useful comparison. Their carbon-concentrating mechanism can allow substantial assimilation at relatively lower intercellular carbon dioxide under appropriate conditions. This changes the relationship between carbon demand and stomatal conductance compared with typical C3 plants. It does not mean that C4 guard cells lack phototropins or that all C4 plants exhibit a fixed conductance lower than all C3 plants. The physiology of stomata depends on the interaction of photosynthetic strategy, anatomy, water availability and environment.

The practical consequence of these interactions is that identical photon input can yield different apertures. In a healthy hydrated leaf under modest VPD, blue-light signaling and photosynthetic demand may favor opening. In the same leaf after a substantial dehydration signal, ABA-associated transport may favor closure. At another internal phase, the sensitivity to the same stimuli may differ. A robust examination answer describes which inputs have changed and identifies the downstream processes that favor net solute accumulation or net solute loss, rather than selecting a single absolute rule.

12. From a microscopic aperture to measurable gas exchange

A stomatal aperture measurement usually describes a geometric feature such as the width of one pore, often expressed in micrometres. Stomatal conductance describes the capacity for gas diffusion through the stomata of a leaf area. Conductance is commonly expressed in mol m⁻² s⁻¹ when the driving force is expressed as a mole-fraction difference. An increase in aperture generally increases stomatal conductance when other factors are held constant, but the relationship is not a simple equality between width and conductance.

The number of pores per leaf area contributes to the overall available pathways. Pore length, width and effective diffusion path depth influence resistance. Diffusion near the entrance and exit of each pore also matters, so doubling pore width does not necessarily double conductance. Leaves with the same mean aperture but different stomatal density or pore anatomy may have different conductance. If an experiment uses microscopy to measure aperture and gas exchange to measure whole-leaf conductance, the two measurements are complementary rather than redundant.

For water vapour, a simplified flux relationship is E ≈ gtw × (wi − wa), where E is the transpiration flux, gtw is total conductance to water vapour, and wi and wa denote the mole fractions of water vapour inside the leaf and in ambient air. If the partial pressure difference is D and total atmospheric pressure is P, the mole-fraction difference is approximately D/P for an ideal gas mixture. Thus E ≈ gtw D/P. The expression assumes a steady diffusion process and a specified effective conductance. A more complete treatment would account for boundary layers, cuticular pathways and departures from the assumed humidity inside the leaf.

It is especially important to distinguish stomatal conductance from total conductance. The air immediately adjacent to the leaf can form a boundary layer that resists diffusion. For a simple model in which stomatal and boundary-layer resistances are in series, the total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances, so 1/gtw = 1/gsw + 1/gbw. Here gsw is stomatal conductance to water vapour and gbw is boundary-layer conductance to water vapour. The expression neglects alternative parallel pathways such as cuticular water loss. If the boundary-layer conductance is very high, its resistance becomes small and the total conductance approaches the stomatal conductance.

Take an illustrative leaf with gsw = 0.30 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ and gbw = 0.60 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The total resistance is 1/0.30 + 1/0.60 = 5 m² s mol⁻¹, giving gtw = 0.20 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. At a vapour-pressure difference of 1.5 kPa and atmospheric pressure of 100 kPa, the predicted flux is 0.20 × 1.5/100 = 0.003 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, or 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Simply using the stomatal conductance would overestimate this illustrative flux because it would omit a non-negligible boundary-layer resistance.

The analogous simplified relationship for carbon dioxide uptake is A ≈ gsc × (Ca − Ci), where A is net assimilation, gsc is stomatal conductance to carbon dioxide, and the concentration difference is represented by mole fractions. This expression is written for the stomatal portion of the diffusion path, assuming the relevant external concentration is at the leaf surface or that boundary-layer resistance can be neglected. Because water vapour diffuses faster than carbon dioxide in air, stomatal conductance to water vapour is commonly approximated as 1.6 times conductance to carbon dioxide for the same pores.

Suppose gsw = 0.24 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. With the factor 1.6, gsc ≈ 0.15 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. If Ca is 420 µmol mol⁻¹ and Ci is 280 µmol mol⁻¹, the difference is 140 µmol mol⁻¹. Multiplying gives 21 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ as an illustrative assimilation flux under the stated simplified steady-state assumptions. The unit conversion follows because multiplying mol of air per area per second by µmol of carbon dioxide per mol of air yields µmol of carbon dioxide per area per second. A real photosynthesis rate is also constrained by biochemical and mesophyll processes; the values in this example are not measurements from a published experiment.

Stomatal conductance can decline while carbon dioxide assimilation increases if the driving concentration difference increases sufficiently. Similarly, transpiration can rise when conductance falls if the vapour-pressure deficit increases sufficiently. Suppose gtw is initially 0.20 and D is 1 kPa at P = 100 kPa, giving E = 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. A humidity change lowers gtw to 0.12 but raises D to 3 kPa. The new flux is 3.6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. This is a useful counterexample to the claim that closing stomata always lowers actual transpiration relative to the previous environmental state. The narrower aperture has limited water loss relative to what it would have been with unchanged conductance, yet the absolute flux is higher than before the environmental perturbation.

Water-use efficiency is a ratio that requires explicit definition. Instantaneous water-use efficiency may be expressed as assimilation divided by transpiration, A/E. Intrinsic water-use efficiency is commonly expressed as A/gsw. These quantities are related but not interchangeable because the transpiration rate also depends on evaporative demand. A plant with high intrinsic efficiency in a measurement chamber can still lose substantial water if the surrounding air is very dry. Longer-term water-use efficiency based on biomass gained per water used includes other processes and cannot be inferred directly from one gas-exchange measurement.

As an illustration, a leaf with A = 18 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and E = 3 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹ has A/E = 6 µmol CO₂ per mmol H₂O. If a second leaf has A = 12 and E = 1.5 in the same units, its ratio is 8. The second leaf has the higher instantaneous ratio even though its carbon assimilation rate is lower. Whether this pattern is advantageous for growth depends on duration, water availability, carbon requirements and other physiological factors. Maximizing a ratio does not necessarily maximize carbon gain or crop yield.

The speed of adjustment matters because natural light frequently changes as clouds pass or leaves move. When light increases suddenly, mesophyll assimilation can rise before stomata fully open, potentially creating a transient diffusion limitation. When light decreases, assimilation can fall before stomata fully close, so water may continue to be lost while little additional carbon is gained. A plant that responds more quickly to these changes may reduce the mismatch, but the benefit depends on the temporal pattern of its environment. Lawson and Blatt emphasized the significance of stomatal dynamics in linking cellular transport and whole-leaf performance. The next step is to examine how experiments distinguish these dynamics from static differences in leaf structure.

13. Experimental evidence: locating a defect along the causal pathway

The guard cell is especially valuable in experimental plant physiology because a series of measurable responses occurs within a highly specialized cell type. One can ask whether the cell absorbs a light stimulus, whether an early signaling protein is activated, whether the proton pump changes activity, whether ion currents change, and whether the pore actually widens. These measurements represent distinct levels of the causal process. Combining them allows a researcher to distinguish defects that might look identical in a photograph of the epidermis.

A typical initial experiment establishes a reproducible opening response in wild-type plants. Plants are grown under comparable conditions, leaves of similar developmental stage are chosen, and the starting aperture is measured or otherwise accounted for. The specimens are exposed to a defined illumination treatment, and measurements are obtained at multiple times. Maintaining comparable temperature, external carbon dioxide and humidity reduces alternative explanations. A dark or red-background comparison provides a baseline against which the specific contribution of an added blue component can be assessed.

Microscopic measurements can reveal stomatal aperture directly. If the pore is elongated, measuring its narrow dimension gives a convenient estimate of width, while length or area may provide additional information. Measurements should be taken from multiple stomata and plants, because stomata within a leaf are not identical and plants are the biological units of interest in many comparisons. Treating a large number of stomata from a single plant as though they were independent plants can create a misleading impression of precision. A comparison based on replicated plants and carefully sampled stomata provides stronger support for a genotype-dependent effect.

A gas-exchange chamber permits control of important environmental variables while measuring fluxes of carbon dioxide and water vapour. The instrument can estimate leaf conductance using information about gas concentrations, flow and leaf temperature. It measures the physiological behavior of a leaf area rather than the aperture of one stoma. Conductance measurements are particularly suitable for constructing time courses following transitions between darkness and light, or between two spectral treatments. A simultaneous measurement of assimilation helps determine whether the change in stomatal behavior is associated with a change in photosynthetic carbon demand.

Thermal imaging can provide a rapid preliminary screen for abnormal evaporative cooling. The BLUS1 mutant work illustrates how a candidate with impaired light-associated cooling can lead to a more specific analysis of stomatal signaling. To interpret such a screen, investigators need direct aperture or conductance measurements and comparable irradiation and water supply. A plant that is warm because its leaves are positioned differently is not equivalent to a plant that is warm because its stomata fail to open. Screening identifies a phenotype worth investigating; it does not establish the causal mechanism by itself.

Biochemical measurements help link a light stimulus to protein regulation. For the plasma-membrane H⁺-ATPase, the phosphorylation state of the regulatory region and association with 14-3-3 proteins are informative. An antibody recognizing a phosphorylated site or an assay of protein association can reveal a change in activation state, while a measurement of proton extrusion provides a functional transport readout. The amount of total pump protein should be considered when interpreting changes in phosphorylation signal. A weaker phosphorylated band could reflect lower total protein abundance rather than a lower fraction of the protein being phosphorylated.

Proton extrusion can be monitored indirectly through changes in the pH of an extracellular solution under carefully controlled conditions. An activated plasma-membrane pump can acidify a relatively weakly buffered solution. In a strongly buffered solution, the same number of exported protons may produce a much smaller measurable pH change. Therefore a comparison of pH traces requires information about buffer capacity and starting pH. Metabolic production or consumption of acidic compounds can also influence solution pH, so proton-pump-specific evidence strengthens interpretation. A direct assay of pump activity or a suitable perturbation can help separate proton transport from unrelated acidification.

Electrophysiological experiments ask a different question: how do membrane currents respond to voltage and signaling? In a voltage-clamp experiment, the electrical potential across the membrane is controlled, and the current required to maintain that potential is measured. By changing voltage and extracellular ion composition, investigators can characterize the properties of currents and infer which ions contribute. Recording inward potassium current at strongly negative voltages reveals a transport capability that may be crucial during opening. Recording anion current after ABA treatment can reveal activation of closing-associated transport.

A current is charge movement per unit time, not simply the number of molecules crossing a membrane. An ion of charge magnitude two contributes twice the charge per ion compared with a monovalent ion. The sign of a recorded current also follows a convention. In standard electrophysiological conventions, positive charge leaving the cell and negative charge entering the cell both contribute to outward current, whereas positive charge entering and negative charge leaving contribute to inward current. Thus a current described as inward is not automatically proof of inward solute movement. The ion species, reversal potential and experimental convention must be known.

The reversal potential of a current is the voltage at which its net current changes sign under specified experimental conditions. A change in extracellular potassium that shifts the reversal potential in the direction predicted by the Nernst relation supports a substantial potassium contribution. It does not prove that the channel is absolutely impermeable to every other ion. Likewise, a channel can be nearly closed at a voltage even when a large electrochemical driving force exists. Voltage-clamp data separate the question of driving force from the question of channel activation, although quantitative analysis may still require a suitable conductance model.

The ion content of guard cells can be assessed by appropriately calibrated analytical methods, but the biological inference depends on the sampling and compartment measured. Measuring total potassium in a population of guard cells is different from measuring free cytosolic potassium activity. Measuring the concentration in a tissue containing both guard and pavement cells is different again. During opening, cell volume changes and ions may redistribute between cytoplasm and vacuole. Reporting only a concentration without considering cell volume can obscure a significant change in total osmotic content.

These techniques become particularly powerful when used together in genetic comparisons. If a phototropin double mutant fails to activate the downstream kinase response, fails to stimulate the pump, and fails to show the normal blue-light-dependent opening increment, the combined observations support an early lesion. If a BLUS1 mutant retains receptor activation but lacks the normal pump response, the lesion is better localized to the relay. If a mutant retains normal pump phosphorylation and hyperpolarization but shows deficient potassium accumulation, a defect downstream of the pump becomes a reasonable hypothesis. In each case, complementation with the functional gene and assessment of other physiological properties would strengthen the conclusion.

A pharmacological bypass experiment can also be informative. Fusicoccin is known to stabilize an activated H⁺-ATPase–14-3-3 complex, providing a means of stimulating pump activity independently of the normal upstream light input in suitable experimental systems. Suppose a mutant fails to open in response to blue light but can open following a pump-activating treatment. The result suggests that at least some downstream machinery is functional and that failure of normal pump activation may be important. It does not prove that the mutant carries a lesion in a particular upstream gene. A pump-activating treatment may have additional physiological effects, and a lack of rescue could arise from inadequate delivery, toxicity or downstream defects.

Testing both opening and closure can expose pathway specificity. A guard cell that is defective in blue-light opening but responds substantially to ABA when it has first been opened by another means is not generally incapable of movement. Conversely, a cell that has a normal opening response but cannot reduce aperture after ABA treatment may have a lesion in the closing network. Comparing changes from similar initial apertures matters because a pore that is already nearly closed cannot display a large further decrease in width. Without that control, a failure to observe closure may be a geometric floor effect rather than a signaling defect.

14. Integrating time courses, perturbations and quantitative predictions

Consider an illustrative set of observations obtained under controlled red background illumination. The starting aperture is 2.0 µm in each genotype. After a small blue-light increment, the wild type reaches 5.0 µm, a phototropin double mutant reaches 2.3 µm, and a BLUS1-deficient line reaches 2.4 µm. The changes are 3.0, 0.3 and 0.4 µm respectively. Both mutants show reduced blue-associated opening relative to the wild type. However, these endpoint measurements alone cannot establish the order of the two affected proteins. Both mutations disrupt the same physiological output, but that observation does not tell us whether one lies upstream of the other.

Now add a measurement of early receptor phosphorylation and plasma-membrane pump activation. Suppose the phototropin-deficient line lacks the relevant receptor activity, whereas the BLUS1-deficient line retains receptor activation but shows impaired pump stimulation. Together these observations favor an ordering in which the receptor acts upstream of the BLUS1-associated relay and pump activation. The inference is stronger than comparing final aperture alone. It is also appropriately limited: the evidence identifies a functional order but does not by itself demonstrate direct physical contact between every component shown in a pathway diagram.

A third hypothetical line shows normal receptor and pump activation, but its potassium uptake is strongly reduced and its aperture changes little. This pattern localizes the physiological failure downstream of the initial signal, but it does not prove a mutation in a potassium channel gene. Changes in channel gating, the potassium gradient, anion availability or vacuolar storage could also reduce potassium accumulation. A direct measurement of potassium conductance at controlled voltage would help test the channel hypothesis, while examination of extracellular and intracellular ion composition would address the driving-force hypothesis.

Suppose a different line accumulates potassium and counterions normally but exhibits a substantially smaller change in pore width. Two possibilities are impaired water permeability and abnormal guard-cell mechanical properties. If the cells reach similar final volumes and pressure but differ in pore width, mechanics becomes a particularly plausible explanation. If the solutes accumulate but cell volume changes very slowly despite an estimated water-potential difference, altered hydraulic permeability becomes plausible. These possibilities are difficult to distinguish using an endpoint aperture alone. The combination of solute, volume, pressure and aperture measurements allows the reasoning to progress.

Kinetic measurements can help distinguish rate and capacity. Imagine two genotypes whose apertures both rise from 2 to 6 µm after sufficiently long exposure to light. One reaches 4 µm after 5 minutes, while the second reaches 4 µm after 20 minutes. The steady-state responses may be similar, but the second genotype adjusts more slowly. A single measurement at 5 minutes would make it appear that the second genotype has a lower maximum aperture, even though it ultimately reaches the same endpoint. Conversely, a single measurement after full equilibration would miss a potentially important physiological difference under fluctuating light.

For a simple first-order model of opening following a step stimulus, aperture a(t) can be represented by a∞ − (a∞ − a0)e^(−t/τ). Here a0 is the starting aperture, a∞ is the final value predicted by the model, t is time and τ is the time constant. At t = τ, the remaining difference from the final value is approximately 36.8% of the initial difference; the response has achieved about 63.2% of its total predicted change. The model is useful for comparing response rates when the data reasonably resemble an exponential approach. Actual stomatal responses may show a lag, multiple phases, overshoot or changing environmental inputs, so a single fitted time constant is not a universal molecular property.

For example, if a0 is 2 µm, a∞ is 6 µm and τ is 10 minutes, the predicted aperture after 10 minutes is 6 − 4/e, approximately 4.53 µm. A genotype with the same starting and final values but τ = 20 minutes would reach approximately 3.57 µm at that time, because e^(−10/20) is about 0.607. The difference occurs even though the modeled final capacity is identical. These are illustrative calculations and must not be interpreted as published response times. Their purpose is to connect a graph of stomatal aperture with an explicit interpretation of kinetic parameters.

Closure can also be analyzed in terms of the initial aperture, final aperture and rate. For a plant initially at 6 µm that approaches 2 µm following ABA exposure, an analogous first-order expression predicts a decreasing rather than increasing curve. Comparing two mutants requires attention to whether the starting and final values are comparable. A line beginning with an aperture of 3 µm cannot be compared fairly with a line beginning at 6 µm merely by subtracting the aperture measured after 10 minutes. Normalized response fractions, absolute apertures and the experimental baseline each provide information that may be needed for the conclusion.

An illustrative VPD experiment demonstrates the distinction between flux and conductance. At a fixed air pressure of 100 kPa, a well-watered leaf has a total water-vapour conductance of 0.25 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ at a deficit of 0.8 kPa. The predicted water flux is 2 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Following an increase in deficit to 2.4 kPa, conductance falls to 0.15 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. The predicted flux is nevertheless 3.6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The observed reduction in conductance has a protective effect relative to a hypothetical unchanged conductance, which would permit 6 mmol m⁻² s⁻¹, but it does not restore the original water flux. This reasoning is essential when interpreting plant responses to changing atmospheric conditions.

Another illustrative experiment measures net assimilation and stomatal conductance before and after an increase in atmospheric carbon dioxide. Before the change, assimilation is 15 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ and stomatal conductance to water is 0.30 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. After the change, assimilation is 20 and stomatal conductance is 0.20 in the same units. Intrinsic water-use efficiency changes from 50 to 100 µmol CO₂ per mol H₂O conductance. This doubling reflects the combination of higher assimilation and lower conductance. It does not provide the actual transpiration rate unless the water-vapour gradient and other resistances are also considered.

Data obtained from whole leaves and isolated epidermis can produce superficially different outcomes. Suppose a mutant fails to display a strong red-light increase in conductance in an intact leaf, but its epidermal strips respond normally to blue light. The finding is compatible with a defect in mesophyll-associated carbon metabolism or communication rather than in the direct blue-light signaling route. An alternative possibility is that the mutant has altered stomatal density or leaf anatomy. To differentiate the alternatives, one would compare assimilation, intercellular carbon dioxide, stomatal density and direct aperture in intact tissues. It would be premature to identify the mutated protein based only on two physiological responses.

A factorial treatment helps test interactions between signals. Four combinations can be used: red background without added blue light and without ABA, red background with added blue light but without ABA, red background without added blue light but with ABA, and red background with both added blue light and ABA. The difference between the first two conditions estimates the added-blue effect under the untreated condition. The difference between the latter two estimates its effect during ABA exposure. If the added-blue increment is much smaller in the presence of ABA, the result indicates an interaction at the level of the measured output. Further measurements of pump phosphorylation, membrane potential and anion current would be needed to identify whether inhibition of opening, activation of closure or both explain the interaction.

A careful exam answer should distinguish necessity, sufficiency and correlation. A mutation abolishing a response under well-controlled conditions supports a requirement for the affected gene in that context. Restoration of the response by a functional copy supports a causal link to that gene. Artificial activation of a downstream process that restores movement supports the sufficiency of the bypass under the particular conditions. A biochemical association between two proteins supports their participation in a complex but does not necessarily demonstrate which protein phosphorylates the other. A temporal sequence supports an ordering hypothesis but may not by itself prove direct causation. These distinctions are central to the experimental study of signaling pathways.

15. Physiological significance and synthesis

The regulatory system described in this chapter can be summarized as a sequence of transformations. Spectral information activates a receptor and signaling proteins. The signaling state changes activities of transporters. The transporters change solute distribution and the electrical and osmotic state of the cell. Water movement and wall mechanics convert the resulting pressure differences into changes in pore geometry. The geometry modifies the conductance of a leaf, and the altered conductance affects the supply of carbon dioxide and loss of water. At every step there are feedbacks capable of changing the next response.

The sequence is not a claim that each process occurs only after the previous one has completely ended. The proton pump can remain active while potassium enters, the vacuole changes volume and water flows. Mesophyll photosynthesis can change intercellular carbon dioxide at the same time as phototropin signaling develops. Closing signals can influence transport while opening signals are still present. The final behavior of the pore reflects the integrated state of these processes. A static pathway diagram is best understood as a causal map rather than a timeline in which each box must finish before the next begins.

The biological value of this regulation is evident in fluctuating environments. When a cloud reduces illumination, rapid reduction of water loss may be useful because photosynthetic demand has fallen. When irradiance increases again, a timely increase in conductance can facilitate carbon dioxide uptake. However, a permanently high conductance may impose an excessive water cost, and a permanently low conductance may constrain assimilation and evaporative cooling. A plant with appropriate dynamic responsiveness can sometimes achieve a better balance than one with an extreme static aperture.

The same relationships explain why efforts to improve crop water use cannot be evaluated solely by measuring the number or width of stomata. Reduced stomatal density may lower the maximum capacity for gas exchange, whereas improved responsiveness may alter how efficiently that capacity is deployed over time. A plant that closes more rapidly after a decline in light could reduce water loss, but the resulting change in growth depends on subsequent reopening, environmental variability and hydraulic capacity. A trait that improves one ratio in a short experiment may not necessarily improve yield over an entire growing season. The relevant biological objective needs to be specified before a physiological modification can be judged beneficial.

The chapter also illustrates the importance of connecting signaling with transport thermodynamics. A kinase does not mechanically move a pore; a channel does not necessarily pump ions uphill; water flow is not powered directly by a photoreceptor. Each component has a particular physical or biochemical role. The causal explanation becomes coherent only when each step respects its own mechanism. A failure to distinguish these roles produces misleading statements such as ATP actively transports water into guard cells or blue light attracts potassium into the pore.

When a complex problem is presented in an examination, start by identifying the measured output and the environmental conditions. If the output is membrane voltage, ask which currents contribute and how their charge moves. If the output is solute content, ask whether the measured quantity is concentration or amount and consider compartmentation. If the output is aperture, consider both water relations and mechanics. If the output is conductance or transpiration, consider pore density, diffusion resistance and the environmental driving force. Then place the experimental manipulation at the appropriate level of the pathway. This strategy connects the small set of core mechanisms to a broad variety of analytical questions.

A final connection concerns time. The guard cells may receive the same immediate light input at different internal phases and respond differently. The capacity of a plant to anticipate daily changes is the subject of the biological clock, which provides an additional layer of regulation beyond direct perception. The next topic examines how a self-sustained oscillator can synchronize with environmental cycles and regulate physiological outputs, including stomatal behavior. The central idea established here is that guard-cell movement is the measurable result of a regulated ionic, osmotic and mechanical system, not the automatic consequence of the presence or absence of sunlight.
