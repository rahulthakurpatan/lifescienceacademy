1. Why a plant must interpret light

A seedling emerging beneath soil encounters a change that is both an energetic opportunity and a source of information. In darkness, a young shoot can use stored food to extend towards the soil surface. Once it encounters illumination, continuing the same pattern of extension is often less useful than expanding photosynthetic tissue. The external change must be detected before the internal pattern of growth can be reorganized. This is the essential problem of plant light signaling: a physical event outside the cell changes the activities of particular molecules inside the cell, and those molecular changes alter development or cellular behavior. The process does not require an animal-like nervous system. Individual plant cells contain sensory proteins and signaling machinery that can communicate with neighboring tissues through other established mechanisms.

A useful distinction is between light as an energy source and light as an informational stimulus. In a chloroplast, photosynthetic pigments absorb light and transfer excitation energy to reaction centers, where charge separation initiates electron transport. In a photoreceptor, absorption changes the state of a sensory molecule; the altered molecule then changes interactions, enzyme activity, localization or the stability of downstream regulators. The resulting change can be much larger than the energy of the absorbed photon because the cell supplies ATP, metabolites and the machinery needed for the subsequent response. One photon does not supply the energy required to synthesize every protein induced by light. Rather, the photon helps determine whether existing cellular resources will be directed toward those processes.

Plants experience large differences in the spectrum, direction, intensity and duration of illumination. A canopy of neighboring plants removes a substantial fraction of the red and blue light from sunlight, while far-red radiation is relatively enriched in transmitted and reflected light. A seedling beneath the canopy can therefore receive a different spectral message from a seedling exposed to open sunlight, even if neither seedling has a precise measure of distance from its neighbor. Direction is another kind of information. Illumination arriving predominantly from one side can initiate unequal growth across a shoot. Neither phenomenon can be explained simply by saying that light makes plants grow. In one circumstance it inhibits longitudinal growth; in another it promotes extension or redistributes it between opposite sides of an organ.

A photoreceptor is a molecule that converts the absorption of light into a biologically usable signal. A typical plant photoreceptor comprises a protein and a light-absorbing chemical group called a chromophore. The protein provides the molecular environment that determines the chromophore's behavior, and the chromophore provides an efficient route for converting particular wavelengths into a change of molecular state. The protein portion without its required chromophore is called an apoprotein. A protein assembled with the chromophore is a holoprotein. The synthesis of a receptor polypeptide therefore does not guarantee production of a photosensory receptor. A mutation affecting the metabolic pathway that makes the chromophore may leave the protein present but substantially impair its ability to respond to light.

The three receptor families central to this chapter illustrate distinct chemical solutions to this problem. Phytochromes contain a linear tetrapyrrole and detect red and far-red radiation particularly effectively. Cryptochromes are flavoproteins that participate in blue-light responses such as regulation of seedling development and gene expression. Phototropins are flavin-binding protein kinases that connect blue-light absorption to phosphorylation and several directional or rapid physiological responses. These families should be identified from the receptor's molecular features rather than from a single visible phenotype. Different receptors can inhibit elongation of a seedling hypocotyl, for instance, while detecting different parts of the spectrum or engaging different immediate partners.

A signaling pathway is normally described by separating reception, transduction and response. In a light-sensing cell, reception is the photochemical event that changes the receptor. Transduction includes the events that relay this change to other molecules, such as altered binding of a transcription factor or phosphorylation of a protein. Response denotes the measurable outcome at a specified biological level. An investigator might define the response as phosphorylation measured after a short exposure, messenger RNA accumulation measured later, or hypocotyl length measured after several days. These are not interchangeable observations. A mutation may eliminate a growth response while leaving early phosphorylation intact, thereby separating the function of an upstream sensor from later execution of the response.

Because plants are multicellular, receptor activation and the eventual response need not occur in precisely the same cells. A light-sensitive region may produce a mobile chemical or modify the transport of a developmental regulator. The receiving tissue can then change its growth without itself being the principal site at which the directional stimulus was detected. An experiment that illuminates an entire organ and records its final shape does not, by itself, identify the cell type in which perception occurred. Local illumination, tissue-specific complementation and measurements of early molecular responses provide complementary information. For this chapter, the central objective is to follow the transmission of information from light to an intracellular response, rather than to enumerate every organ-level consequence.

An important property of a photoreceptor is that its state can change again. If activation persisted indefinitely after a single absorption event, the cell would have difficulty distinguishing an ongoing light environment from a past exposure. Some receptors return to their resting conformation spontaneously in darkness. Some are converted to another state by a second wavelength. Others are removed through regulated protein turnover, while additional receptor molecules are synthesized. These alternatives are biologically distinct forms of resetting a sensory system. A persistent developmental response after light removal can also reflect transcription or growth that has already taken place, even when the receptor itself has returned to an inactive state. Thus sensory memory and persistence of an outcome must be considered at the appropriate molecular level.

2. Wavelength, photon fluence and biological spectra

Light consists of electromagnetic radiation. The wavelength, represented by the Greek letter lambda, is the distance between successive points of the wave with the same phase. For plant photobiology it is commonly expressed in nanometers, where one nanometer equals one billionth of a meter. The frequency of a wave in vacuum is its speed divided by its wavelength. A shorter wavelength therefore has a higher frequency. The energy of an individual photon is the product of Planck's constant and the frequency, giving the relation E = hc/λ. Here h is Planck's constant, c is the speed of light in vacuum and E is energy per photon in joules. Red light near 660 nm and far-red light near 730 nm therefore differ in both wavelength and energy, although both can drive changes in phytochrome.

The fact that blue photons carry more energy than red photons does not imply that blue light activates every plant receptor more effectively. A photon must be absorbed before its energy can initiate the relevant photochemistry. A chromophore has permitted electronic transitions whose absorption probabilities vary with wavelength. Photoreceptor specificity is consequently determined by the combination of photon availability, the absorption properties of the chromophore and the probability that absorption produces the signaling state. Extra photon energy may be dissipated without increasing the desired response. An examination question that ranks the biological effectiveness of wavelengths solely from E = hc/λ is therefore missing at least one essential variable.

In a biological light experiment, irradiance often refers to radiant power received per unit area, expressed in watts per square meter. For a photochemical comparison it is often preferable to use photon flux density, expressed in micromoles of photons per square meter per second. Equal radiant power at two wavelengths does not correspond to equal numbers of photons because each photon carries a wavelength-dependent amount of energy. Using the Avogadro constant N_A, the energy of a mole of monochromatic photons is N_Ahc/λ. Dividing the energy arriving per unit area per second by this quantity gives the molar photon flux density. The result may then be converted to micromoles per square meter per second by multiplying by one million.

Fluence is the time integral of photon flux density. Under constant illumination the photon fluence equals photon flux density multiplied by the exposure duration. For example, a pulse delivering 2 micromoles per square meter per second for 30 seconds supplies 60 micromoles per square meter. Delivering 6 micromoles per square meter per second for 10 seconds supplies the same total number of photons per unit area. This equality does not ensure identical responses in a living organism. Receptor relaxation, competing reactions and downstream signaling can proceed during the exposure. A response obeys reciprocity over a specified range when these different combinations of flux and duration yield the same biological outcome at the same total fluence. Departure from reciprocity is information about the kinetics of the system, not an error in the arithmetic.

An absorption spectrum describes how efficiently a substance absorbs radiation across wavelengths. It can be obtained from a purified chromoprotein or from a preparation containing the pigment, with suitable correction for other absorbing components. An action spectrum describes how effectively wavelengths produce a selected biological response. The investigator defines a response criterion and compares the photon requirements across the spectrum, or measures response under carefully standardized photon exposures. A peak in an action spectrum indicates wavelengths that efficiently evoke that response under the conditions of the measurement. It is not automatically a direct measurement of the concentration of a receptor.

Absorption and action spectra can provide evidence for identifying the receptor that mediates a response. If a purified receptor absorbs blue light and a biological response is sensitive to blue light, the match provides a plausible candidate. However, a tissue contains multiple pigments with overlapping spectra. The action spectrum also depends on light transmission into the tissue, receptor abundance, the efficiency of photochemistry and the downstream dose-response relationship. Two receptors with similar absorption spectra may produce distinct action spectra because one requires sustained activation and the other responds to short pulses. Conversely, a response may have two spectral peaks because more than one receptor family can initiate it. The strongest conclusion requires additional evidence such as a receptor-deficient mutant and restoration of the response by a functional receptor.

To interpret a wavelength experiment, consider exactly what was held constant. If blue and red treatments differ in photon flux, a difference in growth cannot be attributed solely to wavelength. If lamps generate different temperatures, thermal effects can be mistaken for sensory effects. Filters must be characterized because nominally red light may contain other wavelengths. Seed age, genotype, hydration and the time at which growth is scored must be comparable. A dark control establishes the baseline without the intended optical stimulus, but even this control requires careful handling: exposure during transfer or observation may itself provide a biologically significant light dose. These precautions are part of scientific reasoning, not merely technical details.

For phytochrome, an especially useful environmental variable is the red-to-far-red photon ratio. The ratio is normally calculated from defined red and far-red wavelength bands; it is not a universal property of the visual color of a lamp. A decrease can indicate that neighboring leaves have altered the spectrum. The ratio alone cannot determine an exact molecular state in all environments because phytochrome absorbs across overlapping wavelength ranges, and temperature and irradiation history influence the outcome. Within a simplified comparison of red and far-red light under matched conditions, however, a lower ratio generally shifts the phyB population toward the less active red-absorbing state. That relationship provides a mechanistic link between an environmental measurement and downstream growth signaling.

3. The experiments that revealed red and far-red photoreversibility

The existence of a reversible photochemical switch in plants was inferred from biological responses before all of the participating proteins were known. A classical demonstration was reported in 1952 by H. A. Borthwick, S. B. Hendricks and colleagues in their paper on light-controlled seed germination [1]. The investigators studied a light-sensitive lettuce seed system. Germination was promoted by red radiation and the effect could be reversed by a subsequent exposure to far-red radiation. This observation challenged an interpretation in which red light simply contributed an irreversible amount of energy to germination. The order of exposure mattered, which suggested that light was altering a molecular state that could be switched in either direction.

The experiment is best understood through the distinction between a sufficient stimulus and an accumulated energy dose. Suppose a hydrated seed is initially in a condition in which germination is low in darkness. A red pulse raises the probability that it will germinate. If a far-red pulse delivered immediately afterward reduces that probability, then a later photon exposure has changed the effect of the earlier exposure. Repeating the red and far-red treatments can alternately promote and suppress the response. For the classical low-fluence, photoreversible response, the final effective exposure often determines the direction of the result when the pulses are adequate and no irreversible downstream commitment has intervened.

The phrase 'last light wins' is a shorthand for this experimental pattern, not a universal law about all plants. It presumes an appropriate receptor system, a compatible biological response, a suitable developmental state, adequate doses and intervals short enough that the subsequent light treatment can still affect the relevant decision. If germination proceeds past a point at which the downstream processes no longer depend on the original photoreceptor state, changing that state may no longer reverse the outcome. A visible root cannot be made to disappear merely by a far-red flash. The receptor can be photoreversible even when the completed developmental event is not.

A carefully controlled reconstruction of the reasoning uses multiple matched batches of seeds. One batch remains dark, a second receives red light, a third receives far-red light alone, a fourth receives red followed by far-red and a fifth receives red, far-red and red in sequence. The use of comparable seed batches is important because germination depends on seed viability and physiological history. The duration and temperature after treatment must be matched, and the scoring time should be defined in advance. A difference between the red and red-followed-by-far-red groups is especially informative because both received the initial red stimulus. If the dark group already germinates extensively, the experimental conditions may not offer a sensitive baseline for detecting promotion.

The following results are a hypothetical teaching dataset rather than measurements transcribed from the original experiment: 12 of 100 dark-treated seeds germinate, compared with 86 of 100 red-treated seeds, 15 of 100 red-then-far-red-treated seeds and 82 of 100 red-then-far-red-then-red-treated seeds. The germination percentages are 12%, 86%, 15% and 82%, respectively. Addition of far-red reduces the response by 71 percentage points relative to red alone, while an additional red pulse restores most of it. The repeated reversal is inconsistent with a simple model in which every absorbed photon independently increases the probability of germination. It is compatible with a photochemical interconversion that changes the abundance of a biologically effective form.

The same observations do not establish the detailed molecular identity of the pigment, its chromophore, the genes encoding it or every intermediate that connects it to germination. A behavioral experiment may reveal that two forms of a photoreceptor are plausible, but purification, spectroscopic measurements and genetic analysis are required to establish the biochemical mechanism. In 1959 Butler, Norris, Siegelman and Hendricks reported spectroscopic detection and preliminary purification of the predicted pigment [7]. The historical development of phytochrome research illustrates how different kinds of evidence answer different questions: response measurements reveal a property of the system, spectroscopy characterizes the pigment, and mutant studies identify which molecular components contribute to the response.

A particularly instructive follow-up experiment would measure the response when the second exposure is delayed. Under the simplest interpretation, an immediate far-red pulse reduces the signaling-active population of phytochrome before the cellular response has become committed. If the same far-red pulse is applied much later, the inhibition of germination could be weaker even if the photoconversion itself occurs normally. This pattern suggests that events downstream of reception acquire some independence from the receptor's instantaneous state. To distinguish a failure of photoconversion from a failure of biological reversibility, one would measure the receptor-state change as well as the final phenotype. These two measurements are complementary rather than redundant.

Another follow-up uses wavelength rather than timing as the experimental variable. Instead of assuming that the red and far-red bands are sharply separated, the experiment compares several narrow wavelength treatments. The effectiveness of each treatment depends on the absorption properties of the relevant states and the photochemical yields of the transitions. The resulting spectral response may have broad peaks instead of abrupt borders. A student should therefore interpret the conventional values of approximately 660 and 730 nm as representative maxima for plant phytochrome states, not as uniquely effective wavelengths beyond which signaling is impossible.

4. Molecular organization and photochemistry of phytochrome

Phytochrome is a chromoprotein, meaning that the light-absorbing group is associated with a protein that participates in determining its properties. In land plants the chromophore is phytochromobilin, a linear tetrapyrrole derived from heme metabolism. Heme oxygenase opens the heme ring to form biliverdin, and phytochromobilin synthase produces phytochromobilin. The chromophore becomes covalently attached to a conserved cysteine in the photosensory region of plant phytochrome. Formation of the chromoprotein links metabolism to signaling: the receptor gene supplies the polypeptide, while the biosynthetic pathway supplies a chemical group that enables appropriate light perception.

The chromophore contains a series of conjugated chemical bonds. Conjugation permits electronic excitation by visible radiation. When light is absorbed, the chromophore undergoes a change involving isomerization around the bond between the C and D rings of the tetrapyrrole. That local photochemical event is coupled to conformational changes in the protein. The protein's structural response is essential because the remainder of the signaling pathway does not directly observe a double bond in a chromophore. Instead, it responds to altered accessibility, interactions or activity of the photoreceptor. This conversion of a local chemical event into a protein-level functional change is an example of allosteric coupling.

Typical higher-plant phytochromes form dimers. Each polypeptide has an amino-terminal photosensory region containing PAS, GAF and PHY domains, and a carboxyl-terminal region involved in dimerization and signaling regulation. The chromophore is associated with the GAF region of the photosensory module, but the surrounding domains contribute to how the photochemical change is transmitted. The simple teaching model of a dimer should not be taken to imply that both subunits always have identical states or that all members of the family act in precisely the same manner. The central inference for examination purposes is that a photosensory region and an output region cooperate: retaining a chromophore alone is insufficient if the conformational signal cannot reach the downstream signaling interface.

The two classical spectral forms are named Pr and Pfr. Pr is the form that absorbs red light relatively strongly, with a maximum near 660 nm in standard textbook examples. Absorption of red light by Pr promotes conversion toward Pfr. Pfr absorbs far-red light strongly, with a maximum near 730 nm, and absorption of far-red light promotes reconversion toward Pr. The names identify the forms by the wavelengths they preferentially absorb, not by the wavelengths that produce them. A common error is to say that red light creates Pr because of the letter r. In the standard cycle the dominant direction is the opposite: red light converts Pr into Pfr, while far-red light converts Pfr into Pr.

For many classical phytochrome-mediated responses, especially those associated with phyB, Pfr is the signaling-active state. This convention is extremely useful if it is kept within its biological domain. It does not mean that all functions of phytochromes can be predicted from a single global concentration of Pfr, or that far-red radiation cannot promote any response. phyA, for example, can mediate distinct responses under very weak or sustained far-red illumination. The output depends on the receptor family member, the light regime and the kinetics of the signaling network. An accurate molecular explanation therefore identifies the relevant receptor before generalizing from the standard Pr/Pfr diagram.

The absorption spectra of Pr and Pfr overlap. At a red wavelength that strongly favors formation of Pfr, some Pfr molecules may nevertheless absorb photons and return toward Pr. Likewise, far-red light can leave a small Pfr population because Pr is not entirely insensitive to far-red radiation. Consequently, a prolonged monochromatic treatment generally establishes a photostationary mixture rather than converting every receptor molecule into a single form. The limiting mixture depends on the relative rates of the two light-driven transitions and, when appreciable, on nonphotochemical reactions. A diagram showing a single arrow in each direction conveys the dominant conversion, but the underlying population behavior is more nuanced.

Even in the absence of illumination, certain active phytochrome molecules can revert thermally toward Pr. This dark reversion is a chemical relaxation process and differs from photoconversion induced by far-red light. It also differs from receptor degradation because the protein remains available for another photochemical cycle. Dark reversion helps the plant distinguish a recent light exposure from sustained activation. Its rate can vary with receptor identity, temperature and associated molecular interactions. In addition, the active state of some phytochromes can be subject to regulated protein degradation. Both routes reduce signaling-active receptor abundance, but they affect total receptor abundance differently.

A receptor mutant illustrates why these distinctions matter. Consider a phytochrome whose chromophore attaches correctly and whose absorption spectrum looks normal, but whose output region cannot interact with a particular transcriptional regulator. Such a mutant may undergo Pr-to-Pfr photoconversion without producing the normal gene expression change. By contrast, a chromophore-deficient mutant may fail to photoconvert even if its output region is otherwise functional. An assay of receptor abundance alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. A sequence of measurements of protein abundance, absorption changes, partner binding and transcriptional response narrows down the location of the defect.

Phytochromes have evolutionary relationships with sensory proteins found in bacteria and other organisms. Certain bacterial phytochromes have output modules connected to histidine kinase signaling, which provides a useful comparison with the preceding discussion of two-component systems. It is nevertheless incorrect to infer that plant phytochrome signaling must proceed through an obligatory bacterial-style histidine-to-aspartate phosphorelay. Plant phytochromes share structural ancestry with these sensory systems but use distinct output mechanisms, including interactions with transcriptional regulators and control of their stability or activity. Evolutionary homology does not require preservation of every downstream reaction.

5. The two-state model: predicting the fraction of active phytochrome

It is useful to transform the qualitative Pr/Pfr cycle into a model that makes explicit predictions. Consider a fixed population of receptor units, denoted by T, that can exist in either Pr or Pfr. Let x denote the fraction in Pfr, so that the amount of Pfr is xT and the amount of Pr is (1−x)T. Suppose a constant light environment produces an effective first-order rate constant a for conversion from Pr to Pfr and b for conversion from Pfr to Pr. Both constants have dimensions of inverse time. The constants incorporate photon flux, the probability of absorption and the efficiency with which absorption produces the other state. They are not simply the intensities of red and far-red light.

During a short interval dt, the expected increase in Pfr due to conversion of Pr is a(1−x)T dt, and the expected decrease due to the reverse process is bxT dt. If thermal reversion is negligible and receptor synthesis and degradation are insignificant during the measurement, the total T is constant. Dividing the net change by T dt gives dx/dt = a(1−x)−bx. This differential equation expresses an important biological idea: the forward conversion depends on the supply of Pr, and the reverse conversion depends on the supply of Pfr. A constant forward rate coefficient does not mean that the number of forward events per second remains constant as the population changes.

At a photostationary state the fraction x no longer changes with time. This condition means that the forward and reverse conversion fluxes are equal; it does not mean that individual molecules have stopped changing state. Setting dx/dt to zero gives a(1−x) = bx. Rearranging yields x = a/(a+b), provided a+b is greater than zero. The equation predicts that the fraction is bounded between zero and one for nonnegative coefficients. If forward and reverse coefficients are equal, half the population is in Pfr at steady state. If the forward coefficient is four times the reverse coefficient, four-fifths of the population is in Pfr. The two-state expression is a useful idealization rather than a universal calibration of the state of every phytochrome molecule in a plant.

Suppose a red-enriched treatment gives a = 0.08 s⁻¹ and b = 0.02 s⁻¹. The predicted photostationary fraction is 0.08/(0.08+0.02) = 0.80. A far-red-enriched treatment gives a = 0.01 s⁻¹ and b = 0.09 s⁻¹. Its corresponding fraction is 0.01/(0.01+0.09) = 0.10. The second light environment favors Pr strongly but does not eliminate Pfr entirely. These coefficients are hypothetical, not experimentally measured values for a specified plant species. Their role is to demonstrate how the directional tendencies of both reactions, rather than wavelength names alone, determine a predicted state.

The time required to approach the photostationary fraction can also be estimated. Write x∞ = a/(a+b). The kinetic equation then becomes dx/dt = −(a+b)(x−x∞). Integrating from the initial value x₀ gives x(t) = x∞ + (x₀−x∞) exp[−(a+b)t]. The exponential term describes the decay of the difference between the present and limiting fractions. Because a+b has units of inverse seconds when time is in seconds, the exponent is dimensionless. The time constant τ equals 1/(a+b); after one time constant approximately 36.8% of the original difference remains. The half-time for approaching the limiting fraction is ln(2)/(a+b).

For the hypothetical red treatment above, the time constant is 10 seconds and the half-time is approximately 6.93 seconds. If the initial active fraction is 0.10, after 10 seconds it becomes 0.80 + (0.10−0.80)e⁻¹, or approximately 0.5425. This number is lower than 0.80 because the exposure has lasted only one time constant. If both rate coefficients are doubled while their ratio stays fixed, the limiting fraction is unchanged but the time constant is halved. The example explains why an intense pulse and a dim continuous treatment with the same spectral composition need not produce the same immediate receptor-state measurement.

A first-order thermal reversion coefficient d can be introduced as an additional route from Pfr to Pr. The kinetic equation becomes dx/dt = a(1−x)−(b+d)x. Its steady fraction is a/(a+b+d). For a = 0.08 s⁻¹, b = 0.02 s⁻¹ and d = 0.025 s⁻¹, the steady fraction is 0.08/0.125 = 0.64 rather than 0.80. When the light is switched off, both a and b become zero in this model. The remaining equation is dx/dt = −d·x, where d on the right denotes the coefficient, and the solution is x(t) = x₀e^(−dt). To avoid confusing the differential symbol with the rate constant, one can equivalently name the latter k_dark. Its dark half-life is ln(2)/k_dark.

The introduction of thermal reversion changes how light intensity should be interpreted. Without thermal reversion, scaling a and b by the same positive factor leaves their ratio and hence their photostationary fraction unchanged. With thermal reversion, the light-independent term does not scale with light intensity. Increasing both photoconversion coefficients can now raise the steady Pfr fraction because light-driven reactions compete more effectively with dark relaxation. This distinction provides a simple mechanistic explanation for why equal spectral ratios do not necessarily yield identical receptor states at different absolute flux densities.

The model assumes a homogeneous population, constant coefficients during the exposure, negligible changes in total receptor abundance and two effective states. It does not model separate phyA and phyB populations, possible differences between subunits of a dimer, detailed intermediate photochemical states or spatial variation across tissues. It also does not include a transcriptional response threshold or a delayed developmental outcome. These omissions are deliberate for a basic mechanistic problem. If a question gives measurements that violate the constant-total assumption, for instance a substantial decrease in receptor abundance during illumination, the two-state equation alone is not sufficient to explain the data. A changing total population requires additional production and degradation terms.

A final distinction is between active fraction and absolute amount of active receptor. If one sample contains 100 arbitrary units of total receptor with x = 0.80, its active amount is 80 units. A second sample contains 40 units with the same x, giving 32 active units. The fractions are equal, but the active amounts differ. Conversely, a sample with a smaller fraction but a much larger receptor pool may contain more active receptor. Downstream binding partners often encounter absolute concentrations, while an optical assay may report a normalized fraction. Identifying the denominator of a measurement is therefore essential before predicting whether two samples should have the same signaling strength.

6. Phytochrome family members and the response to different light regimes

In the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, five genes, PHYA through PHYE, encode members of the phytochrome family. The proteins are designated phyA through phyE. Their shared photochemical principle does not make them functionally identical. They differ in abundance, stability, tissue context and the responses for which they are particularly important. Two members, phyA and phyB, are especially valuable in explaining the distinction between classical red/far-red reversibility and responses that occur in very weak or sustained far-red light. This comparison also prevents the common mistake of treating a phytochrome as a single protein with one universal response curve.

phyA is the major representative of the light-labile type I phytochromes. It can accumulate to relatively high levels in dark-grown Arabidopsis seedlings and its abundance is reduced upon illumination, particularly when the light-driven active form is destabilized. phyB is a prominent type II phytochrome and is relatively light-stable compared with phyA. Calling phyB light-stable is a relative classification, not a claim that it cannot be degraded or thermally inactivated. This difference has consequences for a newly emerging seedling compared with a plant that has already experienced illumination for a long time. The amount of each receptor present reflects its synthesis and turnover history as well as the current optical stimulus.

Three traditional classes of phytochrome response distinguish the effects of photon dose and the duration or rate of illumination. Very-low-fluence responses can be elicited by exceptionally small light doses. In Arabidopsis they are strongly associated with phyA. Because a minute signaling-active fraction can be sufficient for a measurable biological effect, even wavelengths that establish relatively small fractions of Pfr may initiate a response. This helps explain why a far-red exposure may itself stimulate a phyA-dependent response instead of behaving only as the antagonistic pulse expected in a classical phyB-mediated experiment.

Low-fluence responses are the category exemplified by the classical red promotion and far-red reversal of light-sensitive lettuce germination. In a suitable response and genotype they can obey reciprocity across a defined range of fluences, and the final effective spectral exposure can determine the outcome if the response has not become committed. phyB is a major mediator of this category in Arabidopsis. It is safer to associate the response mode with its experimental properties rather than identify it solely by the name of a wavelength. A red pulse can also activate phyA, and the identity of the dominant receptor can vary with dose, developmental state and genotype.

High-irradiance responses require prolonged illumination or substantial ongoing excitation rather than a single saturating pulse. A well-established example is phyA-dependent de-etiolation in continuous far-red light. The term high-irradiance response refers to the class of response and its dependence on the light regime; it does not mean that only unusually intense light in an ecological sense can activate phyA. Unlike a simplified low-fluence response, a sustained far-red response is not adequately described by the final pulse alone. The ongoing formation, removal and activity of signaling-competent receptor molecules, together with the dynamics of downstream events, matter for the outcome.

To understand how continuous far-red light can be effective despite favoring Pr in the classical photocycle, distinguish the average receptor fraction from signaling activity over time. Far-red illumination is not a perfect one-way eraser of every active molecule. The overlapping spectra allow formation of a small active population, and phyA has signaling properties suited to this regime. If the total pool is initially large or the pathway responds to repeated formation of active molecules, a modest instantaneous fraction can contribute to a substantial cumulative response. This explanation is compatible with the two-state chemistry but cannot be quantitatively predicted from the fraction alone. The distinct dynamics of phyA import, signaling and turnover contribute to the characteristic far-red responses [2,3].

A genotype-by-light-regime experiment illustrates the distinction. Consider wild-type Arabidopsis seedlings and two mutant lines, one deficient in phyA and one deficient in phyB. Measure hypocotyl length in darkness, after a classical red/far-red pulse regime, and under continuous far-red illumination. The purpose of testing more than one regime is to reveal whether a mutant has lost a particular type of response rather than assuming that any one treatment diagnoses the entire receptor family. A strong loss of continuous far-red de-etiolation in the phyA line would be consistent with the established role of phyA. A strong impairment of the appropriate red response in the phyB line would support a role for phyB. Residual responses do not invalidate the experiment because additional receptors and overlapping functions may contribute.

The experimental interpretation depends on how the controls are constructed. If the phyA line has a short hypocotyl even in darkness due to an unrelated mutation, comparing final lengths under far-red illumination alone may be misleading. A useful comparison includes the dark baseline of each genotype and the magnitude of the light-induced change. Complementation with a functional PHYA gene provides a test of whether the relevant lesion explains the phenotype. Verification of receptor abundance is important because a presumed loss-of-function allele may retain some activity. A double mutant can reveal redundancy, but an especially strong double-mutant phenotype does not by itself establish a direct physical interaction between the two proteins.

The ecological relevance of these differences is apparent as plants emerge from soil or encounter vegetation shade. A plant may encounter weak transmitted light before receiving open sunlight. A sensor that can respond at very low fluence or during a sustained far-red regime offers information under conditions where a simple red-dominant switch would be insufficient. In light-grown plants phyB provides a particularly important means of responding to spectral changes associated with neighboring vegetation. These are examples of sensory specialization within a homologous family rather than entirely different forms of photochemistry. Detailed ecological responses and photoperiodic regulation require additional physiological context and are not necessary for the receptor-level distinctions developed here.

7. From receptor photoconversion to nuclear signaling

A change in a light-absorbing molecule becomes biologically useful when it reaches a molecular system capable of modifying cellular function. Many well-studied plant phytochrome responses involve the nucleus, where the activated receptor can influence proteins that regulate transcription. This location is significant because DNA transcription and the control of transcription-factor activity are spatially organized processes. A photoreceptor that undergoes normal photochemistry in the cytoplasm may fail to change expression of a nuclear target if the activated receptor cannot access the necessary nuclear partner.

The nucleus is separated from the cytoplasm by a double membrane and nuclear pore complexes. Proteins that undergo regulated nuclear import use interactions with the nuclear transport machinery. In the phyA pathway, small shuttle proteins FHY1 and FHL are important for efficient light-dependent nuclear accumulation of phyA. These proteins can associate with the receptor and provide nuclear localization capability. phyB follows a distinguishable route and is not dependent on FHY1 and FHL in the same way. The difference is a useful counterexample to the assumption that all members of a receptor family use an identical transport mechanism. A shared chromophore and conserved photosensory domain do not determine every aspect of intracellular trafficking.

The relationship between photoconversion and localization can be investigated by combining a receptor tag with controlled illumination. For instance, fluorescence microscopy can show whether tagged receptor molecules accumulate in the nucleus after treatment. The fusion protein should retain biological function; otherwise localization of the tag may not reflect the behavior of the functional receptor. Protein abundance should be measured separately because a decline in cytoplasmic fluorescence could result from degradation rather than import. Nuclear and cytoplasmic markers help establish the identity of the observed compartments. The conclusion that a protein is nuclear should not depend solely on the apparent brightness of one unidentified region of the cell.

A useful hypothetical perturbation is a receptor that is spectroscopically normal but defective in its nuclear accumulation. Such a variant could retain the ability to sense light but fail to regulate some nuclear transcriptional targets. If an experimentally attached nuclear localization sequence restores the nuclear response, that result supports a requirement for nuclear access in the measured pathway. However, a successful localization rescue would not establish that the artificial sequence reproduces every aspect of normal receptor regulation. It might cause constitutive localization, alter the response timing or expose interactions that occur only transiently in the unmodified plant. Rescue experiments test specific causal hypotheses rather than certify all details of a molecular model.

Illumination can also produce discrete nuclear accumulations of phytochromes referred to as photobodies. These are dynamic associations of receptor molecules and other components, not separate membrane-bounded organelles. Their appearance may correlate with receptor state and the light environment. For NET-level reasoning, it is sufficient to appreciate that spatial concentration of signaling molecules can influence their interactions, and that nuclear localization and formation of a visible photobody are distinct observations. The presence of a photobody alone is not proof that a particular transcriptional response is occurring. A receptor might accumulate in a structure without interacting productively with the required downstream regulator.

A milestone in connecting phytochrome to transcription was the finding that phyB can interact with the nuclear regulatory protein PIF3 in a light-dependent, conformer-selective manner. Work by Ni, Tepperman and Quail published in 1999 provided evidence for this relationship [4]. The conceptual significance is that a photoreceptor need not generate a diffusible second messenger to influence a nuclear response. Its light-dependent conformation can itself determine whether another protein is recognized. This principle resembles the selective interaction of phosphorylated signaling proteins with docking partners in other signaling systems, although the receptor activation chemistry is different.

Physical association is an important piece of evidence but cannot establish all later causal events. If two proteins interact in a biochemical assay, the interaction may occur under conditions that differ from their concentrations and localization in a living cell. To determine whether binding is functionally necessary, a researcher can compare a receptor variant defective in the interaction but competent in photoconversion, or a partner variant that retains its own activity but cannot associate with the receptor. A failure of the light response in such a separation-of-function mutant would connect the interaction more directly to pathway output. Conversely, a normal phenotype despite loss of one interaction may indicate functional redundancy or an alternative signaling branch.

The receptor-to-nucleus sequence therefore contains distinct stages that can be tested independently: chromophore assembly, optical activation, intracellular localization, partner recognition and alteration of downstream activity. The stages are not always a strictly linear sequence in every tissue, and not every phytochrome response requires identical nuclear events. Nevertheless, distinguishing these stages provides a reliable framework for interpreting mutants. A defective transcriptional endpoint cannot be assigned to photoperception without examining whether earlier events remain intact.

8. Phytochrome-interacting factors: changing the activity of growth regulators

Phytochrome-interacting factors, abbreviated PIFs, are a group of basic helix-loop-helix transcriptional regulators. The basic region of a typical bHLH transcription factor participates in DNA recognition, while the helix-loop-helix region supports interactions with other proteins, including dimerization partners. PIFs regulate groups of genes involved in growth and developmental transitions. The terminology can be misleading if a student assumes that every PIF is a component that positively promotes light responses simply because it interacts with a light receptor. Several PIFs sustain the dark developmental program or promote elongation under conditions in which phytochrome signaling is reduced. Their regulation by activated phytochromes can therefore help suppress processes characteristic of darkness or shade.

In a representative dark-grown Arabidopsis seedling, PIF proteins can accumulate and influence transcription of genes associated with skotomorphogenesis, the developmental program characteristic of growth without illumination. Their action includes both activation and repression of different targets. It would be inaccurate to classify PIFs as universal transcriptional repressors: a factor may promote expression of an elongation-associated gene while reducing expression of genes that support light development, directly or indirectly. For a given examination question, the sign of the transcriptional effect should be derived from the stated target relationship rather than inferred solely from the factor's name.

After illumination that activates the appropriate phytochrome, Pfr can recognize particular PIF proteins. Several PIFs undergo light-associated phosphorylation, followed by ubiquitination and degradation by the 26S proteasome. The phosphorylation events involve context-dependent kinase machinery; the pathway should not be reduced to a universal assertion that every phytochrome directly phosphorylates every PIF. Ubiquitination supplies a molecular recognition signal that can promote the destruction of the regulator. Once the regulator is removed, the genes controlled by it experience a changed regulatory environment. A gene that was activated by the removed PIF may decrease in expression, while a gene directly repressed by that PIF may increase.

The speed of this mechanism follows from its dependence on existing protein molecules. The cell does not need to wait for transcription of an entirely new receptor gene before initiating the response. Alteration of the stability of a pre-existing transcriptional regulator can change the amount of that regulator rapidly. Ubiquitin conjugation and proteasomal processing use cellular energy, principally ATP in the activation and processing steps. The light-absorbing receptor determines the timing or specificity of signaling, whereas the cell supplies the chemical energy required for protein turnover. Light-induced degradation is consequently a regulated biological process rather than simple damage to the transcription factor by photons.

A simplified pathway predicts that a receptor mutant unable to activate PIF removal may retain high levels of a PIF-dependent dark-growth program despite receiving red illumination. Conversely, a mutant that lacks several relevant PIFs can show features of light development even in darkness because the normal dark-promoting regulators are absent. This genetic observation is particularly informative because it separates the need for illumination from the action of downstream regulators. A mutation can bypass a requirement for the upstream stimulus by removing a molecular component that normally prevents a response. Such bypass does not imply that the missing downstream factor is itself the photoreceptor.

For a hypothetical one-pathway exercise, suppose PIF-X activates an elongation gene G and light-activated phyB promotes PIF-X degradation. A phyB-null plant retains high PIF-X and high G expression in red light. A pif-X-null plant has low G expression even in darkness. If the double mutant phyB-null pif-X-null also has low G expression, loss of PIF-X masks the effect of losing phyB for this particular output. The result is consistent with a pathway in which phyB acts upstream of PIF-X. The inference is specific to the tested gene and genetic model; real PIF families contain overlapping and compensatory interactions that may complicate the phenotype.

Not all PIF regulation can be understood as elimination of the protein. In some contexts, a change in phosphorylation, localization, DNA binding or interaction with other regulators alters the activity of a PIF without immediately destroying it. PIF7 is an important example in shade-related signaling, where reversible phosphorylation and localization are central features of regulation. This distinction can be tested experimentally. If total PIF abundance is unchanged but nuclear abundance and promoter occupancy change after illumination, a mechanism based solely on degradation is inadequate. A student should look at the type of data presented before choosing a mechanism.

A transcriptional assay must also distinguish direct targets from downstream consequences. A promoter-reporter construct may reveal that a gene responds to illumination, but this observation alone does not show that a PIF binds the promoter. Chromatin immunoprecipitation, interpreted with suitable negative controls and normalization, can test association with a particular genomic region. Mutation of the relevant promoter element can test whether that site contributes to the response. Even together, these experiments should be interpreted in the context of protein abundance and the possibility of indirect effects. Direct regulation is a more specific claim than merely observing a correlated change in expression.

The PIF branch demonstrates a general principle of signaling: an activated receptor can inhibit an intermediate rather than activate every downstream step. A receptor that decreases a negative regulator can increase expression of a light-development gene by releasing repression. A receptor that removes a positive regulator of elongation can decrease elongation. The same initial molecular event can therefore produce opposite effects on different transcriptional targets. Understanding the signs of individual regulatory links is more reliable than memorizing that light always activates or inhibits transcription.

9. COP1, SPA and HY5: regulation by selective protein stability

A second major regulatory branch connects photoreceptor activity with the stability of proteins that promote light development. COP1, or CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1, is a component of ubiquitin ligase machinery in Arabidopsis. It functions with SPA proteins, whose name originates from SUPPRESSOR OF PHYA-105. In dark-grown seedlings, COP1/SPA-dependent regulation helps maintain low abundance of positive regulators of photomorphogenesis. HY5, or ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL 5, is a prominent example. HY5 is a basic leucine zipper transcription factor that promotes aspects of light-responsive gene expression. It is not the enzyme that detects light, nor is it the chromophore of a photoreceptor.

In darkness the COP1/SPA machinery can promote ubiquitination of HY5 and other substrates, contributing to their proteasomal degradation. This maintains a regulatory environment that favors the dark growth program. The process is selective because ubiquitin ligase machinery recognizes particular substrates rather than indiscriminately removing all nuclear proteins. The name constitutive photomorphogenic describes the phenotype of certain loss-of-function mutants: seedlings display aspects of light-like development even when grown without the normal light stimulus. The name must not be interpreted as evidence that normal COP1 protein is a positive activator of photomorphogenesis. Its dark function is largely inhibitory toward the light program.

Illumination activates photoreceptors that can reduce the effective activity of COP1/SPA toward relevant substrates. In representative Arabidopsis pathways, activated phytochromes and cryptochromes influence interactions among components of this machinery. Light-dependent changes in its nuclear abundance or functional organization can also contribute to regulation. When the rate of HY5 degradation falls, HY5 may accumulate and regulate its target genes. This is a classic example of a double-negative regulatory relationship: a light-activated receptor suppresses an activity that normally suppresses accumulation of a positive downstream regulator. The net effect on the positive regulator is therefore an increase.

Protein abundance is the outcome of synthesis and removal, not a direct measure of either process independently. Suppose the synthesis rate of HY5 is constant at s units per minute, and its degradation follows a first-order approximation with coefficient k per minute. If H is its abundance, then dH/dt = s−kH. At steady state H = s/k. In an illustrative system, if synthesis is 12 units per minute and k is 0.6 min⁻¹ in darkness, the steady abundance is 20 units. If light reduces k to 0.15 min⁻¹ without altering synthesis, the predicted steady abundance becomes 80 units. A fourfold protein increase can therefore arise without a fourfold rise in messenger RNA or translation rate.

This calculation should not be mistaken for a complete model of HY5 expression in a real seedling. Transcription, translation, protein localization and regulatory feedback may also change with light. The model isolates one causal possibility so that a student can interpret experiments. If HY5 protein increases while HY5 messenger RNA remains approximately constant, reduced degradation is a reasonable hypothesis, but altered translation is another possibility. A measurement of the decay of a previously labeled protein population or a carefully controlled turnover experiment would help distinguish them. If protein degradation is blocked globally, other regulators can also accumulate, making a phenotype harder to attribute to HY5 alone.

Consider a hypothetical comparison of wild-type seedlings, a cop1 loss-of-function line and a hy5 loss-of-function line. In darkness, wild type has low abundance of several light-induced regulatory proteins. A cop1 mutant may accumulate positive light regulators in the absence of illumination and exhibit aspects of a light-grown phenotype. A hy5 mutant is impaired in a subset of light-dependent transcriptional and morphological responses. If a cop1 hy5 double mutant loses one of the light-like traits of cop1, the result is compatible with HY5 being required for that particular trait downstream of COP1. It does not imply that all consequences of COP1 loss depend exclusively on HY5, because COP1 controls additional substrates.

The difference between the PIF and COP1/HY5 branches is instructive. In the PIF branch, light can reduce the abundance or activity of dark-growth regulators. In the COP1/HY5 branch, light reduces a mechanism that removes positive light regulators, thereby stabilizing them. Thus one branch helps dismantle an existing growth program while the other helps establish an alternative program. They can reinforce a coordinated developmental change without being a single linear cascade. This network structure makes the response robust to changing environmental conditions, but it also creates the possibility of partially overlapping phenotypes when one branch is disrupted.

A dataset measuring both PIF and HY5 can reveal the distinction. Imagine red light causes PIF-X abundance to fall from 100 to 30 units while HY5 abundance rises from 20 to 75 units in the same tissue. A general explanation that light globally inhibits protein degradation cannot account for both changes without additional assumptions. Nor can a general explanation that light globally accelerates protein degradation account for accumulation of HY5. The results are consistent with selective changes in the turnover of different regulatory proteins. Protein-specific ubiquitination assays, receptor-binding measurements and time-resolved abundance data would strengthen the causal interpretation.

The biochemical mechanism of selective protein turnover also helps explain why the time course of gene expression need not mirror the time course of receptor activation. A photoreceptor can switch state within a short period of illumination, while PIF removal, HY5 accumulation, transcription, translation and altered growth occur over distinct timescales. Returning the receptor to its inactive state may halt further changes without instantly restoring the proteins that have already been destroyed. The response can persist until synthesis and degradation establish a new regulatory balance. This is a general feature of pathways that use protein turnover to transmit an environmental signal.

10. Cryptochromes: decoding blue light through flavoprotein signaling

The red and far-red sensing system cannot account for every light-dependent change in a young plant. Blue illumination can also prevent excessive hypocotyl extension, stimulate changes in pigment accumulation and contribute to the transition toward light-grown development. To determine whether these effects require a distinct sensory protein, researchers can compare plants that respond differently to the same blue stimulus. A particularly informative phenotype is an Arabidopsis seedling that continues to elongate its hypocotyl under blue light even though its genetically comparable wild-type seedling suppresses elongation. Such a difference suggests that a component of the normal response has been impaired. It does not immediately reveal whether the defect lies in absorption of blue light or in a later regulatory event.

In 1993 Margaret Ahmad and Anthony Cashmore reported the identification of the Arabidopsis HY4 gene, which encodes a protein related to microbial DNA photolyases [5]. This protein became known as cryptochrome 1, or CRY1. The relationship was important because photolyases are proteins that use light absorption by flavin-containing systems to support repair of certain DNA lesions. The discovery suggested that a protein with a related molecular organization could have been recruited for a different function: detecting light and transmitting information to the regulatory machinery of a plant cell. Subsequent evidence established cryptochrome as a photosensory protein rather than merely a downstream factor required for shortening the hypocotyl.

A mutant phenotype and sequence similarity provide different evidence. The phenotype shows that a particular gene contributes to the measured response. Sequence similarity suggests a plausible biochemical function because conserved amino acids and domains may support related chemical interactions. Neither observation alone demonstrates that the encoded protein absorbs blue light in a physiologically meaningful manner. Characterizing its chromophore and absorption behavior, and showing that an intact receptor restores the mutant response, helps close that gap. This progression from genetics to molecular characterization is an important example of how a receptor can be identified when the earliest observation is an altered whole-organism phenotype.

Plant cryptochromes contain an amino-terminal photolyase-homology region, often abbreviated PHR, associated with flavin adenine dinucleotide, FAD. They also possess a carboxyl-terminal extension involved in signaling. The molecular surroundings of the flavin help determine how absorption changes the protein's state. In contrast with a DNA repair enzyme, the canonical plant sensory cryptochromes use their light-dependent state to regulate molecular partners rather than acting primarily as enzymes that reverse DNA damage. A shared ancestral protein fold can thus support distinct biological outputs. This is a case in which conservation of a cofactor and structural region does not imply conservation of the entire cellular function.

FAD contains an isoalloxazine ring system that participates in electron transfer reactions and can absorb visible radiation. In cryptochrome, the light-sensitive state is coupled to rearrangements and interactions that can include changes in oligomerization and exposure or regulation of signaling regions. The precise flavin chemistry and its relation to activation vary with conditions and have been studied using several mechanistic models. At examination level, a reliable description is that absorption of blue or near-ultraviolet light by the flavin-containing receptor generates a signaling-competent state that modifies interactions with downstream proteins. It is unnecessary and potentially misleading to state that one particular flavin oxidation state universally explains all responses in all cryptochrome families.

An activated cryptochrome need not act as a conventional protein kinase to transmit its signal. A conformationally altered receptor can bind or compete for a partner, thereby changing the activity of another protein complex. For example, in blue-light signaling in Arabidopsis, cryptochromes influence the COP1/SPA system introduced earlier. In representative conditions, blue-light-dependent interactions reduce the ability of this regulatory machinery to promote degradation of positive light-response regulators. HY5 and related downstream transcriptional regulators can consequently accumulate. The convergence on COP1/SPA provides a molecular explanation for why both red and blue illumination can contribute to photomorphogenesis even though the upstream photoreceptors are chemically different.

The distinction between convergent and identical pathways is essential. If activated phytochrome and activated cryptochrome both reduce COP1/SPA activity, they share an output node in a simplified network. They do not thereby become the same receptor, use the same chromophore or exhibit the same photochemical cycle. A phytochrome-deficient mutant can retain some blue-light responses mediated by cryptochromes, and a cryptochrome-deficient mutant can retain red-light signaling mediated by phytochromes. The extent of these residual responses depends on receptor overlap and the downstream phenotype being measured. In a mixed-light experiment, loss of one pathway may be partly compensated by another input to the same regulator.

Arabidopsis CRY1 and CRY2 have overlapping but distinguishable functions. CRY1 is particularly important in classic experiments measuring blue-light inhibition of hypocotyl growth, while CRY2 contributes to blue-light-regulated development and flowering-related signaling and can be especially important under particular low-light conditions. These are representative functional tendencies rather than an absolute division. Their contributions can depend on fluence, duration, developmental stage and the response scored. CRY2 abundance is itself subject to light-dependent regulation, including degradation in relevant conditions. A receptor that is initially activated by illumination may subsequently become less abundant, which helps limit the duration or amplitude of its effects.

A simple genetic comparison illustrates how overlapping cryptochrome functions can be recognized. Suppose a wild-type seedling exhibits substantial hypocotyl inhibition in blue light, a cry1 mutant shows a large reduction in this inhibition, a cry2 mutant shows a smaller reduction under the chosen condition, and a cry1 cry2 double mutant retains very little inhibition. The stronger double-mutant phenotype supports a contribution from both genes to that output. It does not establish that CRY1 and CRY2 must form an obligatory heterodimer. Parallel routes that converge on a common downstream process can yield the same genetic pattern. A biochemical association experiment would be needed to address physical interactions, while measurements across different light regimes would reveal whether the functional overlap changes with the stimulus.

The readout used in such experiments must be chosen carefully. Hypocotyl length is the integrated consequence of growth over many hours or days. It can be affected by changes in hormone action, metabolism and development independently of the primary light-sensing event. A blue-light-specific change in a mutant becomes more informative when compared with dark, red-light and multiple blue-light dose controls. Measuring an early molecular output such as an interaction between a cryptochrome and a known partner can provide evidence at a different level. A combination of early molecular measurements and later phenotypes helps distinguish an immediate receptor defect from a general inability of the plant to alter growth.

Cryptochrome signaling also shows why an organism can contain several flavin-dependent light sensors without functional duplication. The receptor's signaling domains and interaction partners determine what happens after the chromophore is excited. Even if two receptors absorb photons in a similar region of the spectrum, they may control distinct outputs, respond at different timescales or operate in different cells. The next receptor family, phototropins, provides an especially clear comparison because it couples flavin-based photochemistry to an intrinsic protein kinase. Its molecular design is suited to responses in which light can direct the distribution of cellular activity in space.

11. Phototropins: coupling light absorption to protein phosphorylation

A plant shoot that bends toward a unilateral light source demonstrates that the direction of illumination can be biologically meaningful. This process, positive phototropism, requires unequal growth across the organ. A mechanism that merely measures the average illumination of the entire shoot cannot explain the directional asymmetry by itself. The plant must retain or reconstruct information about spatial differences in light reception and translate that information into differential cellular activity. In Arabidopsis, the blue-light receptors phototropin 1 and phototropin 2, abbreviated phot1 and phot2, are major components of this sensory system.

Phototropins were identified through genetic and biochemical studies connecting light-dependent phosphorylation to phototropic responses. The Arabidopsis gene originally known as NPH1 encodes phot1. In 1998 Christie and colleagues described NPH1 as a flavoprotein with characteristics expected of a receptor for phototropism [6]. The crucial advance was linking the candidate gene to a molecule that could absorb blue light and display light-dependent kinase activity. A protein that changes phosphorylation when an intact plant is illuminated could merely be a substrate of some other receptor. Demonstrating that a purified or heterologously expressed candidate protein possesses the relevant light-dependent properties helps establish that it has the capacity for direct photoreception.

The organization of a typical plant phototropin makes the coupling between light absorption and enzyme activity particularly understandable. Its amino-terminal region contains two photosensory LOV domains, designated LOV1 and LOV2. The abbreviation LOV refers to light, oxygen or voltage sensing, a name used for a group of sensory domains within the broader PAS-domain family. Each phototropin LOV domain binds a molecule of flavin mononucleotide, or FMN. The carboxyl-terminal region contains a protein kinase domain with serine/threonine kinase activity. The protein therefore contains both the sensory module and a catalytic output module within a single polypeptide.

FMN is a flavin cofactor related to FAD, but it does not contain the additional adenosine monophosphate moiety of FAD. The two cofactors share an isoalloxazine system that can absorb blue light, while their chemical structures and protein-binding environments differ. Cryptochromes and phototropins should not be classified together as a single receptor solely because both use flavins. For the principal plant examples in this chapter, CRY is associated with FAD and PHOT with FMN. The molecular output differs as well: phototropin possesses a kinase domain that can be activated by changes transmitted from the LOV region.

In the dark-adapted state, FMN is held noncovalently in the LOV binding pocket. Absorption of blue light initiates photochemical events that lead to formation of a transient covalent adduct between the flavin and a conserved cysteine residue. The bond involves the C4a position of the flavin. This change is associated with rearrangements of the surrounding protein and can alter the conformation of regions that communicate with the kinase domain. The LOV2 domain is especially important for regulation of kinase activity in the standard phototropin mechanism. LOV1 can contribute additional regulatory functions, so an accurate description should not imply that it is irrelevant in every response.

The photochemical adduct is reversible. In darkness, spontaneous recovery of the LOV domain restores the ground state over a characteristic timescale. This recovery does not require a far-red pulse and is chemically different from the Pr/Pfr photoconversion of phytochrome. The kinetics of recovery can influence how a receptor responds to pulses of blue light separated by dark intervals. If recovery is rapid relative to the interval, a second pulse encounters a largely reset receptor population. If recovery is slow, a substantial fraction of receptors can remain in the signaling state between pulses. The response to pulse frequency therefore depends partly on the lifetime of the activated state.

Light-dependent activation of the kinase leads to autophosphorylation and regulation of downstream signaling components. Autophosphorylation means that the protein kinase transfers phosphate groups onto sites in its own protein population, using ATP as a phosphate donor. The photochemical reaction in the LOV domain is not the source of the phosphate. The absorbed photon modifies the regulation of an enzyme; cellular ATP provides the substrate for phosphorylation. An exam question that proposes that FMN donates the phosphate group to a phototropin serine residue confuses the photosensory role of the cofactor with the chemistry of protein phosphorylation.

Phototropin can be associated with the plasma membrane even though it is not a classical seven-transmembrane G-protein-coupled receptor. Its positioning is relevant because several downstream outputs involve the spatial organization of cellular transport or membrane-associated components. Membrane association does not establish that a protein spans the membrane or that it must use a heterotrimeric G protein. The biochemical properties of the protein and the identities of its partners are required to classify its signaling mechanism. This comparison links the chapter to the general principles of receptor classification without treating every sensory protein as a member of the membrane receptor families encountered in animal signaling.

A set of hypothetical mutant receptors illustrates the distinction between light reception and catalytic output. Variant A retains kinase activity in an artificial assay but cannot bind FMN properly. Variant B binds FMN and undergoes the usual light-dependent spectral change but has a defective catalytic site in the kinase domain. Variant C undergoes photochemistry and retains an intact catalytic site but fails to transmit the conformational change from the LOV region to the kinase. All three could fail to restore phototropism in a plant deficient in the native receptor. Their molecular defects are different, and their comparison shows why a visible phenotype is not sufficient to assign the location of a pathway lesion.

To separate these variants experimentally, the investigator would first compare the amount and integrity of the receptor proteins. Next, a light-dependent absorption or photochemical assay would determine whether each receptor has normal sensory behavior. A kinase assay performed with an appropriate phosphate donor would reveal whether catalytic activity is present and whether light regulates it. The presence of kinase activity under a condition that artificially releases inhibition would distinguish a coupling defect from loss of the catalytic site. Finally, testing the receptor in the plant would reveal whether the restored molecular functions are sufficient for the biological response. These are conceptual stages of experimental reasoning rather than a prescription for a specialist laboratory procedure.

Phot1 and phot2 are related receptors with partly overlapping functions. Phot1 can mediate phototropic responses at relatively low blue-light fluence rates, whereas phot2 contributes particularly under higher illumination in many experimental contexts. Both receptors can contribute to several blue-light-dependent responses. Their relative importance is not fixed for every plant organ or light regime. A useful experiment therefore varies light intensity across a range rather than drawing a general conclusion from one intensity. If a phot1 mutant responds poorly at low light but better at higher light, the recovery may be consistent with a greater contribution of phot2. A phot1 phot2 double mutant and corresponding complementation lines would strengthen that interpretation.

The molecular architecture of phototropin demonstrates the broader principle of sensory enzyme regulation. In darkness the receptor's sensory and catalytic domains occupy a functional arrangement that limits signaling. Photon absorption alters the sensory module, the conformational change influences the catalytic module, ATP-dependent phosphorylation changes the receptor or its partners, and downstream cellular machinery produces a response. The pathway can be reset through reversal of the photochemical state and changes in phosphorylation or partner interactions. The specific proteins and cellular processes differ from those in an animal receptor kinase cascade, but the underlying strategy of coupling stimulus-dependent conformational change to catalytic output is shared.

12. From a directional blue-light input to asymmetric cellular behavior

The fact that phototropin becomes phosphorylated after blue illumination does not by itself explain bending. Bending is a geometrical consequence of unequal growth on opposite sides of an organ. In a simplified shoot model, cells on the more rapidly elongating side produce a greater increase in length than cells on the slower side. The resulting curvature is directed toward the side that elongates less. For a typical positively phototropic shoot, greater extension on the shaded side contributes to curvature toward the illuminated side. The direction of curvature follows from relative growth, not from the absolute growth rate of either side alone.

The spatial information needed for this response is generated because directional illumination produces differences in receptor activation across the tissue. Activated phototropin influences downstream components such as NPH3, named NON-PHOTOTROPIC HYPOCOTYL 3. NPH3 participates in the signaling route connecting phototropin to the regulation of directional growth. Other components, including RPT2 in relevant contexts, contribute to the adjustment of the response. It is important to distinguish these signal-transduction components from the receptor itself. A mutant lacking NPH3 may have defective phototropism even if its phototropin molecules absorb blue light and undergo light-dependent changes.

The plant hormone auxin provides a means of translating spatial information into growth differences. In representative shoot phototropism, phototropin-mediated signaling influences auxin distribution or transport so that growth-promoting auxin action is unequal across the organ. Auxin-associated growth responses are context dependent; an increase in auxin does not universally increase extension in every plant tissue or at every concentration. For a typical shoot model within a suitable range, greater auxin-mediated extension on the shaded side contributes to positive curvature. The light receptor is not a source of auxin in the same chemical sense that a biosynthetic enzyme produces a hormone. It regulates a pathway that can change the spatial deployment or effect of existing developmental machinery.

Classical experiments on directional growth helped establish the need for a signal transmitted from a light-sensitive region to a growing region. An opaque covering of a sensory tip, compared with a transparent covering, can distinguish obstruction of light from the physical effects of covering. Shielding a different region tests whether the same sensory site is necessary. These experiments are particularly useful in coleoptile models, where light detection near the apex can affect growth in a lower region. The location and organization of perception depend on the organ and species; a result obtained in a coleoptile should not be generalized to every Arabidopsis tissue without further evidence.

A modern conceptual experiment might compare unilateral illumination with symmetrical illumination at a matched overall photon dose. Both treatments can activate photoreceptors, but unilateral illumination provides a spatial cue. A plant exposed symmetrically may exhibit changes in overall growth without showing a consistent curvature toward either side. A plant exposed unilaterally can develop curvature if receptor activation is translated into an asymmetric response. A mutant that displays normal average blue-light inhibition of elongation but fails to bend may have a defect in directional signaling rather than a complete loss of blue-light perception. This is one reason that hypocotyl inhibition and phototropic curvature are useful complementary assays.

To determine whether a defect occurs before or after auxin redistribution, one can compare an early phototropin-dependent phosphorylation readout, a spatial auxin-response reporter and eventual curvature. Suppose the receptor phosphorylation occurs normally in a mutant, but the reporter fails to become asymmetric and the shoot remains straight. The result suggests that the defect lies between receptor activation and formation or expression of a spatial auxin response. A reporter measures the activity of its own regulatory construct, which may reflect auxin signaling rather than directly report auxin concentration. A failure of reporter asymmetry could therefore arise from defective hormone distribution or defective interpretation of a normal distribution. Additional measurements are necessary to distinguish these hypotheses.

If an asymmetrically imposed growth-promoting signal restores curvature to a receptor-deficient shoot, that observation demonstrates that the downstream growth machinery can still generate bending under the tested conditions. It does not show that the receptor normally causes curvature by precisely the same experimentally imposed route. An artificial rescue can bypass multiple missing steps. Conversely, failure of an artificial treatment to restore curvature does not necessarily disprove a role for that downstream factor: the dose, location, developmental state or timing could be inappropriate. Rescue experiments are most informative when the intervention is defined narrowly and the outcome is interpreted relative to explicit controls.

Phototropins also contribute to other spatial or rapid blue-light responses, notably chloroplast relocation and guard-cell responses. Under weak illumination, chloroplast accumulation along appropriately illuminated surfaces can improve light interception, while under stronger illumination, avoidance movements can reduce exposure. Phototropin-dependent pathways help control these movements; in Arabidopsis phot2 has a particularly important role in the high-light avoidance response. These organelle movements demonstrate that a change in the distribution of cellular structures can be a light-signaling output without requiring the same transcriptional changes that establish a light-grown seedling morphology. Detailed mechanics of organelle movement are beyond the central receptor-level pathway.

In guard cells, blue-light signaling through phototropins can lead to activation of plasma-membrane proton-pumping machinery. The subsequent change in membrane electrochemical conditions contributes to ion accumulation and water entry, increasing turgor and promoting opening under appropriate physiological conditions. The photon is not physically pumping each proton; light-dependent signaling changes the activity of an ATP-consuming pump through intervening regulators. This distinction connects sensory reception to energy-dependent membrane transport. The final stomatal aperture reflects other influences as well, including water status and hormonal signals. An open or closed stoma is therefore not an exclusive indicator of phototropin activity. Full stomatal regulation is treated in plant physiology rather than being developed as a separate pathway catalogue in this chapter.

Taken together, these responses explain why phototropins and cryptochromes cannot be identified solely by the fact that their action spectra lie in the blue region. In a representative young plant, cryptochrome signaling makes a major contribution to transcriptional regulation of light development, while phototropin signaling is particularly suited to directional growth and rapid changes in cellular behavior. Both can influence growth and the effects of light in overlapping contexts. Their distinction rests on the identity of the chromophore-binding domains, the immediate signaling output and experimental evidence connecting receptor function to the measured response.

13. Integration of light signals in a developing seedling

The transition from darkness to illumination is sometimes presented as a simple replacement of one phenotype by another. In reality, several processes change at different rates and in distinct tissues. A typical dark-grown Arabidopsis seedling has an elongated hypocotyl, relatively unexpanded cotyledons and plastids that have not developed into fully functional green chloroplasts. The arrangement helps a seedling growing in darkness allocate stored resources toward emergence. Light exposure can inhibit further hypocotyl extension, promote cotyledon expansion and initiate changes associated with chloroplast development. The collective transition is called de-etiolation and illustrates photomorphogenesis, the influence of light signals on developmental form.

The dark phenotype is not simply the absence of all regulation. It is maintained through an active network in which dark-associated growth regulators are present and several positive light-development regulators are kept at relatively low abundance. PIF and COP1/SPA pathways help establish this state in the model described earlier. Light triggers changes in this existing network rather than constructing a signaling pathway from nothing. This distinction explains why mutations in negative regulators can produce aspects of photomorphogenesis in darkness. The mutant has not created a source of visible photons; it has changed the regulatory conditions that normally make a light stimulus necessary for the selected response.

The input network can be understood using the signs of three regulatory relationships. First, activated phytochrome can reduce the activity or abundance of specific PIFs that promote aspects of the dark-growth program. Second, activated phytochrome or cryptochrome can reduce the effective activity of COP1/SPA toward positive light-development regulators. Third, the resulting accumulation of regulators such as HY5 can promote expression of appropriate light-response genes. These routes are not merely successive arrows in a single obligatory linear sequence. They are interacting branches that can converge on the transcriptional and developmental state of the seedling. Phototropins can simultaneously adjust directional or membrane-associated outputs through a distinct initial kinase mechanism.

A hypothetical reporter experiment can illustrate convergence. Let reporter L respond positively to HY5. Assume, for this exercise, that the major light inputs to the reporter are inhibition of COP1/SPA by phyB in red light and by CRY1 in blue light. In red light, a phyB-null mutant should have a reduced reporter response relative to wild type if other phytochromes do not compensate. In blue light, a cry1-null mutant should have a reduced response if other cryptochromes do not compensate. In mixed illumination, loss of either receptor may have a smaller effect than in its preferred wavelength treatment because the remaining receptor can influence the shared regulatory system. The assumptions are important: real promoters have multiple inputs, and no simple reporter is a universal readout for the entire light-signaling network.

The experiment would become more informative if it also included a HY5-deficient line. If the model stipulates that HY5 is absolutely necessary for reporter L expression, loss of HY5 should impair the reporter in both red and blue light even if both receptors are functional. This would support a shared downstream requirement for the reporter, but would not show that HY5 is necessary for all phototropin-mediated movements or for every other light-induced transcript. The phenotype under consideration determines the scope of the conclusion. A pathway diagram represents a particular level of abstraction, and an experiment should be judged by the relationship it actually tests rather than by every potential connection in the broader network.

An especially important distinction is between photomorphogenesis and photosynthesis. A developing seedling can use a photoreceptor signal to initiate regulatory changes before mature photosynthetic machinery is fully established. The accumulation of chlorophyll and development of functional chloroplasts subsequently improve the plant's capacity to use light as an energy source. A block in photosynthetic electron transport may impair growth over longer times without preventing every early photoreceptor-dependent response. Conversely, a plant defective in a particular photoreceptor can still retain chlorophyll and some photosynthetic capacity. Neither the presence of photosynthesis nor its absence identifies the entire state of the photoreceptor network.

An experimental test of this distinction could expose comparable seedlings to a short light treatment while examining an early receptor-specific molecular response and a separate measure of photosynthetic electron transport. If an intervention blocks photosynthetic electron transport but a light-dependent receptor interaction remains detectable, the early interaction does not depend entirely on the measured electron transport process. Long-term interpretation is harder because energy limitation, oxidative stress and changes in plastid-to-nucleus signaling can influence the phenotype. The use of short time courses, matched controls and measurements of cell viability helps avoid mistaking general physiological damage for a direct block of a light-receptor pathway.

Shade provides a second example of integration. In a light-grown plant, a reduction in the red-to-far-red ratio can lower phyB activity under many conditions. Reduced phyB signaling can permit increased activity of certain PIF-dependent growth programs. A plant may then extend particular organs as part of a shade-avoidance strategy. At the same time, neighboring vegetation can reduce blue light, potentially altering cryptochrome inputs. The combined response may therefore depend on multiple features of the spectrum rather than the red-to-far-red ratio alone. This explains why an artificial red/far-red experiment and natural canopy shade need not produce quantitatively identical outcomes even when both change one important spectral variable.

The practical relevance of these mechanisms is apparent in controlled plant cultivation, where lighting can be used to influence plant form as well as energy capture. Altering a spectrum can modify elongation or leaf development without producing a proportional change in the total number of incident photons. Nevertheless, a mechanistic understanding does not justify assuming that one spectrum is optimal for every crop, stage or production goal. Different plants express distinct receptor combinations and have different physiological requirements. An applied experiment would therefore compare receptor-linked morphological responses alongside measures of growth and energy capture rather than interpret every effect of a lighting treatment as a consequence of a single receptor.

Light also supplies timing information to developmental and circadian systems. Cryptochromes and phytochromes can participate in pathways linking light input to the regulation of time-dependent gene expression. The important principle here is that a sensory receptor may supply information to an oscillator or developmental decision system without itself being the entire oscillator. Detailed photoperiodic flowering and circadian feedback mechanisms require their own treatment in sensory plant physiology. In the present chapter, their relevance is limited to understanding that the same primary sensory event may feed multiple outputs, some immediate and others expressed only after particular intervals of environmental exposure.

14. Testing light-signaling models with experiments and data

A molecular pathway becomes scientifically useful when it yields predictions that can be tested independently of the drawing used to represent it. In a light-signaling experiment, one can ask whether the receptor detects the specified wavelength, whether activation changes its conformation or catalytic activity, whether the change modifies a defined partner, and whether the partner is necessary for the later response. These questions require different evidence. A visible mutant phenotype is valuable for identifying candidate components, but it does not by itself establish every intervening step. A biochemical interaction can establish compatibility between molecules, but it does not necessarily show that their interaction is essential in the living tissue.

Consider a hypothetical series of Arabidopsis lines exposed to red, far-red or blue light. Wild type displays red-induced suppression of hypocotyl growth, a blue-light response and phototropic bending under unilateral blue illumination. Line A shows markedly reduced red-induced suppression but retains blue-light inhibition and bending. Line B retains red-induced suppression and bending but has a weak blue-light hypocotyl-inhibition response. Line C retains both red and blue growth inhibition under symmetrical illumination but fails to bend under unilateral blue illumination. These patterns are consistent with defects affecting phytochrome, cryptochrome and phototropin-linked directional signaling, respectively, but are not uniquely diagnostic of particular receptor genes. A downstream component selectively required by each response could give a similar pattern.

To discriminate receptor defects from pathway defects, the next step is to measure a proximal molecular event for each line. For line A, receptor abundance and light-dependent Pr/Pfr spectral changes could help establish whether phytochrome photoperception is intact. For line B, cryptochrome abundance and a blue-light-dependent partner interaction would test an earlier step than final hypocotyl length. For line C, phototropin-dependent phosphorylation and the spatial distribution of a downstream reporter would help distinguish receptor activation from signal transmission. Measurements from all lines should include suitable controls for protein loading, developmental state and optical exposure. If all proximal responses are normal, the most direct interpretation is that the defects occur later in the particular pathways being tested, subject to assay sensitivity.

Genetic complementation provides a stronger test of the identity of a mutation. If introducing a functional gene restores the affected phenotype in an otherwise appropriately controlled mutant background, that result supports a causal role for the gene. A failure to restore the phenotype requires careful interpretation because a transgene may be expressed in the wrong cell type, at an inappropriate level or at the wrong time. A receptor fusion used to visualize localization should ideally also complement a receptor-deficient line. This functional test helps guard against interpreting the position of a fluorescent but inactive protein as its normal physiological localization.

A complementary type of reasoning uses epistasis, the masking or modification of the effect of one gene by another in a multiple mutant. In a simplified negative regulatory pathway, suppose a light-activated receptor inhibits a repressor that normally prevents expression of gene G. A receptor-null mutant has low G expression in light, while a repressor-null mutant has high G expression even in darkness. A receptor-null repressor-null double mutant also has high G expression. These results support a model in which removal of the repressor bypasses the requirement for the receptor for expression of G. They are consistent with the repressor acting downstream of the receptor, but they do not prove direct physical contact or exclude additional branches of the pathway.

The timing of the response provides a different kind of evidence. Imagine that a red pulse causes a measurable phytochrome-state change within seconds, reduces the abundance of a particular PIF over minutes, increases the messenger RNA level of a target gene over a longer interval and alters hypocotyl growth several hours later. The order is compatible with the proposed causal chain. It is not sufficient to prove causality because two events may be triggered in parallel by the same upstream stimulus. A mutant that blocks the PIF change but retains the receptor-state change, combined with a suitable rescue, would make the causal connection stronger. Chronological precedence is necessary for a direct upstream event but is not alone sufficient to establish that event as the cause.

A protein-abundance measurement also requires attention to normalization. Suppose a PIF signal on an immunoblot falls to half of its dark value after illumination. If the amount of sample loaded also fell to half, the apparent loss might reflect sample handling rather than regulated turnover. A stable loading reference or a well-validated total-protein normalization procedure helps distinguish these possibilities. Even a genuine decline in protein abundance could reflect reduced synthesis rather than increased removal. A comparison of messenger RNA, protein synthesis and decay of previously present protein can help discriminate between alternatives. This is especially relevant to signaling pathways that explicitly regulate protein stability.

Hypothetical quantitative data can be used to test a receptor-state model against a biological response. Let two red-light pulse treatments produce the same measured active phytochrome fraction immediately after exposure, but one treatment is followed by a dark interval of 20 minutes and the other by a dark interval of 60 minutes. If the effective dark reversion coefficient is 0.02 min⁻¹ and both begin with x₀ = 0.8, the expected fractions at the end of the intervals are 0.8e⁻⁰·⁴ ≈ 0.536 and 0.8e⁻¹·² ≈ 0.241. A reporter that depends on the instantaneous active fraction might therefore produce different readouts. A reporter that reflects accumulated transcription during the interval may instead integrate past activity. The experiment must specify which quantity is measured before predicting a unique outcome.

The distinction between necessity and sufficiency is especially important when interpreting these experiments. A loss-of-function receptor that abolishes a response shows that its function is necessary in the tested genetic and environmental context. It does not automatically show that activating only that receptor is sufficient in every tissue. A constitutively signaling receptor variant that evokes the response in darkness can provide evidence for sufficiency under the tested conditions, but this interpretation still depends on the presence of the normal downstream machinery. An activated receptor cannot stimulate a transcriptional output that requires a downstream transcription factor if that factor and all compensating routes have been removed. The term sufficient always refers to a specified background and outcome.

A well-designed examination answer therefore begins by identifying the actual readout and the experimental comparison. It then locates the earliest demonstrated defect, distinguishes direct observation from interpretation, and makes a prediction for a further controlled perturbation. This reasoning applies equally to the reversible Pr/Pfr switch, light-dependent cryptochrome interactions and phototropin kinase activation. The receptors differ in their chemistry, but the logic for establishing a causal signaling pathway is shared. A student who can move between molecular structure, optical treatment, genetic perturbation and downstream phenotype has understood the chapter at a deeper level than one who can only match wavelengths to receptor names.

Question 1

What distinguishes a sensory photoreceptor from a photosynthetic reaction center most directly?

  1. It must transfer electrons to a photosynthetic electron-transport chain.
  2. It obtains ATP by splitting every absorbed photon.
  3. It converts photon absorption into a change that regulates downstream cellular processes.

Answer and explanation

C. It converts photon absorption into a change that regulates downstream cellular processes.

A sensory receptor changes its own state and influences regulatory partners. Photosynthetic charge separation is a different primary output; neither ATP generation nor chloroplast localization defines all photoreceptors.

Question 2

Which pairing correctly identifies a major plant photoreceptor chromophore?

  1. Phytochrome — phytochromobilin.
  2. Cryptochrome — FMN in two LOV domains.
  3. Phototropin — phytochromobilin.
  4. Phytochrome — chlorophyll a.
Answer and explanation

A. Phytochrome — phytochromobilin.

The bilin is the phytochrome chromophore. Plant sensory cryptochromes associate with FAD; phototropin LOV domains bind FMN. Chlorophyll participates principally in photosynthetic light capture.

Question 3

In the usual Pr/Pfr nomenclature, what does the suffix fr refer to?

  1. Production of the form exclusively by far-red light.
  2. A form that cannot absorb red photons.
  3. A form found only in far-red-grown plants.
  4. Preferential absorption of far-red light by that form.
Answer and explanation

D. Preferential absorption of far-red light by that form.

The states are named by their absorption properties. Far-red generally converts Pfr toward Pr; spectral overlap prevents exclusive absorption.

Question 4

Which event most directly explains classical red/far-red photoreversibility?

  1. Irreversible accumulation of photosynthetic ATP with each pulse.
  2. Reversible interconversion of spectrally distinct phytochrome states.
  3. De novo translation of a different receptor after each pulse.
  4. Irreversible degradation of all phytochrome after red light.
Answer and explanation

B. Reversible interconversion of spectrally distinct phytochrome states.

Alternating exposures change the receptor-state distribution. The energy-accumulation and irreversible-removal models cannot by themselves explain alternating response reversal.

Question 5

A plant phytochrome polypeptide with a functional output domain lacks its bilin. What is most directly affected?

  1. Translation of every nuclear gene.
  2. Efficient receptor photoperception.
  3. All ATP-dependent protein phosphorylation.
  4. Assembly of all membrane ion channels.
Answer and explanation

B. Efficient receptor photoperception.

A missing chromophore prevents normal light sensing even when the protein exists. Downstream processes may change indirectly; there is no reason for all translation, phosphorylation or transport to fail.

Question 6

Which statement distinguishes absorption from action spectra?

  1. An absorption spectrum must be determined from final hypocotyl length.
  2. The two spectra must have identical maxima in every tissue.
  3. An action spectrum directly determines receptor abundance.
  4. An action spectrum describes wavelength dependence of a specified biological response.
Answer and explanation

D. An action spectrum describes wavelength dependence of a specified biological response.

Action spectra integrate absorption, photochemical yields, tissue optics and signaling. Absorption spectra measure optical properties; coincidence is supportive evidence, not proof of receptor identity.

Question 7

A. phyA is relatively light-labile and important in continuous far-red responses.

Both phyA and phyB are bilin photoreceptors. Their stability and response regimes differ; neither is a GPCR, and far-red response specialization does not imply exclusive far-red absorption.

Which property characterizes a classical low-fluence reversible response?

The pulse-reversal paradigm is the classic LFR, often mediated by phyB. Sustained far-red HIR and phyA VLFR have distinct dose and timing properties.

Loss of HY5 masks the specified COP1 phenotype. Epistasis supports a downstream requirement for this output, not universal substrate exclusivity or direct contact.

Question 34

Red light lowers PIF-X but increases HY5 in the same samples, with validated normalization. Which model is most compatible?

  1. Light globally blocks all degradation as its sole effect.
  2. Selective regulation of different protein turnover or synthesis pathways.
  3. Light globally accelerates all degradation as its sole effect.
  4. Light eliminates all transcription factors equally.
Answer and explanation

B. Selective regulation of different protein turnover or synthesis pathways.

Opposite abundance changes require substrate-specific regulation or additional processes. Abundance data alone do not establish the exact mechanism; turnover measurements help.

Question 35

A cry1-null line is partly defective in blue-light hypocotyl inhibition, cry2-null mildly defective, and the double mutant more defective than either. What is the strongest inference?

  1. CRY1 and CRY2 must form an obligatory physical heterodimer.
  2. CRY2 is a phytochrome chromophore.
  3. Blue light is no longer absorbed by any pigment in the double mutant.
  4. Both genes contribute to the tested response with overlapping functions.
Answer and explanation

D. Both genes contribute to the tested response with overlapping functions.

Genetics supports overlapping contributions, but parallel pathways can explain the same phenotype. Physical interaction requires different evidence.

Question 36

A phototropin mutant binds FMN and forms the light-induced adduct but fails to phosphorylate even when catalytic inhibition is artificially released. Which lesion is most consistent?

  1. A defect in catalytic kinase function.
  2. Loss of the LOV chromophore binding pocket as the only lesion.
  3. Absence of blue photon absorption.
  4. A defect in red-to-far-red photoconversion.
Answer and explanation

A. A defect in catalytic kinase function.

Normal adduct formation demonstrates preserved receptor photochemistry in the assay. Failure even under kinase-permissive conditions points to catalytic competence or required assay factors.

Question 37

A phototropin variant has normal blue-light photochemistry and normal kinase activity in a released state, but blue light fails to stimulate kinase activity in the intact receptor. Which hypothesis is favored?

  1. Missing FMN.
  2. Defective coupling between LOV photochemistry and kinase regulation.
  3. Complete loss of ATP-binding ability.
  4. Conversion into a cryptochrome.
Answer and explanation

B. Defective coupling between LOV photochemistry and kinase regulation.

Both component functions are present separately, but stimulus-dependent coupling fails. A defective ATP site would conflict with normal catalytic activity in the released state.

Question 38

A plant has normal phototropin phosphorylation but lacks spatial auxin-reporter asymmetry and phototropic curvature. Which further test best separates auxin distribution from response sensitivity?

  1. Compare a direct measure of auxin distribution with a calibrated response to imposed auxin.
  2. Repeat the final curvature measurement with no controls.
  3. Measure only total chlorophyll.
  4. Use red/far-red reversal as a definitive assay for auxin concentration.
Answer and explanation

A. Compare a direct measure of auxin distribution with a calibrated response to imposed auxin.

The reporter may reflect response rather than concentration. Direct distribution measurement and calibrated response address the two competing explanations.

Question 39

A plant responds poorly to unilateral light at low blue fluence rate but better at high rate; phot1 is absent. Which experiment most directly tests compensation by phot2?

  1. Remove HY5 only and measure germination in darkness.
  2. Assume high light restores the missing PHOT1 gene.
  3. Compare phot1 phot2 double mutant and phot2 complementation across matched fluence rates.
  4. Measure phytochrome absorption without a phototropism assay.
Answer and explanation

C. Compare phot1 phot2 double mutant and phot2 complementation across matched fluence rates.

The double mutant and complementation directly test the role of the remaining homolog across regimes. High irradiance does not restore a deleted gene.

Question 40

A photosynthetic electron-transport inhibitor suppresses oxygen evolution but a rapid light-dependent phyB–PIF interaction persists. Which conclusion is warranted?

  1. Photosynthesis never influences any long-term light response.
  2. The inhibitor proves PIF is a photosystem subunit.
  3. All plant energy metabolism is unnecessary for photoreceptor signaling.
  4. That early interaction does not require the measured electron-transport activity under these conditions.
Answer and explanation

D. That early interaction does not require the measured electron-transport activity under these conditions.

Persistence separates the measured early signaling event from the inhibited process. It does not rule out energy requirements or long-term plastid signaling.

Original practice questions. Where a dataset or model is hypothetical, interpret it under the stated assumptions.

Question 41

Assertion–Reason: Assertion: A dark-grown cop1 loss-of-function seedling can show light-like traits. Reason: COP1/SPA normally promotes degradation of positive light-development regulators in darkness.

  1. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  2. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
  3. The assertion is true; the reason is false.
  4. The assertion is false; the reason is true.
Answer and explanation

B. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Loss of a dark-active negative regulator can allow positive regulators to accumulate without the normal light input.

Question 42

Assertion–Reason: Assertion: Equal photon fluence does not always produce equal biological responses. Reason: Some responses depend on irradiation duration or flux as well as total photon dose.

  1. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  2. The assertion is true; the reason is false.
  3. The assertion is false; the reason is true.
  4. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
Answer and explanation

D. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.

Ongoing relaxation and signaling during exposure can break reciprocity. The reason directly accounts for the assertion.

Question 43

Assertion–Reason: Assertion: Phototropin is a blue-light-activated protein kinase. Reason: Plant phototropin contains a covalently attached phytochromobilin chromophore.

  1. Both are true, and the reason explains the assertion.
  2. Both are true, but the reason does not explain the assertion.
  3. The assertion is true; the reason is false.
  4. The assertion is false; the reason is true.
Answer and explanation

C. The assertion is true; the reason is false.

Phototropin is a Ser/Thr kinase with FMN-binding LOV domains, not a bilin-containing phytochrome.

Question 44

Multiple statements: I. Red usually favors Pr→Pfr. II. Far-red can drive Pfr→Pr. III. Pfr is absolutely absent under all far-red treatments. IV. Phytochrome spectra overlap. Which set is correct?

  1. I, II and IV only.
  2. I and III only.
  3. II and III only.
  4. I, II, III and IV.
Answer and explanation

A. I, II and IV only.

The overlap permits some conversion in both directions. Statement III makes an unjustified absolute claim; the other three describe the standard photocycle.

Question 45

Multiple statements: I. HY5 is a transcription factor. II. COP1/SPA can promote HY5 turnover. III. Light may stabilize HY5. IV. Every PIF is a direct activator of HY5 transcription. Which set is correct?

  1. II and IV only.
  2. I and IV only.
  3. All four.
  4. I, II and III only.
Answer and explanation

D. I, II and III only.

The first three form a valid simplified pathway. The fourth is an unsupported universalization of a diverse transcription-factor family.

Question 46

Matching: 1 phytochrome; 2 cryptochrome; 3 phototropin; 4 HY5. a FAD-associated PHR; b bZIP regulator; c phytochromobilin; d FMN-binding LOV domains. Select the correct mapping.

  1. 1-c, 2-a, 3-d, 4-b.
  2. 1-a, 2-c, 3-d, 4-b.
  3. 1-c, 2-d, 3-a, 4-b.
  4. 1-b, 2-a, 3-c, 4-d.
Answer and explanation

A. 1-c, 2-a, 3-d, 4-b.

The chromophore and domain pairings distinguish the three receptor families; HY5 is the downstream bZIP regulator.

Question 47

Matching: 1 FHY1/FHL; 2 COP1/SPA; 3 NPH3; 4 PIF. a phyA nuclear accumulation; b phototropin-linked directional signaling; c ubiquitin ligase regulation; d bHLH transcriptional regulation.

  1. 1-c, 2-a, 3-b, 4-d.
  2. 1-a, 2-c, 3-b, 4-d.
  3. 1-a, 2-b, 3-d, 4-c.
  4. 1-d, 2-c, 3-a, 4-b.
Answer and explanation

B. 1-a, 2-c, 3-b, 4-d.

These are representative roles in Arabidopsis. The mapping separates transport, turnover, directional signaling and transcriptional regulation.

Question 48

Numerical: a monochromatic photon flux of 4 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ is applied for 45 seconds. What is the fluence?

  1. 11.25 µmol m⁻².
  2. 49 µmol m⁻².
  3. 180 µmol m⁻².
  4. 180 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.
Answer and explanation

C. 180 µmol m⁻².

Multiply flux density by time: 4×45=180 µmol m⁻². The per-second unit cancels; 180 with s⁻¹ is a flux, not fluence.

Question 49

Experimental data: hypothetical dark, R, R→FR, R→FR→R germination is 12%, 86%, 15%, 82%. What is the reduction in percentage points from R to R→FR?

  1. 71 percentage points.
  2. 82.6 percentage points.
  3. 15 percentage points.
  4. 4 percentage points.
Answer and explanation

A. 71 percentage points.

86−15=71 percentage points. The relative reduction is 71/86≈82.6%, which is a different quantity. The data support reversal, not direct measurement of Pfr.

Question 50

Integrative: a reporter requires HY5 and receives red and blue inputs through phyB and CRY1, respectively. A phyB mutant is rescued by blue light, but a hy5 mutant is not. Which model is consistent?

  1. Blue light directly restores the missing PHYB allele.
  2. HY5 is the chromophore shared by both receptors.
  3. Red and blue inputs must use identical photochemistry.
  4. Parallel sensory inputs converge on a HY5-dependent output.
Answer and explanation

D. Parallel sensory inputs converge on a HY5-dependent output.

A remaining receptor can bypass loss of one upstream input to a shared downstream requirement. The data do not establish identical receptor structures or repair a mutation.
