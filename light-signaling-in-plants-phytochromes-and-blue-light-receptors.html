1. Why a plant must interpret light

A seedling emerging beneath soil encounters a change that is both an energetic opportunity and a source of information. In darkness, a young shoot can use stored food to extend towards the soil surface. Once it encounters illumination, continuing the same pattern of extension is often less useful than expanding photosynthetic tissue. The external change must be detected before the internal pattern of growth can be reorganized. This is the essential problem of plant light signaling: a physical event outside the cell changes the activities of particular molecules inside the cell, and those molecular changes alter development or cellular behavior. The process does not require an animal-like nervous system. Individual plant cells contain sensory proteins and signaling machinery that can communicate with neighboring tissues through other established mechanisms.

A useful distinction is between light as an energy source and light as an informational stimulus. In a chloroplast, photosynthetic pigments absorb light and transfer excitation energy to reaction centers, where charge separation initiates electron transport. In a photoreceptor, absorption changes the state of a sensory molecule; the altered molecule then changes interactions, enzyme activity, localization or the stability of downstream regulators. The resulting change can be much larger than the energy of the absorbed photon because the cell supplies ATP, metabolites and the machinery needed for the subsequent response. One photon does not supply the energy required to synthesize every protein induced by light. Rather, the photon helps determine whether existing cellular resources will be directed toward those processes.

Plants experience large differences in the spectrum, direction, intensity and duration of illumination. A canopy of neighboring plants removes a substantial fraction of the red and blue light from sunlight, while far-red radiation is relatively enriched in transmitted and reflected light. A seedling beneath the canopy can therefore receive a different spectral message from a seedling exposed to open sunlight, even if neither seedling has a precise measure of distance from its neighbor. Direction is another kind of information. Illumination arriving predominantly from one side can initiate unequal growth across a shoot. Neither phenomenon can be explained simply by saying that light makes plants grow. In one circumstance it inhibits longitudinal growth; in another it promotes extension or redistributes it between opposite sides of an organ.

A photoreceptor is a molecule that converts the absorption of light into a biologically usable signal. A typical plant photoreceptor comprises a protein and a light-absorbing chemical group called a chromophore. The protein provides the molecular environment that determines the chromophore's behavior, and the chromophore provides an efficient route for converting particular wavelengths into a change of molecular state. The protein portion without its required chromophore is called an apoprotein. A protein assembled with the chromophore is a holoprotein. The synthesis of a receptor polypeptide therefore does not guarantee production of a photosensory receptor. A mutation affecting the metabolic pathway that makes the chromophore may leave the protein present but substantially impair its ability to respond to light.

The three receptor families central to this chapter illustrate distinct chemical solutions to this problem. Phytochromes contain a linear tetrapyrrole and detect red and far-red radiation particularly effectively. Cryptochromes are flavoproteins that participate in blue-light responses such as regulation of seedling development and gene expression. Phototropins are flavin-binding protein kinases that connect blue-light absorption to phosphorylation and several directional or rapid physiological responses. These families should be identified from the receptor's molecular features rather than from a single visible phenotype. Different receptors can inhibit elongation of a seedling hypocotyl, for instance, while detecting different parts of the spectrum or engaging different immediate partners.

A signaling pathway is normally described by separating reception, transduction and response. In a light-sensing cell, reception is the photochemical event that changes the receptor. Transduction includes the events that relay this change to other molecules, such as altered binding of a transcription factor or phosphorylation of a protein. Response denotes the measurable outcome at a specified biological level. An investigator might define the response as phosphorylation measured after a short exposure, messenger RNA accumulation measured later, or hypocotyl length measured after several days. These are not interchangeable observations. A mutation may eliminate a growth response while leaving early phosphorylation intact, thereby separating the function of an upstream sensor from later execution of the response.

Because plants are multicellular, receptor activation and the eventual response need not occur in precisely the same cells. A light-sensitive region may produce a mobile chemical or modify the transport of a developmental regulator. The receiving tissue can then change its growth without itself being the principal site at which the directional stimulus was detected. An experiment that illuminates an entire organ and records its final shape does not, by itself, identify the cell type in which perception occurred. Local illumination, tissue-specific complementation and measurements of early molecular responses provide complementary information. For this chapter, the central objective is to follow the transmission of information from light to an intracellular response, rather than to enumerate every organ-level consequence.

An important property of a photoreceptor is that its state can change again. If activation persisted indefinitely after a single absorption event, the cell would have difficulty distinguishing an ongoing light environment from a past exposure. Some receptors return to their resting conformation spontaneously in darkness. Some are converted to another state by a second wavelength. Others are removed through regulated protein turnover, while additional receptor molecules are synthesized. These alternatives are biologically distinct forms of resetting a sensory system. A persistent developmental response after light removal can also reflect transcription or growth that has already taken place, even when the receptor itself has returned to an inactive state. Thus sensory memory and persistence of an outcome must be considered at the appropriate molecular level.

2. Wavelength, photon fluence and biological spectra

Light consists of electromagnetic radiation. The wavelength, represented by the Greek letter lambda, is the distance between successive points of the wave with the same phase. For plant photobiology it is commonly expressed in nanometers, where one nanometer equals one billionth of a meter. The frequency of a wave in vacuum is its speed divided by its wavelength. A shorter wavelength therefore has a higher frequency. The energy of an individual photon is the product of Planck's constant and the frequency, giving the relation E = hc/λ. Here h is Planck's constant, c is the speed of light in vacuum and E is energy per photon in joules. Red light near 660 nm and far-red light near 730 nm therefore differ in both wavelength and energy, although both can drive changes in phytochrome.

The fact that blue photons carry more energy than red photons does not imply that blue light activates every plant receptor more effectively. A photon must be absorbed before its energy can initiate the relevant photochemistry. A chromophore has permitted electronic transitions whose absorption probabilities vary with wavelength. Photoreceptor specificity is consequently determined by the combination of photon availability, the absorption properties of the chromophore and the probability that absorption produces the signaling state. Extra photon energy may be dissipated without increasing the desired response. An examination question that ranks the biological effectiveness of wavelengths solely from E = hc/λ is therefore missing at least one essential variable.

In a biological light experiment, irradiance often refers to radiant power received per unit area, expressed in watts per square meter. For a photochemical comparison it is often preferable to use photon flux density, expressed in micromoles of photons per square meter per second. Equal radiant power at two wavelengths does not correspond to equal numbers of photons because each photon carries a wavelength-dependent amount of energy. Using the Avogadro constant N_A, the energy of a mole of monochromatic photons is N_Ahc/λ. Dividing the energy arriving per unit area per second by this quantity gives the molar photon flux density. The result may then be converted to micromoles per square meter per second by multiplying by one million.

Fluence is the time integral of photon flux density. Under constant illumination the photon fluence equals photon flux density multiplied by the exposure duration. For example, a pulse delivering 2 micromoles per square meter per second for 30 seconds supplies 60 micromoles per square meter. Delivering 6 micromoles per square meter per second for 10 seconds supplies the same total number of photons per unit area. This equality does not ensure identical responses in a living organism. Receptor relaxation, competing reactions and downstream signaling can proceed during the exposure. A response obeys reciprocity over a specified range when these different combinations of flux and duration yield the same biological outcome at the same total fluence. Departure from reciprocity is information about the kinetics of the system, not an error in the arithmetic.

An absorption spectrum describes how efficiently a substance absorbs radiation across wavelengths. It can be obtained from a purified chromoprotein or from a preparation containing the pigment, with suitable correction for other absorbing components. An action spectrum describes how effectively wavelengths produce a selected biological response. The investigator defines a response criterion and compares the photon requirements across the spectrum, or measures response under carefully standardized photon exposures. A peak in an action spectrum indicates wavelengths that efficiently evoke that response under the conditions of the measurement. It is not automatically a direct measurement of the concentration of a receptor.

Absorption and action spectra can provide evidence for identifying the receptor that mediates a response. If a purified receptor absorbs blue light and a biological response is sensitive to blue light, the match provides a plausible candidate. However, a tissue contains multiple pigments with overlapping spectra. The action spectrum also depends on light transmission into the tissue, receptor abundance, the efficiency of photochemistry and the downstream dose-response relationship. Two receptors with similar absorption spectra may produce distinct action spectra because one requires sustained activation and the other responds to short pulses. Conversely, a response may have two spectral peaks because more than one receptor family can initiate it. The strongest conclusion requires additional evidence such as a receptor-deficient mutant and restoration of the response by a functional receptor.

To interpret a wavelength experiment, consider exactly what was held constant. If blue and red treatments differ in photon flux, a difference in growth cannot be attributed solely to wavelength. If lamps generate different temperatures, thermal effects can be mistaken for sensory effects. Filters must be characterized because nominally red light may contain other wavelengths. Seed age, genotype, hydration and the time at which growth is scored must be comparable. A dark control establishes the baseline without the intended optical stimulus, but even this control requires careful handling: exposure during transfer or observation may itself provide a biologically significant light dose. These precautions are part of scientific reasoning, not merely technical details.

For phytochrome, an especially useful environmental variable is the red-to-far-red photon ratio. The ratio is normally calculated from defined red and far-red wavelength bands; it is not a universal property of the visual color of a lamp. A decrease can indicate that neighboring leaves have altered the spectrum. The ratio alone cannot determine an exact molecular state in all environments because phytochrome absorbs across overlapping wavelength ranges, and temperature and irradiation history influence the outcome. Within a simplified comparison of red and far-red light under matched conditions, however, a lower ratio generally shifts the phyB population toward the less active red-absorbing state. That relationship provides a mechanistic link between an environmental measurement and downstream growth signaling.

3. The experiments that revealed red and far-red photoreversibility

The existence of a reversible photochemical switch in plants was inferred from biological responses before all of the participating proteins were known. A classical demonstration was reported in 1952 by H. A. Borthwick, S. B. Hendricks and colleagues in their paper on light-controlled seed germination [1]. The investigators studied a light-sensitive lettuce seed system. Germination was promoted by red radiation and the effect could be reversed by a subsequent exposure to far-red radiation. This observation challenged an interpretation in which red light simply contributed an irreversible amount of energy to germination. The order of exposure mattered, which suggested that light was altering a molecular state that could be switched in either direction.

The experiment is best understood through the distinction between a sufficient stimulus and an accumulated energy dose. Suppose a hydrated seed is initially in a condition in which germination is low in darkness. A red pulse raises the probability that it will germinate. If a far-red pulse delivered immediately afterward reduces that probability, then a later photon exposure has changed the effect of the earlier exposure. Repeating the red and far-red treatments can alternately promote and suppress the response. For the classical low-fluence, photoreversible response, the final effective exposure often determines the direction of the result when the pulses are adequate and no irreversible downstream commitment has intervened.

The phrase 'last light wins' is a shorthand for this experimental pattern, not a universal law about all plants. It presumes an appropriate receptor system, a compatible biological response, a suitable developmental state, adequate doses and intervals short enough that the subsequent light treatment can still affect the relevant decision. If germination proceeds past a point at which the downstream processes no longer depend on the original photoreceptor state, changing that state may no longer reverse the outcome. A visible root cannot be made to disappear merely by a far-red flash. The receptor can be photoreversible even when the completed developmental event is not.

A carefully controlled reconstruction of the reasoning uses multiple matched batches of seeds. One batch remains dark, a second receives red light, a third receives far-red light alone, a fourth receives red followed by far-red and a fifth receives red, far-red and red in sequence. The use of comparable seed batches is important because germination depends on seed viability and physiological history. The duration and temperature after treatment must be matched, and the scoring time should be defined in advance. A difference between the red and red-followed-by-far-red groups is especially informative because both received the initial red stimulus. If the dark group already germinates extensively, the experimental conditions may not offer a sensitive baseline for detecting promotion.

The following results are a hypothetical teaching dataset rather than measurements transcribed from the original experiment: 12 of 100 dark-treated seeds germinate, compared with 86 of 100 red-treated seeds, 15 of 100 red-then-far-red-treated seeds and 82 of 100 red-then-far-red-then-red-treated seeds. The germination percentages are 12%, 86%, 15% and 82%, respectively. Addition of far-red reduces the response by 71 percentage points relative to red alone, while an additional red pulse restores most of it. The repeated reversal is inconsistent with a simple model in which every absorbed photon independently increases the probability of germination. It is compatible with a photochemical interconversion that changes the abundance of a biologically effective form.

The same observations do not establish the detailed molecular identity of the pigment, its chromophore, the genes encoding it or every intermediate that connects it to germination. A behavioral experiment may reveal that two forms of a photoreceptor are plausible, but purification, spectroscopic measurements and genetic analysis are required to establish the biochemical mechanism. The historical development of phytochrome research illustrates how different kinds of evidence answer different questions: response measurements reveal a property of the system, spectroscopy characterizes the pigment, and mutant studies identify which molecular components contribute to the response.

A particularly instructive follow-up experiment would measure the response when the second exposure is delayed. Under the simplest interpretation, an immediate far-red pulse reduces the signaling-active population of phytochrome before the cellular response has become committed. If the same far-red pulse is applied much later, the inhibition of germination could be weaker even if the photoconversion itself occurs normally. This pattern suggests that events downstream of reception acquire some independence from the receptor's instantaneous state. To distinguish a failure of photoconversion from a failure of biological reversibility, one would measure the receptor-state change as well as the final phenotype. These two measurements are complementary rather than redundant.

Another follow-up uses wavelength rather than timing as the experimental variable. Instead of assuming that the red and far-red bands are sharply separated, the experiment compares several narrow wavelength treatments. The effectiveness of each treatment depends on the absorption properties of the relevant states and the photochemical yields of the transitions. The resulting spectral response may have broad peaks instead of abrupt borders. A student should therefore interpret the conventional values of approximately 660 and 730 nm as representative maxima for plant phytochrome states, not as uniquely effective wavelengths beyond which signaling is impossible.

4. Molecular organization and photochemistry of phytochrome

Phytochrome is a chromoprotein, meaning that the light-absorbing group is associated with a protein that participates in determining its properties. In land plants the chromophore is phytochromobilin, a linear tetrapyrrole derived from heme metabolism. Heme oxygenase opens the heme ring to form biliverdin, and phytochromobilin synthase produces phytochromobilin. The chromophore becomes covalently attached to a conserved cysteine in the photosensory region of plant phytochrome. Formation of the chromoprotein links metabolism to signaling: the receptor gene supplies the polypeptide, while the biosynthetic pathway supplies a chemical group that enables appropriate light perception.

The chromophore contains a series of conjugated chemical bonds. Conjugation permits electronic excitation by visible radiation. When light is absorbed, the chromophore undergoes a change involving isomerization around the bond between the C and D rings of the tetrapyrrole. That local photochemical event is coupled to conformational changes in the protein. The protein's structural response is essential because the remainder of the signaling pathway does not directly observe a double bond in a chromophore. Instead, it responds to altered accessibility, interactions or activity of the photoreceptor. This conversion of a local chemical event into a protein-level functional change is an example of allosteric coupling.

Typical higher-plant phytochromes form dimers. Each polypeptide has an amino-terminal photosensory region containing PAS, GAF and PHY domains, and a carboxyl-terminal region involved in dimerization and signaling regulation. The chromophore is associated with the GAF region of the photosensory module, but the surrounding domains contribute to how the photochemical change is transmitted. The simple teaching model of a dimer should not be taken to imply that both subunits always have identical states or that all members of the family act in precisely the same manner. The central inference for examination purposes is that a photosensory region and an output region cooperate: retaining a chromophore alone is insufficient if the conformational signal cannot reach the downstream signaling interface.

The two classical spectral forms are named Pr and Pfr. Pr is the form that absorbs red light relatively strongly, with a maximum near 660 nm in standard textbook examples. Absorption of red light by Pr promotes conversion toward Pfr. Pfr absorbs far-red light strongly, with a maximum near 730 nm, and absorption of far-red light promotes reconversion toward Pr. The names identify the forms by the wavelengths they preferentially absorb, not by the wavelengths that produce them. A common error is to say that red light creates Pr because of the letter r. In the standard cycle the dominant direction is the opposite: red light converts Pr into Pfr, while far-red light converts Pfr into Pr.

For many classical phytochrome-mediated responses, especially those associated with phyB, Pfr is the signaling-active state. This convention is extremely useful if it is kept within its biological domain. It does not mean that all functions of phytochromes can be predicted from a single global concentration of Pfr, or that far-red radiation cannot promote any response. phyA, for example, can mediate distinct responses under very weak or sustained far-red illumination. The output depends on the receptor family member, the light regime and the kinetics of the signaling network. An accurate molecular explanation therefore identifies the relevant receptor before generalizing from the standard Pr/Pfr diagram.

The absorption spectra of Pr and Pfr overlap. At a red wavelength that strongly favors formation of Pfr, some Pfr molecules may nevertheless absorb photons and return toward Pr. Likewise, far-red light can leave a small Pfr population because Pr is not entirely insensitive to far-red radiation. Consequently, a prolonged monochromatic treatment generally establishes a photostationary mixture rather than converting every receptor molecule into a single form. The limiting mixture depends on the relative rates of the two light-driven transitions and, when appreciable, on nonphotochemical reactions. A diagram showing a single arrow in each direction conveys the dominant conversion, but the underlying population behavior is more nuanced.

Even in the absence of illumination, certain active phytochrome molecules can revert thermally toward Pr. This dark reversion is a chemical relaxation process and differs from photoconversion induced by far-red light. It also differs from receptor degradation because the protein remains available for another photochemical cycle. Dark reversion helps the plant distinguish a recent light exposure from sustained activation. Its rate can vary with receptor identity, temperature and associated molecular interactions. In addition, the active state of some phytochromes can be subject to regulated protein degradation. Both routes reduce signaling-active receptor abundance, but they affect total receptor abundance differently.

A receptor mutant illustrates why these distinctions matter. Consider a phytochrome whose chromophore attaches correctly and whose absorption spectrum looks normal, but whose output region cannot interact with a particular transcriptional regulator. Such a mutant may undergo Pr-to-Pfr photoconversion without producing the normal gene expression change. By contrast, a chromophore-deficient mutant may fail to photoconvert even if its output region is otherwise functional. An assay of receptor abundance alone cannot distinguish these possibilities. A sequence of measurements of protein abundance, absorption changes, partner binding and transcriptional response narrows down the location of the defect.

Phytochromes have evolutionary relationships with sensory proteins found in bacteria and other organisms. Certain bacterial phytochromes have output modules connected to histidine kinase signaling, which provides a useful comparison with the preceding discussion of two-component systems. It is nevertheless incorrect to infer that plant phytochrome signaling must proceed through an obligatory bacterial-style histidine-to-aspartate phosphorelay. Plant phytochromes share structural ancestry with these sensory systems but use distinct output mechanisms, including interactions with transcriptional regulators and control of their stability or activity. Evolutionary homology does not require preservation of every downstream reaction.
