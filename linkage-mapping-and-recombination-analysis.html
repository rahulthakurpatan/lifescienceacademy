From inheritance to a map

A genetic map answers a question that cannot be settled simply by observing which traits an organism possesses: in what order are the corresponding loci arranged along a chromosome, and how readily are their inherited combinations separated by meiosis? Classical geneticists faced this problem before they could read a DNA sequence. They could distinguish alternative forms of a trait, perform controlled crosses and count descendants, but they could not locate individual genes on a microscope image. The crucial insight was that transmission itself contains information about chromosome organization. If two distinguishable loci reside on the same chromosome, an exchange between their positions can separate alleles that previously travelled together. The frequency of that separation provides a quantitative measure of the interval.

A map based on inheritance is therefore not a photograph of DNA. It is an inference from observations collected across many meiotic products. A physical map describes the order and separation of features in base pairs. A linkage map describes their order and separation in units based on recombination. Both maps refer to the same underlying chromosome, but the scales can vary relative to each other. A short segment of DNA that frequently participates in exchange can occupy a relatively large genetic interval, whereas a long segment with suppressed exchange can occupy a small interval. A student should keep these two types of measurement distinct from the very beginning.

The preceding study of chromosomal linkage introduced the idea that parental combinations can be more common than recombinant combinations. The present problem is the inverse one: given a collection of descendants whose allele combinations are known, reconstruct the chromosome arrangement and estimate the separations between markers. This inversion changes the intellectual task from predicting offspring to interpreting evidence. A testcross is particularly useful because the observed offspring allow the investigator to reconstruct the alleles carried by a single gamete of the heterozygous parent. Three-point analysis extends the same logic to two neighboring intervals and makes some otherwise hidden exchanges detectable.

The historical development of genetic mapping is illustrated by Alfred Sturtevant's 1913 investigation of six sex-linked factors in Drosophila. Rather than treating the observed degree of association as an isolated feature of each pair, he interpreted different pairwise relationships as evidence for an ordered chromosome. A locus that was tightly associated with one marker could be less tightly associated with another, and a series of such comparisons could reveal a linear arrangement. The important achievement was the reasoning method, not merely the production of a particular diagram: a set of hereditary observations could be explained by one underlying spatial model. Modern linkage studies use molecular genotypes rather than exclusively visible traits, yet retain this principle of inferring order from the transmission of alleles.

Genetic mapping is valuable even when genome sequences are available. If a previously uncharacterized phenotype segregates in a family or experimental population, researchers can ask which known markers tend to be inherited with the phenotype. This identifies a region worthy of further study. It does not establish that the marker itself causes the phenotype. A marker may lie near a causal variant without having any functional effect. Linkage mapping localizes an inherited factor through transmission; demonstrating its molecular function is a separate scientific question.

Experimental evidence for exchange and linear order

The inference of a genetic map rests on two related propositions. The first is that loci lie in an ordered arrangement along chromosomes. The second is that a change in the association of alleles can be caused by a physical exchange between homologous chromatids. The first proposition can be investigated statistically by comparing segregation patterns, whereas the second can be investigated by observing chromosome structure independently of phenotype. Distinguishing the two questions helps explain why the classical literature contains both genetic mapping experiments and cytological studies.

Sturtevant's work with sex-linked factors used the fact that inheritance of X-linked alleles could be followed in the progeny of appropriate crosses. A female carrying two distinguishable X chromosomes could produce eggs with parental or recombinant arrangements. A male descendant receiving such an X chromosome from his mother would reveal many recessive X-linked traits because the corresponding Y chromosome does not normally carry the same alleles at those loci. Thus sex linkage provided a natural means of reading the maternal chromosome. An alternative design could use appropriate tester genotypes to read autosomal markers in the same general manner.

The observed associations were not expected to be identical for every pair. If one marker lay between two others, crossing over in either adjacent interval could separate the outside markers. Very short intervals provided the cleanest distance comparisons because repeated exchange was unlikely. The inference was strongest when relationships among several pairs could be reconciled with a consistent order. If A and B were very tightly linked and B and C were moderately linked, a map could place B between A and C if the outside pair had the appropriately larger separation. A rival model putting A between B and C would imply different relationships. This strategy converted a qualitative observation of association into a testable geometrical hypothesis.

A genetic map derived in this way did not by itself demonstrate that visible pieces of chromosome were exchanged. In 1931, Harriet Creighton and Barbara McClintock addressed this issue in maize by using a chromosome pair with distinguishable cytological features and genetically distinguishable factors. One chromosome could be differentiated from its homolog through visible structural differences. This was experimentally important because ordinary homologs with the same gross morphology would not reveal which chromosome segment had moved from one to the other. By coupling cytological landmarks with inherited markers, the investigators could compare two independent kinds of information in offspring.

The prediction of physical crossing over was that the inheritance of recombinant combinations of genetic factors would be accompanied by the corresponding rearrangement of the distinguishable chromosome landmarks. In the alternative interpretation, genetic factors could change associations without detectable exchange of the chromosome regions that carried them. The observed relationship between genetic and cytological recombination supported physical segment exchange. Curt Stern's contemporaneous work with Drosophila supplied independent evidence in another experimental organism. These historical experiments should not be confused with experiments identifying the biochemical enzymes of recombination, which belong to the molecular study of DNA repair and exchange.

Consider a schematic chromosome pair where one homolog carries a visible structure at its left end and another distinguishable structure at its right end, while the second homolog has neither. Suppose that between the landmarks lie two informative genes in coupling phase. An exchange between the genes can produce a chromatid with the left landmark but without the right one, and another with the converse arrangement. If the same products carry the predicted new combinations of gene alleles, the cytological observation and the genetic observation support the same exchange model. The schematic does not reproduce the full experimental chromosome construction or original data; it illustrates the logical relation between the two readouts.

The design is powerful because the investigator is not merely using one phenotype to infer another. The chromosome observation is an independent measurement. Nevertheless, the interpretation depends on reliable identification of the chromosome features and markers. A cytological difference that was itself misclassified would undermine the comparison. Likewise, if a recombinant phenotype had poor penetrance, the genetic classification would become less reliable. The general experimental lesson is that different measurements should be used to test complementary predictions of a mechanism.

Modern DNA markers provide an additional way to track parental ancestry along chromosomes. A dense series of informative variants can show where a transmitted haplotype changes from one homolog to the other. This reduces reliance on visible traits and reveals smaller regions of exchange. It does not make classical mapping reasoning obsolete: an ancestry switch is still inferred by comparing marker states, and the precision of breakpoint localization depends on the spacing of informative markers. A pair of adjacent markers of different ancestry brackets an exchange somewhere between them but does not specify its exact nucleotide coordinate. That distinction is a useful preparation for molecular marker mapping in a later chapter.

Allelic phase and the informative cross

To describe linkage clearly, distinguish a locus, an allele and a haplotype. A locus is a position on a chromosome, an allele is a particular version of the sequence or genetic factor at that position, and a haplotype is an ordered combination of alleles carried together on one homolog. A diploid organism heterozygous at two loci may be written AaBb, but this notation alone does not identify which alleles occupy the same homolog. The alternatives AB/ab and Ab/aB represent the same unphased genotype yet produce different expectations for the frequencies of particular gametes.

The first arrangement is conventionally called coupling or cis phase when both uppercase alleles are together. The second is called repulsion or trans phase. These words describe an arrangement, not a physical force of attraction or repulsion. Uppercase letters commonly identify dominant alleles in examination problems, but dominance is not what defines parental phase. Even if both markers are codominant DNA variants, one can specify the same haplotypes. Furthermore, an allele received from a parent can be dominant or recessive independently of whether its combination is parental or recombinant in the cross being studied.

A useful way to construct a known-phase double heterozygote is to cross two homozygous parents. A cross between AABB and aabb gives an F1 carrying AB/ab; a cross between AAbb and aaBB gives an F1 carrying Ab/aB. The gametes of the two original homozygotes identify the homologs directly. If the F1 is crossed with a suitable tester carrying ab/ab, every tester gamete carries ab. Consequently, observing AaBb offspring identifies an AB gamete of the F1, while Aabb identifies Ab, aaBb identifies aB and aabb identifies ab. This is the reason a recessive tester is so useful when dominance prevents the direct discrimination of certain genotypes in phenotypic scoring.

By contrast, selfing or intercrossing two double heterozygotes combines two independently variable gametes in each offspring. Even if all offspring genotypes are available, some genotypes can be produced by more than one pair of gametes. The simple ratio of visibly recombinant phenotypes to all offspring is then generally not a valid estimate of the recombination fraction. Linkage can still be estimated in such populations, but the model must account for both parents' gamete distributions and the ambiguity of phenotype. In classical testcross exercises, one parent supplies the variation and the other supplies a constant reference, allowing direct counting.

Suppose that the recombination fraction between A and B is r and that reciprocal gamete classes have equal recovery. In coupling phase, AB and ab each have probability (1 − r)/2, while Ab and aB each have probability r/2. In repulsion phase these assignments are reversed. The two alleles at an individual locus still segregate equally in this model. For instance, the total probability of A in the gametes is (1 − r)/2 + r/2 = 1/2. Linkage changes the joint distribution of alleles at different positions without requiring failure of segregation at either individual locus.

The equality of reciprocal classes is a biological and statistical expectation, not a rule that every finite dataset must satisfy exactly. Sampling variation produces small differences even when segregation is unbiased. More severe asymmetry may indicate genotype-dependent survival, scoring errors, meiotic drive, or a difference in the recovery of gametes. Thus a cross with known phase provides an important control: one should not silently relabel the largest observed class as parental if the breeding history establishes a different arrangement. Uncertain phase and distorted recovery require more careful interpretation.

An informative marker must be distinguishable in the descendants. A homozygous marker in the mapping parent cannot reveal which homolog contributed that particular allele. A dominant phenotype can be informative in an appropriately chosen testcross, whereas a codominant molecular marker may permit identification of both alleles directly. The choice of markers and tester is therefore part of the inference, not a procedural detail. The counts only support mapping if they genuinely distinguish the possible gametes of the segregating parent.

Reading meiosis at the level of individual chromatids

A homologous chromosome pair contains two chromosomes, each bearing corresponding loci. Before meiotic prophase I, DNA replication produces two sister chromatids per chromosome. The paired structure therefore contains four chromatids. An ordinary reciprocal exchange takes place between nonsister chromatids. Each participant acquires a segment formerly associated with the other homolog, while the two nonparticipating chromatids retain their original arrangements. If no additional exchange takes place between the same two markers, two of the four meiotic products are recombinant at those markers and two are parental.

This immediately explains a subtle distinction between the fraction of bivalents with an exchange and the fraction of gametes recombinant for the interval. Imagine 100 meioses in which precisely 40 bivalents have a single exchange between two markers and the remaining 60 have none. Each meiosis produces four chromatids, so 400 products are represented. The 40 exchange-containing bivalents yield 80 recombinant chromatids, corresponding to a recombination fraction of 0.20 if all products are equally represented. The crossover occurrence frequency of 40% is not itself the gametic recombination fraction of 20%.

The same distinction appears when interpreting cytological chiasmata, which are visible manifestations of connections between homologs associated with prior crossing over. A chiasma count for a bivalent is not a direct count of recombinant offspring. To make the connection, one needs to know whether the exchange is within the marker interval, which chromatids participate and whether those chromatids are recovered. Genetic mapping usually starts from the observed offspring instead of trying to convert a global cytological count directly to a pairwise distance.

Consider two exchanges, one in the A–B interval and another in the B–C interval. If both exchanges involve the same two nonsister chromatids, those two chromatids undergo both ancestry changes; the other two remain parental throughout. For phase ABC/abc the double recombinant chromatids are AbC and aBc. If instead the second exchange involves one chromatid from the first exchange and another chromatid that did not participate in it, three chromatids are involved in total. One of the four products shows both interval ancestry changes, one shows only the first interval change, one shows only the second and one shows neither. If the two exchanges involve disjoint pairs of nonsister chromatids, four chromatids participate; two products are recombinant only in the first interval and two only in the second.

These three patterns are often called two-strand, three-strand and four-strand double crossovers. The names count chromatids involved rather than DNA strands within one double helix. This is an important terminology distinction. A replicated chromosome contains two double-stranded DNA molecules, one in each sister chromatid. The word strand in the classical crossover classification refers to a chromatid in the bivalent, not one of the two complementary nucleotide chains.

Under random chromatid participation, given the pair used in the first crossover, the second crossover can involve the same pair in one of four equally likely choices, share one chromatid in two choices, or involve the other pair in one choice. Consequently the conventional expectation is a 1:2:1 ratio of two-, three- and four-strand events, conditional on two exchanges in the relevant intervals and the stated random-choice assumptions. This is a model of chromatid participation; it is not a statement that the probability of having two crossovers is one quarter. The probability of having two exchanges and the conditional choice of chromatids are separate quantities.

If all four products of a meiosis are recovered, their joint pattern can discriminate among these possibilities more directly. Ordinary testcross data usually provide one gamete from each of many meioses, rather than a complete group of four related products. As a result, a double recombinant offspring identifies both interval switches along the transmitted ancestry but cannot by itself identify the complete chromatid configuration in its originating bivalent. The interpretation of complete meiotic product sets belongs to tetrad analysis, the next topic.

The chromosome diagram should always be read along a path from left to right. Markers are fixed positions in that drawing; their allelic labels may change with ancestry. A crossover does not normally change the linear order of loci in a structurally normal homologous pair. It exchanges corresponding chromosome segments. Therefore a recombinant haplotype can carry A from one homolog and b from the other without the physical positions of A and B being reversed. Chromosomal inversions or other rearrangements are different events and require their own structural models.

This perspective also clarifies why a recombination fraction is not the same as the fraction of DNA inherited from a grandparent. Recombination classes describe the relative ancestry of selected endpoints, and these endpoints may be separated by a long stretch of DNA. A chromatid with parental endpoint alleles might nevertheless carry an internal segment from the other homolog if two ancestry switches occurred. Dense markers can identify that internal segment even though the two-marker classification cannot. Thus marker data report ancestry at measured positions, not a complete description of every nucleotide between them.

Two-point mapping and the meaning of centimorgans

In a two-point testcross, first classify the offspring into the two parental and two recombinant gamete classes according to phase. Let N denote the number of informative progeny and R the total number of recombinant progeny. The estimated recombination fraction is r = R/N. Multiplication by 100 expresses the result as a percentage. For sufficiently short intervals, 100r is also a useful approximate map distance in centimorgans. The approximation is important: a recombination percentage is an observed proportion of gametes, while a map distance represents the expected amount of exchange along the interval under a specified mapping model.

Consider an illustrative cross with known phase AB/ab yielding 420 AB, 430 ab, 80 Ab and 70 aB offspring. The parental total is 850 and the recombinant total is 150. The denominator is 1,000, including parental offspring. Consequently r = 150/1,000 = 0.15. The uncorrected two-point estimate is 15 cM. Each recombinant class need not contain exactly 75 offspring, and the small observed imbalance does not on its own overturn the mapping interpretation. If another problem provides Ab/aB as the known phase, the same numerical counts associated with the same four haplotypes cannot be interpreted in this way. Class identity is established before arithmetic.

A genetic distance is expressed in morgans (M) or centimorgans (cM), with one morgan equal to 100 centimorgans. For a very short interval, 1 cM corresponds approximately to 1% recombinant gametes. It is not a fixed number of base pairs. The estimate becomes progressively less reliable as multiple exchange events within the same interval become more frequent. It is therefore incorrect to suppose that a chromosome cannot have a map length greater than 50 cM simply because no ordinary two-point recombination fraction exceeds one half.

The source of the ceiling is the inability of two endpoints to reveal all exchange histories. If a particular transmitted chromatid switches homologal ancestry once between A and B, its endpoint combination is recombinant. If it switches twice, its original endpoint association is restored. Three switches again yield a recombinant association. An odd number of ancestry switches produces a detectable recombinant, while an even number, including zero, does not. This statement concerns switches along the transmitted chromatid, not the total number of exchanges anywhere in the entire four-chromatid bivalent. A double crossover in a bivalent can involve two, three or four chromatids and need not restore the endpoint combination on every chromatid.

In a small interval, many chromatids have no switch, some have one switch and very few have more. Thus the fraction with an odd number of switches is nearly equal to the mean number of switches. In a larger interval, even and multiple odd classes become appreciable. The endpoint classes lose information about exchange history, so r no longer increases linearly with map distance. When phase information at the endpoints becomes effectively randomized, half the gametes are recombinant and half are parental. Loci on different chromosomes can yield this same pattern through independent assortment. Hence an estimate near one half is not sufficient evidence that two loci must reside on separate chromosomes.

A linkage group can be extended by intermediate markers. If A is detectably linked to B, and B is detectably linked to C, evidence of a continuous chromosomal relationship may exist even if A and C show nearly independent segregation. The use of multiple markers avoids depending entirely on a saturated endpoint measurement. It also improves spatial resolution, because exchange events can be assigned to a narrower interval when several distinguishing sites are present.

The sample size constrains the precision of any such inference. With independent informative progeny and unbiased recovery, the number of recombinant gametes may be modeled as a binomial variable. For a fixed true recombination fraction r, its variance is Nr(1 − r); the approximate standard error of the estimated fraction is the square root of r(1 − r)/N. For the illustrative estimate 0.15 with N = 1,000, this is approximately 0.0113, or 1.13 percentage points. This is sampling uncertainty, not a correction for multiple crossovers. Increasing the sample size improves precision but cannot recover an exchange history that the marker configuration is intrinsically unable to distinguish.

Three-point mapping and the middle marker

A three-point testcross extends the two-point strategy by introducing one additional informative locus. A heterozygous parent has two complementary haplotypes at A, B and C, and a homozygous tester makes it possible to identify the haplotype transmitted to each offspring. There are eight possible haplotypes when the loci are biallelic. Under the usual short-interval teaching model, these group into four reciprocal pairs: parental products, single recombinants in the first interval, single recombinants in the second interval and double recombinants involving both intervals. Each pair has the same pattern of ancestry changes but begins with the opposite homolog.

For clarity, assume the actual order is A–B–C and the parental haplotypes are ABC and abc. A single switch between A and B can generate Abc or aBC. A single switch between B and C can generate ABc or abC. Switching between A and B and again between B and C can generate AbC or aBc. The last pair preserves the parental association of the outside markers while reversing the allele at the middle marker. This provides the key evidence for identifying the middle locus.

In a classical short-interval dataset with unbiased recovery, the most frequent reciprocal pair is usually parental and the least frequent pair usually represents double recombinant products. However, this approach rests on an assumption about the relative probabilities of zero, one and two interval switches. If viability selection strongly reduces the recovery of a parental class, or if particular gamete classes are scored incorrectly, ranking by frequency may be misleading. Known parental phase provides stronger evidence than the rank of an individual class. Moreover, the least frequent pair identifies the double recombinant category only when the experimental design and crossover model support that interpretation.

The order test compares each presumed double recombinant with the parental haplotype that matches it at two of the three positions. If ABC and AbC are compared, only the B allele differs. The corresponding comparison between abc and aBc gives the same result. The middle gene is therefore B, and the outer genes are A and C. The reversed order C–B–A is equivalent because the cross determines relative order, not an absolute orientation with respect to the chromosome's centromere or reference sequence.

Suppose that the observed parentals are ABC = 350 and abc = 350. The remaining illustrative classes are Abc = 70, aBC = 70, ABc = 75, abC = 75, AbC = 5 and aBc = 5. The total is 1,000. The two rarest classes together comprise 10 double recombinant gametes. The comparison with the parental pair identifies B as the middle locus. The first interval contains 140 first-interval-only recombinant gametes and 10 double recombinants, so rAB = 150/1,000 = 0.15. The second interval contains 150 second-interval-only recombinant gametes and the same 10 double recombinants, so rBC = 160/1,000 = 0.16. Approximate interval distances are 15 cM and 16 cM, respectively, under the ordinary classroom short-interval calculation.

The double recombinant pair contributes to both interval numerators because each such transmitted haplotype shows a change of ancestry in both intervals. It contributes only once to the total number of progeny. When scoring A and C alone, however, these same gametes appear parental at the endpoints. The endpoint recombinant count is therefore 140 + 150 = 290, giving rAC = 0.29. The sum of adjacent observed fractions is 0.31, which differs by twice the double recombinant fraction. This apparent discrepancy is an expected consequence of parity, not an inconsistency in the data.

The three-point cross distinguishes the intervals more effectively than two separate endpoint observations. Nonetheless, it does not necessarily detect every exchange. If there are two ancestry switches within the first interval and no switch in the second, the marker haplotype appears parental. If there are three switches within one interval, that interval appears singly recombinant. The categories inferred from marker data describe interval parity. The labels single and double crossover are conventional approximations in short-interval problems where additional undetected switches are unlikely. Longer intervals require more markers or explicit mapping models.

When multiple plausible orders exist, candidate orders can be compared by reconstructing the implied interval categories and assessing whether the resulting exchange pattern is compatible with the data and model. For example, a candidate order that makes a large reciprocal class appear double recombinant in two very short intervals is less plausible than an order that makes a small reciprocal class double recombinant. Yet a rigorous evaluation must also take account of parental phase and any evidence of unequal survival. A map is a model justified by observations rather than a mechanical consequence of placing the least common phenotype in the middle.

A second three-point map: nonalphabetical order and repulsion

The order of gene symbols in a problem often reflects nothing more than notation. To prevent the alphabet from suggesting an answer, consider an illustrative testcross with loci named P, Q and R. The recorded haplotypes are expressed in the fixed reporting order P, Q, R, which need not correspond to their physical arrangement. Suppose the known parental haplotypes are PqR and pQr. These differ at every locus, so they are valid complementary haplotypes of a triple heterozygote. The investigator obtains PqR = 300, pQr = 310, PQR = 10, pqr = 10, Pqr = 105, pQR = 95, PQr = 90 and pqR = 80. The counts sum to 1,000.

The rare reciprocal pair is PQR and pqr. Comparing PQR with PqR shows that only the Q allele has changed. Comparing pqr with pQr gives the same result. Therefore Q is central, and the order is P–Q–R or its reversal. This example illustrates that a parental combination need not contain all uppercase or all lowercase symbols. It also shows why the most reliable procedure is to compare complete haplotypes rather than count uppercase alleles.

Now classify each gamete according to the origin of the alleles. PqR carries all three alleles from the first homolog, while pQr carries all three from the second. Pqr carries P and q from the first homolog but r from the second; its ancestry changes only between Q and R. Its reciprocal pQR changes between the same positions. Together these two classes contribute 200 gametes recombinant only in the right interval. PQr carries P from the first homolog but Q and r from the second, so the switch is between P and Q. Its reciprocal pqR has the opposite ancestry sequence. Together these classes contribute 170 gametes recombinant only in the left interval. PQR and pqr carry the outside-marker ancestry of one homolog but the Q allele of the other; their combined count of 20 contributes to both intervals.

It follows that rPQ = (170 + 20)/1,000 = 0.19 and rQR = (200 + 20)/1,000 = 0.22. The conventional approximate map is P — 19 cM — Q — 22 cM — R, with total interval sum of 41 cM. The outside-pair fraction is not 0.41. The outside pair recognizes the 170 and 200 single-interval classes but not the 20 double recombinant gametes. Its recombinant fraction is 370/1,000 = 0.37. Applying the parity check gives 0.19 + 0.22 − 2(0.02) = 0.37, confirming consistency.

The expected double recombinant count under independence is 1,000 × 0.19 × 0.22 = 41.8. Fractional expected counts are not a problem; an expectation is an average over hypothetical repeated experiments, not an integer count that must occur in one trial. The observed coincidence is 20/41.8 ≈ 0.4785, and the observed interference is 0.5215. A student who rounds 41.8 to 42 before division obtains a slightly different answer, so retaining intermediate precision is preferable.

This dataset also provides an opportunity to compare short-interval estimates with map functions. If the problem explicitly instructs use of the Haldane function for each interval, the estimates are −50 ln(0.62) ≈ 23.90 cM and −50 ln(0.56) ≈ 28.99 cM. These sum to approximately 52.89 cM. The direct Haldane transformation of the observed outside fraction 0.37 gives −50 ln(0.26) ≈ 67.35 cM, which differs from the sum. The reason is not an arithmetic contradiction: the observed joint recombinant fraction is much lower than rPQ rQR, so the data do not satisfy the independent-interval model underlying Haldane additivity. Applying Haldane mechanically to every pair does not guarantee a consistent model when its assumptions fail.

By comparison, the Kosambi transformations of the adjacent fractions are 25 ln(1.38/0.62) ≈ 20.00 cM and 25 ln(1.44/0.56) ≈ 23.61 cM. The resulting sum is approximately 43.61 cM. The outside fraction gives 25 ln(1.74/0.26) ≈ 47.52 cM. Although the discrepancy is smaller in this example, the observed dataset is not guaranteed to follow the specific Kosambi interference model either. These calculations illustrate how distance estimates can be model-dependent. In an examination that requests the ordinary three-point map and interference, the uncorrected interval calculations are normally the intended results unless an explicit map-function model is supplied.

A final useful observation concerns the separate reciprocal class counts. PqR and pQr differ by ten, while Pqr and pQR differ by ten in the other direction. These differences could easily result from sampling. They are not sufficiently large on their own to establish transmission distortion. A severe pattern, such as nearly complete disappearance of every gamete carrying a particular allele, would raise a different concern. The biological validity of a map cannot be assessed solely by checking that the arithmetic works. One must also examine whether class recovery is compatible with the crossing design and whether the underlying model is reasonable.

Coincidence and crossover interference

If recombinant status in two neighboring intervals were statistically independent, the probability that a gamete is recombinant in both would equal the product of the two marginal recombination fractions. Thus the expected number of double recombinant progeny would be N r1 r2. The marginals must include the double recombinant classes: otherwise the expected value is constructed from probabilities for the wrong events. This independence expectation is a useful null model against which the observed number can be compared.

In the illustrative three-point dataset, the first-interval fraction is 0.15 and the second-interval fraction is 0.16. Under independence the expected double recombinant fraction is 0.024, so the expected number among 1,000 progeny is 24. The actual number is 10. The coefficient of coincidence C is observed double recombinants divided by expected double recombinants, giving C = 10/24 = 0.4167. Interference I is 1 − C, or approximately 0.5833. This corresponds to about 58.3% fewer double recombinant products than the independence prediction. It does not mean that 58.3% of all crossover events on the chromosome were prevented.

Positive interference corresponds to coincidence below one: joint interval recombination is less frequent than expected from independent marginal probabilities. Zero interference corresponds to coincidence of one. Negative interference corresponds to coincidence greater than one: joint interval recombination is more frequent than the null prediction. Negative estimates need not automatically demonstrate a biological tendency for crossovers to cluster, because sampling variability and distorted recovery can also produce that result. The inference about mechanism must be distinguished from the descriptive statistic.

A value of complete observed interference, I = 1, arises if no double recombinant progeny are recovered despite a nonzero expectation. The reliability of this estimate depends strongly on the expected count. If the expected count is 0.4, observing zero is quite unsurprising even under independence. If the expected count is 40, zero is much stronger evidence against independence, assuming accurate scoring and comparable recovery. Reporting only the interference value without the numerator, expected value and sample size hides this essential distinction.

Interference is biologically connected to the regulation of meiotic crossover placement. Many organisms show spacing patterns in which the occurrence of a crossover makes an additional nearby crossover less likely. This regulation does not imply a uniform reduction of exchange everywhere on the chromosome. Nor does it mean that every species or every chromosomal region follows the same pattern. In genetic mapping, the coincidence statistic describes joint recombinant recovery over specified intervals; mechanistic conclusions about the bivalent require attention to chromatid participation, exchange placement and the way gametes were sampled.

A particularly useful distinction is between crossover interference and chromatid interference. The former concerns the placement or occurrence of crossover events along a bivalent. The latter concerns whether the chromatids participating in one exchange influence the choice of chromatids for another exchange. A three-point gamete dataset gives incomplete information about the full set of products of a meiosis, so it cannot generally establish both independently. Tetrad analysis, introduced in the next node, can reveal additional information because products from the same meiotic division may be recovered together.

When the expected double recombinant count is zero, coincidence is undefined rather than zero or one. For example, if a short interval has no observed recombinants, the plug-in independence expectation is zero and the ratio is not interpretable. The scientific response is to report that the available observations cannot estimate coincidence for these intervals, rather than divide by zero or declare complete interference.

Sampling, distortion and evidence for linkage

The counts in a testcross are observations of a random biological process. Even when the theoretical probabilities of four classes are exactly equal, a finite experiment rarely yields identical counts. Thus finding one class slightly larger than another is not sufficient proof of linkage. A null model is required. For an ordinary two-point testcross in which both loci segregate equally and independently, the four gamete classes each have probability one quarter. With N progeny, the expected count in each class is N/4.

A chi-square goodness-of-fit statistic can quantify the discrepancy between four observed and expected counts. It is the sum of (observed minus expected) squared divided by expected over the four classes. For a fully specified four-class equal-probability null model, the degrees of freedom are three. This test answers a broad question: are the observed class frequencies consistent with equal recovery of the four categories? It does not uniquely test for linkage, because departures from single-locus segregation or genotype-dependent survival can also produce a significant result.

A more targeted analysis is available when parental phase is known and the observations can be grouped into parental and recombinant products. Under the unlinked model, the parental and recombinant totals are each expected to be N/2. If all other assumptions are satisfied, a binomial comparison of the recombinant count with N and probability one half directly evaluates departure from the expected recombinant fraction. A one-degree-of-freedom chi-square comparison of the two totals is an approximate alternative when expected counts are adequate. The choice of test depends on the precise null hypothesis. Four-class goodness of fit and pooled parental-versus-recombinant comparison do not necessarily yield the same interpretation when individual loci exhibit distortion.

Suppose a known coupling-phase cross produces AB = 265, ab = 255, Ab = 240 and aB = 240. The total is 1,000 and the recombinant fraction is 0.48. The parental and recombinant expected totals under independence are 500 each. The observed totals differ from expectation by plus and minus 20. The pooled chi-square statistic is 400/500 + 400/500 = 1.6, which is not a large departure under a one-degree-of-freedom comparison. This illustrative dataset should not be treated as compelling evidence of linkage merely because the fraction is below 0.5. On the other hand, failure to reject independence does not prove that the markers reside on different chromosomes. A long same-chromosome interval and a genuinely unlinked pair may produce almost identical observed fractions.

For contrast, suppose a second cross produces AB = 330, ab = 330, Ab = 170 and aB = 170. The recombinant fraction is 0.34 and the parental total is 660. The pooled statistic is twice 160 squared divided by 500, giving 102.4. Under the assumed null and recovery conditions this is far larger than expected from ordinary sampling fluctuation. The pattern provides strong evidence of preferential inheritance of the parental associations. Still, a biological interpretation should check that the alleles at individual loci show approximately balanced segregation and that the cross was designed to score the four possible gametes without bias.

One can examine single-locus totals even before applying a formal statistical test. In the second cross, A is represented by AB and Ab, giving 500 observations; a is represented by ab and aB, also 500. The B and b totals are similarly balanced. This pattern is consistent with linkage changing the joint association while preserving individual allele frequencies. If instead A appeared in 800 observations and a in only 200, something beyond the basic unbiased segregation model would be implicated. A significant four-class test in that situation might primarily reflect the imbalance at A rather than ordinary linkage.

A useful way to organize a three-point cross is to collapse its eight haplotypes into a two-by-two table of interval parity states. The rows represent absence or presence of recombination in the first interval, and the columns represent absence or presence in the second. For the first illustrative dataset, the cells are 700 for neither, 150 for the second only, 140 for the first only and 10 for both. The margins are 850 and 150 for the rows and 840 and 160 for the columns. Under independence, the expected joint cells are 714, 136, 126 and 24. This exposes the whole association pattern rather than only the rarest cell.

The resulting chi-square statistic for independence in this two-by-two table is approximately 11.44. The discrepancy is not restricted to the cell containing double recombinants; because the margins are fixed by the observed data, a deficit in that cell is accompanied by compensating changes in other cells. In a formal analysis of small counts, an exact test may be more appropriate than a large-sample approximation. At NET level, the main lesson is that a coincidence value describes the size and direction of a departure, while a statistical assessment addresses the strength of evidence for that departure.

Zero observations also have a sampling interpretation. If no recombinants are observed in N independent progeny, the probability of that outcome under a true fraction r is (1 − r) raised to N. Setting this probability to 0.05 and solving gives an approximate upper one-sided 95% confidence bound r = 1 − 0.05 raised to 1/N. For N = 100, this is about 0.0295. For N = 1,000, it is about 0.0030. These values illustrate why absence of observed recombination in a small experiment cannot establish complete physical linkage. The so-called rule of three approximates the bound as 3/N for a large sample with zero events.

Missing genotypes introduce a related difficulty. If a descendant is typed at A and B but not at C, it may contribute to the two-point A–B analysis while being uninformative for the full three-point classification. A denominator must match the set of individuals that could have been assigned to every relevant category for that analysis. Counting a missing class as a parental gamete would bias the estimated fraction. Conversely, excluding every sample without considering whether it was informative for a particular pair could discard useful information. In textbook problems all progeny are often fully scored, but in real marker data the distinction is essential.

The purpose of such checks is not to transform classical genetics into a full statistics course. It is to prevent a common analytical error: treating a map calculated from any set of numbers as if it were necessarily a reliable map of the chromosome. Genetic inference requires the experiment, observed categories, probability model, and uncertainty to be considered together.

Mapping functions and multiple-exchange correction

A mapping function relates the probability of an observable recombinant haplotype to a model-based genetic distance. Let d be distance in morgans, and let K be the number of ancestry switches along a transmitted chromatid within an interval. In the idealized Haldane model, K follows a Poisson distribution of mean d, representing independent exchange increments without crossover interference and with random chromatid participation. The endpoints identify only whether K is odd or even, so the recombination fraction is the sum of the probabilities of odd counts.

The parity calculation can be derived without memorizing a formula. The expectation of (−1) raised to K equals the probability that K is even minus the probability that K is odd. Because these two probabilities sum to one, the same expectation equals 1 − 2r. The probability-generating function of a Poisson variable with mean d is exp[d(t − 1)]. Substituting t = −1 gives exp(−2d). Equating the two results yields 1 − 2r = exp(−2d), and rearrangement gives the forward Haldane relation r = (1 − exp(−2d))/2. Taking the natural logarithm gives the inverse d = −ln(1 − 2r)/2. The logarithm is natural, not base ten.

The formula is expressed in morgans when d is used in the exponent. Therefore the inverse distance in centimorgans is −50 ln(1 − 2r). For a measured fraction of 0.20, the uncorrected approximation is 20 cM. The Haldane correction is −50 ln(0.60), approximately 25.54 cM. The difference reflects exchanges that are not individually identified by the two endpoints under this model. The corrected distance is not a directly observed number of base pairs and is not universally exact if the independence assumptions are violated.

The Kosambi mapping function provides an alternative relationship incorporating a particular model of interference. In morgans the inverse relation is d = (1/4) ln[(1 + 2r)/(1 − 2r)]. Its forward form is r = tanh(2d)/2. In centimorgans the inverse is 25 ln[(1 + 2r)/(1 − 2r)]. For r = 0.20, the ratio inside the logarithm is 1.40/0.60, or 7/3, and the estimate is approximately 21.18 cM. The Kosambi estimate lies closer to the short-interval approximation than the Haldane estimate in this example. This reflects the different assumptions about the occurrence of nearby exchanges.

It is inappropriate to choose whichever function happens to give a desired map length. The function is part of the analysis model. Haldane is especially useful for understanding parity and independent exchange; Kosambi is a traditional alternative accounting for interference in a specified manner. Neither is an empirical description of every possible species, chromosomal region or cross. An exam problem may explicitly prescribe one of them. Where it does not, a classical three-point short-interval question usually expects direct interval percentages; a question about correction or model-based genetic distance requires the specified transformation.

Both inverse formulas are defined for recombination fractions from zero up to, but not including, one half. As r approaches one half, the denominator 1 − 2r approaches zero and the estimated distance rises without bound. This mathematical behavior expresses the loss of endpoint information at saturation. It does not mean that a chromosome with a measured fraction of one half has infinite physical length. An observed value slightly above one half may occur due to sampling, inappropriate phase assignment, or other departures from the assumed model. Substituting such a value into an inverse mapping function is not meaningful.

Near zero, both Haldane and Kosambi agree to first order with the approximation d ≈ r in morgans. One way to understand this is that the probability of multiple switches becomes small compared with the probability of a single switch. A more formal comparison uses the expansion of the natural logarithm. Haldane gives d = r + r² + higher terms; Kosambi gives d = r + (4/3)r³ + higher terms. Thus the difference from direct conversion appears at second order for Haldane and at third order for Kosambi. The approximations explain why short-distance estimates often agree despite differences in the underlying models.

Deriving the logic of the Kosambi interval relation

The Haldane relation illustrates how an assumed distribution of exchange increments connects endpoint observations to additive distance. The Kosambi relation is based on a different assumption about the dependence of recombination in neighboring intervals. It is useful to see how the dependence enters the calculation, because otherwise the two formulas look like arbitrary logarithmic expressions to memorize.

Consider a marker at the left end of an interval of length d and a second marker at its right end. Let r(d) be the recombination fraction for these endpoints. Extend the map by a very short interval of length delta d. In the short interval the recombinant fraction is approximately delta d when distance is measured in morgans. Let the coefficient of coincidence describing joint recombinant status in the long interval and the new short interval be c. The joint fraction is approximately c times r(d) times delta d. The parity identity gives the recombination fraction for the enlarged interval as the sum of the two marginal fractions minus twice their joint fraction.

Subtracting the original fraction and dividing by the incremental distance gives an approximate rate of change of r with d equal to 1 − 2cr. With c = 1, representing the independent-interval assumption, this becomes dr/dd = 1 − 2r. Solving with r(0) = 0 gives the Haldane relation. Thus one can derive the same result using the incremental interval argument rather than the Poisson parity calculation.

For the Kosambi model the relevant infinitesimal coincidence relation is c = 2r. The differential equation becomes dr/dd = 1 − 4r². Separating variables gives dd = dr/(1 − 4r²). Integration from zero to the observed r yields d = (1/4) ln[(1 + 2r)/(1 − 2r)]. This derivation is especially useful because it makes the assumptions visible. The functional form emerges from a particular dependence model; it is not a universal law specifying that every observed coincidence coefficient in any arbitrary pair of finite intervals must equal twice one of the marginal fractions.

The equivalent forward relation r = tanh(2d)/2 is obtained by exponentiating and rearranging the inverse equation. If two intervals satisfy the same additive Kosambi model, the hyperbolic tangent addition identity gives rAC = (rAB + rBC)/(1 + 4rABrBC). Unlike the Haldane independent-interval rule, this expression predicts a different relationship between the outside fraction and the two adjacent marginal fractions. Comparing the model prediction with measured data is a way to assess whether that mapping model is plausible for the observed cross.

For example, let the adjacent recombinant fractions be 0.10 and 0.20. Independence predicts an outside fraction of 0.10 + 0.20 − 2(0.10)(0.20) = 0.26. The Kosambi model predicts 0.30/(1 + 0.08) ≈ 0.2778. The larger predicted outside fraction follows because the Kosambi model gives fewer double recombinant products than independence in this example. Solving the parity equation for the implied joint fraction gives approximately 0.0111, compared with 0.0200 under independence. Neither numerical prediction is guaranteed to match actual recovery unless the assumed model adequately describes the cross.

One might ask which model predicts more recombinants for the same map distance. At d = 0.5 M, Haldane predicts (1 − exp(−1))/2 ≈ 0.3161, whereas Kosambi predicts tanh(1)/2 ≈ 0.3808. The direction of the difference is consistent with the role of repeated switches. For the same total exchange length, a model that reduces the tendency toward nearby repeated switches can preserve more detectable odd-parity products than a model with independent events. Conversely, at a fixed measured r, the Haldane model often assigns the larger distance.

At examination level, the important steps are to distinguish the forward and inverse functions, use morgans in the mathematical expressions, and recognize that different models imply different assumptions about crossover dependence. If the question supplies a distance and asks for a recombinant probability, the forward relation is required. If it supplies a recombinant fraction and asks for a model-corrected distance, use the inverse relation. Multiplying or dividing by 100 at the wrong stage can produce errors large enough to make the answer biologically nonsensical. The domain of the inverse functions offers a useful check before numerical substitution.

Map additivity, endpoint saturation and biological limits

For three ordered loci A, B and C, a genetic map distance is intended to be additive along the chromosome: dAC = dAB + dBC, when the map measures the same expected exchange process in each interval. Raw recombination fractions are generally not additive. Let X equal one if the first interval is recombinant and zero otherwise, and let Y similarly represent the second interval. The endpoint A–C is recombinant if exactly one of X and Y is one. Consequently rAC = rAB + rBC − 2P(X = 1,Y = 1). This identity follows from the parity of ancestry switches and does not require independence between the intervals.

Under the independent-interval model, the joint term is rAB rBC. In this case 1 − 2rAC equals (1 − 2rAB)(1 − 2rBC). Taking logarithms transforms that product into a sum, giving the Haldane additivity relation. If the intervals are not independent, the observed joint probability must be used instead. This is why measuring double recombinant recovery in a three-marker cross provides additional information not contained in two isolated marginal fractions.

A useful example of saturation is a chromosome segment with four adjacent short intervals, each 20 cM under a Haldane model. Its map length is 80 cM, but the predicted endpoint fraction is (1 − exp(−1.6))/2, approximately 0.399. The outside markers produce about 40% recombinant gametes, even though the additive map length is 80 cM. Adding still more intervals makes the fraction approach 50%. Inferences based on densely spaced informative markers can therefore preserve much more of the map's structure than direct endpoint analysis.

The physical and genetic scales differ because crossover placement is biologically heterogeneous. For instance, consider two illustrative intervals of equal physical length, one producing a recombinant fraction of 0.01 and the other 0.08. In the short-interval approximation their genetic distances are 1 and 8 cM. They occupy the same number of base pairs but differ eightfold in their approximate genetic lengths. This may reflect differences in chromosomal context or crossover placement. Conversely, equally long genetic intervals may encompass very different quantities of DNA.

Sex-specific recombination is another reason that genetic distances depend on the cross. In Drosophila melanogaster, ordinary meiotic crossing over is absent in males. A testcross using a heterozygous male therefore cannot reveal ordinary crossing-over distances in the way a corresponding female cross can. The absence of recombinant classes in such a cross does not prove that the physical interval has zero length. When reading a problem, identify the species, the sex of the informative parent, the markers and the stated assumptions before interpreting the counts.

Other departures from simple mapping expectations can include chromosomal inversions, in which recombinant products may be recovered poorly, or selection acting on particular genotypes before they are scored. Suppressed recovery is not necessarily suppression of the molecular exchange itself. Gene conversion at a marker can also alter an allele without a reciprocal crossover spanning an interval. These possibilities become especially important when class counts are anomalous or when data from different crosses disagree.

Genetic linkage is a relationship observed through transmission in families or crosses. It should not be confused with linkage disequilibrium, a statistical association of alleles in populations that may be influenced by recombination, demographic history and other processes. A strong family linkage signal can motivate the search for a causal variant, but population association and functional causation require distinct evidence. Later Academy chapters address those analytical frameworks separately.

At the conclusion of a mapping problem, state the gene order, the interval distances with their units, the method used to estimate them, and any important assumptions or limitations. Check that the class totals match the stated number of progeny, that double recombinants are included in both interval numerators, and that endpoint recombination obeys the parity identity. Such checks are often more valuable than memorizing an isolated formula because they reveal arithmetic errors and inconsistencies in the biological model.

Practice problems

1. Part B

Which notation identifies the coupling phase?

Answer and explanation

AB/ab Both uppercase alleles share one homolog; AaBb alone does not specify phase.

2. Part B

What is the upper model bound of a two-point recombination fraction?

Answer and explanation

0.5 At saturation, parental and recombinant endpoint combinations are equally probable.

3. Part B

Which unit measures physical rather than genetic distance?

Answer and explanation

Base pairs Centimorgans measure map length inferred from exchange, not DNA sequence length.

4. Part B

Which crossover products are invisible to a two-marker test when considering ancestry switches on the transmitted chromatid?

Answer and explanation

Even numbers of switches Even parity restores the parental endpoint arrangement.

5. Part C

A repulsion-phase heterozygote produces 390 Ab, 410 aB, 90 AB and 110 ab gametes. Estimate r.

Answer and explanation

0.20 AB and ab are recombinant in this phase; (90 + 110)/1,000 = 0.20.

6. Part C

For parentals ABC and abc, the rarest reciprocal pair is ABc and abC under the standard short-interval model. Identify the middle locus.

Answer and explanation

C Only C differs between ABC and ABc, making C the middle marker; A–C–B and B–C–A are equivalent.

7. Part C

For adjacent interval fractions 0.12 and 0.18, with 8 double recombinants in 1,000 progeny, calculate coincidence and interference.

Answer and explanation

0.3704 and 0.6296 Expected count is 1,000 × 0.12 × 0.18 = 21.6; 8/21.6 = 0.3704 and 1 − 0.3704 = 0.6296.

8. Part C

If rAB = 0.15, rBC = 0.16 and joint recombinant fraction is 0.01, calculate rAC.

Answer and explanation

0.29 The parity identity gives 0.15 + 0.16 − 2 × 0.01 = 0.29.

9. Part C

Calculate Haldane map distance when r = 0.10.

Answer and explanation

11.16 cM −50 ln(0.8) = 11.157 cM; the distance exceeds the uncorrected estimate of 10 cM.

10. Part C

Zero double recombinants occur in a cross with an expected count of 0.3. Does this strongly demonstrate complete interference?

Answer and explanation

No Zero yields an observed estimate of I = 1, but the expected count is so small that zero is plausible under independence.
