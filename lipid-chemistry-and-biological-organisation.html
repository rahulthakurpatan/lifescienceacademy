1. Why life needs a family of water-avoiding molecules

Place a drop of vegetable oil in a glass of water and stir. For a while the water may appear cloudy, but left undisturbed it separates again. Now consider a living cell, which also contains mostly water. The cell must store substantial quantities of chemical fuel, keep its cytoplasm distinct from the surrounding solution, communicate with distant tissues and protect its surface. It performs all these tasks in part by using molecules with the very property that makes oil separate from water. Lipid chemistry is the study of how this apparent incompatibility becomes a biological advantage.

Carbohydrates contain numerous hydroxyl groups and can generally establish favourable contacts with water. Lipids typically contain extensive nonpolar carbon skeletons, although many also contain a polar region. The contrast is not absolute: cellulose is insoluble despite its hydroxyl groups, and small fatty acids can dissolve readily. What matters is the balance of molecular interactions, not a rule that every molecule with oxygen is soluble and every molecule with carbon is insoluble. Review the hydrophobic effect in 1.A.02 if necessary.

The term lipid describes a chemically diverse family rather than a single polymer type. Unlike proteins, whose backbones repeat a peptide-bond motif, the lipid family has no universal repeating linkage or obligatory monomer. A triacylglycerol contains three fatty acyl residues esterified to glycerol, whereas cholesterol is a compact fused-ring alcohol. Both are rich in nonpolar surface. Some lipids contain phosphorus, nitrogen or sulfur, and some contain no fatty acid at all. Neither a universal empirical formula nor the presence of glycerol identifies every lipid.

Historically, the ability to extract fat-like molecules into organic solvents was a practical starting point for their classification. Modern chemistry also uses biosynthetic origin and structural frameworks. Common textbook groupings distinguish simple lipids such as fats and waxes, compound lipids such as phospholipids and glycolipids, and derived substances such as fatty acids and sterols. These categories can overlap in ways that make an exclusive classification unreliable. Modern LIPID MAPS distinguishes fatty acyls, glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, sterol lipids, prenol lipids, saccharolipids and polyketides. For examination purposes, learn to read the structural framework first and then identify relevant functional groups.

Three independent questions guide this chapter. What is the covalent architecture of a lipid? How does that architecture determine the molecule's interaction with water and neighbouring lipids? How does the resulting organisation support storage, boundaries, recognition or signalling? Answering these questions will allow you to predict the behaviour of a lipid you have not memorised, including the effect of changing one double bond or replacing one head group. The full pathways of lipid biosynthesis and degradation belong to 1.F; here we establish the molecular reasoning those pathways require.

2. Fatty acids: hydrocarbon chain, carboxyl group and notation

A fatty acid is a carboxylic acid with an aliphatic hydrocarbon chain. Its two ends behave differently: the carboxyl group forms hydrogen bonds and can lose a proton, whereas the chain largely participates in dispersion interactions. The conventional numbering starts at the carboxyl carbon, which is carbon 1, not carbon 0. The second carbon is the alpha carbon, the third is beta, and the terminal methyl carbon is omega. These names matter because a double bond and an oxidation reaction may be described using different reference points.

For an unbranched, saturated, acyclic monocarboxylic acid containing n carbons, the neutral molecular formula is CnH2nO2. Introducing one carbon–carbon double bond removes two hydrogens without changing the carbon or oxygen count. With d such double bonds, the formula becomes CnH2n−2dO2, provided that there are no other rings or unsaturations. This is why palmitic acid (16:0) is C16H32O2 and oleic acid (18:1) is C18H34O2. The carboxyl carbonyl already belongs to the acid framework; do not count it as an extra fatty-acid chain double bond.

The shorthand 18:2 describes an 18-carbon chain with two carbon–carbon double bonds, but says nothing about their exact locations or geometries. Delta notation supplies positions from the carboxyl end. In 18:2 Δ9,12, the first bond lies between carbons 9 and 10, and the second between 12 and 13. In omega or n notation, the family is determined by the double bond nearest the methyl terminus. For an n-carbon chain with the largest delta index j, the omega number is n − j. Thus 18:2 Δ9,12 belongs to the n−6 family; 18:3 Δ9,12,15 belongs to n−3. The family name alone does not specify all double bonds or their geometries.

Even-numbered chains predominate in many organisms because common biosynthetic routes extend chains in two-carbon increments. Odd-chain, branched and hydroxylated fatty acids nevertheless occur, especially in particular microbial lipids and specialised animal lipids. Chain length also varies greatly. In general, increasing hydrocarbon length reduces water solubility and increases the strength of cumulative dispersion contacts. Small carboxylic acids such as acetate behave quite differently from palmitate, despite sharing the carboxyl group.

The protonated species R–COOH and the deprotonated carboxylate R–COO− interconvert. In an ideal dilute solution of an acid, the ratio of carboxylate to acid is 10pH−pKa. At pH 7.0 and an illustrative pKa of 4.8, the ratio is about 158:1, so about 99.4% is ionised. In real long-chain fatty acid aggregates, the local electrostatic environment can shift the apparent pKa; the dilute-solution calculation is not a universal membrane speciation rule. The charged head favours contact with water, yet the chain remains hydrophobic, making long-chain fatty acid salts amphipathic rather than freely soluble at arbitrary concentrations.

Distinguish a free fatty acid from a fatty acyl residue. Esterification replaces the acid hydroxyl with an oxygen bonded to another group; the carboxyl proton is no longer present. A palmitoyl residue in a glycerol ester therefore cannot be treated as a free palmitate anion at neutral pH. This distinction will explain why triacylglycerol is nearly nonpolar even though it contains three fatty acids in esterified form.

3. Saturation, cis/trans geometry and physical behaviour

A saturated chain contains carbon–carbon single bonds. Rotation about these bonds allows different conformations, although in an ordered environment extended conformations maximise close contacts. In a crystal or ordered bilayer, many such chains can approach each other over much of their length. Each individual dispersion interaction is weak, but the sum over a long chain is significant. Consequently, increasing the length of otherwise comparable saturated chains generally increases their melting temperature. A longer chain has more surface to interact with, and separating the ordered chains requires more thermal energy.

A double bond changes this situation in two ways. Its π bond restricts rotation, and the substituents can lie on the same side (cis) or opposite sides (trans). Most common unsaturated fatty acids have cis double bonds. In a cis chain, the two continuing chain segments depart at an angle, introducing a persistent change of direction that neighbouring straight chains cannot accommodate as easily. A trans chain is more extended, although it is not chemically identical to a saturated chain. The cis isomer generally has a lower melting point than the corresponding trans isomer because its crystal packs less efficiently.

Compare stearic acid (18:0), oleic acid (cis-18:1 Δ9) and elaidic acid (trans-18:1 Δ9). Their approximate pure-compound melting points are 69–70 °C, 13–14 °C and 44–45 °C, respectively. The comparison between the last two is particularly instructive because their elemental compositions and double-bond counts are identical. A difference in physical property therefore reveals the role of geometry, not the number of carbon atoms. Natural oils and fats are mixtures of triacylglycerols, however, so one must not transfer these pure free-fatty-acid melting temperatures directly to an entire oil or membrane.

Polyunsaturated chains contain more than one double bond. In common methylene-interrupted fatty acids, successive double bonds are separated by a –CH2– group. These chains sample a range of conformations that strongly modify packing. A higher fraction of cis-polyunsaturated chains usually favours a more disordered lipid phase at a given temperature. A conjugated chain, in contrast, has alternating double and single bonds, and electronic delocalisation changes chemical reactivity. It is a mistake to predict every physical property from double-bond count alone: position, geometry, chain length, head group and the surrounding mixture also matter.

Hydrogenation adds hydrogen across chain double bonds. Full hydrogenation of oleate's single double bond yields stearate; partial hydrogenation can also promote cis-to-trans isomerisation and migration of double bonds. The resulting mixture may have a higher melting range. In living organisms, enzyme-catalysed desaturation introduces double bonds with specific positions and geometry. The ability to change unsaturation provides a way to adjust lipid physical properties without constructing an entirely new head group.

Consider two model lipid preparations containing identical head groups and equal chain lengths. If one contains straight saturated chains and the other contains cis-unsaturated chains, cooling should generally induce chain ordering earlier in the saturated sample. If experimental data show the opposite, first check that the comparison is truly matched: the unsaturated preparation may have longer chains, a different head-group composition or sterols. This controlled-comparison habit is central to Part C reasoning. Biological function emerges from competing contributions, not a single memorised factor.

4. Essential fatty acids and chemical reactivity

The body can synthesise many fatty acids from small carbon precursors and alter their chains. Essential fatty acids are a nutritional subset: humans cannot synthesise enough of particular structures de novo because the required desaturation reactions are missing. Mammalian Δ9 desaturase can introduce a double bond into suitable saturated chains, producing oleate, but humans lack the Δ12 and Δ15 desaturases that would turn the oleate framework into linoleate and alpha-linolenate. Plants and many other organisms possess different desaturase repertoires. Thus the statement that a fatty acid is essential must be qualified by organism.

Linoleic acid (18:2 n−6) and alpha-linolenic acid (18:3 n−3) are the two classical essential fatty acids for humans. They contribute to membrane composition and supply the starting frameworks for longer polyunsaturated derivatives. Elongation adds carbons at the carboxyl end: when an 18-carbon n−6 chain becomes a 20-carbon chain, both the total carbon count and the delta positions of existing double bonds increase by two, so the n−6 identity is conserved. Further desaturation introduces additional bonds without creating the missing terminal family pattern. This explains why a supply of n−9 oleate cannot simply replace n−6 linoleate or n−3 alpha-linolenate.

Arachidonic acid is usually produced from dietary linoleate and is present in many membrane phospholipids. EPA and DHA can be formed from alpha-linolenate, but conversion, especially to DHA, is limited and variable in humans. A derivative may become conditionally essential in certain physiological or developmental circumstances. Do not memorise every polyunsaturated fatty acid as strictly essential. Essentiality concerns synthetic capacity and physiological demand, while unsaturation describes covalent structure; these are different classifications.

Unsaturation also creates chemical vulnerability. Removing a hydrogen from a carbon between two double bonds can generate a resonance-stabilised lipid radical. Oxygen reacts with the radical to produce a peroxyl radical, which can remove a hydrogen from another lipid, leaving a lipid hydroperoxide and propagating the chain reaction. The damage is not restricted to the original molecule: neighbouring chains can become oxidised. Cleavage products and altered tail polarity can perturb membrane packing and proteins. Vitamin E can interrupt propagation by donating a hydrogen to lipid peroxyl radicals; enzymatic peroxide-removal systems are also important. Detailed antioxidant metabolism belongs in later nodes.

Differentiate this oxidative rancidity from hydrolytic rancidity, in which ester cleavage releases fatty acids. Both can alter odour and quality, but only oxidative rancidity necessarily involves radical oxidation. Increasing the number of methylene-interrupted double bonds generally increases susceptibility to lipid peroxidation, although antioxidants, oxygen exposure and metal ions affect the observed rate. In a comparison of saturated and polyunsaturated vesicles, one therefore expects more oxidation in the latter under matched pro-oxidant conditions, not because every double bond spontaneously breaks in water, but because radical intermediates and propagation become chemically accessible.

A useful measure of average unsaturation is the iodine value: grams of iodine equivalent consumed by 100 g of a lipid sample in a standard addition assay. Idealised one-to-one addition of I2 to each chain double bond gives iodine value = 100 × 253.8 × d/M, where d is the number of reactive double bonds per molecule and M is the molar mass in g mol−1. Pure oleic acid (M ≈ 282.46, d = 1) has a theoretical value of about 89.9; linoleic acid (M ≈ 280.45, d = 2) gives 181.0. In actual assays the reagent and reaction conditions are standardised; mixtures give an average and the value cannot tell cis from trans or determine exact double-bond locations. Two isomers of identical formula can have the same theoretical iodine value but different melting points.

5. Neutral lipids: glycerol esters, waxes and energy storage

Glycerol is a three-carbon alcohol with a hydroxyl group on each carbon. Each hydroxyl can become esterified to a fatty acid. The simplest product has one acyl residue (monoacylglycerol), the next has two (diacylglycerol), and complete esterification yields triacylglycerol, often called triglyceride. A triacylglycerol with three identical acyl chains is a simple triacylglycerol; with two or more different chains it is mixed. Most natural fats contain many mixed molecular species. The stereospecific numbering system (sn) distinguishes glycerol positions; a molecule with different groups at sn-1 and sn-3 can be chiral. This positional information matters for enzyme recognition.

Formal ester formation joins an alcohol oxygen to an acyl carbon and removes one water molecule per ester bond. In a cell the acyl group is usually delivered in activated form rather than by direct spontaneous dehydration of glycerol and free acid in water. The mass balance nevertheless helps read structures. Glycerol plus three stearic acid molecules minus three water molecules yields tristearin, C57H110O6: C = 3 + 3×18, H = 8 + 3×36 − 6, O = 3 + 3×2 − 3. This stoichiometric accounting avoids the common error of counting the acid oxygen atoms twice.

Because all three glycerol hydroxyls are esterified and the free carboxylates are masked, triacylglycerols have little capacity to hydrate and carry no ordinary ionisable head group at physiological pH. They therefore separate from water into an oil-like phase. This is appropriate for storage but unsuitable as the sole material of a hydrated bilayer. A cellular lipid droplet solves the interface problem with a single surface layer of amphipathic lipids and associated proteins; inside it lies a neutral lipid core. The surface is a monolayer rather than a bilayer because its inner side contacts oil instead of aqueous solution.

Fat stores concentrated chemical energy. Fatty acid chains contain many reduced carbon atoms and comparatively little oxygen, so complete oxidation to CO2 and water transfers many electrons to cellular electron acceptors. Carbohydrates are already partly oxidised. As a rough nutritional energy comparison, fat supplies about 9 kcal per gram and digestible carbohydrate about 4 kcal per gram. These values are not bond energies or universal ATP yields. An additional storage advantage is that neutral lipid is stored with relatively little associated water, whereas glycogen is stored with substantial hydration. If a cell stores 90 kcal as dry fat, the mass is approximately 10 g, compared with 22.5 g of dry carbohydrate at these conventional values. Including hydration would further increase the difference in stored mass.

Plants commonly store triacylglycerol in seeds as oil bodies; animals store it in lipid droplets, particularly in adipose tissue. Saturated-chain-rich mixtures are often solid or semisolid at room temperature, while mixtures rich in cis-unsaturated chains often remain liquid. Exceptions arise from chain length and mixture composition, so the everyday distinction between fats and oils is physical rather than a different fundamental ester chemistry.

Waxes use a different alcohol framework. A typical wax ester connects one long-chain fatty acid to one long-chain alcohol. This produces an extensive nonpolar surface with relatively little polar functionality, useful for reducing water penetration or loss. Plant cuticles, insect surface coatings and some marine energy reserves contain wax esters. Many natural waxes also contain hydrocarbons and other compounds, so not every component of a wax mixture is an ester. The key structural comparison is one ester bond between two long chains in a wax ester versus three ester bonds on a glycerol framework in triacylglycerol.

6. Hydrolysis, saponification and interpreting lipid measurements

Ester bonds can be cleaved by hydrolysis. In acid-catalysed hydrolysis the products of complete triacylglycerol cleavage are glycerol and three fatty acids. In base-promoted hydrolysis, hydroxide attacks an ester carbonyl, a tetrahedral intermediate forms, and collapse of that intermediate releases the alcohol-derived leaving group. Proton transfer ultimately gives a carboxylate salt. Its negative charge makes it a poor electrophile and favours the net hydrolysis under the alkaline conditions. The process is called saponification when applied to fats and oils because the long-chain carboxylate salts are soaps.

For a triacylglycerol with three ester bonds, complete alkaline hydrolysis consumes three equivalents of hydroxide per molecule in the ideal net stoichiometry: TAG + 3 KOH → glycerol + 3 RCOO−K+ (R groups need not be identical). Soaps clean because their chains can associate with nonpolar material while charged heads remain exposed to water; calcium and magnesium may form less-soluble salts, reducing effectiveness in hard water. Lipases also hydrolyse ester bonds but catalyse reactions in biological settings and often show positional or interfacial selectivity.

The saponification value is milligrams of KOH required to saponify one gram of sample under standard conditions, including neutralisation of free fatty acids when present. For a pure neutral lipid with e hydrolysable ester groups and molar mass M, the ideal value is 1000 × e × 56.11/M mg KOH g−1. For tripalmitin (M ≈ 807.34 g mol−1, e = 3), this is about 208.5 mg g−1; for tristearin (M ≈ 891.49 g mol−1), it is about 188.8 mg g−1. The shorter-chain TAG has more ester molecules per gram even though every molecule has three bonds. Therefore higher saponification value generally indicates shorter average chain length when comparing otherwise similar TAG mixtures. It does not independently measure unsaturation.

Suppose an unknown pure TAG has a saponification value of 210.0 mg KOH g−1. Rearranging gives M = 1000×3×56.11/210.0 ≈ 801.6 g mol−1. Subtract the glycerol contribution (92.09 g mol−1) and add back the three waters lost (54.05 g mol−1) to obtain the sum of the three free-fatty-acid molar masses: about 763.6 g mol−1, or 254.5 on average. This is consistent with chains around C16, but does not establish identity; mixed lengths or unsaturation can yield similar average masses. If the sample includes free acid or non-TAG molecules, the calculation requires a different model.

Acid value, expressed as mg KOH needed to neutralise free acids per gram, is distinct from saponification value. A rising acid value during storage can indicate increased free fatty acid content following hydrolysis. Iodine value estimates unsaturation. Peroxide value measures primary oxidation products in a defined assay and can later decrease as hydroperoxides decompose, so a low value alone does not guarantee that an old sample is unoxidised. These classical measurements are most useful when interpreted together: a molecule-count-sensitive quantity, a double-bond-sensitive quantity and an oxidation-sensitive quantity answer different questions.

Traditional textbooks contrast saponifiable lipids (containing bonds susceptible to alkaline hydrolysis, especially esters) with nonsaponifiable lipids such as free cholesterol and hydrocarbons. A cholesteryl ester is an important counterexample to the assumption that sterol lipids are all nonsaponifiable: it can release cholesterol and a fatty acid salt. Sphingolipids typically contain amide-linked fatty acids, which are less readily hydrolysed than esters under ordinary saponification conditions; avoid assigning their hydrolysis behaviour simply from the word “lipid”. Always inspect the linkage.

7. Glycerophospholipids: turning an oil molecule into an interface

Now imagine replacing the third fatty acyl residue of a triacylglycerol framework with a phosphate-containing group. Two hydrocarbon chains remain, but a hydrated polar region appears. The new molecule has an architectural asymmetry: it can lower the exposure of the chains to water while retaining contact between its phosphate-containing region and water. This is the central design principle of many structural membrane lipids.

In the common glycerophospholipid framework, glycerol's sn-1 and sn-2 positions carry fatty acyl esters and sn-3 bears phosphate. Phosphatidic acid is the simplest such framework. Attachment of an alcohol to the phosphate yields a phosphodiester; glycerol is on one side and the head alcohol on the other. Different head groups change charge, hydration, hydrogen bonding and effective cross-sectional area while the two chains supply hydrophobic interactions. Natural molecular species often have a more saturated sn-1 chain and a more unsaturated sn-2 chain, but this is a common pattern, not a chemical requirement.

Phosphatidylcholine (PC, lecithin in older terminology) contains choline, whose quaternary ammonium group carries a permanent positive charge. The phosphate diester carries a negative charge at physiological pH. The net charge of the head group is approximately zero, but it has spatially separated charges and substantial hydration. Phosphatidylethanolamine (PE, historically cephalin in some usage) contains a protonated primary amine under ordinary physiological conditions and also has net zero charge. Its smaller head group compared with PC changes packing and favours curvature in many mixtures. Neither lipid is “nonpolar” simply because its net charge is zero.

Phosphatidylserine (PS) carries a phosphate negative charge, a carboxylate negative charge and an ammonium positive charge, giving net charge about −1. Phosphatidylinositol (PI) contains an inositol ring, a cyclic polyol, and ordinarily has net charge −1. Additional phosphorylations on that ring create phosphoinositides with more negative charge. Phosphatidylglycerol (PG) bears a glycerol head group and is also commonly anionic. Phosphatidic acid (PA) has a phosphomonoester rather than a head-group phosphodiester; its effective charge can vary between approximately −1 and −2 as pH, hydrogen bonding and lipid environment change. Do not count every phosphate group as exactly −2 regardless of its esterification state.

Cardiolipin, or diphosphatidylglycerol, is an especially instructive extension of the framework. Two phosphatidyl groups are linked through a central glycerol, giving four hydrophobic chains and two phosphate groups. It is abundant in the inner mitochondrial membrane and occurs in many bacteria. Its unusual geometry and interactions contribute to the organisation of respiratory membrane proteins; the detailed mechanisms of respiration will be studied later. Its charge state depends on molecular environment, with a commonly used physiological formal representation of −2. It is not simply a diacyl phosphatidylglycerol.

8. Ether lipids, lysolipids and phospholipase reasoning

Not all glycerophospholipids have two ester linkages. Ether glycerophospholipids contain an alkyl chain linked through C–O–C rather than C(=O)–O–C. Plasmalogens contain a vinyl ether at sn-1 and commonly an ester at sn-2. They occur prominently in certain animal tissues, including heart and nervous tissue. The linkage changes hydrolysis and oxidation behaviour. An ether has no acyl carbonyl for an ordinary esterase to attack; a vinyl ether, however, can undergo acid-sensitive cleavage. The terms ester, ether and vinyl ether must therefore be recognised from the molecular structure, not inferred from the head group alone.

Archaeal membrane lipids demonstrate that the same principle of amphipathicity can be realised through a chemically different architecture. Typical archaeal lipids have isoprenoid chains ether-linked to a glycerol-1-phosphate framework, whereas bacterial and eukaryotic glycerophospholipids usually use glycerol-3-phosphate and ester-linked fatty acids. These glycerol phosphate frameworks are stereochemical opposites. Some archaeal tetraether lipids span the entire membrane and form a monolayer rather than two independent leaflets. This distinction is important in comparing membrane chemistry across domains, although not every archaeon has the same lipid composition and unusual ether lipids occur outside Archaea too.

Specific phospholipases allow us to map a glycerophospholipid's bonds. Phospholipase A1 cleaves a conventional ester at sn-1, and A2 cleaves the ester at sn-2. Each reaction releases a free fatty acid and leaves a monoacyl or lysophospholipid. The chain loss changes effective molecular geometry: the polar head is still present while the hydrophobic cross-sectional area becomes smaller. Many such lysolipids prefer positive curvature and can destabilise bilayers at sufficient concentration. Their effects depend on concentration and other lipids present; a trace amount does not necessarily destroy an entire membrane.

Phospholipase C cleaves between glycerol and phosphate, producing diacylglycerol (DAG) and a phosphorylated head-group product. Phospholipase D cleaves between phosphate and the head-group alcohol, generating phosphatidic acid and the free head alcohol. These enzyme classes reveal a functional difference between a tail-cleaving reaction and a head-cleaving reaction. The former liberates a fatty acid and changes the number of tails, whereas the latter leaves both tails attached but changes the polar region. Product identity follows directly from which side of the bond retains phosphate.

One important signalling example is phospholipase C cleavage of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2). The two-tailed DAG remains preferentially associated with the membrane; the phosphorylated inositol head product IP3 is highly water soluble and can act in the cytosol. They arise from a single cleavage but occupy different environments because of their chemistry. The complete receptor and calcium signalling pathways belong to Unit 4. Likewise, phospholipase A2 can release arachidonate from membrane lipids, supplying substrate for eicosanoid formation.

Try a simple inference: a radiolabel is present exclusively on the sn-2 acyl chain of a phosphatidylcholine molecule. Treatment with A2 transfers the label into the released fatty acid fraction, whereas C releases a labelled DAG into the lipid fraction and a largely unlabelled phosphorylated head group. D yields labelled phosphatidic acid. This prediction assumes complete, specific cleavage without secondary reactions or label exchange. Such a controlled labelling question tests your understanding of covalent architecture rather than your ability to recall enzyme names alone.

9. Sphingolipids: a different backbone for a familiar interface

A two-tailed amphipathic lipid does not have to be built from glycerol. Sphingolipids instead derive their fundamental architecture from a long-chain amino alcohol. The common mammalian example is sphingosine, typically an 18-carbon base with a hydroxyl at C1, an amino group at C2, a hydroxyl at C3 and a trans double bond between C4 and C5. The hydrocarbon region is already one tail. Consequently, this backbone needs only one additional fatty acid to construct a two-tailed lipid. Other sphingoid bases have different saturation, hydroxylation or chain length; sphinganine is the corresponding saturated base, and phytosphingosine has an additional hydroxyl group.

The amino group at C2 can be acylated by a fatty acid, creating an amide linkage –NH–C(=O)–. The product is ceramide, a central structural framework of sphingolipids. Unlike glycerol-based phospholipids, the second hydrocarbon chain is attached through nitrogen rather than oxygen. The polar end retains hydroxyl groups, and the primary hydroxyl at C1 can be modified with different head groups. It is useful to read ceramide as a building block rather than memorise every sphingolipid separately. Changing the C1 substituent can convert this same two-tailed framework into a phospholipid or a glycolipid without replacing its core skeleton.

Sphingomyelin contains a phosphocholine head attached to ceramide, usually with a net-zero zwitterionic head charge. It is therefore a phospholipid but not a glycerophospholipid. It occurs widely in animal plasma membranes and is prominent in myelin, the lipid-rich insulating membrane surrounding many nerve axons. The word sphingomyelin tells us both the sphingoid backbone and the phosphate-containing head; it does not mean that this lipid occurs exclusively in myelin. A glycerophospholipid with the same phosphocholine head is PC. Both may hydrate similarly at the head-group level, but their backbone, linkage and chain-packing interactions differ.

In many sphingolipids the amide and free hydroxyl groups can participate in intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Long and often relatively saturated chains can also pack well. These features help explain why many sphingolipids favour more ordered lipid environments, often together with cholesterol. The actual behaviour depends on the precise molecular species and temperature; it would be incorrect to say that every sphingolipid has a saturated acyl chain or that every sphingolipid is immobile. This structure–function prediction is more reliable than treating lipid names as independent facts.

Ceramide and sphingosine-1-phosphate are also biologically active lipid mediators. Addition of a phosphate to sphingosine at C1 creates sphingosine-1-phosphate, which has only the long-chain base rather than an amide-linked second acyl chain. These molecules illustrate how the addition or removal of a relatively small polar or acyl group changes cellular localisation and signalling. We will reserve the enzymatic conversions and detailed receptor responses for metabolism and cell signalling. Here the important distinction is that ceramide is not the same molecule as free sphingosine, and sphingosine-1-phosphate is not sphingomyelin.

To classify an unfamiliar structure, identify its backbone first. If there is a long-chain amino alcohol and an amide-linked fatty acid, look for ceramide derivatives. If the head is phosphocholine, choose sphingomyelin; if it is a sugar, choose a glycosphingolipid. If the backbone is three-carbon glycerol and the tails are ester-linked, an apparently identical phosphocholine head instead identifies PC. This two-step procedure remains valid even when the exact fatty acid species are unknown.

10. Glycolipids: displaying carbohydrate information on lipid surfaces

In 1.B.01 we studied how sugars form glycosidic bonds and how changing sugar identity or linkage can alter biological recognition. A glycolipid joins this carbohydrate chemistry to lipid organisation. The hydrophobic region keeps the molecule associated with a lipid surface while the carbohydrate region can contact water and other molecules. This arrangement transforms a membrane from a passive boundary into a chemically informative surface: neighbouring cells, receptors and pathogens can encounter particular sugar structures without first crossing the hydrophobic interior.

Animal glycosphingolipids are generally based on ceramide with carbohydrate attached to its C1 hydroxyl by a glycosidic bond. A cerebroside is a monohexosylceramide, commonly glucosylceramide or galactosylceramide. The single sugar adds hydroxyl groups and hydration but typically does not introduce a net formal charge. Galactosylceramide is abundant in myelin. Globosides contain more than one neutral sugar; they are neutral glycosphingolipids, not phosphate-containing lipids. The more extensive head can support different recognition interactions while the ceramide framework remains related.

Gangliosides contain one or more sialic acid residues, commonly N-acetylneuraminic acid, in their oligosaccharide head. A sialic acid carboxyl group is usually negatively charged near physiological pH. Therefore a ganglioside can carry negative charge even though it contains no phosphate. In traditional ganglioside nomenclature, GM, GD and GT indicate one, two and three sialic acid residues respectively; the following number helps distinguish structures within the series and should not be read as a count of acyl chains. GM1, for instance, contains one sialic acid residue. Its carbohydrate portion provides a binding site for cholera toxin, a classic example of highly specific molecular recognition on a cell surface.

Sulfatides are sulfated glycosphingolipids, commonly derived from galactosylceramide. A sulfate ester supplies negative charge through a different chemical group from the carboxylate of sialic acid. Thus the terms neutral glycolipid and acidic glycolipid distinguish head-group chemistry, not whether the ceramide tail region is hydrophobic. Cerebrosides and globosides are generally neutral; sulfatides and gangliosides are anionic. Negative charge does not uniquely identify a phospholipid.

Glycolipids are not universally sphingolipids. Plant chloroplast membranes are rich in glyceroglycolipids, especially monogalactosyldiacylglycerol (MGDG) and digalactosyldiacylglycerol (DGDG). These lipids contain a diacylglycerol framework and galactose-containing heads rather than ceramide. MGDG tends to favour negative curvature in suitable conditions because of its relatively small head compared with the tails, whereas DGDG commonly supports bilayer organisation. Sulfoquinovosyldiacylglycerol (SQDG) is another important chloroplast glycolipid; its sulfonate makes the head anionic. Such examples clarify why the categories glycolipid and sphingolipid overlap rather than stand as mutually exclusive alternatives.

Asymmetry matters for biological function. In the plasma membrane of many animal cells, the carbohydrate-containing heads of glycosphingolipids face the extracellular space. The sugars remain exposed to water and do not normally cross the membrane core simply by spontaneous rotation of the entire lipid between leaflets. The carbohydrate-rich cell surface includes contributions from glycoproteins as well as glycolipids, forming part of the glycocalyx. ABO blood-group determinants can occur on both glycoproteins and glycolipids; the determinants depend on the terminal carbohydrate structure, not on one universal lipid carrier. The details of membrane trafficking and glycan biosynthesis are studied in subsequent units.

11. Cholesterol, sterols and other isoprenoid-derived lipids

So far we have built lipids from long chains arranged around glycerol or a sphingoid base. Steroids offer a contrasting solution. Their characteristic skeleton contains four fused rings, three six-membered and one five-membered. Ring fusion restricts conformational freedom, so the core is comparatively rigid rather than freely flexible like an acyl chain. Sterols are steroids bearing a hydroxyl group, commonly at C3, and cholesterol is the major sterol of many animal membranes. It contains 27 carbons, a 3β-hydroxyl group, a Δ5 double bond between C5 and C6, and a hydrocarbon side chain at C17.

The small hydroxyl group permits a favourable position near the membrane–water interface, while the ring system and side chain associate with the hydrocarbon region. Cholesterol is amphipathic, but it does not generally form a stable ordinary aqueous phospholipid-like bilayer by itself. In a mixed membrane the rigid rings can restrict motion of neighbouring acyl chains. In a tightly ordered low-temperature lipid phase, however, the irregular sterol structure interrupts the regular packing needed for crystallisation. These apparently opposite consequences are compatible because the starting state is different.

Esterifying the C3 hydroxyl with a fatty acid produces a cholesteryl ester. The hydroxyl is no longer free, substantially reducing its amphipathic character. Free cholesterol consequently associates with phospholipid surfaces, while cholesteryl ester is well suited to neutral lipid cores such as those of lipid droplets and lipoproteins. Both contain the steroid skeleton; their distinct locations reflect masking of a single functional group. In saponification, a cholesteryl ester can be hydrolysed to cholesterol and a fatty acid salt, while free cholesterol lacks that ester bond.

Cholesterol supplies the structural precursor for several classes of biological molecules. Steroid hormones such as cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, estradiol and progesterone retain the characteristic fused-ring framework with different side chains and functional groups. Those changes affect receptor binding and physiological function. Vitamin D formation proceeds from a sterol precursor; opening a ring creates a secosteroid. Bile acids are more highly hydroxylated derivatives with a carboxylic acid side chain; their hydroxyl-rich face and hydrophobic face make them amphipathic in a different manner from a conventional head-and-tail phospholipid. Conjugation with glycine or taurine favours bile salt formation, useful for handling hydrophobic dietary components in an aqueous environment. Detailed endocrine and digestive physiology is beyond this chapter.

Fungi contain ergosterol as a prominent sterol, and plants contain phytosterols such as sitosterol and stigmasterol. Many bacteria lack cholesterol, although some acquire or synthesise sterols and some use hopanoid molecules with partially analogous effects on packing. It is therefore unsafe to identify all membranes as cholesterol-containing. Membranes require an appropriate physical state, but the molecules that achieve it vary among organisms.

Sterol lipids originate from isoprenoid chemistry. Prenol lipids are a related category assembled from five-carbon isoprene-derived units. Carotenoids have long conjugated systems that enable light absorption; in photosynthetic organisms they contribute to light harvesting and photoprotection. Ubiquinone has a quinone head capable of redox chemistry and a hydrophobic isoprenoid chain that anchors it within a membrane. Dolichol uses a long hydrophobic isoprenoid chain to associate with membranes and participate, in its phosphorylated forms, in glycan assembly. These examples show how an isoprenoid tail can localise a functional head even when there are no conventional fatty acyl chains. The full electron-transfer and glycosylation mechanisms belong to other nodes.

Do not confuse the terms lipid, steroid and sterol. Every sterol is a steroid-related lipid, but not every steroid is a sterol and most lipids are not steroids. Nor is cholesterol simply “fat” in the sense of a TAG. An examination structure with a fused four-ring core and hydroxyl can be identified from its architecture, whereas a molecule with three ester-linked chains on glycerol must be classified differently regardless of whether both molecules are isolated from the same tissue.

12. Fatty-acid-derived mediators: eicosanoids and related signals

A fatty acid in a membrane need not remain a permanent structural component. Many membrane phospholipids contain polyunsaturated acyl residues at sn-2. An appropriate stimulus can activate a phospholipase A2, releasing arachidonic acid. The free chain can then become a substrate for enzymes that introduce oxygen and reorganise the chain. The products act as lipid mediators rather than simply as energy sources. Eicosanoids are named for the twenty-carbon frameworks from which many classical examples are formed. Arachidonic acid (20:4 n−6) is a particularly important precursor, but EPA and other twenty-carbon polyunsaturated fatty acids can also give rise to related products.

The common structural precursor explains why a change in the available fatty acid composition can alter the set of mediators a tissue is capable of producing. The same precursor does not imply identical biological effects: enzymes create different oxygenation patterns, ring structures and receptor-binding surfaces. Cyclooxygenases (COX) convert arachidonic acid through cyclic endoperoxide intermediates to a common precursor of prostaglandins, prostacyclin and thromboxanes. Prostaglandins characteristically contain a five-membered ring; prostacyclin has a distinctive bicyclic oxygen-containing structure and thromboxanes have an oxygen-containing six-membered ring in the active TXA2 framework. These features distinguish the families chemically.

Lipoxygenases (LOX) introduce oxygen at particular positions on the polyunsaturated chain. The 5-LOX route produces intermediates for leukotrienes, many of which act in immune and inflammatory responses. Lipoxins arise through related oxygenation routes and can contribute to the resolution of inflammation. Cytochrome P450 enzymes also generate epoxy and hydroxy derivatives of arachidonate. It is inaccurate to equate all oxygenated fatty acid products with prostaglandins or to claim that all eicosanoids arise from COX. The shared precursor branches into chemically and functionally distinct products.

The molecular products often act locally through specific receptors and are generally short-lived compared with many circulating hormones. For example, thromboxane A2 generated in platelets promotes aggregation and vasoconstriction, whereas prostacyclin produced by vascular cells generally opposes platelet aggregation and favours vasodilation. These opposite effects demonstrate why one cannot assign a single physiological action to “arachidonic acid products”. PGE2 has important roles in inflammatory responses, fever and other processes; leukotriene B4 participates in leukocyte recruitment, and cysteinyl leukotrienes can affect smooth muscle and vascular permeability. Outcomes depend on product, receptor, concentration and biological context.

A classical reasoning experiment uses selective enzyme perturbation. If COX activity falls while the availability of free arachidonate and LOX activity are held constant, direct COX-dependent products should decrease. It does not follow that all leukotrienes must also decrease, because their formation follows a different branch. Similarly, inhibiting the upstream release of arachidonate can reduce substrate supply to multiple routes, but the extent depends on alternative sources and enzyme conditions. This distinction between upstream substrate availability and downstream branch-specific catalysis is useful throughout biochemistry.

Related fatty-acid-derived signals include oxylipins from other chain lengths and families, and endocannabinoids such as anandamide and 2-arachidonoylglycerol, which are arachidonate-containing derivatives with distinct polar groups. They illustrate another key principle: esterification or amidation changes charge and recognition, and a fatty-acid-derived mediator is not necessarily a free fatty acid. These are contextual examples rather than a complete signalling pathway inventory.

13. The hydrophobic effect and spontaneous lipid self-assembly

We can now return to the opening oil-and-water observation with a more powerful molecular vocabulary. Water interacts favourably with polar and charged groups through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. A long hydrocarbon surface lacks comparable interactions. Water in the vicinity of a nonpolar surface is constrained in its possible arrangements, and exposing extensive nonpolar surface carries a free-energy cost. When many hydrocarbon tails cluster, the total water-exposed nonpolar surface decreases. Water molecules are released from some constrained environments and the aggregated system can have lower free energy. This solvent-driven tendency is the hydrophobic effect, not a new covalent or electrostatic attraction between tails.

At constant temperature and pressure, spontaneous assembly is favoured when the change in Gibbs free energy ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is negative for the process under the stated conditions. T is absolute temperature in kelvin; ΔH and ΔG are energies per stated amount of material, and ΔS is energy per kelvin for the same amount. The entropy change includes both lipid and solvent. Individual lipid molecules lose translational and sometimes conformational freedom on aggregation, but the surrounding water can gain entropy. Dispersion interactions between tails, hydration of heads, electrostatic repulsion and other contributions also affect ΔH and ΔS. It is therefore misleading to say that aggregation always increases the entropy of the lipids themselves or that the hydrophobic effect is purely entropic at every temperature.

At an air–water interface, amphipathic lipids may form a monolayer with polar groups toward water and chains toward air. In bulk water a monolayer sheet with hydrocarbon chains exposed on one side would be unfavourable. The molecules can instead form micelles, bilayers or other phases in which most tails are hidden while the heads remain hydrated. Which organisation is selected depends on molecular geometry and environmental conditions. A neutral oil phase can also be dispersed in water if an amphipathic coating covers the surface. The structure follows the need to avoid unfavourable interfaces, not an assumption that all lipids form the same aggregate.

The critical micelle concentration (CMC) is the approximate concentration above which micelle formation becomes important for a surfactant under specified conditions. Below the CMC, monomers predominate in many idealised systems; near and above it the additional surfactant increasingly enters aggregates, and monomer activity changes relatively little over a suitable concentration range. Longer hydrophobic chains commonly reduce the CMC in a matched homologous series because transfer of larger nonpolar surface away from water is more favourable. Ionic surfactants can show substantial salt dependence because ions screen repulsion between charged heads. Temperature, counterion identity and surfactant structure influence the CMC. Double-tailed phospholipids often have extremely low aqueous monomer solubility and form bilayer assemblies rather than conventional spherical micelles.

For a deliberately simplified nonionic-surfactant model, micellisation free energy per mole of surfactant is approximately RT ln XCMC, using a mole-fraction standard state and a sufficiently large micellar aggregation number. R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1, T is kelvin, and XCMC is dimensionless. A tenfold decrease in CMC in the same dilute solvent corresponds to ΔΔG ≈ −RT ln 10, or −5.71 kJ mol−1 at 298 K. This is an illustrative comparison, not a universal formula for ionic surfactants or finite aggregates: counterion binding, nonideality and the standard state change the quantitative treatment. The sign tells us why a lower CMC indicates stronger aggregation tendency under the matched assumptions.

Self-assembly does not require ATP input for every lipid association, but this does not mean every cellular membrane is at equilibrium. Lipid synthesis, transport, leaflet asymmetry and membrane remodelling can consume energy. A model bilayer may form spontaneously while its exact living counterpart is maintained in a regulated nonequilibrium state. Distinguish the thermodynamic drive for aggregate formation from the cellular processes that construct and preserve a particular composition.

14. Molecular shape predicts micelles, bilayers and curvature

Imagine attempting to tile the surface of a sphere with amphipathic molecules. The outside surface has a larger area than a surface closer to the centre, so molecules with a large hydrated head and narrow hydrophobic tail are naturally suited to positive curvature. Many single-chain detergents and lysophospholipids approximate this shape. Two-chain phospholipids with a head region comparable to the tail cross-section are more nearly cylindrical and can fill a flat sheet without leaving large gaps. Lipids with small heads and wide tail regions can instead favour negative curvature, as in inverted phases. The geometry is effective molecular geometry in the particular environment, not merely the outline of an isolated molecule in a drawing.

A useful semi-quantitative model is the critical packing parameter P = v/(a0lc), where v is the hydrophobic chain volume, a0 is the effective area per polar head at the interface, and lc is the maximum effective chain length. If v is in nm³, a0 in nm² and lc in nm, P is dimensionless. Simple sphere geometry illustrates the origin of the familiar thresholds: sphere volume divided by surface area is radius/3, so for tails filling a sphere of radius no greater than lc, v/a0 is roughly at most lc/3. A long cylinder has volume-to-lateral-area ratio radius/2. A planar leaflet has an approximate volume-to-area ratio equal to leaflet thickness. This geometric argument motivates, but does not rigorously determine, the preferred packing regime.

As a rough guide, P less than about 1/3 favours spherical micelles; between approximately 1/3 and 1/2, cylindrical micelles may occur; between approximately 1/2 and 1, bilayer-based assemblies are common; above 1, inverted structures become possible. These ranges are heuristic. Lipid mixtures can contain multiple phases; electrostatics, hydration, salt, temperature, chain flexibility and membrane tension shift the effective dimensions. An examination question with fixed numerical dimensions is asking for the tendency predicted by the simple model, not a guarantee that every sample at that P forms exactly one structure.

For example, a surfactant has v = 0.36 nm³, a0 = 0.70 nm² and lc = 1.60 nm. Its P is 0.36/(0.70×1.60) = 0.321, suggesting spherical micelles. If a second hydrophobic chain increases the effective total tail volume to 0.72 nm³ while a0 and lc are held fixed in this simplified model, P becomes 0.643, making a bilayer more plausible. If head-group charge is screened by salt and effective a0 decreases, P increases. This calculation gives a direction of change; actual dimensions would need experimental measurement.

Why does a bilayer often close into a vesicle rather than remain an open flat disk? The rim of a flat sheet exposes hydrocarbon regions to water, creating an energetic cost that increases with exposed perimeter. Bending the sheet eliminates the rim but requires curvature energy. For suitable compositions and sizes, eliminating edge exposure outweighs bending cost and a closed bilayer forms. Its interior is an aqueous lumen, unlike the hydrocarbon core of a micelle. A liposome is such a lipid vesicle, often prepared as a model system. A unilamellar vesicle has one bilayer shell; a multilamellar vesicle has several concentric shells. These are not the same as an oil droplet covered by a monolayer.

Detergents illustrate the practical consequences. At an appropriate concentration a detergent can insert into a bilayer, change curvature and, in sufficient excess, promote mixed micelles containing detergent and membrane lipid. This can release membrane proteins from their lipid environment. Whether a protein remains functional depends on the chemical conditions and how much of its native lipid environment is preserved. Not all amphiphiles destroy membranes: normal membrane phospholipids are themselves amphipathic, and the distinction lies in their geometry, concentration and interactions. Bile salts also promote mixed micelles with dietary lipid digestion products, but the physiological details are reserved for digestion and lipid metabolism.

15. Lipid bilayers: molecular arrangement and experimental evidence

A lipid bilayer consists of two leaflets with hydrophobic chains directed inward and polar heads directed toward the aqueous solutions on opposite sides. Each leaflet is a two-dimensional molecular assembly, not a solid covalent sheet. A typical biological bilayer has a thickness of several nanometres, often around 4–5 nm depending on composition and the measurement definition. The hydrophobic core is thinner than the complete hydrated head-to-head distance. Lipid chains fluctuate, and the two leaflets interact through their tail regions. Because the assembly has no continuous covalent crosslinks between all lipids, it can bend, change shape and self-seal small disruptions.

A classical experiment by Gorter and Grendel in 1925 provided evidence for a two-layer lipid arrangement. They extracted lipids from red blood cells, spread the lipids as a film at an air–water interface, and compared the film's area with the estimated total surface area of the cells. A ratio near two suggested that enough lipid was present to cover the cell surface twice. Mature mammalian red cells were useful for this inference because they lack the abundant internal organelle membranes of typical nucleated cells. The argument assumes that the extraction and area estimates are adequate and that the effective monolayer packing is comparable. Historical measurements contained compensating uncertainties, so the experiment was important evidence rather than exact proof of every structural detail of modern membranes.

For a model problem, suppose N red cells have total estimated plasma membrane area 1.20×108 µm². Extracted lipids spread into a compact monolayer of 2.34×108 µm². Dividing yields 1.95, close to the value of 2 expected from a bilayer model. If only 75% of all lipids had been recovered, with the same packing assumptions, the corrected ratio would be 1.95/0.75 = 2.60, no longer a close match. A real interpretation must examine extraction efficiency, cell area, monolayer pressure and the contribution of nonlipid components. The lesson is to assess assumptions before turning a ratio into a structural claim.

One can make an independent molecular counting estimate. If a vesicle has an outer radius of 50 nm and an inner radius of 46 nm, the sum of the approximate leaflet surface areas is 4π(50² + 46²) nm², or about 58,006 nm². For an assumed mean area per lipid of 0.65 nm², the estimated count is about 89,240 lipids. The outer leaflet accounts for approximately 48,330 and the inner leaflet 40,910 molecules. The calculation uses spherical geometry and approximates head-group surface positions by the stated radii, with equal average areas per molecule in both leaflets. It neglects proteins, curvature-dependent area changes and the finite size of head groups. It nevertheless explains why equal leaflet compositions do not imply equal molecule counts in a small vesicle.

The bilayer's aqueous faces and hydrophobic interior also explain selective permeability. Small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen can partition into the hydrocarbon region relatively readily. A hydrated ion must give up much of its favourable hydration and enter a low-dielectric environment to cross the bare lipid interior; this is energetically expensive. Large polar molecules face related barriers. Small uncharged polar molecules may cross to varying extents depending on size, hydration and lipid composition. Thus a lipid bilayer is a selective barrier rather than an absolute wall. Protein-mediated transport, electrochemical gradients and channels are important applications but belong primarily to Unit 2.

Membrane leaflets need not have the same lipid composition. In many animal plasma membranes, PC and sphingomyelin are enriched in the outer leaflet, whereas PE and PS are enriched in the cytosolic leaflet; PI and phosphoinositides are also generally concentrated on the cytosolic side. These are tendencies, not fixed ratios applicable to every organelle. Lipids move laterally within a leaflet relatively readily, but spontaneous transbilayer movement of a conventional phospholipid is often much slower because its polar head would need to traverse the hydrophobic core. Proteins can catalyse or drive transbilayer movement. Exposed PS can become a recognition signal during apoptosis, illustrating a biological consequence of changing asymmetry without changing chemical identity. The detailed transport machinery and fluid mosaic model will be studied in Unit 2.

16. Membrane fluidity, phase transitions and adaptation

A biological membrane must provide a stable barrier while allowing molecular movement. The word fluidity describes aspects of molecular mobility and ordering, not simply whether a membrane feels wet or whether it dissolves in water. In a model saturated phospholipid bilayer at low temperature, chains can pack into a relatively ordered gel phase with restricted motions. Heating can produce a cooperative transition to a liquid-crystalline phase in which chains have greater conformational disorder and lateral mobility. The transition temperature Tm is defined operationally for a particular system and technique. A mixed cellular membrane does not usually have one perfectly sharp melting point because its lipids have heterogeneous compositions and interactions.

Chain length and geometry change the free-energy balance of ordering. Longer saturated tails form more cumulative dispersion contacts, tending to raise Tm when head group and other variables are matched. Cis unsaturation disrupts efficient chain packing and generally lowers Tm. Trans-unsaturated chains pack more like saturated chains than their cis counterparts. A classic model comparison is dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC, two 16:0 chains), which has a main transition around 41 °C, and dioleoylphosphatidylcholine (DOPC, two cis-18:1 chains), which has a main transition near −17 °C in common hydrated preparations. DOPC has longer chains yet a much lower transition temperature; cis unsaturation dominates that particular comparison. These values are approximate and specific to the model phospholipids, not the melting points of free fatty acids.

Cholesterol can moderate the change in physical state with temperature. In a fluid membrane, rigid steroid rings restrict some motions of adjacent chains and generally increase their order, often lowering permeability to small polar solutes. In a gel-prone lipid mixture, cholesterol interferes with the regular interactions needed for tight crystallisation. It can broaden or suppress the sharp cooperative gel-to-fluid transition. This is the basis of the textbook statement that cholesterol buffers membrane fluidity. Do not translate it into the unqualified claim that cholesterol always increases or always decreases fluidity: the temperature, concentration, composition and particular measure of motion matter. Chain order and lateral diffusion are also related but not identical quantities.

Membranes may exhibit ordered and disordered regions within the same overall fluid assembly. Cholesterol and certain long-chain sphingolipids can favour a relatively ordered fluid environment, whereas many cis-polyunsaturated glycerophospholipids favour less ordered regions. The concept of lipid rafts describes small, dynamic, compositionally biased membrane assemblies in living cells; it should not be treated as a permanent macroscopic solid island in every membrane. Model lipid mixtures can show phase coexistence clearly, but cellular organisation also depends on proteins, cortical structures, temperature and active processes. The examinable principle is preferential packing among chemically compatible lipid species.

Many organisms adjust membrane composition when environmental temperature changes, a phenomenon often called homeoviscous adaptation. On cooling, some bacteria and other organisms increase the fraction of cis-unsaturated or shorter-chain lipids, or alter branching and head groups, helping maintain appropriate mobility. On warming, an opposite change may be advantageous. Different organisms use different combinations of mechanisms; for example branched-chain fatty acids contribute substantially in many bacteria. The immediate physical effect of cooling is increased ordering, whereas subsequent regulated lipid remodelling is a biological response. Keeping these timescales separate prevents the erroneous prediction that an organism instantly becomes more fluid on cooling simply because it has adaptive enzymes.

Suppose two bacterial strains have identical lipid head groups, similar growth conditions and similar chain lengths, but strain B cannot increase cis unsaturation after cooling. Immediately after a temperature drop both membranes tend to order. During acclimation, strain A can adjust its lipid composition and recover some mobility, while strain B may remain more ordered if it has no alternative compensatory mechanism. A rescue with an appropriate unsaturated fatty acid may restore fluidity if the strain can incorporate it. Such a rescue tests the hypothesised role of lipid composition but cannot by itself exclude other effects of the mutation. This is the level of causal reasoning required in an experimental NET question.

Thermal and chemical measurements can distinguish composition from state. Differential scanning calorimetry measures heat absorbed during transitions and can show a sharper endotherm for a relatively homogeneous lipid mixture; cholesterol can broaden the signal. Fluorescent probes can report local order or diffusion, depending on probe design. A higher chain-order signal need not mean that every membrane protein stops moving, and a change in probe behaviour may reflect probe localisation. The experimental method must match the physical quantity being claimed. The full diffusion methods and membrane model history belong to Unit 2 and analytical techniques to Unit 14.

17. Lipoproteins and lipid droplets: carrying neutral lipids through water

Triacylglycerol and cholesteryl ester cannot be freely dispersed as individual molecules in blood at the concentrations required for transport. The physical problem resembles the opening oil-and-water example. The solution is a particle with a hydrophobic core and a water-compatible surface. Plasma lipoproteins are composed of a central neutral lipid phase, mainly TAG and cholesteryl esters, surrounded by a surface monolayer of phospholipids, free cholesterol and apolipoproteins. Phospholipid heads and polar protein surfaces contact the aqueous surroundings while hydrophobic surfaces associate with the core. This particle is not a liposome: there is no aqueous lumen enclosed by a bilayer.

Free cholesterol can sit in the surface because its hydroxyl remains available near the water interface. Cholesteryl ester lacks that free hydroxyl and is more compatible with the core. Apolipoproteins are not inert coverings. Some serve as structural scaffolds, some provide recognition sites for receptors, and others regulate enzymes. Amphipathic protein regions can place their hydrophobic side toward lipid and their polar side toward water. A particle's identity therefore reflects both the cargo and the proteins that organise or direct its movement.

Chylomicrons are large, relatively low-density particles that carry mainly dietary TAG from the intestine. VLDL particles are produced by the liver and carry mainly endogenous TAG. As TAG is removed, remnant particles including IDL can become relatively richer in cholesterol ester; LDL particles are important carriers of cholesterol to cells through receptor-dependent mechanisms. HDL particles are relatively protein-rich and participate in cholesterol transport and exchange, including reverse cholesterol transport. These descriptions identify characteristic roles rather than exclusive cargoes. LDL and HDL are not two chemically different forms of cholesterol; the cholesterol molecule can be the same, but the particle transporting it differs.

The classical density sequence is chylomicrons < VLDL < IDL < LDL < HDL, reflecting typical differences in lipid-to-protein proportions. Protein generally has higher density than neutral lipid. Consider a simplified particle containing 75% lipid and 25% protein by mass, with component densities of 0.90 and 1.35 g mL−1. Assuming additive component volumes, its estimated density is 1/(0.75/0.90 + 0.25/1.35) = 0.982 g mL−1. A particle with equal masses of the same two components has estimated density 1/(0.50/0.90 + 0.50/1.35) = 1.08 g mL−1. This model explains the direction of the density trend, not measured density boundaries of real hydrated lipoproteins, which depend on composition, hydration and other features.

18. Reading lipid structures and evidence as a biochemist

Because the term lipid covers many chemically distinct compounds, one experiment rarely identifies an unknown molecule completely. A good biochemical analysis combines separations, chemical reactions and quantitative evidence. Organic solvent extraction exploits the tendency of lipids to partition away from bulk water. More polar lipids can require mixed solvents to disrupt lipid–protein interactions and permit extraction. Extraction is not a universal operation that recovers every lipid equally: short-chain fatty acids, ionised acidic lipids and highly polar head groups can behave differently depending on pH and solvent composition. An apparent compositional difference may therefore be a recovery difference.

In normal-phase thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on polar silica, a lipid that interacts more strongly with the stationary phase tends to move less far under a specified solvent system. Neutral TAG and cholesteryl esters often travel farther than strongly polar phospholipids in suitable normal-phase solvents. The retention factor Rf is the distance from the origin to the compound spot divided by the distance from the origin to the solvent front, so it is dimensionless and ordinarily lies between zero and one. If the solvent front travels 8.0 cm and a spot travels 2.4 cm, Rf = 0.30. A second spot at 6.4 cm has Rf = 0.80. With appropriate standards, these observations can suggest a more polar and a less polar fraction, but Rf alone cannot establish an exact molecular identity. Solvent composition, silica hydration, load and derivatisation can alter migration.

Fatty acid analysis often begins by converting acyl chains to volatile derivatives such as fatty acid methyl esters for gas chromatographic separation. This reveals chain composition but typically loses the original information about which acyl chain occupied which position on a glycerol backbone and which head group it originally accompanied. A sample giving equal quantities of 16:0 and 18:1 might contain mixed-acyl PC, a mixture of two symmetrical TAG species or other molecular arrangements. The observed chain composition alone cannot distinguish these structures. Conversely, intact lipid mass spectrometry can report molecular masses and fragment clues to head-group identity and acyl composition; isomers can still share masses, so exact double-bond positions, geometry or sn assignments may require additional evidence. Detailed instrumentation belongs to Unit 14.

Consider an unknown purified lipid that contains nitrogen and phosphorus, releases choline on suitable head-group cleavage, and yields one free fatty acid after selective cleavage of an amide linkage. These results are compatible with sphingomyelin. PC also contains nitrogen and phosphorus and can yield choline, but it has ester-linked acyl chains rather than the amide-linked fatty acid of ceramide. To distinguish the possibilities conclusively one would examine the backbone or confirm characteristic products, rather than infer glycerol solely from the presence of phosphocholine. A separate sample with a sugar head, no phosphorus and a ceramide framework belongs among glycosphingolipids; negative charge might narrow the possibilities but would not alone identify the sugar sequence.

Experimental reasoning about assemblies requires a different set of observations. An aqueous dye trapped in a particle and released when the lipid shell is solubilised suggests an enclosed aqueous compartment. A hydrophobic dye can partition into many lipid assemblies, including micelles and droplets, and by itself does not establish a vesicle. A size measurement alone does not determine whether an aggregate has a bilayer or a monolayer. The interpretation is strongest when independent measurements of composition, physical organisation and compartmentalisation agree. This is the same principle that made the classic red-cell monolayer-area experiment meaningful but not sufficient by itself.

We can summarise the workflow for an unfamiliar lipid: identify the backbone, distinguish ester, ether, amide and glycosidic bonds, count and characterise hydrocarbon chains, determine polar-group ionisation, then predict organisation under specified conditions. Use controlled comparisons to test the prediction. If a result differs, revisit assumptions about temperature, concentration, phase, mixture and geometry. This workflow provides a general bridge between chemistry, cellular organisation and metabolism rather than a disconnected catalogue of lipid names.

These questions are original examination-style exercises, not reproduced previous-year questions. Attempt the options before opening the worked answer. The Part C problems use idealised datasets and models; read all assumptions carefully.

Part B — Conceptual discrimination

B1. Which structural property is shared by all conventional triacylglycerols?

  1. Three acyl ester linkages on glycerol
  2. A negatively charged phosphate
  3. An amide-linked sphingosine
  4. Four fused rings
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. A TAG contains three glycerol ester bonds even when the chains differ. B describes phosphate-containing lipids; C describes a ceramide framework; D describes steroids.

B2. A fatty acid is 22:6 Δ4,7,10,13,16,19. Which family does it belong to?

  1. n−6
  2. n−9
  3. n−3
  4. n−4
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Subtract the largest delta index from the total chain length: 22−19 = 3. A and B are different omega families, and D confuses the first delta position with methyl-end numbering.

B3. Which pair constitutes the classical essential fatty acids in humans?

  1. Palmitate and stearate
  2. Linoleate and α-linolenate
  3. Oleate and palmitate
  4. Arachidonate and oleate
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Humans cannot introduce the required Δ12 and Δ15 bonds. Saturated acids in A and C can be synthesised; oleate can be formed by Δ9 desaturation. Arachidonate is usually derived from linoleate, although requirements vary with physiological state.

B4. Which compound contains a phosphate-containing head and a ceramide backbone?

  1. Phosphatidylcholine
  2. Glucosylceramide
  3. Triacylglycerol
  4. Sphingomyelin
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Sphingomyelin is a phosphosphingolipid. PC has a glycerol framework; glucosylceramide carries a sugar, not phosphate; TAG is a neutral glycerol triester.

B5. What is the usual formal net charge of phosphatidylserine near neutral pH?

  1. −1
  2. 0
  3. −2
  4. +1
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Phosphate (−1), carboxylate (−1) and ammonium (+1) sum to −1. Zero ignores one negative group, −2 ignores the ammonium, and +1 ignores both acidic groups.

B6. Which statement best distinguishes a cerebroside from a ganglioside?

  1. Both necessarily contain phosphate
  2. Cerebrosides contain one hexose; gangliosides contain sialic acid
  3. Gangliosides have no hydrophobic region
  4. Only gangliosides use ceramide
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The distinction lies in the glycan head. Both have ceramide, neither requires phosphate, and both are amphipathic. Sialic acid makes gangliosides acidic.

B7. Which product pair follows phospholipase D cleavage of phosphatidylcholine?

  1. Lysophosphatidylcholine and fatty acid
  2. Diacylglycerol and phosphocholine
  3. Phosphatidic acid and choline
  4. Ceramide and choline
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. D cleaves the phosphate–head bond. A is an A-type phospholipase product; B follows C-type cleavage; D assumes a sphingoid rather than glycerol backbone.

B8. Which claim about cholesterol is correct?

  1. It contains three ester-linked fatty acids
  2. It is always a strong membrane fluidiser
  3. It is the same chemical entity as LDL
  4. Its free hydroxyl associates near the hydrated membrane interface
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Cholesterol's small OH anchors the polar end near the interface. A describes TAG; B ignores temperature-dependent packing; C confuses a lipid molecule and a transport particle.

B9. Which eicosanoid group is formed downstream of lipoxygenase rather than directly through cyclooxygenase?

  1. Leukotrienes
  2. Thromboxanes
  3. Prostacyclin
  4. Prostaglandins
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. LOX routes generate leukotrienes. Thromboxanes, prostacyclin and prostaglandins are downstream of COX. Distinct branch enzymes explain the selectivity.

B10. Which particle contains a neutral lipid core surrounded by a surface monolayer?

  1. Unilamellar liposome
  2. Plasma lipoprotein
  3. A closed phospholipid bilayer with aqueous lumen
  4. A double-stranded DNA helix
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. A lipoprotein encloses TAG and cholesteryl ester. A and C describe aqueous vesicles; D is a nucleic acid assembly rather than a lipid transport particle.

20. Part C — Quantitative and experimental reasoning

C1. An unbranched fatty acid has formula C20H32O2 and no ring. How many chain double bonds are present?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 5
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The saturated acid is C20H40O2. Eight fewer hydrogens correspond to four C=C bonds. Two, three and five would remove four, six and ten hydrogens respectively. The acid carbonyl is included in the starting formula.

C2. A 20:4 Δ5,8,11,14 fatty acid is elongated by two carbons at its carboxyl end without additional desaturation. What is the product notation?

  1. 22:4 Δ7,10,13,16; n−6
  2. 22:4 Δ5,8,11,14; n−8
  3. 22:4 Δ7,10,13,16; n−8
  4. 22:6 Δ5,8,11,14,17,20
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. All pre-existing delta positions increase by two, so 22−16 = 6. B ignores the shift, C miscalculates the family and D introduces two new double bonds even though elongation alone does not do so.

C3. In an ideal dilute model, a fatty acid has pKa 4.8 and pH 5.8. What fraction is ionised?

  1. 10%
  2. 50%
  3. Approximately 99%
  4. Approximately 91%
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. [A−]/[HA] = 10^(5.8−4.8) = 10. The fraction is 10/(10+1) = 0.909. A reverses the ratio, B requires pH=pKa, and C is closer to a two-unit pH difference. An aggregate can have a different apparent pKa.

C4. Pure triolein has three oleoyl chains and molar mass 885.45 g/mol. Its ideal iodine value is approximately:

  1. 28.7
  2. 86.0
  3. 172.0
  4. 253.8
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Three carbon–carbon double bonds react per molecule. 100×3×253.8/885.45 ≈ 86.0 g I2 equivalent per 100 g. A counts only one chain, C counts twice the correct number, and D ignores the lipid mass and stoichiometry.

C5. Two pure neutral TAGs have saponification values 210 and 180 mg KOH/g. Both have exactly three ester groups. Which statement follows?

  1. The 210 sample has larger molar mass
  2. The 180 sample must be more unsaturated
  3. The 210 sample has smaller molar mass
  4. The 210 sample must have three identical acyl chains
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. M = 168330/SV gives approximately 801.6 versus 935.2 g/mol. A reverses the relationship; B and D infer chain geometry or identity not established by saponification alone.

C6. A neutral TAG yields glycerol and three molecules of palmitic acid on complete hydrolysis. What is its molecular formula?

  1. C51H98O6
  2. C51H104O9
  3. C48H96O6
  4. C51H100O8
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Glycerol C3H8O3 + 3 C16H32O2 − 3 H2O gives C51H98O6. B fails to subtract waters, C omits the glycerol carbons, and D subtracts the wrong numbers of hydrogens and oxygens.

C7. A membrane PC species contains labelled choline only. After complete specific phospholipase C action, which main product carries the label?

  1. Free fatty acid
  2. Diacylglycerol
  3. Phosphatidic acid
  4. Phosphocholine
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. PLC cleaves glycerol–phosphate, leaving the choline attached to phosphate. DAG lacks the head; PA is the product of PLD; a free fatty acid would be produced by PLA and does not contain choline.

C8. A lipid is phosphorus-positive, releases choline after head cleavage and contains one fatty-acid amide bond. The best identification is:

  1. PC
  2. Sphingomyelin
  3. Ganglioside GM1
  4. Cholesteryl ester
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The phosphocholine plus amide-linked acyl chain fits sphingomyelin. PC has ester-linked tails; GM1 contains a carbohydrate head without phosphate; cholesteryl ester has neither phosphocholine nor an amide.

C9. A mixture contains 60 mol% PC, 20 mol% PE, 15 mol% PS and 5 mol% PI. Assuming the usual formal charges and no other lipids, what is the net charge per 100 lipids?

  1. −40 elementary charges
  2. 0
  3. −20 elementary charges
  4. −100 elementary charges
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. PC and PE each contribute zero, PS and PI each contribute −1, giving −15−5=−20. A treats both anionic groups as doubly negative, B disregards PS and PI, and D assigns a charge to every molecule. Actual surface potential also depends on ions and arrangement.

C10. A ganglioside containing two sialic acid residues is treated with an enzyme that removes one without damaging its other groups. Which outcome is most defensible?

  1. Its formal negative charge decreases by one unit in magnitude
  2. It becomes a glycerophospholipid
  3. Its acyl chain must become shorter
  4. Its phosphate head becomes neutral
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. One carboxylate is removed, shifting the formal net charge by +1 near neutral pH. The ceramide and tails have not been changed, and a phosphate is not required in a ganglioside; B, C and D therefore do not follow.

C11. In a model surfactant, v = 0.54 nm³, a0 = 0.60 nm² and lc = 1.80 nm. If the head area increases to 0.90 nm² at fixed v and lc, how does P change?

  1. 0.50 to 0.75, favouring negative curvature
  2. 0.30 to 0.50, favouring bilayers
  3. It remains constant
  4. 0.50 to 0.333, shifting toward more positive curvature
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Initially P=0.54/1.08=0.50; afterwards P=0.54/1.62=0.333. A and B reverse or miscompute the trend; C ignores the denominator. Near heuristic boundaries the precise phase cannot be guaranteed.

C12. Two nonionic surfactants have CMC values differing by a factor of 100 under identical dilute conditions at 298 K. What is the approximate micellisation free-energy difference (lower minus higher CMC)?

  1. +11.4 kJ/mol
  2. −11.4 kJ/mol
  3. −5.7 kJ/mol
  4. 0
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Using RT ln(Xlow/Xhigh), the difference is RT ln(0.01) ≈ −11.4 kJ/mol. A reverses the sign, C corresponds to a tenfold difference, and D ignores the chemical-potential relation. The model assumes comparable standard states.

C13. Extracted red-cell lipids cover 3.6×10^7 µm² in a monolayer; measured cell area is 2.0×10^7 µm². A control estimates 90% lipid recovery. Correcting only for recovery yields which ratio?

  1. 1.62
  2. 1.80
  3. 2.00
  4. 2.22
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The observed ratio is 1.8; dividing by recovery 0.90 gives 2.0. A multiplies instead, B does not correct, and D overcorrects. The corrected ratio is consistent with two leaflets under the stated packing and area assumptions.

C14. A vesicle has outer and inner head-group radii 20 and 16 nm. With equal 0.64 nm² area per lipid in each leaflet, what is the approximate total number of lipids?

  1. 12,880
  2. 7,854
  3. 5,027
  4. 2,050
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Total count is 4π(400+256)/0.64 ≈ 12,881. B counts only the outer leaflet, C only the inner leaflet, and D omits the surface-area factor or misuses units. Equal lipid areas do not mean equal leaflet counts.

C15. At 25 °C, a DPPC membrane (Tm ≈ 41 °C) and DOPC membrane (Tm ≈ −17 °C) are compared. What is the best expected contrast?

  1. Both are necessarily ordered gel phases
  2. DOPC is more ordered because its chains are longer
  3. Both are necessarily in identical fluid states
  4. DPPC is relatively gel-like and DOPC relatively fluid
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. 25 °C is below DPPC's and above DOPC's transition. Cis unsaturation disrupts chain packing despite DOPC's greater chain length. A and C ignore transition temperatures; B ignores geometry. Pure hydrated model membranes are assumed.

C16. Assertion: Cholesterol can reduce chain mobility in a fluid membrane yet oppose crystallisation in a cold gel-prone membrane. Reason: its rigid irregular rings constrain nearby flexible chains but disrupt a regular ordered lattice.

  1. Both true, but the reason is unrelated
  2. Both true, and the reason explains the assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. The same structural features have different effects depending on the starting state. The reason explains both effects; A incorrectly separates them, C denies the packing mechanism and D incorrectly rejects the assertion. This is not a universal claim about every mobility measurement.

C17. A lipoprotein loses much of its core TAG while retaining most of its protein. In a simple density model what is the most plausible trend?

  1. Density decreases and its aqueous lumen expands
  2. Density cannot change because cholesterol is unchanged
  3. Protein mass fraction and density tend to increase
  4. It becomes a DNA-containing vesicle
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Removing a relatively low-density component increases the relative contribution of denser protein; the expected particle density rises. A assumes a nonexistent aqueous lumen and reverses the trend, B ignores changing composition, and D has no structural basis.

C18. In a controlled cell-free assay, phospholipase A2 is inhibited, but adding free arachidonic acid restores COX products. What is the most direct inference?

  1. The inhibited step limited precursor release upstream of COX
  2. PLA2 directly makes prostaglandins from arachidonate
  3. COX was irreversibly inactivated
  4. LOX must have been absent
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Free substrate bypasses the inhibited release step, supporting upstream substrate limitation. B assigns the downstream reaction to PLA2, C conflicts with restoration of COX activity, and D makes an unsupported claim about another branch. Alternative effects require further controls.

C19. An unknown lipid fraction has Rf 0.25 on a given silica TLC plate. It co-migrates with a PS standard. What can be concluded from that evidence alone?

  1. It has an exactly known acyl chain sequence
  2. It must be a neutral TAG
  3. It has the same molecular mass as the standard
  4. PS is a plausible identity, requiring further confirmation
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Co-migration in one solvent system is compatible with PS, not a proof of molecular identity. A and C require structural or mass measurements, and B is unsupported. The same Rf can occur for different compounds.

C20. A membrane-active detergent is added to liposomes containing a water-soluble dye. Above a concentration threshold the dye becomes accessible to an external quencher. Which interpretation is best?

  1. The dye has become a fatty acid
  2. The lipid barrier has become permeable or disrupted
  3. The vesicles necessarily became impermeable
  4. The dye assay proves that every lipid is in a spherical micelle
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Accessibility indicates loss of the initial barrier, whether through leakage, pores or solubilisation. A is a chemical non sequitur, C contradicts increased accessibility, and D goes beyond what the dye assay establishes; direct structural evidence would be needed.

21. Consolidation and misconceptions

Begin with fatty acid structure: identify chain length, double-bond number, positions and geometry. Then recognise the backbone and bonds that determine the chemical class. A glycerol triester provides a neutral storage phase; a two-chain glycerophospholipid supplies a polar hydrated surface; a ceramide supports sphingomyelin or carbohydrate heads; a sterol uses a rigid ring skeleton; an oxidised polyunsaturated chain can become a local mediator. None of these structural descriptions alone specifies every biological property, but each constrains what is possible.

High-yield misconceptions and corrections
MisconceptionCorrect reasoning
All lipids are polymers of fatty acids.There is no universal lipid monomer; sterols lack the conventional acyl backbone.
All unsaturated chains pack equally poorly.Geometry and position matter; trans chains generally pack more tightly than cis isomers.
A zero-net-charge lipid is nonpolar.PC and PE have hydrated zwitterionic heads.
All phospholipids contain glycerol.Sphingomyelin is based on ceramide.
All glycolipids contain sphingosine.Plant galactolipids often use diacylglycerol.
All polyunsaturated fatty acids are essential.Essentiality depends on an organism's synthetic capacity.
Cholesterol always increases fluidity.The effect depends on temperature, composition and physical measurement.
Micelles and liposomes have equivalent interiors.A micelle has a hydrophobic core, a liposome an aqueous lumen.
LDL and HDL are two types of cholesterol.They are compositionally different lipoprotein particles carrying the same cholesterol molecule.
All eicosanoids are prostaglandins.COX, LOX and P450 routes generate different mediator classes.

For a physical prediction, ask which portions of a molecule contact water and which contact other chains. Estimate whether the head is large or small relative to the tail region, compare chain interactions at the specified temperature, and consider whether the surface surrounds oil or water. This method connects neutral storage droplets, micelles, vesicles, lipid bilayers and lipoproteins. For chemical calculations, write the reaction stoichiometry before substituting numbers; verify units, and distinguish an average composition measurement from an exact structural identification. For an experimental problem, ask what was measured, which variables were controlled and what competing explanations remain.

The next chapter, 1.B.03 — Nucleotide Chemistry and DNA Conformation, develops another kind of molecular organisation. Lipid bilayers are assemblies held together largely by noncovalent interactions between distinct lipid molecules. Nucleic acids have covalently linked sugar–phosphate backbones carrying sequence information, yet their higher-order structures also rely on interactions with water, ions and neighbouring molecular groups. That contrast provides the bridge from lipid organisation to the molecular basis of genetic information.
