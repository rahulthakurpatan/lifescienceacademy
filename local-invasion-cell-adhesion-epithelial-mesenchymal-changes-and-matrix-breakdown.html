1. Growth is not the same as invasion

A multicellular animal is not simply a collection of cells. Its survival depends on the arrangement of those cells into reproducible tissue structures. An epithelial cell lining an airway, a gland or the intestine occupies a particular position relative to neighboring cells, to the lumen or external surface, and to underlying connective tissue. Even when a normal epithelial cell divides, the resulting daughters usually remain in a position appropriate to the tissue. Cell division increases cell number; it does not necessarily erase the tissue boundary. The distinction becomes particularly important when studying a tumor. An abnormal population may expand dramatically within a compartment without crossing the boundary that separates the compartment from its surroundings. Another population may be small yet extend into the connective tissue. The second observation demonstrates a change in spatial behavior that cannot be inferred from tumor size alone.

The word invasion describes extension of abnormal cells into tissue that they do not normally occupy. In a carcinoma, which is a malignant neoplasm of epithelial origin, the characteristic boundary is the basement membrane underlying the epithelium. A carcinoma in situ is a preinvasive epithelial neoplastic lesion in which the malignant-appearing cells remain confined by the relevant tissue boundary. Invasive carcinoma contains cells extending beyond that boundary into the surrounding stroma. This is a conceptual distinction rather than a comprehensive account of diagnostic criteria for all organs. The basement membrane is not the plasma membrane of a single epithelial cell: it is an extracellular structure produced by cells and organized into a continuous or specialized supporting network. The location of the tumor cells relative to that structure is more informative about invasion than the appearance of a cell nucleus viewed in isolation.

Consider a cross section through a gland. The luminal surface faces the glandular space, the lateral surfaces contact other epithelial cells and the basal surface faces the basement membrane. A growing abnormal clone may fill the glandular space and crowd the epithelial sheet. This could distort the boundary without necessarily crossing it. A nest of abnormal epithelial cells on the stromal side of the original boundary, however, cannot be explained by simple multiplication inside the gland. Figure 1 represents this distinction geometrically. It also illustrates why the word invasive should not be substituted automatically for aggressive-looking. Histological appearance, extent of growth, local invasion and distant dissemination answer related but different biological questions.

Local invasion is also distinct from metastasis. Metastasis requires the appearance and establishment of a secondary tumor at a discontinuous site. Crossing into the connective tissue may create opportunities for later dissemination, but many invading cells will not successfully establish a secondary lesion. Invasion is therefore an important component of the metastatic process without being synonymous with its completion. In the present chapter the endpoint is a tumor cell or cohesive tumor group occupying adjacent tissue beyond its original compartment. Later vascular entry, transit and colonization belong to the following chapter. Keeping these boundaries clear prevents an examination question about a local tissue section from being answered with an unsupported claim about the distant organs.

Not every cancer begins in an epithelial sheet. Sarcomas arise from mesenchymal tissues, and leukemias concern cells that normally circulate or reside in hematopoietic compartments. Their patterns of tissue infiltration cannot be described solely by the rupture of an epithelial basement membrane. The carcinoma model is nevertheless especially useful because it exposes a recognizable structural barrier and connects cancer biology directly to normal cell adhesion. When an unfamiliar question uses a carcinoma example, ask first what the normal tissue arrangement is, which boundary is being crossed and whether the observation concerns local movement, multiplication or the establishment of a new lesion.

A useful way to understand the boundary problem is to distinguish three scales of organization. At the molecular scale, the cell has receptors, membranes and polymers that can form or break contacts. At the cellular scale, these molecules allow a cell to occupy a particular place, change shape and interact with its neighbors. At the tissue scale, the combined behavior of many cells and extracellular structures establishes a recognizable compartment. A mutation is an event at the first scale, whereas invasion is a description of behavior at the third. A molecular alteration can contribute to the final tissue phenotype, but an observation at one scale cannot simply be substituted for evidence at another. This is especially important in questions that provide a genetic result and ask about a histological outcome. The effect of the alteration must be traced through the organization of the cell and its surroundings before the tissue consequence can be predicted.

The presence of a boundary does not mean that a normal epithelium is completely motionless. Cells may move within the plane of an epithelial sheet during turnover, and individual cells can be replaced while the sheet remains structurally continuous. For example, epithelial turnover in a renewing tissue involves changes in the position of cells along the tissue's established axis. These movements occur in relation to the normal organization of the tissue. If the whole sheet shifts relative to an external reference point during a normal developmental process, that observation does not by itself establish local tumor invasion. An invasion claim requires a suitable reference boundary and identification of the abnormal population. Movement within a normal compartment and movement into another compartment are different outcomes even if both involve the same molecular motor proteins.

The terms hyperplasia and dysplasia help separate growth from tissue architecture. Hyperplasia refers to an increase in cell number and can arise as a regulated response to a physiological stimulus. Dysplasia refers to abnormal cellular and tissue organization in relevant epithelial contexts. These terms do not specify that cells have entered adjacent connective tissue. A lesion may show an alteration in differentiation, orientation or density while remaining within the original compartment. Conversely, tissue infiltration by abnormal cells provides a distinct form of information even if the invasive focus is not large. The relationships among these terms are best understood as descriptions of different properties rather than a required fixed sequence through which every tumor passes. This prevents the erroneous deduction that a particular morphological change is always sufficient to establish the next step.

2. The extracellular matrix as a physical and signaling environment

The extracellular matrix, abbreviated ECM, consists of material outside cells that provides mechanical support and conveys information to the cells contacting it. It is not an inert packing substance. Connective tissue contains a meshwork of fibrous proteins, adhesive glycoproteins and hydrated molecules. Collagen fibers resist tension, whereas proteoglycans and their glycosaminoglycan chains influence hydration and compression. Fibronectin provides binding sites for receptors and other extracellular components. The exact combination varies across tissues and changes during repair. A cell moving into connective tissue encounters not just a chemical environment but also a space that has geometry, stiffness and resistance. A soluble growth signal cannot by itself create an opening large enough for a cell to move through an intact network.

A basement membrane is a specialized sheet-like form of ECM. Its major structural components include laminins and type IV collagen, supported by associated molecules such as nidogens and heparan sulfate proteoglycans. Laminins are extracellular glycoproteins that connect with cellular receptors and contribute to network assembly. Type IV collagen forms a network distinct from the long fibrils formed by type I collagen in many interstitial connective tissues. The contrast matters because a cell passing through a basement membrane and a cell moving through a fibrous collagen-rich stroma face different physical structures. Both are ECM, but they are not equivalent barriers, and a result obtained in one model matrix cannot automatically be generalized to the other. Figure 2 distinguishes the sheet-like basement network from interstitial fibers.

The barrier function of a basement membrane is inseparable from its organizing function. By providing a basal binding surface, it contributes to the orientation and survival of epithelial cells. It distributes mechanical loads and helps establish the difference between a basal surface and a luminal surface. Its molecules also influence receptor signaling and can bind extracellular signaling molecules. Thus a change in the matrix may affect cell behavior even before there is an obvious discontinuity in the tissue. A tumor cell contacting a remodeled basement membrane may experience different receptor occupancy, altered force transmission and altered survival signaling. It is misleading to imagine invasion as merely dissolving a wall in front of a mechanically independent cell.

The interstitial environment presents another class of challenge. In a relatively open matrix, cells may pass between fibers without extensive cleavage of all surrounding material. A narrow opening can resist passage, especially when the nucleus is larger or less deformable than the cytoplasmic extensions. The cell may deform, the matrix may deform, or the local network may be remodeled. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. Geometry provides a useful basis for prediction: a result observed in an open, two-dimensional surface assay may fail to predict what happens when the entire cell must traverse a three-dimensional network. The question is not simply whether an enzyme is present, but whether the relevant physical obstruction is overcome.

Basement membranes can be remodeled in normal physiology. For example, tissue remodeling during development and repair involves coordinated changes in cells and ECM. Controlled remodeling is not a sufficient diagnosis of malignancy. Normal cells may move as part of a regulated process that restores organized tissue; invasive tumor growth violates the boundaries of normal tissue architecture. Matrix discontinuities can also arise from damage, sample preparation or ordinary turnover. Demonstrating an invasive tumor requires spatial evidence linking abnormal cells to penetration into surrounding tissue rather than treating the absence of a single matrix stain as a self-sufficient conclusion.

At a smaller structural scale, a fibrillar collagen and a network-forming collagen both depend on the properties of protein chains and their extracellular assemblies. Collagen molecules contain regions with a characteristic triple-helical organization, but the way molecules associate outside the cell differs among collagen types. In the basement membrane, the noncollagenous regions of type IV collagen participate in network organization. A network distributes mechanical constraints in multiple directions. A bundle of elongated fibers presents a different landscape, in which orientation may be more conspicuous than continuity of a sheet. The molecular distinction therefore becomes relevant to the behavior of an entire cell: a narrow fiber arrangement may create anisotropic resistance, whereas a continuous basement network constrains entry across a surface.

Laminin illustrates a complementary aspect of extracellular architecture. A laminin molecule consists of three polypeptide chains arranged into a multidomain structure. Different regions of the protein participate in association with other matrix components or cell-surface receptors. A cell interacting with laminin does not need to synthesize the entire basement membrane itself; the extracellular assembly depends on contributions from cells and the compatibility of the secreted components. An alteration in laminin organization may change the availability of basal attachment sites without eliminating every molecule of laminin. In tissue images, intensity of a stain reports detectable material under particular conditions, whereas the continuity and organization of the material are spatial properties. These features should not be collapsed into a single measurement of matrix abundance.

The network also contains molecules capable of influencing water retention and the local distribution of other proteins. A highly hydrated matrix occupies a volume that is not explained by dry protein mass alone. This matters when comparing tissues with different extracellular compositions. Two tissues with similar amounts of a particular collagen can differ in accessible space because the other components, hydration and fiber arrangement differ. A cell encounters the assembled network rather than an isolated ligand dissolved in a uniform solution. This is why a claim such as 'more collagen means less invasion' is not a general law. Increased collagen could present an obstacle, but orientation, cross-linking, available openings and the behavior of the cells may alter the relationship. A quantitative comparison should identify exactly what feature of the matrix has been measured.

The mechanical concepts of stiffness and strength deserve separate definitions. Stiffness describes resistance to deformation under an applied force, whereas strength concerns the ability to withstand stress without failure. A network may be relatively stiff but susceptible to breaking, or it may deform substantially without losing continuity. The cell's experience of a matrix is further influenced by whether the deformation is elastic, whether material relaxes over time and whether fibers can rearrange. A simple spring analogy can represent one limited aspect of stiffness: in an ideal linear regime, a force proportional to displacement implies a constant effective stiffness. Living matrices are heterogeneous and often nonlinear, so a single spring cannot describe the entire tissue. This distinction supports interpretation of exam questions about force without confusing a material property with the number of cells that cross it.

3. Cell–cell adhesion and the organization of epithelial collectives

A normal epithelial sheet withstands mechanical stress while preserving its shape because neighboring cells form several types of junction. Adherens junctions couple cells to the actin cytoskeleton; desmosomes provide important links to intermediate filaments; tight junctions help restrict passage between cells and maintain membrane-domain organization. These junctions are not interchangeable. The loss of a sealing function does not prove the loss of all tensile coupling, and a defect in one adhesion molecule does not establish that every junction has vanished. During local invasion, a particularly important change can occur in the way the adherens junction connects epithelial cells to each other.

Classical cadherins are transmembrane proteins whose extracellular domains participate in calcium-dependent adhesion. E-cadherin, encoded by CDH1, is especially characteristic of many epithelial tissues. On the cytoplasmic side, the cadherin-associated proteins include p120-catenin and beta-catenin, and alpha-catenin participates in coupling the complex to the actin machinery. This arrangement connects extracellular recognition with forces generated inside the cell. It explains why measuring surface E-cadherin alone is not identical to measuring the mechanical integrity of a junction. Adhesive function also depends on localization, organization, the state of the cytoplasmic linkage and the behavior of neighboring cells. Figure 3 places these molecular components on their appropriate sides of the plasma membranes.

The junction can be thought of as a dynamic connection rather than a permanent weld. Cadherins are trafficked, and junctions can be assembled, rearranged or disassembled in response to normal tissue needs. During division or wound repair, neighboring cells must sometimes change their contacts without abandoning the epithelial identity of the entire population. In a tumor, reduced expression, altered localization or impaired coupling of the adhesion complex can reduce the tendency of cells to remain integrated with their neighbors. A decrease in adhesion may make separation easier, but it does not provide the cell with an independent source of motive force. If its cytoskeleton cannot move the cell or the matrix is impassable, reduced cohesion alone does not prove invasion.

The association of cadherin loss with invasive behavior raised a question that could not be resolved by comparing different tumors: does disrupted adhesion contribute to invasion or is it merely a consequence of another change? In 1989 Behrens and colleagues examined epithelial behavior after interference with uvomorulin, the protein now commonly referred to as E-cadherin. Their findings connected altered cell contact to acquisition of invasive characteristics in the system studied. A later study by Frixen and colleagues examined the suppressive role of E-cadherin-mediated adhesion in carcinoma cells. These classical papers helped move the field from descriptive observation toward functional reasoning. Their conclusions must nevertheless be understood in the context of the tested cells and experimental readouts, not transformed into an absolute rule that every invasive cell has lost CDH1.

The multifunctionality of beta-catenin illustrates a common exam trap. Beta-catenin participates in adherens junctions, but it can also participate in transcriptional regulation in canonical Wnt signaling. A change in one pool of a protein does not establish that all other pools have changed in a particular direction. For example, reduced staining at a junction does not by itself prove activation of Wnt-dependent transcription. Protein stabilization, localization and the state of the relevant regulatory pathway matter. When a question supplies only a change in junction staining, the strongest defensible conclusion concerns junction organization. Additional information would be required to infer nuclear transcriptional effects.

Cohesive invasion makes the role of cadherin especially interesting. If some cells remain physically attached to each other while an entire group crosses a tissue boundary, adhesion is no longer merely a restraint. Contacts can transmit forces and help coordinate neighboring cells during movement. The same broad class of junction that stabilizes a normal epithelium can contribute to the internal organization of a moving group. The biological outcome depends on where the connections persist and where the group interacts with its surroundings. This is why a static diagram showing individual cells separating must be treated as one possible model of invasion, not as the universal mechanism.

The molecular basis of calcium-dependent adhesion offers a clear bridge from chemistry to tissue organization. Calcium ions stabilize the arrangement of extracellular repeats in classical cadherins. This helps maintain an ectodomain configuration capable of forming appropriate intercellular contacts. The ions are not themselves a replacement for the receptor, nor do they act as ATP molecules powering a mechanical motor. The receptor must still be present and properly organized. Because many extracellular molecules and intracellular pathways can depend on calcium, an observation made after a broad change in calcium availability is not specific evidence about cadherins alone. Selectivity of an interpretation depends on which competing mechanisms the evidence excludes.

A cell contains cadherins that are not all necessarily engaged in an adhesive contact at a particular moment. Newly synthesized protein must travel through the secretory pathway before arriving at an appropriate membrane surface. Existing protein can be removed from the surface, recycled or degraded. An internal pool of cadherin and a junctional pool therefore have different immediate consequences for tissue cohesion. The total quantity in the cell may remain constant during redistribution. A change in cell contact can arise on a shorter time scale than a change in synthesis or gene sequence. The student should distinguish regulation through trafficking from regulation through transcription; both can change the effective amount available at a junction but they need not leave the same experimental signature.

Tissue cohesion is an emergent property of many contacts. If one intercellular bond is disrupted, surrounding bonds may continue to carry force. Conversely, a modest alteration distributed across many contacts may have a larger tissue consequence than expected from considering one bond alone. This distinction between individual binding events and a collectively organized adhesive interface resembles the difference between a single receptor interaction and the avidity of a multivalent contact. The forces carried by different contacts can be redistributed as the sheet deforms. Thus the amount of E-cadherin measured across an entire tissue does not translate into a simple numerical prediction of the force required to separate two cells.

Junctions also help coordinate cell shape. Tight junctions are usually positioned toward the apical part of a polarized epithelium, whereas adherens junctions and desmosomes have distinct structural functions. Hemidesmosomes, despite the similar name, are basal cell–matrix adhesion structures rather than connections between neighboring epithelial cells. In normal epithelia, integrin-based hemidesmosomal connections can link the basement membrane to intermediate filaments. This contrasts with many focal adhesions that couple to actin. A change in basal adhesion may influence the stability of the entire epithelial arrangement even if lateral contacts remain intact. A well-designed exam question may therefore ask the student to distinguish failure of a basal linkage from failure of a lateral junction. Each affects a different interface.

4. Cell–matrix adhesion and the traction problem

Once a cell is capable of changing its contacts with neighboring cells, it still faces the problem of moving relative to the extracellular environment. A cell cannot advance simply by contracting its interior in free space: forces must interact with the surroundings. Many animal cells solve this problem using adhesive receptors called integrins, which bind extracellular ligands and couple to intracellular proteins. Integrins are alpha–beta heterodimers, and the pairing of the subunits helps determine ligand recognition. Depending on the heterodimer, relevant ligands can include fibronectin, collagen or laminin. This receptor family is therefore a major connection between matrix composition and cellular behavior.

Integrins do not ordinarily possess an intrinsic protein kinase catalytic domain. Signals produced following integrin engagement involve associated proteins, including focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and Src-family kinases in appropriate contexts. Other proteins, including talin and vinculin, connect integrin-associated assemblies to actin and help regulate force transmission. An integrin molecule spans the membrane, while a focal adhesion is an assembly of many molecules rather than a single covalent linkage. Figure 4 shows the position of ligand, receptor and cytoskeleton. The distinction is useful when predicting the effect of a mutation: ligand recognition, receptor abundance and intracellular force coupling are different variables.

Adhesion creates a balance between attachment and release. At one extreme, a cell making very few effective attachments may have difficulty transmitting traction to the substrate. At another extreme, very stable contacts may impede repositioning of the trailing region. Between those extremes, attachment and turnover can permit efficient movement. This reasoning is often represented by a qualitative peaked relationship between migration speed and adhesion strength, but such a curve is a context-dependent model, not a universal experimental law. In a confined environment some cells rely less strongly on conventional focal adhesions than cells crawling across a planar substrate. The physical geometry and mode of migration are essential qualifications.

Integrin activation also illustrates bidirectional signaling. Intracellular events may alter ligand-binding capacity or receptor organization, a process often described as inside-out regulation. Conversely, extracellular ligand engagement and force can promote intracellular signaling, often called outside-in signaling. This reciprocity allows a cell to adjust to its surroundings rather than respond only to the concentration of an external ligand. A change in matrix stiffness can alter the forces sensed through adhesive structures even when the same protein is present. Likewise, the concentration of a matrix molecule cannot specify the strength of attachment without information about its accessibility and receptor state.

A further consequence of matrix adhesion concerns survival. Many normal anchorage-dependent epithelial cells undergo apoptosis after losing appropriate matrix attachment, a response known as anoikis. An abnormal cell that survives without its usual attachment has escaped one safeguard against occupying an inappropriate location. Survival and locomotion are still distinct properties. A cell that resists anoikis may remain viable after detachment but fail to move; a highly motile cell may die if it loses essential survival signals. This connects the present topic to the chapter on apoptosis escape without replacing its account of the biochemical death pathway. In a Part C problem, read viability and movement as separate endpoints before interpreting a change in the number of observed cells.

One example of ligand recognition is the interaction of fibronectin with appropriate integrins through sequence-dependent recognition. A familiar fibronectin motif is RGD, which denotes arginine, glycine and aspartate. The motif is not a universal binding sequence recognized identically by all integrins. Receptor identity, the surrounding molecular structure and the presentation of the ligand influence the interaction. A molecule containing these three amino acids in an arbitrary location is not necessarily an effective physiological adhesive ligand. This is an example of the larger principle that a short chemical feature functions within a structural context. In tissue, the accessibility and orientation of a ligand may differ from its accessibility as an isolated fragment.

An integrin can change its affinity for a ligand as its conformation and intracellular interactions change. Receptor clustering can also change the effective strength of a cellular contact without requiring the same change in the affinity of each receptor molecule. Affinity describes the behavior of an individual binding interaction under specified conditions; avidity or collective attachment reflects the combined action of multiple interactions. These distinctions are useful in interpreting a measurement of adhesion. A population could express the same number of receptors but exhibit different attachment because the receptors differ in conformation or spatial organization. Conversely, more receptors do not guarantee that all of them participate in a force-bearing contact.

The lifetime of an attachment is relevant to movement as well as its initial formation. A force-bearing contact that persists long enough to transmit stress can permit the cell to reposition. If contacts are continuously lost before they transmit useful force, the shape of the cell may change without effective motion of its center. If contacts persist after their useful period, they can oppose release of the trailing region. These are distinct reasons why movement can be inefficient. The same observation of a low average speed is therefore compatible with defects at opposite ends of the adhesion spectrum. Direct information about the contacts and the position of the cell over time is needed to distinguish them.

Adhesion signaling can be considered in relation to the cell's prior regulatory state. FAK is a cytoplasmic kinase associated with adhesive complexes, not a catalytic domain intrinsic to an integrin receptor. Phosphorylation can create or modify binding sites for proteins in a signaling assembly. Such associations connect adhesion with pathways that regulate cytoskeletal organization and survival. However, detection of a phosphorylated signaling protein at a cell edge does not demonstrate which extracellular ligand originally caused the change. Several receptor systems can influence overlapping downstream signals. This is another example of convergence in cell signaling: observing a downstream state is informative but often insufficient to identify a unique upstream cause.

A common misconception is that the mechanical coupling provided by an adhesive complex must always remain stable throughout an entire migration event. Dynamic regulation is needed because the cell encounters different local surfaces as it changes position. The front of a moving cell may interact with material that has not been encountered by the rear, and forces can change as the body passes through a restricted space. In a group, cells at the center can experience cell–cell forces without touching every external matrix surface. Thus receptor localization should be understood in relation to the position of each cell in the tissue. This gives a structural explanation for why a measurement averaged across a tumor can conceal important differences between the boundary and the interior.

5. Polarity, actin dynamics and movement through tissue

A stationary epithelial cell is polarized with respect to the sheet: its apical and basal domains differ in position and function. A moving cell may also be polarized, but the relevant axis can be front to rear. These two forms of polarity are not equivalent. A shift away from the organization of the original epithelium may allow a cell to establish a moving front and a trailing region. A leading extension alone is not proof of productive movement, because the rest of the cell may remain stationary. To demonstrate movement, the position of the cell body must change over time relative to its environment.

Actin polymerization helps generate pushing forces at membrane protrusions. The molecular machinery that controls actin assembly can create broad sheet-like lamellipodia or other forms of protrusive activity. Contractility generated by myosin interacting with actin can reorganize the cell cortex and transmit force to adhesive contacts. These processes require energy: assembly dynamics depend on nucleotide-associated actin states, and myosin uses ATP hydrolysis to produce mechanical work. The whole cell operates away from equilibrium. An externally imposed concentration gradient can orient signaling, but the gradient is not itself the source of all mechanical work. Energy metabolism remains necessary for the cellular machinery that executes movement.

In a simplified adhesion-dependent crawling model, the front extends, new contacts form, internal contractile forces advance the cell body and trailing contacts release. These activities overlap temporally rather than occurring as four perfectly separated stages. A real cell may extend in more than one direction and subsequently retract unsuccessful protrusions. Net directional movement depends on the persistence and coordination of protrusive and contractile behavior. Figure 5 separates these mechanical operations while emphasizing that the cell remains a continuous object. A question asking which process becomes defective if a cell forms protrusions without moving must be answered with the available evidence rather than by assuming that protrusion is sufficient.

Small GTP-binding proteins help organize actin and contractility. Rac-associated signaling commonly supports lamellipodial organization; RhoA-associated signaling can promote actomyosin contractility, and Cdc42 is frequently involved in polarity and protrusive organization. These are useful associations, not exclusive assignments that hold in every cell type. Their activity is regulated in space and time through nucleotide cycling and interacting regulatory proteins. The same cell can use several of these activities simultaneously in different regions. It is often more informative to ask where a signal is active than to ask only whether a protein is present in a whole-cell lysate.

Movement is not synonymous with invasion because the surrounding matrix may impose a further obstacle. Two cells may show identical speeds when moving on an unobstructed surface but differ dramatically in their capacity to pass through tissue. One might transmit sufficient force through a matrix, whereas the other remains confined by its geometry. In a dense network, the position and deformability of the nucleus can become important because the nucleus is a relatively large intracellular structure. The cell does not need to dissolve every fiber along its route, but the combined cell and matrix deformation must be compatible with passage. These relationships link cytoskeletal organization, extracellular structure and cellular-scale physical constraints.

Directional movement can be influenced by soluble cues, substrate-bound ligands and mechanical differences. Chemotaxis denotes directionally biased movement associated with a soluble chemical gradient. Haptotaxis denotes directional responses to spatial differences in substrate-associated adhesive cues. A change in average speed without a preferred direction is not sufficient evidence for either kind of directional response. Durotaxis describes biased movement in relation to stiffness gradients in relevant systems. These concepts are helpful for distinguishing a signal that increases general motility from one that provides spatial information. They are not a claim that every invading tumor follows the same cue.

The actin filament is a polar polymer. Its ends differ in their assembly properties, and accessory proteins regulate the availability of polymerizable actin, branching and disassembly. At a protruding edge, the arrangement of growing filaments can contribute to outward displacement of the membrane. This is possible because the force generated by polymer assembly is transmitted to the boundary of the cell. The plasma membrane also has tension and interacts with the underlying cortex, so protrusion is not a simple consequence of producing more filament mass everywhere. The spatial arrangement of the filaments and the mechanical state of the membrane influence the shape that develops. A cell can contain large amounts of filamentous actin without showing a persistent protrusion in any one direction.

A lamellipodium and a filopodium exemplify different arrangements of actin. A lamellipodium is a broad protrusion often associated with branched networks, whereas filopodia are narrow projections with more bundled filaments. These are structural descriptions; their presence does not automatically specify the rate of movement or the cell's ability to cross a tissue barrier. Different protrusions can coexist, and their appearance may change when the cell encounters a new geometry. The student should distinguish the microscopic identity of a protrusion from its functional contribution to a given movement. For example, an image of many narrow projections does not establish that the whole cell moved farther than a cell with one broad projection.

Myosin-driven contraction depends on how motor proteins interact with the actin network. A motor's activity generates relative movement between filaments or tension when movement is constrained. That tension can be transmitted through cell–matrix contacts or through junctions in a collective. Force production alone does not fix the direction of movement; the asymmetry of the cell, its attachments and the constraints around it matter. In a symmetric idealization, equal and opposite forces can produce internal stress without translating the entire object. Directional migration therefore requires coordination between mechanical activity and spatial organization. This principle links basic mechanics to the concept of cellular polarity.

Membrane blebs provide another example of the need to distinguish different protrusive processes. A bleb is a local outward deformation of the membrane associated with the mechanical state of the cortex and internal pressure. The initial expansion of a bleb differs from protrusion driven primarily by actin assembly at a lamellipodium. The cortex can subsequently reorganize around the bleb. Rounded cells in some environments can display bleb-associated movement. Yet observing a rounded cell in a fixed preparation is not enough to determine that it was moving by this mechanism. The evidence must include behavior or other information relevant to the proposed process. The structural categories are useful starting points, not replacements for dynamic observations.

The GTPase switch mechanism introduces a second type of energy-related distinction. Rho-family regulatory proteins can cycle between nucleotide-bound states, with guanine nucleotide exchange factors and GTPase-activating proteins influencing the cycle. The GTP-bound state generally favors interaction with particular effectors, while GTP hydrolysis contributes to resetting the switch. The chemical energy associated with the switch is not the same as the ATP hydrolysis performed by myosin. One set of reactions helps regulate where and when other activities occur; the other directly contributes to mechanical work. An exam that supplies a defective nucleotide cycle should therefore be interpreted in terms of signaling control, not automatically as a defect in the catalytic site of a motor protein.

6. Matrix remodeling and metalloproteinase activity

The previous sections explain how a cell changes its connections and generates movement. Neither process guarantees that a dense matrix barrier will permit passage. One important means of altering that barrier is proteolysis, the cleavage of peptide bonds by enzymes. Matrix metalloproteinases, or MMPs, are a family of enzymes whose catalytic activity depends on a metal ion, typically zinc at the active site. Some are secreted, and others are associated with membranes. Different members have overlapping but nonidentical substrate preferences. Consequently, a measurement of one MMP transcript does not identify all of the matrix-remodeling processes occurring around a tumor.

An MMP molecule can be synthesized as an inactive precursor, or zymogen, in which a propeptide prevents productive catalytic activity. In many family members activation involves removal or displacement of the inhibitory propeptide. The mature catalytic domain binds zinc and facilitates peptide-bond hydrolysis through a coordinated water molecule. Zinc is a catalytic cofactor, not an energy currency consumed in proportion to the number of bonds cleaved. The reaction is hydrolysis, rather than ATP-powered cutting by the enzyme. ATP may still be required for protein synthesis, trafficking and the motile cell's mechanical operations. Separating the energy requirements of these different processes avoids attributing the entire phenomenon of invasion to a single ATP-dependent reaction.

MMP-2 and MMP-9 are often called gelatinases because of their ability to digest gelatin and certain other substrates. MMP-14, also called membrane-type 1 MMP or MT1-MMP, is an example of a membrane-associated enzyme important in matrix remodeling. Anchoring a protease at the cell surface can restrict where it acts, creating a spatial relationship between the cell and the altered matrix. This does not mean that secreted proteases are unimportant. It means that the position of an enzyme may matter as much as its amount. The localized distribution of proteolytic activity helps explain why a matrix can be modified at one cell boundary while remaining relatively intact elsewhere.

Tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases, or TIMPs, contribute to extracellular control of MMP activity. The relationships are more nuanced than the assertion that every TIMP always decreases every MMP-dependent process. For example, TIMP-2 can participate in a complex involved in cell-surface activation of proMMP-2 under appropriate conditions, while inhibitory interactions constrain activity. For NET and SET purposes, the essential principle is that extracellular enzyme activity reflects more than transcription: synthesis, secretion or membrane localization, activation, inhibitor availability and substrate accessibility all contribute. The level of messenger RNA is one step in this chain, not its final output.

Proteolysis can modify matrix signaling as well as geometry. Cleaving an ECM protein may alter an adhesive binding site or liberate a fragment with biological activity. Matrix alteration can also change how a force is transmitted to the cell. Therefore a protease-dependent phenotype does not necessarily arise only from creation of a larger physical opening. The two effects may occur together. Conversely, an opening in a network may result from mechanical displacement or preexisting anatomy rather than enzyme cleavage. A visual gap is evidence of an altered boundary, but it cannot by itself identify the chemical mechanism responsible for that alteration.

Research on membrane-associated MMPs has helped establish a functional connection between spatially localized proteolysis and movement through collagen-rich tissues. The study reported by Sabeh and colleagues in 2004 compared cells with altered protease function and found that MT1-MMP was important for collagen-invasive behavior in the model investigated. A conceptual examination of that result distinguishes the specific matrix and cells tested from other tissue environments. Some cells can move through sufficiently open spaces with little need for matrix cleavage, and some collective groups follow paths already altered by surrounding cells. A selective decrease in collagen barrier crossing is informative about the experimental system, not proof that all invasion everywhere depends on exactly the same enzyme.

The distinction between matrix degradation and matrix remodeling is worth retaining. Degradation implies molecular breakdown, whereas remodeling includes rearrangement, deposition, alignment, deformation and degradation. Fibroblasts can deposit new matrix while other processes remove or modify existing components. A remodeled matrix may be denser or more aligned rather than uniformly depleted. The behavior of an invading population is governed by the local organization of this changing material. Figure 6 depicts intact and interrupted matrix segments alongside an extracellular protease, while avoiding a claim that every matrix molecule must be digested.

An extracellular protease encounters substrates with different degrees of accessibility. A protein in an extended, soluble form does not present the same constraints as the same protein incorporated into a tightly organized extracellular assembly. Cleavage depends on recognition of susceptible regions and the ability of the enzyme to reach them. Consequently, the statement that a purified enzyme can cleave a protein under one set of conditions does not establish that every copy of that protein in a tissue is equally accessible. The substrate's conformation, its interactions with neighboring molecules and the location of the enzyme can influence the observed outcome. This helps explain why substrate specificity and tissue-level effectiveness must be considered separately.

The catalytic chemistry of an MMP can be distinguished from the regulation of its localization. In hydrolysis of a peptide bond, water participates in breaking the covalent linkage, yielding products with altered terminal groups. An enzyme lowers the kinetic barrier to the reaction without changing the equilibrium dictated by the reaction's thermodynamics. For a biological extracellular network, the important consequence is often that cleavage changes how its components remain connected. The reaction can therefore have a macroscopic effect even if the total mass of matrix proteins near the cell has not immediately disappeared. A cleaved fragment may remain in the region, yet its ability to transmit tension or bind another structure can differ from that of the intact protein.

For many MMPs, the propeptide contributes to latency through a cysteine-containing region interacting with the catalytic zinc. Activation changes the relationship between the inhibitory segment and the catalytic center. A protein assay that recognizes both precursor and mature forms can give a strong signal while failing to reveal the relative quantities of those forms. Conversely, a change in the fraction of mature enzyme could change activity without an increase in total protein. These biochemical principles explain the value of separating abundance, maturation and catalytic function in an examination dataset. They also show why the word expression should be used carefully: it may refer to RNA, total protein or a specific protein form depending on what was measured.

Matrix cleavage also occurs in normal tissues, and its products are not inherently pathological. Tissue remodeling involves the balance of synthesis, modification and removal. During wound repair, changes in this balance are coordinated with new tissue formation and the restoration of a functional boundary. In a tumor, altered cellular interactions can disrupt normal spatial and temporal control. A single enzyme family is therefore not exclusive to malignant tissue. Detecting an MMP is not sufficient evidence of invasion because normal tissue remodeling and immune-cell functions also involve proteases. The question is whether the activity contributes to an observed abnormal tissue behavior in the context under discussion.

7. Epithelial–mesenchymal transition as a change of cellular state

Epithelial–mesenchymal transition, abbreviated EMT, refers to changes in cellular organization through which epithelial cells acquire some or many properties associated with a mesenchymal state. A mesenchymal cell is generally less organized into an epithelial sheet and may interact with the extracellular environment using a different pattern of adhesion and polarity. The word transition emphasizes that these properties can change. EMT is not synonymous with genetic transformation into cancer, and a cell acquiring mesenchymal characteristics need not become malignant. During embryonic development, transitions of this general kind are part of normal morphogenesis. Their use in cancer provides an example of altered regulation of existing cellular capabilities.

An epithelial state can include coordinated expression of junctional proteins, epithelial intermediate filaments and apical–basal polarity components. Mesenchymal-associated changes may involve increased expression of vimentin or particular cadherins, rearranged cytoskeletal architecture and altered adhesive behavior. The exact combination depends on the tissue and the state of the cells. A cadherin switch, often described in terms of reduced E-cadherin and increased N-cadherin in particular systems, is one possible feature rather than a universal definition. Marker expression is evidence about a feature of a state. It is not an independent observation of the complete biological process.

Signals from the environment can influence gene regulation. Transforming growth factor beta, or TGF-beta, is a well-known example of a signal that can promote epithelial plasticity in suitable cellular contexts. Receptor signaling can influence transcriptional regulators such as members of the SNAIL, ZEB and TWIST families. These regulators participate in changes in gene expression affecting epithelial organization and mesenchymal-associated features. The important principle is that signaling can alter a program of expression across several genes instead of changing only the catalytic activity of one protein. The resulting cellular state may persist longer than an initiating transient signal, yet its persistence does not necessarily imply that the DNA sequence has changed.

A signal's biological effect depends on cellular context. TGF-beta can have growth-inhibitory effects in some epithelial settings while being associated with altered migration or invasion in other cancer settings. This apparent contrast is not a contradiction: downstream pathways, existing alterations and the state of the tissue can change the response. A question reporting activation of a receptor is therefore not always sufficient to predict the final phenotype without specifying the system. The interpretation must follow the supplied context rather than importing a universal label of tumor suppressor or tumor promoter.

EMT does not have to be an all-or-none switch. Cells may retain some epithelial characteristics while gaining some mesenchymal properties, producing intermediate or hybrid states. The international consensus on EMT terminology published in 2020 stresses the importance of cellular plasticity and functional evidence. Figure 7 represents a range of possible states, not a compulsory linear trajectory traversed by every cell. Changes can sometimes reverse, a process related to mesenchymal–epithelial transition (MET). Reversibility is conceptually useful because it distinguishes gene-regulatory and phenotypic changes from irreversible genetic deletion. A cell may, however, also acquire mutations that make a state persist; the two classes of explanation are not mutually exclusive.

One marker is rarely sufficient to demonstrate a whole transition. An immunostain showing lower E-cadherin could reflect changes in transcription, protein trafficking, the identity of the sampled cells or an altered proportion of normal stromal cells. An increase in vimentin in a whole-tissue sample might likewise reflect fibroblasts rather than tumor cells. Cell-type identification and evidence from more than one feature help distinguish these alternatives. Functional observations of cell organization and behavior provide complementary information. In an exam question, the strongest answer usually acknowledges the observed change while refusing to infer a complete EMT when the necessary evidence is absent.

The word phenotype refers to the observable properties of a cell or organism resulting from the interaction of genetic information with its environment and regulatory state. It is not interchangeable with genotype. The same epithelial tumor lineage may contain cells showing different marker patterns because they experience different surroundings or because their regulatory states differ. A phenotype can also persist after the initiating signal has changed, depending on regulatory feedback and the lifetime of the relevant molecules. Such persistence does not by itself establish a DNA sequence alteration. Comparing cells by genotype and by phenotype therefore answers different questions, and both kinds of information may be needed to explain heterogeneous behavior.

The regulatory logic of an EMT-associated transcriptional program differs from the logic of a direct change in one extracellular adhesive bond. A transcription factor can influence the production of multiple proteins, leading to coordinated changes that appear over time as existing molecules turn over. In contrast, a preexisting receptor can be redistributed or change its interactions on a different time scale. The two processes can interact, because changing transcription changes the pool of molecules available for redistribution. A question showing rapid alteration in the position of a preexisting protein does not necessarily require transcriptional repression as its explanation. A longer-term change in several gene products is compatible with a broader regulatory program, but the relevant evidence must distinguish it from changes in cell composition.

The epithelial intermediate filament keratins and mesenchymal-associated vimentin offer a useful contrast in structural phenotype. Intermediate filaments contribute to mechanical properties and cellular organization, but expression of these proteins does not specify the direction in which the cell moves. Some cells can express proteins associated with more than one conventional category. Furthermore, marker identity depends on lineage and tissue context. A marker useful in one system may be present in a normal cell population of another system. When examining cancer-associated epithelial plasticity, it is therefore important to know whether the cells being compared originate from the epithelial lineage and whether the measurements concern the same population over time.

Reversibility does not imply that all individual cells are able to return to their previous state under every circumstance. A population may change its average marker pattern because some cells altered state, because one preexisting group became more numerous, or because both occurred. These possibilities have different implications for a model of cancer progression. Repeated observations of the same identified cell or lineage can help distinguish state transition from shifts in population composition. A static comparison of two groups cannot do so unambiguously. This is a general problem in biological inference, also encountered when interpreting differentiation and selection. The term plasticity is most informative when supported by evidence of the capacity for a change rather than only by an association between two populations.

8. Diverse patterns of invasion and stromal assistance

The classical picture of invasion often begins with an individual cell leaving an epithelial group. This is biologically important, but it is incomplete. Tumors may invade as individual cells or as multicellular clusters, strands or sheets. A single-cell mode can be elongated and traction-dependent, or in certain environments relatively rounded and associated with cortical contractility and deformation. A collective mode preserves enough intercellular connection to maintain a mechanically and functionally connected population. The morphology of a cell is a useful clue, but a rounded shape alone does not establish the exact force-generating mechanism. Direct evidence of movement and organization is required.

Collective movement creates a division of spatial roles. Cells at a leading boundary encounter matrix that trailing cells do not contact in the same manner. Contacts between cells may transmit stresses and coordinate directional movement across the group. Followers need not display exactly the same marker pattern as leaders. Cheung and colleagues reported a basal epithelial program associated with collective breast cancer invasion in a 2013 study; leading cells expressed markers such as keratin 14. The finding is important because it illustrates that epithelial characteristics can remain associated with an invasive population. It does not establish keratin 14 as a universal marker of invasive behavior across all cancers. Figure 8 contrasts isolated and connected movement without classifying every tumor into one permanent mode.

The surrounding normal cells can influence this behavior. Cancer-associated fibroblasts are capable of modifying the composition and organization of the extracellular matrix, applying mechanical forces to it and exchanging soluble signals with nearby tumor cells. A tumor cell may thus encounter a path shaped by cells that are not members of the malignant clone. Fibroblasts themselves are mesenchymal cells, and their presence in a sample is not evidence that the neighboring epithelial tumor cells have undergone EMT. A tissue-level observation must distinguish the identities and positions of cells before assigning molecular changes to the malignant population.

Macrophages and other immune cells may also contribute signals or extracellular remodeling in particular contexts. Their roles are heterogeneous: some responses limit tumor growth or remove abnormal cells, whereas others can support aspects of tumor progression. The preceding chapter covers the broader tumor microenvironment. Here the emphasis is on the immediate invasion interface. A signal released by a stromal cell could change tumor-cell behavior, and changes in the tumor could alter stromal behavior in return. The interaction is reciprocal rather than a one-way chain in which the malignant cell acts on passive surroundings.

Because several cell populations can affect the same matrix, a reduction in matrix degradation after perturbing a stromal population does not automatically establish that a protease was produced by tumor cells. Similarly, observing a malignant cell in a remodeled track does not establish that the malignant cell itself created the track. Such ambiguities are central to experimental reasoning. Cell-specific observations, spatial localization and comparative evidence are more informative than a single bulk measure of matrix alteration. Figure 9 locates malignant cells, fibroblasts and matrix in one schematic to emphasize this difference between the origin of a change and its consequence.

The diversity of invasion modes also limits overly simple therapeutic conclusions. A molecule that is important in one form of movement might not be essential in a different environment or cell population. A reduction in invasion in a simplified assay is not proof of prevention of all tumor spread in an organism. The concept of plasticity is therefore relevant not as a detailed therapeutic prescription, but as a reminder that biological functions can emerge through different combinations of cellular and extracellular processes. The transition from this chapter to metastasis will preserve that principle when considering later barriers.

Cell groups have geometry of their own. A compact cluster with many internal contacts and a long, narrow strand differ in how many cells contact the surrounding matrix. In a simple two-dimensional idealization, the perimeter of a compact shape increases more slowly than its area as the shape grows. The ratio of perimeter to area therefore changes with size. Real tumor groups are three-dimensional, irregular and mechanically active, but the geometric principle suggests why cells at a surface can experience a distinct environment from cells inside. It does not follow that every boundary cell is a leader. Position is one component of the evidence; directional movement and the organization of the group are also needed to characterize a role.

The behavior of followers in a collective illustrates a distinction between cell-autonomous capacity and an emergent group property. A cell that does not independently move through a particular matrix may nevertheless change position when connected to a moving group. Its displacement could reflect force transmitted through junctions or coordinated responses to neighboring cells. The result cannot be fully predicted from an isolated-cell measurement alone. Conversely, an individual cell that migrates well when separated may behave differently when constrained by neighbors. This is a basic reason that models studying cells in isolation and models studying organized tissues can yield different outcomes without either observation being intrinsically wrong.

The matrix can preserve a physical history of tissue activity. Fibers deposited or reorganized earlier may remain when the cell responsible is no longer at the same position. A present-day cell contacting such a region is responding to a structure that reflects past events as well as current signaling. In a tissue image, the source of matrix organization cannot always be identified from the nearest cell. Distinguishing spatial coincidence from the time order of events is central to reconstructing mechanisms. A static image can suggest an interaction, while a sequence of observations is more informative about whether matrix alteration preceded movement or followed it. The distinction prevents interpreting every remodeled region as the direct product of the tumor cells currently occupying it.

9. Scientific evidence: separating observation from explanation

The simplest evidence of local invasion is spatial: abnormal epithelial cells can be seen beyond the boundary that normally defines their compartment. Yet even this observation requires interpretation. A two-dimensional tissue section samples only one plane of a three-dimensional structure. A curved gland can appear as disconnected islands in a section. Likewise, discontinuous staining of a matrix component may reflect technical or biological variation. Spatial evidence becomes stronger when cellular identity, tissue organization and the boundary are considered together. A single image is often the start of a mechanistic question rather than the complete answer.

Historical comparisons of invasive and less invasive epithelial populations revealed differences in cell cohesion. Functional experiments then asked whether changing adhesion could alter behavior. The classical E-cadherin studies by Behrens and Frixen are useful examples. Their scientific logic is that a perturbation of a candidate adhesion system followed by an observed difference in invasive behavior is more informative about causation than a correlation between expression and invasion across unrelated tumors. However, a causal contribution in one system is not proof of necessity in every tumor. Changes may involve several linked features, and experimental cell populations may not reproduce the whole tissue environment. The distinction between contribution, necessity and sufficiency is a recurring theme in the literature on adhesion.

Evidence for collective invasion provides a complementary lesson. A model that asserts all invasive cells must become fully mesenchymal predicts that the invading population should lack important epithelial features. Direct observations of connected epithelial tumor groups and their leaders challenge that universal claim. The relevant inference is limited but significant: at least some invasive behaviors can occur without wholesale elimination of epithelial organization. Evidence for such an exception does not show that EMT has no role in other settings. A good scientific model should accommodate the range of observations rather than replace one absolute statement with its opposite.

Migration and invasion readouts are frequently confused. Consider an imaginary assessment containing an unobstructed surface and a separate matrix-containing barrier. A decrease in movement across both might reflect loss of general motility or viability. A selective decrease in barrier crossing suggests that a barrier-dependent process has been affected, but it does not by itself identify which molecular activity is responsible. To distinguish explanations, a question may supply observations about cell number, survival, adhesion, movement or matrix organization. These are not redundant controls: they exclude different competing interpretations. The reader should identify which interpretation each observation makes less likely.

Expression data can reveal associations but must be connected to localization and function. For instance, a bulk assay showing increased MMP messenger RNA does not establish that catalytically active enzyme is present at the invasion front. A protein can be present in an inactive form, bound by an inhibitor or produced by a different cell population. Conversely, a local change in activity may be significant even if the average abundance measured across a tissue changes only modestly. The scale at which a measurement is made must match the scale of the proposed mechanism. This principle is valuable across signaling, physiology and cancer biology.

A final distinction concerns experimental endpoints. A smaller number of cells beyond a barrier could result from fewer viable cells being available, slower migration, stronger cohesion, altered matrix interaction or an effect on the relevant physical barrier. If the question supplies a viability measurement that is unchanged, one broad alternative is weakened. If general movement is also unchanged, a barrier-specific contribution becomes more plausible. Neither observation identifies a unique target without further evidence. The scientific task is to rank explanations according to the observations supplied, not to claim certainty where several mechanisms remain compatible.

The 1989 Behrens study has a useful conceptual implication because it included a comparison involving epithelial cells not initially transformed in the way that the other studied cells were. In the reported model, disrupting E-cadherin-mediated contacts was associated with acquisition of matrix-invasive behavior. The result indicates that a change in adhesion can make a functional contribution to an invasive phenotype even though adhesion is not the same biological process as malignant transformation. This is a reminder that an individual cancer-associated behavior is not sufficient to define the entire disease. The source supports a causal contribution within the model, but the inference must not be extended to the claim that every human carcinoma follows that route.

A later line of research focused on the relationship between matrix structure and localized proteolysis. Sabeh and colleagues' 2004 study compared cells deficient in particular matrix-remodeling functions and identified a requirement for MT1-MMP in their collagen-invasion model. The distinction between soluble and membrane-associated protease activity was scientifically important because cleavage close to a cell surface can have different consequences from cleavage distributed through the surrounding space. In evaluating this kind of result, the matrix is part of the experimental condition, not a neutral background. Evidence from a collagen-rich model supports a conclusion for that setting and need not establish the same requirement in a matrix with different geometry or composition.

Cheung and colleagues' breast cancer study addressed a different limitation of the traditional detached-cell picture. Their evidence associated collective invasive leaders with a basal epithelial gene-expression program. This example changes the question from 'which single cell loses all epithelial features?' to 'what organization of cells can support invasive movement?' The answer can involve a group of cells with different positions and states. A conclusion that the study demonstrates one biologically relevant collective program is stronger than a broad assertion that epithelial markers either always suppress or always promote invasion. A scientific model should identify the evidence that distinguishes its predictions from other possible explanations.

10. Quantitative interpretation of movement and barriers

Quantitative reasoning allows a student to distinguish changes that sound similar in descriptive language. Speed is distance traveled per unit time, whereas velocity is a vector with magnitude and direction. A cell can travel a long path and end close to its starting point. If a cell travels along a curved trajectory of length L over a time interval t, its mean path speed is L divided by t. The displacement magnitude d is the straight-line separation between initial and final positions, and d divided by t gives the magnitude of mean velocity. Both may be expressed in micrometers per hour if length and time are measured in those units. Figure 10 shows two trajectories with different relationships between path length and net displacement.

A convenient dimensionless measure of directedness is d/L for a nonzero path length. Because the straight line between two points cannot exceed the length of a continuous path joining them, the ratio lies between zero and one. A value near one indicates a relatively straight observed path. A value near zero indicates little net displacement compared with the distance traveled. The measure does not identify the molecular source of the behavior or distinguish purposeful reorientation from random turning. It is also affected by sampling interval and observation duration: coarse sampling can underestimate a tortuous path. A single ratio is therefore a geometric summary, not a universal measure of biological invasion.

A second distinction concerns the fraction of an observed population occupying a region beyond a defined boundary. If N is the number of relevant viable cells under consideration and C of those cells are classified beyond the boundary, the observed fraction is C/N. Multiplying by one hundred converts the dimensionless fraction to a percentage. This simple expression assumes that the denominator is defined consistently and the counted objects are comparable. If some cells divide or die during the observation, comparing the raw C values across conditions without accounting for changes in N can be misleading. The fraction is also an observed endpoint rather than a rate unless time is explicitly incorporated.

Percentage-point differences and relative changes are often mixed up. A change from 40 percent to 30 percent is a decrease of ten percentage points. The proportional decrease relative to the initial value is ten divided by forty, or 25 percent. Both statements can be correct, but they answer different questions. If the initial value is zero, the proportional change expressed relative to that baseline is undefined. A scientific report must specify which convention is being used and should avoid treating fractions from small populations as more precise than the counts justify.

Geometry provides another simple model. If the cross section of a cell is approximated by a circle of radius r, its area is pi times r squared. For a circular opening of radius a, the ratio of areas is r squared divided by a squared. A cell diameter and an opening diameter can be compared without confusing the two radii, because the ratio of their diameters is the same as the ratio of their radii. The area ratio scales with the square of that diameter ratio. Real cells deform, and real matrices are not fixed circular apertures, so this calculation does not establish passage or failure. It simply indicates why modest changes in a characteristic linear dimension can correspond to substantial differences in cross-sectional area.

Force and pressure can also be distinguished. Force is measured in newtons, and pressure is force per unit area, measured in pascals, or newtons per square meter. The same total force distributed over a smaller contact area produces a higher average pressure under the assumption of uniform distribution. A living cell, however, distributes forces unevenly across adhesive sites and a heterogeneous surrounding matrix. Average pressure cannot reveal the detailed stress experienced by an individual fiber. Such simple physical relationships are useful when an exam supplies idealized data, provided the assumptions are stated. They should not be turned into quantitative predictions about tissue invasion without experimental evidence.

The fraction of cells beyond a boundary can be interpreted at different levels. A proportion calculated from all visible cells in one tissue area may not be directly comparable with a proportion calculated only from identified malignant cells in another area. The choice of denominator determines the population to which the fraction refers. Suppose the tissue also contains many normal stromal cells. Adding those cells to the denominator changes the proportion even if the number of abnormal epithelial cells crossing the boundary remains identical. This is not an arithmetic error, but a difference in the biological question being answered. The student should read the definition of the counted population before comparing percentages. A result expressed as a fraction of tumor cells is not the same as a result expressed as a fraction of all tissue cells.

For two independently measured groups, variation among individual observations may be large even when their average values differ. A mean movement speed gives no direct information about the spread of the underlying observations. One group may contain cells moving at widely different speeds, whereas another has a narrow distribution. A representative cell trajectory may be illustrative without representing the entire population. At NET level, the important statistical principle is that a comparison of means should be interpreted with information about variation, sample size and the nature of the observations. Several measurements taken from the same cell do not necessarily constitute independent observations of several cells. Similarly, several cells from one tissue region may experience shared conditions. These distinctions matter when evaluating claims about generality.

Consider the geometric relation between a path and its displacement as a check on a supplied dataset. A reported displacement greater than the measured path length for the same start and finish would be inconsistent with the ordinary geometric definition if the path was continuously measured. In practice, differing definitions, measurement errors or sampling conventions might explain an apparent inconsistency. Such a check can detect a numerical problem before a molecular interpretation is attempted. A second check concerns units: a distance divided by time has the dimension of speed, whereas displacement divided by path length is dimensionless. A ratio reported in micrometers per hour when both numerator and denominator are lengths should prompt a review of the calculation.

Simple models of migration often idealize a cell as a point, but invasion involves an extended deformable object. The center of a cell may enter an opening while part of the cell remains on the original side. Different imaging methods might classify that event according to the position of the nucleus, the cell center or the leading edge. These criteria can produce different apparent times of crossing. The physical movement is continuous while the classification changes when a defined threshold is passed. Such distinctions are common in quantitative biology. A threshold is useful for summarizing an event, but it must be stated clearly before the numerical outcome can be interpreted.

11. Integration with normal physiology, evolution and cancer progression

The machinery employed during invasion is not uniquely invented by a malignant cell. Normal epithelial organization relies on cadherins, integrins and cytoskeletal regulation. Embryonic cells change shape and location to produce tissue patterns; repairing tissues can reorganize cell contacts and the extracellular matrix. Immune cells move through tissues to perform defensive functions. These normal processes help explain why a tumor can acquire invasive behavior by changing how existing biological programs are regulated. The distinction from malignancy lies not simply in whether cells move, but in the regulation, identity and tissue-level consequences of movement.

Embryonic EMT and cancer-associated epithelial plasticity share conceptual features without being identical processes. Development is governed by spatial and temporal signals that coordinate cell changes with construction of a functional organism. A tumor develops within a tissue whose normal regulation has been disrupted. Cancer-associated changes may be incomplete, heterogeneous or poorly coordinated with the needs of the tissue. Comparing the two settings is useful because it explains why familiar molecular machinery can have different consequences depending on the biological context. It is not evidence that every cancer reenacts a particular embryonic developmental sequence.

Plants also possess sophisticated mechanisms of cell adhesion and communication, but their rigid extracellular walls and patterns of growth make the animal carcinoma invasion model inappropriate as a general description of plant tissue organization. The concept of movement of an animal cell through basement membrane is not directly transferable to cells confined within plant cell walls. Bacteria lack the animal epithelial junction and basement-membrane architecture discussed here. The relevant comparative lesson is that multicellular organization and the physical environment constrain the meaning of invasion. General principles such as cytoskeletal regulation, cell-surface interactions and extracellular barriers must be interpreted within the structures actually present in the organism.

The evolution of multicellularity required ways of coordinating individual cells into functioning tissues. Many elements of animal cell adhesion, polarity and cytoskeletal machinery are conserved across diverse animals. Tumor progression can be understood in part through selection among heterogeneous cells in a changing local environment. A phenotype that confers an advantage in one setting may be disadvantageous in another. This does not imply that all invasive traits were selected for the benefit of the whole organism; selection among abnormal cells within a tumor can conflict with organismal fitness. Nor does it mean that every observed feature of a cancer cell is adaptive. The evidence for a selective advantage must be distinguished from the observation of a change.

A complete causal picture of local invasion connects molecular and tissue scales. Altered cell–cell adhesion changes collective organization; matrix receptors connect extracellular ligands to intracellular signals and forces; actin and myosin generate and transmit movement; matrix architecture determines whether the movement is physically possible; extracellular remodeling can alter that architecture; and stromal cells can influence several of these relationships. These changes operate in a living population with variable viability, growth and differentiation. Figure 11 presents the relationships as interacting contributions rather than a sequence of compulsory universal stages. The chapter on distant metastasis begins after this local tissue boundary has been crossed in the carcinoma model, and it considers additional barriers that cannot be inferred from local invasion alone.

The functional meaning of a boundary also differs across normal tissues. An epithelial barrier contributes to controlled exchange and tissue compartmentalization, whereas connective tissues accommodate cells with different relationships to the ECM. An immune cell that normally passes through a tissue is not displaying malignant behavior merely by occupying a position that a stationary epithelial cell would not normally occupy. Cell identity, expected physiological route and regulation of behavior determine the interpretation. This principle prevents misleading comparisons between a mobile leukocyte and an invasive carcinoma cell based on a superficial similarity in their shape or movement.

A second physiological comparison concerns development and repair. During development, tissue organization changes as cells adopt different fates and interact with changing surroundings. Repair can involve temporary relaxation of established contacts followed by the restoration of organized tissue. These are processes with organismal functions and regulatory endpoints. In tumor invasion, the position and behavior of the abnormal cells may instead compromise the organization of the original tissue and adjacent tissue. The difference is not that normal cells never use proteases, alter adhesion or move. It is that these activities occur within different systems of control. A gene product with a normal developmental function can participate in an abnormal phenotype when its expression, localization or timing is altered.

It is useful to connect the discussion to the earlier chapters on oncogenes and tumor suppressors without reducing invasion to a single gene. Genetic changes may alter signaling, cell-cycle control or survival; signaling and gene regulation may in turn affect adhesive behavior and responsiveness to the matrix. A proliferative advantage can increase the number of abnormal cells available to experience different tissue conditions, but it does not specify which of those cells will cross a particular boundary. The phenotype of a tumor is multidimensional: growth, cell survival, cell organization and spatial movement can vary in partially independent ways. This is why a question about reduced tumor size and a question about altered local invasion should not automatically be answered using the same mechanism.

Worked analytical examples

Trajectory

A hypothetical cell travels 180 µm in 6 h, ending 90 µm from its start. Path speed = 180/6 = 30 µm/h; mean velocity magnitude = 90/6 = 15 µm/h; directedness = 90/180 = 0.5. The ratio is geometric and does not identify a cue.

Population fraction

In a hypothetical tissue image set, 24 of 120 identified cells lie beyond the boundary. Fraction = 24/120 = 0.20 = 20%. This is an endpoint, not a rate; classification and denominator must be consistent.

Relative difference

Observed fractions are 32% and 24%. Absolute decrease = 8 percentage points; relative decrease = 8/32 = 25%. Neither value alone establishes a mechanism.

Circular opening

A cell cross section is modeled as a circle with diameter 12 µm and an opening with diameter 8 µm. Area ratio = (12/8)² = 2.25. This geometric comparison does not account for deformability or matrix remodeling.

Pressure

An idealized 20 nN force acts uniformly over 10 µm². P = 20×10⁻⁹ N/(10×10⁻¹² m²) = 2,000 Pa. If the area doubles at fixed force, average pressure becomes 1,000 Pa. Real contact stresses vary spatially.

Interpretation of supplied observations

In a hypothetical comparison, viable cell number and open-surface speed remain comparable, while the fraction beyond a matrix barrier differs. The evidence supports a barrier-associated difference; it does not identify a unique molecular cause. A change in matrix geometry, traction or extracellular remodeling remains possible.

Part B — Conceptual questions

Original Academy practice questions.

1. What distinguishes local invasion from growth within an epithelial compartment?

  1. An increase in cell number
  2. Extension beyond the original tissue boundary
  3. Increased nuclear size
  4. A higher mitotic index
Answer and explanation

B. Invasion is a spatial change. Cell number, nuclear size and mitoses may describe growth or morphology but do not establish boundary crossing.

2. Which collagen is characteristic of basement membrane networks?

  1. Type I
  2. Type II
  3. Type III
  4. Type IV
Answer and explanation

D. Type IV forms a sheet-like network; type I is typical of many interstitial fibrils, type II of cartilage, and type III of reticular fibers.

3. Which protein directly participates in classical epithelial adherens junction adhesion?

  1. E-cadherin
  2. Laminin
  3. Myosin II
  4. MMP-9
Answer and explanation

A. E-cadherin is a transmembrane adhesion molecule. Laminin is matrix, myosin generates force, and MMP-9 is a protease.

4. Which statement about integrins is most accurate?

  1. They are soluble collagenases
  2. They form tight junction pores
  3. They are alpha–beta receptor heterodimers
  4. They are exclusively nuclear regulators
Answer and explanation

C. Integrins connect extracellular ligands to cellular signaling and adhesion. They are not collagenases, tight junction pores or exclusively nuclear proteins.

5. What is a focal adhesion?

  1. An extracellular collagen fiber
  2. A multiprotein cell–matrix coupling assembly
  3. A single cadherin molecule
  4. An organelle for DNA replication
Answer and explanation

B. Focal adhesions link integrins to intracellular signaling and force-transmitting machinery; the other structures do not describe that assembly.

6. What is the catalytic metal associated with MMPs?

  1. Sodium
  2. Potassium
  3. Iron only
  4. Zinc
Answer and explanation

D. MMPs are zinc-dependent proteases. The alternatives do not identify their characteristic catalytic metal.

7. A zymogen is best described as:

  1. An inactive enzyme precursor
  2. An extracellular receptor
  3. A cytoskeletal filament
  4. A matrix carbohydrate
Answer and explanation

A. Zymogens require activation for mature catalytic function. The other categories are not enzyme precursors.

8. Anoikis is associated with:

  1. An increase in DNA content during S phase
  2. A switch from collagen I to IV
  3. Apoptosis following loss of appropriate anchorage
  4. Cell division without spindle formation
Answer and explanation

C. Anoikis is a response to inappropriate matrix detachment in susceptible cells, not replication, collagen identity or spindle failure.

9. Which statement about EMT is defensible?

  1. It always results in cancer
  2. It is a context-dependent change in cellular organization
  3. It requires irreversible loss of all epithelial genes
  4. It is identical to metastasis
Answer and explanation

B. EMT can occur in development, may be partial or reversible, and is distinct from the entire metastatic process.

10. Which observation directly demonstrates collective invasion?

  1. MMP RNA is elevated
  2. A cell expresses vimentin
  3. E-cadherin is present
  4. A connected group extends beyond a tissue boundary
Answer and explanation

D. Spatial evidence of connected cells crossing the boundary demonstrates collective invasion; markers alone do not.

11. What distinguishes interstitial collagen-rich matrix from a basement membrane?

  1. They differ in architecture and component organization
  2. Only interstitial matrix contains proteins
  3. Basement membrane is a plasma lipid bilayer
  4. Interstitial matrix is always liquid
Answer and explanation

A. Both are extracellular protein-rich structures but differ in geometry and composition. The alternatives misidentify their nature.

12. Which cellular process supplies motor-driven mechanical work?

  1. Cadherin binding calcium
  2. MMP hydrolysis alone
  3. ATP hydrolysis by myosin
  4. Passive diffusion of ligand
Answer and explanation

C. Myosin couples ATP hydrolysis to force generation. Binding and proteolysis are distinct chemical processes.

13. Which directionality term describes bias associated with a soluble chemical gradient?

  1. Haptotaxis
  2. Chemotaxis
  3. Anoikis
  4. Apoptosis
Answer and explanation

B. Chemotaxis concerns soluble directional cues; haptotaxis concerns substrate-associated cues, and the others concern cell death.

14. Which is a characteristic of MT1-MMP?

  1. It is a nuclear DNA polymerase
  2. It forms tight junction strands
  3. It is a soluble cadherin
  4. It is membrane-associated MMP-14
Answer and explanation

D. MT1-MMP is MMP-14, a membrane-associated protease rather than the other molecule classes.

15. A tumor in situ is conceptually distinguished by:

  1. Confinement within the relevant original epithelial boundary
  2. Presence of distant tumor deposits
  3. Absence of any abnormal cell
  4. Universal lack of mitosis
Answer and explanation

A. In situ lesions contain abnormal cells but remain confined; distant deposits indicate another stage.

16. Which protein can function at adherens junctions and in canonical Wnt signaling?

  1. Laminin
  2. Myosin heavy chain
  3. Beta-catenin
  4. Collagen IV
Answer and explanation

C. Beta-catenin has junctional and signaling roles. The alternatives have other structural or mechanical roles.

17. Which observation concerns activity rather than abundance?

  1. Amount of MMP mRNA
  2. Evidence of cleavage of a relevant substrate
  3. Total protein concentration
  4. Number of nuclei
Answer and explanation

B. Substrate cleavage indicates a functional biochemical event, although the responsible enzyme requires identification.

18. A stromal fibroblast differs from an epithelial cancer cell primarily in this chapter because:

  1. It cannot interact with ECM
  2. It necessarily lacks all signaling
  3. It must be malignant
  4. It belongs to a distinct cell population
Answer and explanation

D. Fibroblasts are stromal cells that can affect matrix and signaling; their presence is not proof of malignancy.

19. Which is a possible form of cell movement with continued intercellular contacts?

  1. Collective migration
  2. DNA replication
  3. Anaphase
  4. Necrosis
Answer and explanation

A. Collective migration involves coordinated movement of connected cells.

20. Which statement correctly distinguishes invasion from metastasis?

  1. They are interchangeable
  2. Metastasis always precedes invasion
  3. Local invasion does not by itself demonstrate distant colonization
  4. Invasion requires a distant lesion
Answer and explanation

C. Local tissue crossing is not sufficient evidence for establishment of a secondary tumor.

Part C — Analytical questions

Original Academy practice questions.

1. Two lesions have similar mitotic indices. Only lesion Y has abnormal epithelial cells within adjacent stroma. What can be inferred?

  1. X is metastatic
  2. Y has evidence of local invasion independent of the similar mitotic indices
  3. Both have equal invasive extent
  4. Y must have completed EMT
Answer and explanation

B. The spatial evidence establishes local invasion for Y. Equal mitosis does not imply equal movement; neither distant spread nor complete EMT follows.

2. An image shows reduced junctional beta-catenin but no nuclear measurement. Which inference is strongest?

  1. Canonical Wnt transcription is proven
  2. All cadherins are absent
  3. The cell has metastasized
  4. Junctional localization changed; nuclear signaling is unresolved
Answer and explanation

D. The measurement describes one pool. The other conclusions require independent evidence.

3. A connected tumor strand is seen beyond an intact-looking epithelial boundary at an adjacent discontinuity and retains epithelial keratins. Which universal claim is challenged?

  1. All invasion requires total loss of epithelial traits
  2. Cells contain cytoskeleton
  3. Tumors interact with stroma
  4. ECM exists
Answer and explanation

A. Collective movement with retained epithelial properties contradicts the universal EMT requirement, not the other statements.

4. Two groups travel 120 µm and 80 µm in 4 h. Their displacements are 40 µm and 72 µm. Which comparison is correct?

  1. Both have equal speed
  2. The first has greater directedness
  3. The first has greater path speed but lower directedness
  4. The second has greater path speed
Answer and explanation

C. Speeds are 30 and 20 µm/h; directedness values are 1/3 and 0.9.

5. A sample contains more vimentin protein and a larger fibroblast fraction. What is justified?

  1. Every epithelial cancer cell completed EMT
  2. The bulk result cannot assign the change to tumor cells
  3. Vimentin is exclusively epithelial
  4. Fibroblasts are absent
Answer and explanation

B. The changing cell mixture provides an alternative explanation; cell-specific evidence is necessary.

6. Equal viable populations show equal path speeds on an open surface, but one has fewer cells beyond a dense barrier. What is the best conclusion?

  1. The latter is dead
  2. Its mitotic rate is necessarily zero
  3. All motility is absent
  4. A barrier-associated difference is plausible
Answer and explanation

D. The observations argue against general loss of speed or viability as the sole explanation; they do not identify a specific mechanism.

7. A path length is 100 µm and displacement is 0 µm. What must be true?

  1. The observed net displacement is zero despite movement
  2. No movement occurred
  3. Directedness is one
  4. The cell crossed a boundary
Answer and explanation

A. The nonzero path length establishes movement; return to the starting point yields zero displacement.

8. An enzyme precursor is abundant but substrate cleavage is low. Which explanation fits?

  1. All zymogens are catalytically maximal
  2. The substrate has no peptide bonds by definition
  3. The precursor may remain inactive or be inhibited
  4. High expression guarantees high activity
Answer and explanation

C. Activation and inhibition separate abundance from activity; substrate accessibility may also matter.

9. An observed fraction falls from 40% to 30%. Which statement is correct?

  1. Relative decrease is 10%
  2. Decrease is 10 percentage points and 25% relative
  3. Decrease is 25 percentage points
  4. Relative increase is 25%
Answer and explanation

B. The difference is 10 percentage points; 10/40 = 25% relative decrease.

10. In an idealized circular geometry, a cell diameter is twice the opening diameter. Their cross-sectional area ratio is:

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 8
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

D. Area is proportional to diameter squared, so the ratio is 2² = 4. Deformation is not addressed by this model.

11. A tissue section shows an apparent isolated cell cluster. A subsequent section reveals continuity with an epithelial gland. What was the initial limitation?

  1. Two-dimensional sampling of a three-dimensional structure
  2. A failure of ATP synthesis
  3. Proof of distant colonization
  4. A universal MMP requirement
Answer and explanation

A. The initial section did not capture the structure's continuity, illustrating a spatial interpretation limitation.

12. Two cells have the same net displacement and time, but one traverses a longer path. Which quantity differs?

  1. Mean velocity magnitude only
  2. Time only
  3. Mean path speed
  4. Net displacement
Answer and explanation

C. Mean velocity magnitude is the same; path speed equals path length/time and differs.

13. A tumor group retains junctions between followers but shows changes at its boundary. Which explanation is consistent?

  1. All internal junctions must vanish
  2. Adhesion can have different functions at different positions
  3. No mechanical coupling exists
  4. Every cell must behave independently
Answer and explanation

B. Contacts may organize the collective while the leading boundary interacts with the surrounding matrix.

14. One tissue contains fragmented matrix with no abnormal epithelial cells beyond the boundary. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Distant spread is confirmed
  2. Carcinoma invasion is established
  3. The cells are mesenchymal
  4. Matrix alteration is observed but invasion is not established
Answer and explanation

D. A matrix gap is not enough to demonstrate abnormal cells crossing into stroma.

15. For a nonzero path length of 240 µm, a displacement of 60 µm gives directedness of:

  1. 0.25
  2. 4
  3. 180 µm
  4. 300 µm
Answer and explanation

A. The ratio 60/240 = 0.25 is dimensionless.

16. A cell retains E-cadherin abundance, but it is predominantly internal rather than at cell contacts. What distinction matters?

  1. All internal protein is DNA
  2. Protein abundance and adhesion are identical
  3. Localization and abundance can differ
  4. Internal localization proves distant spread
Answer and explanation

C. Adhesion depends on correctly localized and functional complexes, not simply total abundance.

17. A fibroblast-rich region contains altered collagen fibers and an adjacent invading tumor strand. Which interpretation is best supported?

  1. Only tumor cells can remodel ECM
  2. The source of the matrix alteration requires cell-specific evidence
  3. All fibroblasts have undergone EMT
  4. Matrix remodeling cannot influence movement
Answer and explanation

B. Either population may contribute; spatial association alone does not determine origin or causality.

18. Equal force is distributed uniformly over two idealized contact areas, one half the other. Average pressure over the smaller area is:

  1. Half
  2. Unchanged
  3. Four times
  4. Twice
Answer and explanation

D. P=F/A; halving A at constant F doubles average pressure. Real stresses are not uniformly distributed.

19. A cell loses attachment, survives, but its position does not change. Which properties are supported?

  1. Survival after detachment, but not locomotion
  2. Successful distant colonization
  3. Proof of collective movement
  4. No survival
Answer and explanation

A. The observations distinguish viability after detachment from movement.

20. A tumor shows a shift in several epithelial markers, yet its observed local boundary remains intact. What follows?

  1. Metastasis is proven
  2. No change in state is possible
  3. The marker change supports altered phenotype but not demonstrated invasion
  4. All markers are useless
Answer and explanation

C. Molecular state and spatial behavior are separate readouts. Markers may be useful without proving boundary crossing.

Misconceptions and examination traps

Large means invasive

Size measures expansion, not location. A small focus beyond a boundary is spatial evidence.

In situ means normal

Abnormal epithelial cells can remain within the original compartment; confinement is not normality.

Basement membrane means cell membrane

Both use the term membrane, but the basement membrane is extracellular matrix rather than a lipid bilayer.

All collagen is fibrillar

The familiar type I fiber is not the type IV network characteristic of basement membranes.

Cadherin loss proves invasion

Loss of cohesion can facilitate separation but does not establish movement or barrier crossing.

Integrins are proteases

Both participate in matrix-related processes, but integrins are receptors and MMPs are enzymes.

More adhesion always slows movement

Attachments may also transmit traction; effects depend on geometry and adhesion turnover.

All MMP protein is active

Zymogens and inhibitor binding separate protein amount from catalytic activity.

All matrix gaps are enzymatic

Displacement, preexisting openings and section artifacts are alternative explanations.

EMT is all or none

Intermediate and reversible states occur; marker panels and functional observations matter.

Vimentin in tissue proves tumor EMT

Normal stromal cells can contribute the measured protein.

Individual cells are the only invaders

Connected epithelial groups can cross tissue boundaries.

Motility equals invasion

A cell may move on an open surface without penetrating a barrier.

Anoikis escape means motility

Survival and displacement are independent endpoints.

Local invasion proves metastasis

Distant colonization requires additional evidence and biological steps.

Revision and mastery

Local invasion is a spatial change. Junction remodeling, cell–matrix adhesion, force generation, matrix architecture and stromal interactions can contribute without forming a compulsory sequence. Partial or reversible epithelial plasticity and collective movement qualify a simple detachment model. Separate molecular expression, enzyme activity, viability, locomotion, barrier crossing and distant colonization when interpreting evidence.
