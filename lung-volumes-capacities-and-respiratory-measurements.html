1. From breathing movements to measurable quantities

A respiratory movement is easy to observe, but it is not obvious how much air has actually entered the lung or how much of it can exchange gases with the blood. Two mammals may show the same number of respiratory movements in a minute and yet supply quite different amounts of fresh gas to their alveoli. Conversely, two individuals with similar resting lung volumes may differ in their ability to empty the lungs rapidly. The distinction between the size of a gas compartment, the rate at which gas passes through a boundary, and the fraction of that gas which participates in exchange is the organising idea of respiratory measurement. Mechanics describes why air moves; measurement describes how much moves and how its movement can be interpreted.

Consider the lung at the end of a relaxed expiration. Airflow at the mouth is momentarily zero, but the lung has not become empty. The inward elastic recoil of the lung and the outward recoil of the chest wall are balanced at this operating volume when the respiratory muscles are relaxed. The gas that remains provides a reservoir that buffers changes in alveolar gas composition between breaths. When a person contracts the diaphragm, alveolar pressure falls slightly relative to atmospheric pressure, gas enters, and lung volume increases. When quiet inspiratory activity subsides, elastic recoil drives air out. The interval between two end-expiratory points includes gas entry and gas exit, and its net change in lung volume is approximately zero if the breathing pattern is steady.

Lung volume is a state variable, expressed in litres or millilitres. Flow is the time derivative of volume, expressed in litres per second. Ventilation is a volume per unit time, usually litres per minute. These related quantities must not be substituted for one another. A flow of one litre per second sustained for two seconds transfers two litres; it does not mean that the lung contains two litres. A volume-time graph and a flow-time graph describe the same gas movements in different mathematical forms. The slope of a volume-time graph gives flow, provided the sign convention is specified. The area under a flow-time curve gives volume change. A flow sensor coupled to electronic integration can therefore recover volume changes without measuring absolute lung volume.

Measurements also depend on the physical state of gas. A fixed number of molecules occupies different volumes when pressure, temperature or water vapour changes. Respiratory volumes are conventionally compared under body temperature, ambient pressure, saturated with water vapour conditions (BTPS), while metabolic gas output may use standard temperature and pressure, dry (STPD). A numerical exercise must either supply consistently expressed quantities or specify appropriate conversion. Simply adding a dry gas volume collected at room temperature to a saturated lung volume at body temperature can introduce systematic error.

The purpose of standardisation is not to make every healthy individual produce the same result. Height, age, sex and the reference population influence expected lung function, while posture and effort influence the measurement itself. Physiological interpretation separates these sources of variation. A small lung capacity in a small individual may be appropriate; the same absolute capacity in a much taller individual may warrant a different interpretation. Likewise, repeated measurements can differ because of leaks, an incomplete inspiration or early stopping rather than a change in physiology. A well-designed investigation therefore establishes measurement quality before inferring a biological mechanism.

A volume measurement also requires a clear choice of what counts as the respiratory system. In physiological calculations the lung gas compartment is usually the gas within the intrathoracic airways and alveoli. A measuring system may additionally contain a mouthpiece, filter and connecting tubing. These are not lung volumes, but the gas contained in them can influence measurement and rebreathing. For example, a sensor installed several centimetres away from the mouth may record the same volume displacement as a sensor at the mouth in the absence of leaks, yet the gas composition sampled at the two positions can differ transiently. Thus measurements of volume and composition require different attention to apparatus geometry. This becomes particularly important when a calculation combines expired volume with carbon dioxide concentration.

The same distinction applies to pressure measurements. Atmospheric pressure provides a useful reference for the small pressure differences driving airflow, but an absolute pressure is necessary when applying a gas law. A reading of zero gauge pressure means equality with the reference pressure rather than absence of molecular collisions. A gas at zero gauge pressure at sea level still has an absolute pressure of approximately 760 mmHg. In a conceptual gas compression experiment, using the gauge pressure in Boyle's law would give nonsensical estimates. The use of absolute pressure in lung-volume measurement is a direct continuation of the mechanics developed in the preceding node.

One can distinguish three kinds of respiratory endpoints. A mechanical endpoint is set by the balance of elastic and muscle forces or by airway closure. A behavioural endpoint is the point at which a subject chooses to stop a manoeuvre. An instrumental endpoint is the point at which the recording system defines completion. These endpoints coincide in a satisfactory maximal manoeuvre, but not necessarily in an unsatisfactory one. For example, the disappearance of expiratory flow can indicate complete emptying of the accessible compartment, or it can indicate closure of the glottis while more gas is still available. Observing no flow therefore has more than one possible explanation. This is why quality assessment uses the shape of the trace, the circumstances of the manoeuvre and reproducibility rather than relying solely on its last recorded value.

2. Four primary lung volumes

Tidal volume (VT or TV) is the gas inspired or expired in one ordinary breath. For a hypothetical resting adult it may be 500 mL, although that is an illustrative value rather than a universal normal. In quiet breathing, inspiration typically increases lung volume by about the same amount that the following expiration decreases it. In a steady state the average inspired and expired volumes are close, but small differences arise from oxygen uptake, carbon dioxide release and the physical conditions under which gas volumes are measured.

Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) is the additional volume that can be inspired after completing a normal tidal inspiration. Its starting point is the end of a quiet inspiration, not the end of quiet expiration. If a quiet breath adds 0.5 L and a further maximal inspiration adds 2.5 L, the latter quantity is IRV. The absolute volume at the upper limit reflects maximal inspiratory muscle effort against lung and chest wall recoil. The effort required to approach this limit rises because the pressure-volume relationship is not linear across the entire range.

Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) is the additional volume that can be expelled following a normal tidal expiration. Active expiratory muscles can reduce lung volume below its relaxed resting level. The lower boundary for the manoeuvre is not zero lung volume; small airways may close and respiratory structures resist further collapse. ERV can change when the operating volume of quiet breathing changes, even if maximal lung volume is unchanged. For example, an upward displacement of the diaphragm in a different posture may reduce end-expiratory volume and ERV without requiring a primary change in alveolar structure.

Residual volume (RV) is the gas remaining after maximal expiration. It is not a measurement of all gas in the lung after quiet expiration, and it is not identical to conducting airway dead space. RV includes gas in alveolar and conducting compartments at the maximal expiratory endpoint. The gas remains partly because of mechanical limits on emptying and airway closure; even strong expiratory effort cannot make the living lung behave like a bag that can be completely flattened. Changes in elastic recoil and airway closure can increase RV, reducing the amount of gas that can be expelled for a given total lung capacity.

These four volumes partition the span between an empty reference state and maximal inspiration. In practice the zero reference is a conceptual baseline rather than a point reached during ordinary physiological measurement. An instrument connected to the mouth can detect gas leaving and entering, but it cannot establish how much gas remained before the measurement began. That observation explains why the residual compartment requires an additional method. It also explains why a graph of spirometric volume change can be correctly calibrated in litres while its vertical offset remains unknown.

A useful problem-solving strategy is to mark three endpoints first: maximum inspiration, quiet end-expiration and maximum expiration. Then mark the top of an ordinary tidal inspiration. IRV occupies the segment above the tidal inspiration, VT occupies the tidal swing, ERV lies between quiet and maximal expiration, and RV lies below maximal expiration. The relationships follow from the endpoints, not from memorising a list of isolated formulae.

The primary volumes vary across the respiratory cycle and across physiological states. To understand exercise, imagine a lung at rest with a relatively large inspiratory reserve and a smaller expiratory reserve. When metabolic demand rises, the tidal swing widens. Part of this increased volume is recruited from the inspiratory reserve, and under some conditions the lower endpoint also shifts so that the expiratory reserve becomes smaller. The lung has not acquired additional maximal capacity merely because tidal volume rises; it is using a greater fraction of the existing movable volume. As the tidal volume approaches the available limits, further increases in minute ventilation depend increasingly on a rise in respiratory frequency.

The residual volume is particularly useful for distinguishing the consequences of airway closure from the properties of the lung as an elastic body. At low lung volumes, the radial traction that surrounding tissue exerts on small airways is reduced. The airways are consequently more likely to narrow or close as expiration proceeds. Once a pathway closes, further contraction of the expiratory muscles cannot simply force gas through that closed path. If elastic recoil decreases, as it can when supportive tissue is lost, the tendency to dynamic airway compression can become more important. In a measurement comparing two individuals, a greater residual volume may reflect these mechanical changes even if their maximal inspiratory capacities are otherwise similar.

By contrast, maximal inspiratory volume can be reduced by a system that is difficult to expand, or by reduced inspiratory muscle force. A relatively stiff lung requires greater pressure to achieve the same increment in volume. A person with inadequate inspiratory muscle force may fail to generate that pressure even when the intrinsic lung material is not unusually stiff. Consequently, a decrease in TLC has several possible mechanisms. The primary volumes give a description of the resulting partition, but cannot by themselves reveal whether the limitation originated in lung tissue, the chest wall, muscle activity or some combination of these.

The fact that lung volumes are measured in litres does not make them comparable across all species or sizes. A small mammal may have a small tidal volume but a high respiratory rate and a substantial ventilation relative to its body mass. A large mammal can move a large absolute volume in each breath without necessarily having a proportionately large metabolic demand. Comparative physiology uses scaling and appropriate normalisation when comparing animals. The relationships between primary volumes remain conceptually useful for mammals, but the patterns of ventilation in birds and fish involve different anatomical and functional arrangements and cannot be described completely by a human spirometric scheme.

3. Capacities and the logic of endpoint addition

A lung capacity combines two or more of the primary volumes. Inspiratory capacity (IC) is the volume that can be inspired from the end of a quiet expiration to maximal inspiration. It includes the ordinary tidal inspiration and the inspiratory reserve volume, so IC = VT + IRV. It provides information about the available inspiratory expansion above the resting operating level. If end-expiratory volume rises while the upper limit remains unchanged, IC falls. This inverse relationship matters when expiratory emptying is incomplete.

Functional residual capacity (FRC) is the gas volume remaining at the end of relaxed expiration. It is ERV + RV. Unlike RV, FRC includes gas that can still be expelled by active expiration. Its name describes the residual gas at the functional resting position of the respiratory system rather than the residual gas at maximal emptying. When quiet breathing occurs at an elevated operating volume because expiration is incomplete, the measured end-expiratory volume may exceed the relaxed equilibrium volume. The distinction is useful when considering dynamic hyperinflation; one must identify the actual condition under which the measurement was made.

Vital capacity (VC) is the maximal movable gas volume between full inspiration and full expiration. It is IRV + VT + ERV, equivalently TLC − RV. Slow vital capacity can be measured with a slow manoeuvre. Forced vital capacity (FVC) refers specifically to a forceful expiratory manoeuvre after maximal inspiration. In healthy subjects the two may be close; in airflow obstruction a forceful effort may promote earlier airway closure and the forced volume may be smaller. The capacities must therefore be distinguished by the method and not merely by their units.

Total lung capacity (TLC) is the entire lung gas volume at maximal inspiration. TLC = IRV + VT + ERV + RV = VC + RV = FRC + IC. These equivalent expressions permit internal consistency checks. If independent estimates of FRC and IC give a TLC quite different from the sum of VC and RV, one should first examine whether the measurements used comparable endpoints, body positions and gas-volume conditions.

Take a hypothetical adult with VT 0.50 L, IRV 2.50 L, ERV 1.00 L and RV 1.20 L. The IC is 3.00 L, FRC is 2.20 L, VC is 4.00 L and TLC is 5.20 L. The sum of FRC and IC gives 5.20 L, in agreement with the sum of VC and RV. If a second adult has the same TLC but RV is 1.80 L, VC must be 3.40 L. The decrease in movable gas does not establish that total lung size has decreased. It can result from an enlarged residual compartment. This is the conceptual reason a low vital capacity alone is insufficient to establish restriction.

The equations are geometric bookkeeping identities, not causal laws. They describe the differences between endpoints and remain true for the same set of endpoint measurements. To explain why one endpoint changes, one must consider airway resistance, elastic recoil, respiratory muscle force and timing. This distinction prevents a frequent circular explanation: claiming that a fall in VC causes an increase in RV because the equation has been rearranged. The equation constrains possible combinations but does not identify the underlying cause.

Capacity identities allow one to infer an unmeasured difference when enough independent endpoints have been determined. For instance, if FRC is measured by gas dilution as 2.4 L and the ERV obtained by spirometry is 0.9 L, their difference estimates RV as 1.5 L. If IC is independently measured as 3.1 L, the estimated TLC is 5.5 L. The inferred VC is therefore 4.0 L. A discrepancy should lead to investigation, not arbitrary adjustment of the data to make the equations agree.

The independence of measurements matters when interpreting apparent confirmation. Suppose TLC is calculated by adding IC and FRC, and RV is then calculated by subtracting VC from the calculated TLC. Adding that RV back to VC will necessarily reproduce the calculated TLC. This is a mathematical identity, not independent experimental confirmation. A genuinely independent check would use another measurement method, or a separately obtained manoeuvre whose result is not already algebraically constrained by the first calculation. This distinction between derived and independently observed quantities is a valuable feature of CSIR-NET Part C questions.

A change in the distribution of lung volumes can occur without changing the absolute total. Imagine that FRC rises from 2.2 to 3.0 L while TLC remains 5.2 L. IC necessarily declines from 3.0 to 2.2 L. If resting VT remains 0.5 L, the available inspiratory reserve falls from 2.5 to 1.7 L. This leaves less room for expansion of tidal volume in response to exercise. The difficulty experienced by the respiratory system can therefore result not simply from a reduction in maximal capacity but from the relocation of the operating point within that capacity. This is why a capacity describing the space available above the current resting point provides information different from the total capacity alone.

A similar reasoning exercise can distinguish loss of expiratory reserve from an increase in residual gas. If both FRC and RV increase by the same amount, ERV remains unchanged. If FRC stays unchanged while RV increases, ERV must fall. Neither observation implies that VT has changed. A useful method is to draw the endpoints at the two conditions on adjacent axes and compare differences visually before selecting an equation. This prevents the common error of attributing every change in one capacity to all of its constituent volumes.

4. Spirometry: recording changes rather than absolute volume

The classical spirometer recorded gas movement by displacement of a chamber connected to the subject. John Hutchinson's nineteenth-century work on vital capacity helped establish the use of quantitative lung-volume measurements. The basic experimental question was whether respiratory function could be compared using a repeatable measurement rather than a subjective impression of breathlessness. Recording maximal movable gas and comparing individuals made it possible to study how body dimensions relate to lung function. Modern flow-based instruments extend the same principle by measuring the rate of gas passage through a sensor and integrating that rate over time.

Suppose a spirometer initially reads zero at a quiet end-expiratory point. The person inspires 0.5 L, expires 0.5 L, then inspires maximally by 3.0 L from the original point and expires to the maximal endpoint, 1.0 L below the original point. The instrument has measured a 4.0 L excursion. It has no information about how much gas is still in the lung at the lower endpoint. Adding an arbitrary constant to every absolute lung volume would generate the same recorded changes. This mathematical indeterminacy cannot be solved by increasing the accuracy of the same sensor.

Calibration and signal quality matter because a spirometer measures movement through a particular boundary, not all movement inside the respiratory system. A mouth leak allows gas to bypass the sensor. A cough can abruptly alter the volume-time curve. An incomplete initial inspiration reduces the available exhaled volume, while early termination may underestimate FVC. Flow integration can accumulate an error if a sensor has an incorrect zero offset. Repetition under standardised conditions helps distinguish an isolated poor manoeuvre from a reproducible physiological pattern. The ATS/ERS 2019 spirometry standard sets procedures for assessing measurement acceptability and repeatability; it is not necessary to memorise every clinical grading rule to understand the physiological principles.

The directly measurable ordinary volumes are VT, IRV and ERV, with suitable manoeuvres. IC and VC can also be obtained from their endpoint differences. RV cannot be directly measured by simple spirometry, and neither can capacities that contain it: FRC and TLC. When a question asks which capacity is inaccessible, inspect its expression. IC contains no RV; FRC and TLC both contain RV. This limitation is independent of whether the instrument is mechanical or electronic.

The distinction between precision and validity is especially important. A trace can show highly repeatable tidal swings even though its absolute vertical origin is arbitrary. A very precise estimate of expired volume therefore does not become an estimate of residual volume. To determine the unknown baseline, a separate physical measurement such as gas dilution or plethysmography is required.

A typical modern flow sensor can infer flow from a calibrated pressure difference across a resistive element. In a simple linear approximation the pressure difference is proportional to gas flow. Electronic integration then gives the net volume crossing the sensor. If the zero-flow reading is biased by a constant 0.02 L/s, integration over ten seconds generates an apparent volume error of 0.20 L. The error can remain invisible in a short portion of the flow trace yet become substantial in a longer volume manoeuvre. Zeroing and calibration are therefore important before measurements that depend on integration.

Leaks are not equivalent to zero drift. A leak changes the relationship between the gas movement in the respiratory system and the gas passing through the instrument. If a person expires through both the mouthpiece and the nose, the mouth sensor may underestimate total expiration. In contrast, a calibration error changes the relationship between gas passing through the instrument and the numerical signal it produces. These mechanisms require different checks. A system can be perfectly calibrated yet give an invalid physiological estimate when the intended gas boundary is not maintained.

Consider the difference between a slowly performed vital capacity manoeuvre and a forced expiration. If the slow manoeuvre is initiated at full inspiration, the volume difference is defined by the same ideal endpoints as in the forced test. However, the pressure conditions and airway compression differ. A slow expiration may permit some units to empty before their pathways close, whereas rapid forced expiration can accentuate narrowing. The difference between slow VC and FVC therefore contains physiological information in suitable measurements. It should not be assumed to occur in all individuals, nor should a difference be interpreted without confirming that both manoeuvres reached appropriate starting and finishing endpoints.

Repeated testing poses a subtle experimental issue. If a subject performs three attempts and one has a poorly sustained expiration, averaging all three values may reduce the reported FVC without improving biological validity. The first task is to identify acceptable manoeuvres and then evaluate reproducibility among those manoeuvres. The statistical treatment of repeated values should not replace scrutiny of their quality. This principle is general to physiology: averaging a biased or invalid measurement does not restore the missing information.

5. Measuring the unexpired gas: dilution and plethysmography

In closed-circuit helium dilution, a person breathes from an apparatus containing a known gas volume and known helium fraction. Helium is chosen because it is relatively insoluble and does not undergo substantial metabolic consumption during the measurement. Suppose the person joins the circuit at quiet end-expiration. The helium initially in the apparatus spreads through the apparatus and the communicating lung gas. Its concentration falls as it occupies a greater volume. The decrease can reveal the initially unknown lung compartment.

Let V1 be the known initial circuit volume, C1 its initial helium fraction, C2 the final equilibrium helium fraction, and VL the communicating lung gas volume at connection. If there are no relevant leaks or helium losses and the gas volumes are consistently expressed, helium conservation gives C1V1 = C2(V1 + VL). Rearranging gives VL = V1(C1/C2 − 1). If the apparatus contains 3.0 L at a helium fraction of 0.10 and equilibrium occurs at 0.06, the communicating lung volume is 3.0(0.10/0.06 − 1) = 2.0 L. Because connection occurred at the end of quiet expiration, the estimated quantity is communicating FRC, not TLC.

The simple derivation assumes effective mixing and a known circuit volume. Real instruments must account for changes due to oxygen consumption, carbon dioxide absorption and gas conditions. A poorly ventilated compartment may not reach equilibrium within a practical measurement interval. Trapped gas that does not communicate with the circuit will not dilute the tracer. As a result, gas dilution can underestimate total thoracic gas volume in an individual with marked gas trapping. The result is not necessarily a broken instrument; it reflects the physical compartment accessible to the tracer.

An open-circuit nitrogen washout follows a related conservation principle. When a person breathes an oxygen-rich gas mixture, resident nitrogen is progressively washed out and its expired quantity can be measured. With corrections and assumptions, the original accessible lung gas volume can be inferred from the amount of nitrogen recovered. The approach is not identical to helium dilution: one measures the recovery of gas initially in the lung, while the other observes dilution of gas introduced from the apparatus. Both rely on communication between the measured airways and lung gas compartments.

Body plethysmography uses a different physical signal. A subject sits inside a sealed chamber and makes small respiratory efforts against a briefly closed airway shutter. If the airway pressure tracks alveolar pressure during the manoeuvre, changes in mouth pressure estimate changes in intrathoracic gas pressure. Small changes in thoracic volume cause complementary changes in chamber gas pressure. Boyle's law relates pressure to volume for a fixed amount of gas under approximately isothermal conditions. In differential form PΔV + VΔP ≈ 0 for small changes. Hence V ≈ −PΔV/ΔP, with absolute pressure and a consistent sign convention. Actual measurements require calibration and appropriate thermal and apparatus corrections.

Plethysmography can measure compressible thoracic gas that does not freely communicate with the mouth. It therefore may yield a higher estimate than dilution in substantial airway obstruction. However, the mouth-to-alveolar pressure assumption can fail, particularly with rapid panting and severe obstruction; plethysmography is not immune to error. A comparison between methods is informative only after checking quality and the conditions of measurement. For example, FRC estimates of 2.8 L by dilution and 3.6 L by plethysmography suggest that some gas may have been inaccessible to the dilution measurement, but the difference cannot be labelled exact trapped gas volume without considering technical uncertainties.

The tracer mass balance can be examined as a limiting case. If the final helium fraction equals the initial fraction, the formula predicts zero additional communicating volume. This would be impossible for a correctly connected living adult and would prompt a check for a closed valve or an incorrect concentration measurement. If the final concentration is substantially lower, the calculated communicating compartment is larger. A final fraction greater than the initial fraction would produce a negative estimate, indicating a violation of the assumed dilution model or an error in the inputs. These simple boundary checks allow a student to judge the plausibility of an answer without a detailed calculation.

The sensitivity of the dilution estimate depends on the final concentration. With a 3 L circuit and an initial helium fraction of 0.10, a final fraction of 0.06 implies 2.0 L of communicating gas. A measured value of 0.061 instead implies approximately 1.92 L, whereas 0.059 implies approximately 2.08 L. A small absolute concentration uncertainty therefore generates an uncertainty in the volume estimate. Precision depends on the accuracy of gas analysis and the initial apparatus volume, as well as the biological extent of mixing. These numbers are hypothetical and illustrate error propagation rather than instrument performance specifications.

Slow equilibration can be understood by representing the lung as two communicating compartments, one with low airway resistance and the other with higher resistance. The easily ventilated unit exchanges tracer rapidly with the circuit. The poorly ventilated unit contributes more slowly. A short measurement may show an apparently stable concentration determined mainly by the fast unit, even though further redistribution would occur with sufficient time. Repeated observation of concentration over time can help reveal this pattern. The compartment model makes clear that the tracer does not directly measure the amount of tissue; it measures the gas that has participated in mixing during the observation interval.

Plethysmography relies on a complementary signal. Imagine that small changes in thoracic volume displace gas in the chamber while the mouth is temporarily occluded. When the subject attempts inspiration, intrathoracic gas tends to expand and its pressure decreases. The increase in body volume compresses the chamber gas and tends to increase its pressure. The two pressures therefore change in opposite directions for this idealised event. In expiration the signs reverse. The quantitative instrument converts the measured chamber pressure change to a volume displacement by calibration; the mouth pressure change supplies information about the compression or expansion of thoracic gas. It is the relationship between the two signals that yields the volume estimate, not the isolated pressure change alone.

A gas dilution result and a plethysmographic result should be compared under matched physiological conditions. If a subject changes posture between the tests, the operating volume may change for reasons unrelated to inaccessible gas. If the subject makes forceful or irregular efforts during the pressure manoeuvre, mouth pressure may no longer provide a good representation of the pressure throughout the lung. For a conceptual question the two methods are often assumed ideal and measured at the same endpoint. In experimental interpretation those assumptions must be tested. This difference between a textbook calculation and an empirical inference is one of the most important limitations of method comparison.

6. Forced expiration and reference-dependent interpretation

A forced expiratory test asks how quickly a person can expel gas after maximal inspiration and how much can be expelled by the end of the manoeuvre. Forced vital capacity is the total measured expired volume. FEV1 is the volume expelled during the first second. Their ratio is dimensionless and is often expressed as a percentage. An FEV1 of 3.2 L and FVC of 4.0 L gives 0.80 or 80 percent. The fraction does not represent the percentage of TLC expired in one second, because the denominator is FVC rather than TLC.

During forced expiration, alveolar pressure rises because expiratory muscles increase pleural pressure and the lung has elastic recoil. Pressure falls along the airway towards the mouth. In intrathoracic airways, the pressure inside may eventually become lower than surrounding pleural pressure, causing dynamic compression. Beyond a certain effort at a given lung volume, stronger expiratory effort need not produce proportionately higher flow because the compressing pressure also rises. Loss of elastic recoil or increased airway resistance changes the pressure distribution and can make flow limitation more pronounced. The volume-time trace then rises more slowly and may take longer to reach its final plateau.

A low FEV1 is not uniquely a marker of obstruction. It can occur when FVC is small, even if a normal fraction of that small volume is expelled in one second. The ratio helps distinguish disproportionately reduced early flow from a proportionate reduction in both volumes. In obstructive ventilatory impairment, FEV1/FVC is below its reference lower limit. A low FVC with a preserved ratio suggests possible restriction or another pattern with reduced movable volume; a low TLC is required to confirm restriction. If both ratio and FVC are low, mixed impairment is possible, but TLC is needed to separate true restriction from gas trapping.

Interpretation must use appropriate reference equations. The lower limit of normal commonly corresponds to the fifth percentile of a healthy reference distribution, approximately a z-score of −1.645 under the standard-normal model. A fixed ratio of 0.70 is not a universal physiological boundary; normal ratios vary with age and other characteristics. Percent predicted and z-scores are also not interchangeable, because the spread of expected values is not constant across all ages and body sizes. Examination questions may provide a reference LLN explicitly, in which case the stated LLN should govern the interpretation.

Consider three illustrative records. Subject A has FEV1 3.6 L and FVC 4.5 L, ratio 0.80; subject B has 1.8 L and 4.0 L, ratio 0.45; subject C has 2.0 L and 2.4 L, ratio 0.83. If a hypothetical appropriate ratio LLN is 0.70 for all three, B shows an obstructive pattern. C has preserved fractional emptying but a smaller total expired volume. Without TLC and an appropriate FVC reference, it is not legitimate to diagnose restriction from this record. If C has TLC below its LLN, restriction is established as a physiological pattern, without thereby identifying its disease cause.

A useful way to interpret FEV1 is to ask what fraction of the final forced expiration remains after one second. For a ratio of 0.80, 20 percent of the eventual forced volume remains to be expelled after the first second. If FVC is 4 L, that corresponds to 0.8 L. With a ratio of 0.50 and the same FVC, 2 L remains after the first second. These statements follow directly from the ratio and do not require knowing TLC. They also do not identify the cause of the slower emptying; resistance, recoil and test performance must be considered.

Time constants help relate the measured curves to mechanical properties. In a simplified passive compartment with linear resistance R and compliance C, the product RC has units of time. For example, resistance expressed as cm H₂O per L/s multiplied by compliance in L per cm H₂O yields seconds. A larger time constant means that the compartment approaches its new equilibrium more slowly after a pressure change. Although forced expiration is not a simple passive exponential process, the concept helps explain why regions with different resistance and compliance can empty at different rates. In a heterogeneous lung, rapid breathing or early termination of expiration preferentially leaves gas in slow-emptying units.

The reference lower limit is derived from the expected distribution of a measurement in a suitable population. A fifth-percentile threshold implies that some healthy individuals will lie below that boundary, even when measurement is technically satisfactory. Conversely, an individual whose result is still within the reference interval can have declined substantially from their own previous baseline. A reference classification and a longitudinal comparison answer different questions. NET-style datasets frequently supply a personal baseline or reference values precisely so that the student can avoid equating a reference threshold with a perfect disease boundary.

If both FEV1/FVC and TLC are below their respective lower limits, the measurements support a mixed ventilatory pattern. If the ratio is below LLN and TLC is normal or elevated, a low FVC may be explained by an elevated RV or other mechanisms without establishing restriction. If the ratio is preserved and TLC is low, restriction is supported. These classifications are statements about the measured mechanics, not diagnoses of specific causes. The same pattern can arise through multiple biological mechanisms, and a physiological examination should distinguish the classification from clinical attribution.

7. Flow–volume loops

A flow–volume loop plots flow against the volume coordinate during a respiratory manoeuvre. The axis convention must be read before interpreting its shape. A commonly used convention places exhaled volume on the horizontal axis, increasing from left to right, and expiratory flow above the horizontal axis. The loop begins at full inspiration, rises rapidly to peak expiratory flow, descends as the lung empties, and returns to zero flow at the expiratory endpoint. The subsequent inspiratory limb may be drawn below the axis. Some instruments instead plot absolute lung volume decreasing to the right or reverse the orientation, so a memorised visual shape is not sufficient without labels.

Peak expiratory flow is the greatest instantaneous expiratory flow in the test; FEV1 is an integrated volume over one second. They differ in units and physiological determinants. The descending expiratory limb reflects the combination of recoil, airway calibre and dynamic compression. Obstruction often produces a concave descending limb and reduced early flow. A restrictive pattern can produce a smaller loop because the maximal movable volume is reduced, with a relatively preserved shape. The absolute flow may still be reduced, and no particular loop shape alone proves reduced TLC.

A flow–volume diagram can also expose poor technique. A hesitant start may depress the initial peak, a cough can interrupt the limb, and stopping expiration prematurely can truncate the curve. These possibilities must be considered before relating morphology to mechanical disease. The experiment becomes stronger when repeated acceptable curves agree, when volume-time measurements provide a complementary view and when independent lung-volume measurements answer questions that spirometry cannot resolve.

For a simple idealised time course, if a subject exhales 1.2 L in the first half-second and 0.8 L in the next half-second, FEV1 is 2.0 L. The mean flows during these intervals are 2.4 and 1.6 L/s, respectively. Neither is necessarily the peak flow because each averages a finite interval. If FVC is 3.5 L, the ratio is approximately 0.57. To interpret this as obstruction one needs the ratio reference limit and a technically acceptable manoeuvre. The calculation links the area under a flow-time record to the volume-time record and demonstrates why a single peak cannot determine FEV1.

Flow–volume plots can be constructed from sampled flow and volume signals without explicitly displaying time. This removes direct information about how long the subject occupied each point. Two curves might have similar shapes in volume coordinates but different temporal behaviour, especially if they differ in initial effort or in the manoeuvre used to obtain them. To recover the amount of gas expired within the first second, one needs a time-based record or equivalent information. The loop alone, without time markers, does not automatically reveal FEV1.

At maximal inspiration, the airways are generally more open because surrounding tissue exerts greater radial traction. Early forced expiration therefore allows a relatively high flow when the driving pressure is large and the airways are relatively wide. As volume falls, elastic recoil and airway dimensions change. The descending limb is consequently not simply a measure of declining muscular effort. A subject may sustain high expiratory effort even while flow falls. The phenomenon of effort-independent flow helps explain why the later portion of an acceptable curve can remain limited despite an attempt to blow harder.

When comparing loops, the horizontal and vertical scales must be identical or interpreted explicitly. A loop covering three centimetres on a printed page is not necessarily smaller in litres than another loop covering four centimetres if the axes have different scales. The same is true of peak flow. Normalising each trace to its own maximal volume can highlight shape differences while concealing absolute volume differences. A scientific interpretation should state whether the plotted coordinates are absolute values or percentages of a reference or individual maximum. This elementary graph-reading rule prevents an otherwise correct description of physiological patterns from being applied to the wrong data.

8. Dead space and the route of a tidal breath

The conducting airways deliver gas to the exchange region and also warm, humidify and filter it. Their walls do not provide a significant alveolar blood–gas exchange surface. The gas volume occupying this pathway is anatomical dead space. At the start of inspiration, gas already present in the conducting airways moves deeper into the lungs before newly inhaled atmospheric gas reaches the alveoli. At the start of expiration, the gas that was left in the conducting region near the end of inspiration leaves first. It contains relatively little carbon dioxide compared with gas from exchanging alveoli.

In a healthy adult, a value of around 150 mL is a convenient illustrative anatomical dead-space volume, not a fixed value for every adult or posture. Airway size, lung volume and attached breathing apparatus can change the volume of the pathway. A tube may add apparatus dead space if gas is rebreathed from it. Bypassing the upper airway can reduce the anatomical pathway traversed by gas. One must distinguish a physical change in conduit volume from a change in alveolar gas-exchange efficiency.

Alveolar dead space refers to ventilation of alveoli that receives insufficient perfusion for effective gas exchange. In the extreme, an alveolus receives fresh air but no blood, so it cannot transfer meaningful oxygen to the circulation or remove carbon dioxide from it. A partially perfused high ventilation–perfusion unit can also contribute to wasted ventilation. Physiological dead space includes anatomical and alveolar contributions. In a healthy lung the latter contribution is often small, but it can increase when perfusion is uneven or reduced.

Consider a simplified lung with two alveolar compartments receiving the same amount of ventilation. If one compartment loses its blood supply while the other retains it, the total ventilation arriving at the alveoli may remain constant. Nevertheless, ventilation of the unperfused compartment no longer supports gas exchange with the circulating blood. The distinction between total alveolar inflow and effective exchanging ventilation becomes essential. A dead-space estimate based on carbon dioxide elimination addresses the latter issue rather than simply measuring airway geometry.

A shunt is different: blood passes through a region that receives little or no ventilation. In a pure shunt ventilation is zero despite perfusion, whereas in pure alveolar dead space perfusion is zero despite ventilation. Both impair efficient respiratory exchange, but their mechanisms are opposite. The detailed distribution of ventilation and perfusion belongs to the following chapter; here the distinction explains why a measurement based on arterial carbon dioxide may reflect more than physical dead space.

Anatomical dead space can be estimated conceptually from the changing concentration of a tracer during a single expiration. The Fowler single-breath nitrogen method illustrates this principle. After inspiration of oxygen-rich gas, the early expired gas contains little nitrogen because it comes primarily from the conducting airways. Later gas has a higher nitrogen fraction as gas from alveolar regions reaches the sampling point. The transition is not perfectly abrupt because gas from different regions mixes. An equal-area construction can estimate an effective boundary between conducting and alveolar gas. This is a geometrical measurement concept rather than an assertion that all airway paths have identical lengths.

The Fowler and Bohr approaches ask different questions. Fowler's approach estimates a conducting volume from the transition between gas compartments. Bohr's approach estimates wasted ventilation from the dilution of carbon dioxide in mixed expired gas. If alveolar ventilation is well matched to perfusion, the two estimates may be relatively close. If significant ventilation reaches poorly perfused alveoli, the Bohr physiological dead-space estimate can exceed the anatomical estimate. The difference is informative about the exchange compartment only to the extent that both methods meet their assumptions and the respiratory conditions are comparable.

Dead space is necessary in many respiratory architectures. Conducting airways prevent the fragile exchange surfaces from being directly exposed to unconditioned air and distribute gas to many branching regions. Eliminating the conducting pathway is not a meaningful physiological objective, because the pathway serves protective and mechanical functions. The cost of moving gas through a conduit is balanced against the advantages of transporting air to a large, internal, moist exchange surface. In very small animals or in insects with a different mode of respiratory delivery, the geometry and functional interpretation of dead space differ.

An alveolus that receives less blood than expected may still exchange some gas. It need not be either fully efficient or completely wasted. The extreme two-compartment picture is useful because it clarifies the directions of ventilation and perfusion, but an actual lung contains a continuum of relationships. An effective dead-space estimate summarises that distribution into a volume equivalent. The result does not necessarily identify a physically bounded anatomical region. Distinguishing an equivalent volume inferred from gas exchange from an anatomical volume measured directly becomes essential when interpreting carbon dioxide dilution equations.

9. Minute ventilation and effective ventilation

Minute ventilation is the gas volume breathed per minute. For regular breaths of tidal volume VT and respiratory frequency f, the expression is V̇E = VT × f. If VT is in litres per breath and f is in breaths per minute, V̇E is in litres per minute. A person breathing 0.5 L at 12 breaths per minute therefore has minute ventilation of 6.0 L/min. For unequal breaths, the total volume over the observation period is divided by elapsed time, rather than multiplying a single unrepresentative breath by frequency.

In the simplified model, the dead-space volume VD of each breath is not available for effective exchange. Effective alveolar ventilation is V̇A = (VT − VD)f. Using an illustrative VD of 0.15 L, a 0.5 L breath delivers 0.35 L of effective fresh gas and at 12 breaths per minute supplies 4.2 L/min. The remaining 1.8 L/min is dead-space ventilation. The balance V̇E = V̇A + V̇D follows by expanding the expression. When anatomical dead space is used, the formula estimates fresh gas reaching alveolar regions; when an effective physiological VD is used, it estimates ventilation participating in exchange under the simplified model.

Compare a rapid shallow pattern of 0.25 L at 24 breaths/min and a slower pattern of 0.75 L at 8 breaths/min. Both produce minute ventilation of 6.0 L/min. With VD 0.15 L per breath, the first delivers only 2.4 L/min to effective alveolar ventilation, while the second delivers 4.8 L/min. The difference arises because a dead-space charge is paid on every breath. A larger fraction of the shallow breath is consumed by the conducting pathway. These hypothetical numbers illustrate an important exam principle, not a recommendation to choose a particular breathing pattern.

The mathematical relationship can also be written as V̇A = V̇E − fVD. If minute ventilation and dead-space volume remain fixed, increasing frequency decreases effective ventilation linearly. This result depends on the fixed-VD approximation; anatomical dead space can vary with lung volume, and physiological dead space can vary with perfusion. If VT approaches VD, the simple expression predicts effective ventilation approaching zero. At such small volumes, diffusion, mixing and departures from plug-flow assumptions become important, so the equation should not be extrapolated indiscriminately to every possible ventilatory pattern.

Carbon dioxide elimination links these quantities to metabolism. In a simplified steady state, arterial or alveolar carbon dioxide pressure is inversely related to effective alveolar ventilation at fixed metabolic carbon dioxide production. Thus the rapid shallow pattern above would tend to increase carbon dioxide pressure relative to a deeper pattern with the same minute ventilation, if other variables remain fixed. The precise conversion requires consistent gas-volume conventions and a defined relationship between alveolar and arterial gas. Oxygenation also depends on perfusion and gas exchange, so equal calculated ventilation does not establish equal arterial oxygen content.

The minute ventilation equation can be rearranged to determine the breathing frequency needed for a specified effective ventilation in the simple fixed-dead-space model. If the required effective ventilation is 4.2 L/min and each breath is 0.5 L with 0.15 L dead space, each breath contributes 0.35 L and the frequency is 4.2/0.35 = 12 breaths/min. If the tidal volume falls to 0.35 L while dead space remains 0.15 L, each breath contributes 0.20 L and the required frequency increases to 21 breaths/min. The corresponding minute ventilation is now 7.35 L/min. Thus the respiratory system would need to move more total gas to achieve the same effective ventilation.

The fraction of each tidal volume occupied by dead space provides another perspective. For VT of 0.5 L and VD of 0.15 L, VD/VT is 0.30. If VT falls to 0.3 L while VD remains constant, the fraction increases to 0.50. The first pattern devotes 70 percent of its minute ventilation to the effective component, the second only 50 percent. Multiplying the minute ventilation by one minus the dead-space fraction yields the same effective ventilation as the per-breath calculation. Both methods are equivalent provided all quantities refer to the same respiratory pattern.

It would be wrong to infer that making every breath arbitrarily large is energetically optimal. Increasing tidal volume can increase elastic work, especially near the upper lung-volume limit where further expansion becomes difficult. Increasing frequency can increase the cost of overcoming flow resistance and can reduce the available time for expiration. The respiratory system balances several constraints, including metabolic demand, airway resistance, lung compliance, and timing. The equations in this chapter establish the ventilation consequences of chosen patterns but do not independently determine the pattern that minimises total work.

A nonsteady pattern requires care with averaging. Suppose during a minute a subject takes ten breaths of 0.4 L and five breaths of 0.8 L. Total expired volume is 10×0.4 + 5×0.8 = 8 L. If VD is 0.15 L for every breath, total wasted ventilation is 15×0.15 = 2.25 L and effective ventilation is 5.75 L/min. The mean tidal volume is 8/15 L; multiplying it by frequency and subtracting the dead-space cost produces the same result. But if dead space differs systematically among breaths, using an inappropriate average can produce a different estimate. The safest approach is to sum the individual effective volumes when breath-by-breath data are available.

10. The Bohr equation and the Enghoff substitution

Christian Bohr introduced an approach to quantifying wasted ventilation from the dilution of carbon dioxide in expired gas. The conceptual experiment separates a breath into a volume VD containing negligible carbon dioxide and a volume VT − VD containing gas of representative alveolar carbon dioxide concentration. Gas from these two components mixes during collection. The mixed expired concentration therefore lies below the alveolar concentration if the dead-space contribution is positive.

Let PACO₂ denote representative alveolar carbon dioxide partial pressure and PECO₂ denote volume-weighted mixed expired carbon dioxide partial pressure. Under consistent pressure and humidification conventions, and assuming negligible carbon dioxide in inspired and dead-space gas, conservation of exhaled carbon dioxide gives VT × PECO₂ = (VT − VD) × PACO₂. Dividing by VT × PACO₂ yields PECO₂/PACO₂ = 1 − VD/VT. The classical Bohr equation follows: VD/VT = (PACO₂ − PECO₂)/PACO₂. The ratio is dimensionless, and both pressures must be expressed in the same units. Multiplying by VT recovers the volume equivalent of dead space for the model.

For illustrative measurements of PACO₂ 40 mmHg and PECO₂ 28 mmHg, the fraction is 12/40 = 0.30. With an expired tidal volume of 0.50 L, the equivalent dead-space volume is 0.15 L. If frequency is 12 breaths per minute, dead-space ventilation is 1.8 L/min. The calculation does not reveal how much of the 0.15 L is anatomical and how much alveolar; an independent measure or model is required to partition the components.

In a heterogeneous lung, the representative alveolar value is not necessarily equal to arterial carbon dioxide pressure. Enghoff's modification substitutes PaCO₂ for PACO₂, because arterial carbon dioxide can be measured independently. The resulting index reflects overall inefficiency of gas exchange, including the effects of shunt and ventilation–perfusion inequality, and should not automatically be treated as the same true dead-space volume as the classical Bohr quantity. If PACO₂ is 35 mmHg, PaCO₂ is 42 mmHg and mixed expired pressure is 25 mmHg, the Bohr fraction is 10/35 ≈ 0.286, while the Enghoff index is 17/42 ≈ 0.405. The values are calculated from the same mixed expired gas but answer different physiological questions.

End-tidal carbon dioxide is the pressure measured near the end of an expiration. It is not automatically the volume-weighted mixed expired pressure, which includes the earlier gas with little carbon dioxide. A volumetric capnogram plots carbon dioxide against expired volume. The early region contains mostly conducting gas, the transition reflects mixing, and the alveolar region contains gas from exchanging units. Integrating carbon dioxide concentration over expired volume and dividing by that volume estimates the mixed expired concentration. A representative alveolar value requires a justified estimate, particularly when the plateau slopes. Replacing the mixed expired value with the end-tidal value would generally underestimate dilution and can produce an incorrect dead-space calculation.

The Bohr model assumes negligible inspired carbon dioxide. If gas is rebreathed and inspired carbon dioxide is appreciable, the simple balance must be modified to account for carbon dioxide entering with the breath. Likewise, pressure fractions are only interchangeable with gas fractions under consistent physical conditions. These limitations are central to experimental reasoning: a formula is a compact statement of a conservation model, not an unconditional definition detached from the way the data were collected.

The derivation of the Bohr equation can be generalised when the gas occupying the wasted compartment contains a nonzero carbon dioxide pressure PI. If the two-compartment mixing model remains appropriate, VT PE = VD PI + (VT − VD) PA. Rearrangement gives VD/VT = (PA − PE)/(PA − PI). Here all pressures denote carbon dioxide partial pressure under comparable physical conditions, PA is representative alveolar pressure, PE is mixed expired pressure, and PI is the pressure assigned to the wasted gas. When PI is negligible, the equation reduces to the classical expression. If PI is appreciable, ignoring it alters the estimate. The generalisation is a teaching model; actual rebreathing may produce more complicated spatial and temporal variations than a single PI can represent.

Using the generalised model with PA = 40 mmHg, PE = 31 mmHg and PI = 10 mmHg gives a fraction of 9/30 = 0.30. Applying the classical expression despite the stated nonzero PI would give 9/40 = 0.225. This difference illustrates the cost of neglecting an assumption rather than a failure of arithmetic. The correct formula depends on the defined experimental conditions. In a question where inspired carbon dioxide is not mentioned and the classical Bohr equation is requested, negligible inspired carbon dioxide is normally intended.

A mixed expired sample must be weighted by volume, not simply by the number of gas samples collected. Suppose the first 0.15 L of an expired breath has effectively zero carbon dioxide, the next 0.10 L has a mean pressure of 20 mmHg and the final 0.25 L has 40 mmHg. The volume-weighted pressure is (0.15×0 + 0.10×20 + 0.25×40)/0.50 = 24 mmHg. The unweighted arithmetic mean of the three stated pressures is 20 mmHg, which is not appropriate because the segments have different volumes. If representative alveolar pressure is 40 mmHg, the Bohr fraction is 0.40. The calculated equivalent volume of 0.20 L differs from the 0.15 L segment with negligible carbon dioxide because the transition segment also dilutes the expired gas.

Enghoff's substitution is especially important in interpreting comparative datasets. Consider two subjects with identical mixed expired carbon dioxide and identical representative alveolar carbon dioxide but different arterial carbon dioxide. Their classical Bohr fractions are identical. Their Enghoff indices differ because the latter use arterial measurements. It is therefore incorrect to conclude from the Enghoff difference alone that the subjects have different conducting-airway volumes. The result can instead reflect a difference in the relationship between alveolar and arterial gas due to heterogeneous exchange and blood flow. An independent anatomical measurement or regional information is necessary before assigning the change to a specific physical compartment.

The limiting values of the classical equation provide another check. If mixed expired carbon dioxide equals the representative alveolar pressure, the model estimates zero wasted fraction. If mixed expired pressure is zero while alveolar pressure is positive, the formula returns one, corresponding to an expired sample composed entirely of gas without the representative alveolar carbon dioxide contribution. Values outside the range zero to one indicate inconsistent measurements or assumptions for the ideal two-compartment model. If representative alveolar pressure itself is zero, the ratio cannot be evaluated; there is no concentration contrast on which the calculation can rely.

11. Integrated interpretation and experimental design

A useful investigation begins by specifying the physiological question. To determine whether two individuals have different maximal movable gas volumes, a suitably performed vital capacity manoeuvre may suffice. To determine whether low FVC indicates a small total lung capacity or an increased residual volume, an independent absolute lung-volume measurement is necessary. To determine whether identical minute ventilation provides identical effective ventilation, measurements of tidal volume and dead space are needed. Each method resolves a different unknown.

Suppose a study compares an adult before and after a change in posture. The measured IC falls by 0.4 L and TLC remains unchanged within measurement uncertainty. From TLC = FRC + IC, FRC must have increased by approximately 0.4 L if all endpoints are comparable. However, the observation does not by itself identify whether altered chest wall mechanics or incomplete expiration produced the change. Recording the breathing pattern, allowing a stable baseline and repeating the manoeuvre under the same apparatus conditions would strengthen the interpretation. A control condition without the posture change would help distinguish a true effect from drift or repeated-test variability.

In another illustrative dataset, subject D has FEV1 1.6 L, FVC 2.5 L, TLC 6.0 L and RV 3.2 L. Its FEV1/FVC is 0.64, and the slow vital capacity inferred from TLC and RV is 2.8 L. If the appropriate ratio is below LLN while TLC is within reference limits and RV elevated, the pattern supports obstruction and gas trapping rather than confirmed restriction. The difference between inferred slow VC and FVC may reflect dynamic airway closure during forced expiration, although measurement quality must also be examined. If another subject has a similarly reduced FVC but genuinely low TLC and a preserved ratio, the mechanisms are different despite similar FVC values.

An experiment examining a breathing circuit can compare otherwise identical ventilation before and after adding a known apparatus volume. In the simple model, adding 0.05 L to dead space at 12 breaths/min reduces effective ventilation by 0.6 L/min if tidal volume and frequency remain unchanged. In a living subject compensatory changes in breathing may occur, so an observed change in carbon dioxide cannot be interpreted without the measured breathing pattern. A conceptual model using supplied numbers is sufficient to establish the prediction; an actual study would require ethical and safety oversight.

Measurement uncertainty should be carried through reasoning. If two estimated absolute volumes differ by an amount comparable to their repeatability error, the data do not demonstrate a meaningful difference. If a ratio is close to its reference limit, it is inappropriate to treat the threshold as an exact biological discontinuity. Classification aids interpretation, but physiological variation is continuous. Similarly, a hypothetical numerical result calculated to four decimal places should not be reported with precision that exceeds the quality of the input data.

The measurements developed so far allow us to move between compartment diagrams, measured traces, conservation equations and biological mechanisms. A student who understands the endpoint structure can derive capacities, a student who understands sensor boundaries can identify what spirometry misses, and a student who understands dead-space dilution can predict the consequences of changing frequency or perfusion. The following sections extend this reasoning to gas-volume conventions, timing and experimental evidence before the analysis of gas exchange in 7.C.05.

A final form of integrated analysis uses multiple measurements to distinguish two competing hypotheses. Imagine a small FVC could result from either a reduction in maximal lung volume or premature airway closure. The first hypothesis predicts a low TLC; the second allows a normal TLC with elevated RV. A measurement of TLC and RV therefore separates predictions that cannot be separated by FVC alone. If both hypotheses receive partial support, for example when TLC is low and RV/TLC is increased, the interpretation may involve more than one mechanism. The data should determine the conclusion rather than the investigator forcing every subject into one simple model.

A second investigation might compare carbon dioxide clearance in two respiratory patterns at equal minute ventilation. The prediction from the fixed-dead-space model is that a pattern of smaller, more frequent breaths has lower effective ventilation. An adequate test must establish that metabolic carbon dioxide production is comparable, because exercise or other changes in metabolism can independently change expired carbon dioxide. It should also identify whether the apparatus contributes dead space and whether ventilation has reached a steady state. A transient measurement immediately after changing the pattern may reflect body carbon dioxide stores rather than the steady relationship. The design thus links the mathematical prediction to the controls needed to test it.

Students should distinguish measured values from estimates in their own reasoning. FEV1 may be directly derived from a calibrated flow-time signal; TLC may be inferred from two measured endpoints; physiological dead space may be inferred through a gas mixing model. All can be scientifically useful, but each carries different assumptions. A robust explanation traces the chain from biological compartment to instrument signal to calculated quantity and then to interpretation. This makes it possible to identify which additional measurement would reduce uncertainty instead of merely restating an observed pattern.

Lung-volume measurements do not directly establish the rate at which oxygen reaches tissues. Normal TLC and an acceptable spirometric ratio do not guarantee normal diffusion across the alveolar barrier, and normal minute ventilation does not guarantee adequate perfusion of ventilated regions. Conversely, an abnormal spirometric pattern does not determine arterial oxygen content without additional information. This is the conceptual bridge to the next chapter: effective gas exchange requires not only gas movement but also the appropriate partial-pressure gradients, exchange surface properties and matching of gas delivery to blood flow.

12. Gas-volume conventions and the meaning of a litre

The reason for correcting gas measurements to common conditions becomes apparent when a sample of expired gas is cooled. Water vapour may condense, the remaining gas contracts, and the volume registered by a measuring chamber can be smaller than the volume that gas occupied inside the body. No change in lung anatomy is required to produce this difference. If two devices operate at different temperatures or report different gas-volume conventions, a comparison of their uncorrected volume readings can therefore be misleading even if both instruments operate correctly.

The ideal gas equation is PV = nRT, where P denotes absolute pressure, V denotes volume, n denotes the amount of gas in moles, R denotes the gas constant, and T denotes absolute temperature in kelvin. For the dry components of a humid gas sample, the effective pressure available to those components is the total pressure minus the partial pressure of water vapour. If the amount of dry gas is conserved, the expression (P − PH₂O)V/T has the same value before and after a change of conditions. Rearranging allows conversion between two volume conventions. This reasoning requires that dry gas has not leaked or been selectively absorbed during the transformation.

Consider a deliberately simplified hypothetical sample occupying 1.00 L at 293 K and a total pressure of 760 mmHg, with water vapour partial pressure of 17.5 mmHg. At 310 K and the same total pressure, suppose it is saturated with water vapour at approximately 47 mmHg. The corresponding volume is 1.00 × (310/293) × [(760−17.5)/(760−47)] ≈ 1.10 L. The increase follows from both warming and the change in water vapour content. These are illustrative physical inputs; actual instrument corrections depend on measured environmental conditions. The calculation is not a conversion to apply blindly to every recorded spirometry value, because a modern instrument may already have corrected its output to BTPS.

A partial pressure is not itself a gas volume. At a fixed total dry gas pressure, a mole fraction can be converted into the corresponding partial pressure by multiplication. The distinction matters when carbon dioxide concentrations are combined with measured volumes. A fraction of 0.04 multiplied by a total dry pressure of 713 mmHg corresponds to approximately 28.5 mmHg. If an instrument has already reported a pressure rather than a fraction, multiplying again by total pressure would be an error. In a Bohr calculation the same conventions must be used for the alveolar and mixed expired variables, allowing the common pressure conversion factor to cancel.

In an experiment comparing metabolic carbon dioxide output and respiratory ventilation, the two quantities may use different gas-volume reporting conventions. To derive the alveolar ventilation relationship, assume that a stable amount of CO₂ is added to exchanging alveolar gas each minute and removed in the expired effective component. If inspired CO₂ is negligible and the system is in steady state, the amount removed per minute must match the amount entering the alveolar gas. The alveolar CO₂ fraction is therefore proportional to the metabolic CO₂ output divided by effective ventilation. At a specified gas pressure the partial pressure is proportional to this fraction. The often-used relationship between CO₂ production and alveolar PCO₂ consequently follows from mass conservation, not merely from an empirical correlation.

When carbon dioxide output is expressed in mL/min at STPD and alveolar ventilation in L/min at BTPS, a familiar approximate conversion is PACO₂ = 0.863 × V̇CO₂/V̇A, with the resulting pressure in mmHg. The factor incorporates the gas-volume and pressure conventions and is not dimensionless in this form. For a hypothetical V̇CO₂ of 200 mL/min and effective ventilation of 4.3 L/min, the predicted alveolar pressure is approximately 40.1 mmHg. If effective ventilation decreases to 3.0 L/min at the same production, the prediction rises to about 57.5 mmHg. These examples assume a steady state and a representative alveolar compartment. The use of arterial pressure as a surrogate introduces further assumptions about gas exchange, which are examined in the next chapter.

A physical limit appears when total gas pressure changes substantially. At higher altitude the pressure available to the dry components of humidified inspired gas is lower, even though its oxygen fraction can be nearly unchanged. The respiratory system may respond by altering ventilation, but the initial effect of changing ambient pressure must be distinguished from that response. This chapter needs only the measurement consequence: comparisons of gas volumes and gas fractions require the pressure convention to be stated. The physiological responses to environmental hypoxia and the full gas exchange equations belong to later nodes.

A laboratory can check a volume-measuring instrument with a known displaced volume, but the calibration does not automatically validate its gas-condition corrections. If a system reports 3.0 L for a known 3.0 L displacement under room conditions, that is evidence for its response under those conditions. It is not evidence that every human expiration at a different temperature will be reported with the appropriate correction. The measurement chain includes a physical sensor, the conversion of its output to flow or volume, and the conversion to the desired reporting conditions. Distinguishing those stages enables a student to explain several seemingly contradictory measurement results without invoking an unsupported biological change.

13. How timing and mechanics alter measured endpoints

A lung volume is not independent of the time allowed for a manoeuvre. The lung contains many branching airways and gas compartments; these do not all fill and empty at the same rate. A simple mechanical model represents a compartment as an elastic gas space supplied through a resistance. Let C denote compliance, defined locally as change in volume divided by change in distending pressure, and let R denote resistance, defined as pressure difference divided by flow in the linear approximation. After a step change in driving pressure, the volume of such a compartment approaches its new equilibrium gradually rather than changing instantly.

For passive emptying of a linear compartment relative to its equilibrium volume, a simple relationship is dV/dt = −V/(RC), where V is the excess gas volume above equilibrium. The negative sign indicates that the excess volume decreases during expiration. Separating the variables gives dV/V = −dt/(RC). Integrating from the initial excess volume V₀ at time zero gives V(t) = V₀ exp(−t/τ), where τ = RC is the time constant in seconds. The model assumes constant resistance and compliance over the relevant volume range and no active change in expiratory driving pressure. A real forced expiratory manoeuvre violates several of these assumptions, so the expression should be used for conceptual passive behaviour rather than fitted uncritically to FEV1.

After one time constant, about 37 percent of the initial excess volume remains, and approximately 63 percent has been expelled. After three time constants, about 5 percent remains. Thus a compartment with a time constant of 0.5 s has expelled roughly 86 percent of its initial excess gas after one second, whereas one with a time constant of 2 s has expelled only about 39 percent. Equal initial excess volumes need not result in equal expired volumes over a short interval. A measurement made before slow compartments have substantially emptied may therefore reflect the timing of the observation as well as the available gas volume.

The relevance to breathing frequency can be understood without introducing the full mathematics of dynamic lung mechanics. If inspiration occupies a similar fraction of each respiratory cycle while frequency increases, less absolute time is available for expiration. A healthy system with relatively rapid emptying may still approach its resting volume before the next inspiration. A system with markedly prolonged expiratory time constants may begin the next breath before that return has occurred. Over successive breaths its end-expiratory operating volume can rise. The higher volume may itself increase elastic driving pressure and help the system reach a new repeating pattern, but the available inspiratory capacity becomes smaller if TLC is essentially unchanged.

This phenomenon distinguishes dynamic hyperinflation from a static alteration of resting lung volume. The static component reflects the mechanical balance and residual trapping under the measurement conditions. The dynamic component reflects incomplete expiration at the current ventilatory timing. An exercise experiment may show a reduction in inspiratory capacity during increased respiratory frequency even though maximal inspiratory volume is unchanged. Under that assumption the reduction in IC indicates a rise in end-expiratory lung volume. The assumption is essential: if maximal inspiratory effort changes, the measured IC can change for an additional reason.

A two-compartment example illustrates how time dependence can create a misleading average. Imagine two ideal passive units each initially containing 1.0 L above their equilibrium gas volume. Unit A has τ = 0.5 s and unit B has τ = 2.0 s. After one second, approximately 0.135 L remains in A and 0.607 L in B. Total expelled volume is therefore approximately 2.0 − 0.742 = 1.258 L. After two seconds, the remaining excess volumes are about 0.018 and 0.368 L, so approximately 1.614 L has been expelled. If only the first second is measured, the slow unit contributes relatively little to the recorded expiration. If a longer interval is allowed, its contribution increases. The combined curve is not described exactly by a single time constant because it is the sum of two exponential functions.

This conceptual model also explains why gas dilution can take longer in a heterogeneous lung. The tracer must enter and leave each accessible unit through its conducting pathways. If a unit exchanges only a small fraction of its gas on each breath, the circuit concentration approaches equilibrium slowly. The same pattern of poorly communicating regions can therefore influence both dynamic volume measurements and tracer dilution, although the physical experiments and their assumptions are different. An examiner may provide one abnormal measurement and ask which independent test would support the proposed mechanism. Recognising the role of slow exchange allows the student to choose a measurement that actually discriminates between hypotheses.

The mechanical model has explicit boundaries. Compliance is not constant across the entire pressure-volume curve, airway resistance can vary with lung volume and flow, and the airways can be dynamically compressed during forced expiration. When flow becomes effort-limited, an increase in expiratory muscle pressure does not behave like a simple increase in the pressure difference across a fixed resistance. Thus it would be inaccurate to derive an individual's FEV1 directly from a single RC value as though the lung were a rigid container connected to an unchanging tube. The model is valuable precisely because its predictions and limitations are both clear.

14. Scientific discovery and the design of respiratory measurements

The historical progression of lung-function testing shows why different experiments were developed rather than why one measurement replaced every other measurement. Hutchinson's published work in 1846 used measurable expired volume to investigate variation in respiratory capacity. The scientific question was whether a reproducible mechanical measurement could reveal systematic differences between individuals. A volume-displacement apparatus transformed a gas movement into a visible reading. Comparison with body dimensions helped establish that a meaningful interpretation requires an expected value rather than an absolute capacity applied to everyone.

The historical evidence must be read with its limitations. An association between vital capacity and height does not by itself establish the mechanisms by which height affects lung size, and observations from a nineteenth-century sample do not automatically provide representative reference values for modern populations. Instrumentation, sampling and the range of individuals studied all influence interpretation. The lasting contribution was the conversion of respiratory capacity into a quantitative, comparable observation, not the establishment of an immutable definition of normality.

The subsequent introduction of timed forced expiratory measurements addressed a different problem. A total movable volume can be relatively preserved even when gas cannot be expelled rapidly. The early work of Tiffeneau and Pinelli introduced a timed measure that became the basis of FEV1. Conceptually, the hypothesis was that the time course of emptying contains information not captured by a final volume alone. Comparing total volume with an early timed volume provides a way to test this claim. If two recordings approach the same final plateau but diverge markedly during the first second, a capacity-only test fails to distinguish them, whereas a timed test succeeds.

This argument does not establish a unique causal mechanism for every difference in early flow. It establishes that the dynamic measurement adds information. A person with a smaller total lung volume may expel a smaller absolute quantity during the first second while maintaining a relatively high fraction of their forced capacity. That is the reason for using both FEV1 and FVC and examining their relationship. Modern interpretive standards extend this approach by requiring reference-dependent classification and, when necessary, independent measurements of total lung capacity.

The classical Bohr approach illustrates a separate historical transition from mechanical gas movement to the efficiency of gas exchange. The question was whether the fraction of expired gas that had not effectively participated in carbon dioxide exchange could be inferred without physically separating every airway and alveolus. A mass balance across a mixed expired breath makes the inference possible under specified assumptions. Enghoff's later substitution increased practical accessibility by using arterial rather than representative alveolar carbon dioxide. In doing so, it changed the physiological meaning of the index when alveolar and arterial pressures differ. The two approaches demonstrate a general scientific principle: replacing a difficult-to-measure variable with a surrogate can improve practicality but can also change what is being measured.

The Fowler single-breath method used the changing composition of expired gas to estimate an effective conducting volume. To understand the experimental logic, consider why a tracer must create a contrast between the gas in the conducting pathway and the gas in exchanging regions. After a suitable single inspiration, the early expired gas has a different composition from gas that has mixed with resident alveolar gas. A rapid gas analyser tracks this transition while the expired volume is recorded. The early and later portions of the trace are not interchangeable. The geometrical method estimates the division between gas compartments while accounting for the transition rather than assigning an arbitrary threshold.

In the ideal equal-area construction, a vertical dividing line is placed within the transition such that the area of tracer concentration above the early-gas baseline before the line balances the missing concentration relative to the extrapolated alveolar portion after the line. The position of that line, measured along the expired-volume axis, estimates anatomical dead space. The construction does not assume that the lungs contain a literal sharp interface at one fixed anatomical location. Different pathway lengths and mixing smear the transition across a range of expired volumes. The resulting effective volume is influenced by the measurement conditions and cannot be equated without qualification to the geometrical volume obtained from a detailed anatomical reconstruction.

A useful control for a conceptual tracer experiment is an apparatus with a known conducting volume and a chamber containing a different tracer concentration. If the analyser is sufficiently responsive and the volume sensor correctly calibrated, the measured transition should respond predictably to changes in the known conducting volume. Delaying the analyser signal relative to the flow signal could artificially shift the transition along the volume axis. Adding a known tube volume should shift the early gas segment under conditions where the extra volume is rebreathed. Such controls reveal how an experimental signal depends on both the biological compartment and the measurement apparatus.

The combined history of respiratory measurement is thus a history of distinguishing questions. How much movable gas is available? How quickly can it be moved? How much gas remains after expiration? How much of each breath fails to participate effectively in exchange? These questions cannot all be answered by the same observation. The measurement methods in this chapter form a complementary toolkit, and the choice of method should follow the specific unknown to be resolved.

15. Reading integrated datasets and evaluating competing explanations

An examination problem may supply several measurements simultaneously, without naming the physiological impairment. The first task is to classify the available quantities. Some describe absolute volume, others describe a difference between endpoints, and others describe time-dependent flow or exchange efficiency. It is useful to calculate derived quantities before attempting a verbal diagnosis, while preserving a distinction between what the problem states and what is inferred.

Consider the following entirely hypothetical set of lung-function measurements. Subject P has TLC 5.6 L, RV 1.4 L, FVC 4.1 L and FEV1 3.4 L. Subject Q has TLC 6.4 L, RV 3.0 L, FVC 3.0 L and FEV1 1.5 L. Subject R has TLC 3.5 L, RV 1.1 L, FVC 2.3 L and FEV1 2.0 L. Suppose appropriate individual reference limits classify TLC as normal for P and Q but low for R, and classify FEV1/FVC as low only for Q. The ratios are approximately 0.83, 0.50 and 0.87 respectively. The slow VC inferred from TLC minus RV is 4.2, 3.4 and 2.4 L respectively. Q has reduced early fractional emptying and a larger residual compartment despite preserved TLC. R has a small total capacity and relatively preserved early fractional emptying. Both Q and R have smaller FVC than P, but their other measurements support different explanations.

The dataset also illustrates why every derived quantity should be interpreted in context. The residual fraction RV/TLC is 0.25 for P, approximately 0.47 for Q and approximately 0.31 for R. Q has a larger fraction of its total gas volume remaining at maximal expiration. However, a ratio can rise because the numerator rises, the denominator falls, or both. The same residual fraction in another subject would not necessarily indicate the same absolute gas volume. One must inspect numerator and denominator as well as the ratio. Actual reference limits for RV and RV/TLC would be needed before classifying them as abnormal in real subjects.

A second hypothetical experiment considers the effect of respiratory pattern on carbon dioxide elimination. Condition A consists of 0.5 L breaths at 12/min. Condition B consists of 0.3 L breaths at 20/min. Condition C consists of 0.6 L breaths at 10/min. If each breath has 0.15 L effective dead space, the minute ventilation is 6 L/min in all three conditions. Effective ventilation is respectively 4.2, 3.0 and 4.5 L/min. At a constant CO₂ production and steady state, the relative alveolar pressure in B compared with A is 4.2/3.0 = 1.4, and that in C compared with A is 4.2/4.5 ≈ 0.933. These predictions depend on an unchanged effective dead-space volume, which cannot be assumed in every living system.

If the actual measured arterial carbon dioxide pressures do not follow these ratios, there are multiple possible explanations. Carbon dioxide production may have changed, effective dead space may not be constant, the observations may have been made before steady state, or arterial pressure may not closely represent the alveolar quantity used in the derivation. The data do not justify discarding conservation of mass. Instead they direct attention to the assumptions connecting mass conservation to the simplified prediction. This is a common structure of analytical examination questions: a theory predicts a pattern under given conditions, and a changed observation requires identification of the failed assumption rather than invention of a new physiological law.

Another type of question compares two measurement methods after an intervention affecting airway calibre. If the dilution estimate of FRC rises while the plethysmographic estimate remains nearly unchanged, one hypothesis is that previously poorly communicating regions have become more accessible to the tracer without a major change in total thoracic gas volume. An alternative is a difference in technical quality between the measurements. Demonstrating repeatable equilibration curves and acceptable pressure signals, under matched posture and timing, would strengthen the physiological explanation. A change in agreement between methods is often more informative than the change in either value considered independently.

Graph interpretation adds a further dimension. Imagine that a volume-time trace rises very rapidly during the first 0.3 s but then becomes almost flat while the subject has not completed a full expiration. The initial peak flow may be high, but the observed FVC could be underestimated. Conversely, a slower initial rise followed by a sustained increase to a stable final plateau may reflect a genuine reduction in early emptying. The visual pattern must be considered together with the conditions of the manoeuvre. A test-quality problem and a physiological problem can generate superficially similar derived numbers. An appropriate experiment uses repeatability, trace inspection and independent measurements to distinguish them.

A final comparison brings the dead-space and spirometry measurements together. Consider a subject with a normal forced ratio and apparently normal total lung capacity, but an increased Enghoff index. These data provide no reason to reject the volume measurements: the forced ratio and TLC describe different aspects of respiratory function from the CO₂-based efficiency index. The increased index may reflect perfusion inequality or another source of alveolar–arterial separation. Conversely, a subject with an obstructive spirometric pattern need not have a particular dead-space fraction unless the gas-exchange data establish it. A complete physiological explanation must integrate measurements rather than treating one result as a proxy for every respiratory function.

When solving such problems, an effective sequence is to state the question, identify the measured variables and their units, derive only the quantities supported by those variables, compare with the supplied references, identify alternative explanations, and propose the independent observation that would distinguish them. The result is not merely an answer to a numerical exercise; it is a defensible chain of scientific inference. The next chapter develops this reasoning further by analysing how diffusion and ventilation–perfusion relationships connect the gas in the alveolar compartment with the gas carried by blood.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. RV equals FRC

FRC contains RV plus ERV; distinguish maximal from relaxed expiration. Both terms contain the word residual, which encourages a false equivalence. If an exam gives ERV and RV, add them for FRC; do not subtract one from the other.

2. A zero spirometer reading means an empty lung

The instrument records change relative to an arbitrary origin. A plotted origin resembles a physical zero. Compare two hypothetical lungs with different starting volumes but identical recorded excursions to see why a zero reading cannot reveal the absolute gas remaining.

3. A low FVC proves restriction

Only a TLC below its reference lower limit confirms restriction. Students may associate reduced exhaled volume with a small lung. In a dataset, calculate the ratio and inspect TLC and RV independently before classifying the pattern.

4. FEV1 is a flow

It is a volume integrated over one second, not litres per second. The name contains a time interval, which is easily mistaken for a rate. Check units: FEV1 has units L, while mean first-second flow has units L/s.

5. FEV1/FVC is FEV1/TLC

The denominator is the forced movable volume, excluding residual gas. Both TLC and FVC appear to describe the maximum capacity. Draw the upper and lower endpoints: FVC excludes gas remaining after forced expiration.

6. A fixed ratio of 0.70 defines normality for everyone

Reference limits depend on the expected physiological distribution. A convenient threshold is memorable, but it is not a universal reference. Use the LLN supplied in an examination dataset, and do not assume that equal ratios have equal reference status in different ages.

7. Helium dilution detects every trapped compartment

Only gas communicating with the circuit dilutes helium. Tracer equilibration sounds as though it includes the entire lung. Identify the route by which helium reaches each compartment; a closed pathway cannot dilute the circuit gas.

8. Plethysmography is always exact

Pressure equilibration, technique and calibration affect the estimate. A method that detects trapped gas is easily assumed to have no limitations. Examine whether mouth pressure is representative of thoracic pressure during the measurement before accepting a discrepancy.

9. Minute ventilation equals effective alveolar ventilation

Subtract dead-space ventilation before comparing effective gas supply. Both are expressed in L/min. In numerical questions identify VD and frequency, then subtract their product from total ventilation before comparing patterns.

10. Anatomical dead space is always 150 mL

That is an illustrative adult value; airway dimensions and apparatus change it. Textbooks often use one representative adult value in worked examples. Treat it as an assumption supplied by the problem, not a universal property; apparatus and lung volume may modify it.

11. Alveolar dead space is a shunt

Dead space is wasted ventilation; a shunt is perfusion without ventilation. Both impair exchange, leading to reversed definitions. Sketch two compartments and label airflow and blood flow; lack of perfusion and lack of ventilation are opposite limiting cases.

12. End-tidal CO₂ equals mixed expired CO₂

The mixed value includes early low-CO₂ conducting gas. Both measurements sample expiration, but at different scales. Use the entire volume-weighted sample for mixed expired pressure and the terminal sample only for end-tidal pressure.

13. Bohr and Enghoff are identical equations in heterogeneous lungs

Enghoff uses arterial pressure and incorporates broader exchange inefficiency. Both equations share the same algebraic form, so the substituted variable is easy to miss. Compare results using the separately supplied alveolar and arterial pressures rather than assuming they are equal.

14. Rapid shallow breathing is equivalent to deep breathing at equal minute ventilation

A fixed dead-space cost recurs on every breath. Multiplication of rate by depth gives the same total for some patterns, concealing the per-breath cost. Recalculate with the dead-space term for each pattern before drawing a conclusion.

15. Peak flow is a substitute for FEV1

Peak is instantaneous; FEV1 is the time integral of flow in the first second. Both are taken from a forced manoeuvre, but they represent different aspects of a trace. Use the peak for maximum instantaneous flow and the integral over one second for FEV1.

Original examination practice

1. Part B — Which primary volume remains after maximal expiration?

Answer and explanation

RV; ERV is the additional gas that can be expelled below quiet expiration, whereas RV remains even after maximal effort.

2. Part B — Which capacity equals VT + IRV?

Answer and explanation

IC, because its lower endpoint is quiet end-expiration and its upper endpoint is maximal inspiration.

3. Part B — Which capacity is inaccessible to simple spirometry: IC, VC or FRC?

Answer and explanation

FRC, because it contains RV, which never crosses the mouth during the manoeuvre.

4. Part B — What is the unit of FEV1?

Answer and explanation

Litres or millilitres. FEV1 is a volume, not a rate.

5. Part B — What is the denominator of the FEV1/FVC ratio?

Answer and explanation

Forced vital capacity, not total lung capacity.

6. Part B — Why does helium concentration fall during dilution?

Answer and explanation

The same tracer amount distributes into the larger communicating gas volume.

7. Part B — Which gas-space component lacks alveolar exchange surfaces?

Answer and explanation

Anatomical dead space, principally the conducting airways.

8. Part B — Which pressure is used in the classical Bohr numerator besides mixed expired pressure?

Answer and explanation

Representative alveolar PCO₂, not arterial PCO₂.

9. Part B — What establishes restrictive ventilatory impairment?

Answer and explanation

TLC below the appropriate LLN; a small FVC merely suggests possible restriction.

10. Part B — How is peak expiratory flow related to FEV1?

Answer and explanation

Peak flow is an instantaneous maximum; FEV1 is the integral of flow during the first second.

11. Part C — VT = 0.4 L, IRV = 2.1 L, ERV = 0.9 L and RV = 1.3 L. Find FRC and TLC.

Answer and explanation

FRC = 0.9 + 1.3 = 2.2 L. TLC = 0.4 + 2.1 + 0.9 + 1.3 = 4.7 L.

12. Part C — TLC remains 6 L while RV increases from 1.5 to 2.1 L. Predict the change in VC.

Answer and explanation

VC falls from 4.5 to 3.9 L, a decline of 0.6 L. The reduction does not imply reduced TLC.

13. Part C — Two patterns produce 6 L/min: 0.3 L × 20 and 0.6 L × 10. With VD 0.15 L, compare effective ventilation.

Answer and explanation

(0.3−0.15)×20 = 3.0 L/min versus (0.6−0.15)×10 = 4.5 L/min.

14. Part C — A 4 L helium circuit starts at 9% and equilibrates at 6%. What volume communicates?

Answer and explanation

4(0.09/0.06−1) = 2 L. It estimates communicating FRC if connected at relaxed end-expiration.

15. Part C — Dilution FRC is 2.5 L and plethysmographic FRC is 3.4 L. Propose a hypothesis and a control.

Answer and explanation

Poorly communicating gas may explain the difference; repeat with calibrated instruments, comparable posture and acceptable mixing and pressure manoeuvres.

16. Part C — FEV1 = 1.5 L and FVC = 3 L, ratio LLN = 0.68. TLC is not supplied. What can be concluded?

Answer and explanation

Ratio 0.50 is low, supporting obstruction. TLC is required to determine whether restriction is also present.

17. Part C — FEV1 = 2.4 L, FVC = 2.8 L and ratio LLN = 0.70. FVC is below its LLN. Is restriction established?

Answer and explanation

Ratio ≈ 0.857 is preserved. A low FVC suggests possible restriction, but TLC must be measured.

18. Part C — Alveolar PCO₂ is 40 mmHg, mixed expired 24 mmHg, VT 0.6 L. Calculate Bohr dead space.

Answer and explanation

Fraction (40−24)/40 = 0.40; VD = 0.24 L.

19. Part C — PACO₂ = 36, PaCO₂ = 45 and PECO₂ = 27 mmHg. Compare indices.

Answer and explanation

Bohr = 9/36 = 0.25; Enghoff = 18/45 = 0.40. The substitution incorporates additional gas-exchange inefficiency.

20. Part C — At constant metabolic CO₂ production and unchanged conditions, effective ventilation falls from 4.8 to 3.2 L/min. Predict relative steady alveolar PCO₂.

Answer and explanation

It rises by a factor 4.8/3.2 = 1.5 under the simplified inverse relationship.

21. Part C — An apparatus adds 80 mL dead space at 15 breaths/min without changing VT. Quantify effective ventilation change.

Answer and explanation

0.08 L × 15/min = 1.2 L/min decrease, assuming other dead-space components are constant.

22. Part C — An instrument loses 0.2 L through a leak during forced expiration. Why is the apparent FVC not reliable?

Answer and explanation

The sensor misses gas that bypasses it. A low measured value can arise from technique rather than lung mechanics.

23. Part B — Which volume is added to ERV to obtain FRC?

Answer and explanation

RV. These adjacent compartments extend from the conceptual zero reference to resting end-expiration.

24. Part B — What capacity equals TLC minus FRC?

Answer and explanation

IC. Both capacities share the same maximal inspiratory endpoint when combined.

25. Part B — What does the slope of a volume-time curve represent?

Answer and explanation

Flow, with units of volume per time. The sign depends on the coordinate convention.

26. Part B — Which gas law underlies body plethysmography?

Answer and explanation

Boyle's law, relating absolute pressure and volume for fixed gas amount and approximately constant temperature.

27. Part B — Which quantity is directly estimated when helium dilution begins at quiet end-expiration?

Answer and explanation

Communicating FRC. The connection endpoint defines the estimated compartment.

28. Part B — Which physiological property helps explain why an airway closes at low lung volume?

Answer and explanation

Reduced radial traction from surrounding lung tissue, combined with pressure conditions and airway mechanics.

29. Part B — What is meant by a reference LLN?

Answer and explanation

The lower boundary of an appropriate reference distribution, often the fifth percentile.

30. Part B — What is the unit of minute ventilation when VT is in L and frequency in breaths/min?

Answer and explanation

L/min. The breath unit cancels.

31. Part B — What is the distinction between a shunt and alveolar dead space?

Answer and explanation

A shunt is perfusion without ventilation in the limiting case; dead space is ventilation without effective perfusion.

32. Part B — Which gas is monitored in the Fowler single-breath method?

Answer and explanation

Nitrogen, whose changing expired concentration after oxygen inspiration helps estimate conducting-airway dead space.

33. Part C — FRC is 2.8 L and IC is 2.7 L. ERV is 0.8 L. Find RV, TLC and VC.

Answer and explanation

RV=2.0 L; TLC=5.5 L; VC=3.5 L. Each calculation follows the endpoint identities.

34. Part C — IC falls by 0.5 L while TLC and VT remain constant. What happens to FRC and IRV?

Answer and explanation

FRC rises 0.5 L; IRV falls 0.5 L because IC=VT+IRV.

35. Part C — A flow sensor has a constant positive bias of 0.03 L/s over 8 s. What is the integrated volume bias?

Answer and explanation

0.03×8=0.24 L. Integrating a constant offset accumulates systematic error.

36. Part C — A hypothetical helium circuit has volume 2.5 L, initial fraction 0.12 and final fraction 0.08. Estimate communicating lung volume.

Answer and explanation

2.5(0.12/0.08−1)=1.25 L. This assumes conserved tracer and consistent volume conditions.

37. Part C — Why might a short dilution test underestimate FRC in a heterogeneous lung?

Answer and explanation

Slowly ventilated units may not equilibrate before the test ends. A concentration-time trace and adequate mixing assessment help evaluate this.

38. Part C — FEV1 is 2.0 L, FVC is 3.8 L, TLC is below LLN, and ratio LLN is 0.67. Classify the pattern.

Answer and explanation

Ratio≈0.526, below LLN, and TLC low: mixed ventilatory impairment. This does not identify its disease cause.

39. Part C — Two people have FEV1 2.0 L; one has FVC 4.0 L, the other 2.4 L. Compare early fractional emptying.

Answer and explanation

The ratios are 0.50 and approximately 0.83. Equal FEV1 does not mean equal fractional emptying.

40. Part C — At a fixed minute ventilation of 9 L/min, VD=0.15 L and f=18/min. Calculate VT and effective ventilation.

Answer and explanation

VT=9/18=0.5 L; effective ventilation=9−2.7=6.3 L/min.

41. Part C — A breath contains 0.2 L of gas at 0 mmHg CO₂ and 0.3 L at 40 mmHg. Find mixed expired pressure and the Bohr fraction if alveolar pressure is 40 mmHg.

Answer and explanation

PE=24 mmHg; fraction=(40−24)/40=0.4. The volume equivalent is 0.2 L.

42. Part C — If PA=40, PE=31 and PI=10 mmHg in the two-compartment model with nonzero inspired CO₂, calculate VD/VT.

Answer and explanation

(40−31)/(40−10)=0.30. The classical zero-inspired formula is not applicable as stated.

43. Part C — At fixed VT=0.6 L, frequency 10/min and VD=0.15 L, effective ventilation is changed by increasing VD to 0.25 L. Compute both values.

Answer and explanation

Initial 4.5 L/min; final 3.5 L/min. Total minute ventilation remains 6 L/min.

44. Part C — A subject has a 3 L slow VC and a 2.5 L FVC after acceptable manoeuvres. What physiological explanation is consistent with these results?

Answer and explanation

Forced expiration may enhance dynamic airway compression or premature closure; other measurement limitations should still be considered.

45. Part C — Two groups have equal minute ventilation and dead-space fraction. Is arterial oxygen content necessarily equal?

Answer and explanation

No. Gas exchange depends on perfusion, diffusion and oxygen carriage, not ventilation alone.

46. Part C — A study estimates TLC from FRC and IC and then derives RV from TLC minus VC. Does adding RV to VC independently validate TLC?

Answer and explanation

No. It reproduces the original number by algebra; independent data are needed.

47. Part C — At a fixed CO₂ production, why might an immediate PaCO₂ measurement after a breathing-pattern change fail to fit the steady-state prediction?

Answer and explanation

Body CO₂ stores and circulation introduce transient dynamics; the simplified inverse relationship assumes steady state.

48. Part C — A subject performs 10 breaths of 0.4 L and 5 of 0.8 L during a minute with dead space 0.15 L per breath. Find effective ventilation.

Answer and explanation

Total 8 L/min minus 2.25 L/min dead-space ventilation = 5.75 L/min.

49. Part C — Two lung regions receive equal ventilation, but perfusion to one ceases. Predict the direction of effective dead space.

Answer and explanation

It increases because ventilation of the unperfused region no longer supports blood-gas exchange; total minute ventilation need not change.

50. Part C — A graph reports an end-tidal PCO₂ of 40 mmHg and mixed expired PCO₂ of 25 mmHg. Why should the latter be used in the Bohr numerator?

Answer and explanation

Mixed expired PCO₂ represents the volume-weighted entire expiration, including diluted early gas; end-tidal PCO₂ is a late sample.

51. SET — Which pair cannot be measured directly using simple spirometry? A: IC and VC; B: FRC and TLC; C: VT and IRV; D: ERV and IC.

Answer and explanation

B. Both FRC and TLC contain residual volume. A, C and D are differences between accessible endpoints and are measurable by appropriate manoeuvres.

52. SET — Assertion: a low FVC establishes restriction. Reason: FVC does not include RV. Select: A both true and reason explains; B both true but unrelated; C assertion true reason false; D assertion false reason true.

Answer and explanation

D. FVC can be low because of gas trapping, reduced TLC or technical factors. The reason is correct, since FVC measures movable gas, but restriction requires low TLC.

53. SET — Which statements are correct? I: FEV1 is a volume. II: peak flow is a volume. III: mixed expired PCO₂ is volume weighted. IV: Enghoff uses arterial PCO₂. A I and II; B I, III and IV; C II and III; D all four.

Answer and explanation

B. Peak flow is volume per time, not volume; I, III and IV correctly distinguish the measured quantities.

54. SET — In the passive emptying graph with τ = 0.5 s and 2 s, which unit retains more excess gas after 1 s? A fast; B slow; C equal; D cannot determine even with equal initial volume.

Answer and explanation

B. The slow unit retains e^(−1/2) ≈ 0.607 of its original excess volume; the fast one retains e^(−2) ≈ 0.135. A and C reverse or ignore the time constants; D ignores the supplied model.

55. SET — A and B both breathe 6 L/min. A breathes 0.5 L × 12; B 0.3 L × 20; dead space 0.15 L per breath. Which statement is correct? A B has higher effective ventilation; B A and B equal; C A has higher effective ventilation; D neither exchanges gas.

Answer and explanation

C. A delivers 4.2 L/min and B 3.0 L/min. A and B have identical total ventilation only; D is false because both tidal volumes exceed dead space.

56. SET — A tracer measurement underestimates FRC relative to plethysmography. Which conclusion is best? A proves low TLC; B proves technical error; C consistent with inaccessible gas, subject to measurement quality; D RV must be zero.

Answer and explanation

C. Dilution requires tracer communication, whereas plethysmography estimates compressible thoracic gas. A, B and D are not established by the stated observation.

Revision and mastery

Separate volume, capacity, flow and ventilation. Use endpoint identities to calculate capacities and use independent absolute-volume methods where RV is involved. Interpret forced manoeuvres with reference limits and measurement quality. Use carbon dioxide conservation to distinguish ventilation from effective ventilation.
