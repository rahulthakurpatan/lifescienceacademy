1. The biological problem: controlled destruction

A macrophage engulfs a bacterium; a liver cell removes a worn-out mitochondrion; another cell takes up cholesterol carried by a lipoprotein. All three must dismantle complex structures into smaller molecules, but their own proteins, membranes and nucleic acids must remain intact. The solution is not to avoid digestion. It is to perform digestion in a place whose boundary and chemical environment can be controlled. The lysosome is that place in most animal cells.

Recall from membrane biology that a phospholipid bilayer separates two aqueous spaces. Its hydrophobic interior restricts the free passage of charged molecules and large hydrophilic molecules. A soluble enzyme in an organelle lumen cannot normally wander into the cytosol, and a large cytoplasmic protein cannot simply diffuse into the lumen. Consequently a membrane creates both an opportunity and a problem: destructive reactions can be isolated, but substrates, enzymes and digestion products must be moved across or around the boundary in an organized way.

Hydrolysis is the cleavage of a chemical bond with water participating in the reaction. A peptide bond can be broken to produce shorter peptides and ultimately amino acids; glycosidic bonds can be cleaved to release sugars; nucleic acids can be fragmented and their components further processed. These reactions are catalysed by different enzymes because their substrates and bond chemistries differ. Digestion is therefore not an indiscriminate dissolution of all matter by acid. The low pH makes many enzymes effective; the enzymes accomplish the selective bond cleavage.

A cell must solve four connected problems. It must deliver the right enzymes without digesting the compartments in which they are made, establish a favorable lumenal environment, deliver material that is to be destroyed rather than recycled intact, and return useful small products to the rest of the cell. Failure at any one of these steps may produce an enlarged compartment filled with undigested material, even when the other three steps operate normally. This causal distinction will guide our interpretation of storage disorders and experiments.

Lysosomes are often described as the digestive system of the cell. This analogy is useful only if we remember that they are dynamic intracellular compartments, not a separate organ with a permanent cavity. They repeatedly exchange material with endosomes and autophagosomes, and their number, location and size vary. Plant and fungal cells perform related degradation in lytic vacuoles, a connection that will become important in the next chapter.

2. Discovery: from latent acid phosphatase to an organelle

The lysosome was recognized through a biochemical puzzle before its role became obvious in electron micrographs. In work on liver fractions in the early 1950s, Christian de Duve and colleagues noticed that acid phosphatase activity was unexpectedly low in freshly prepared particulate fractions, but became more accessible after treatments or storage that damaged particles. The enzyme was not necessarily absent from the fresh sample. Its activity was latent because the experimental substrate could not readily reach enzyme molecules trapped within intact membrane-bound structures. This line of investigation led to the identification of lysosomes in 1955.

Acid phosphatase catalyses the hydrolysis of phosphate ester bonds under acidic conditions. Its activity can be assayed by providing a suitable substrate and measuring product formation. Imagine two portions of a liver fraction, assayed at the same acidic pH and with the same amount of substrate. In one portion the membranes remain intact; in the other they are disrupted in a way that does not denature the enzyme. Greater activity in the second portion indicates that the membrane was restricting substrate access. If the first assay were at neutral pH and the second acidic, we could not separate the effects of enzyme accessibility from the effects of pH.

Cell fractionation supplies a second piece of evidence. Homogenization separates cell contents while a suitable medium preserves many organelles; successive centrifugation or a density gradient enriches different particles. If several different acid hydrolases show similar sedimentation behavior, their co-distribution suggests association with a common compartment. It is stronger evidence when activity does not parallel markers for mitochondria, ER or peroxisomes. Yet overlapping distributions alone do not establish that enzymes share a lumen: different organelles can have similar sedimentation properties. Electron microscopy and cytochemical localization of enzyme activity helped establish the structural identity of the particles.

The logic of enzyme latency is also a useful way to estimate membrane integrity. Suppose an intact fraction gives 18 units of acid phosphatase activity and a disrupted aliquot gives 90 units under identical assay conditions. Accessible activity is 18/90 = 0.20, or 20% of total. The latent fraction is (90 − 18)/90 = 0.80, or 80%. This is an activity-based estimate, not a direct count of intact lysosomes. Some enzyme may already be outside organelles, different particles may contain different quantities of enzyme, and detergent may change activity independently of disruption. A control using soluble enzyme plus detergent is therefore important.

The discovery teaches a general scientific lesson. A low measured reaction rate may reflect a barrier between enzyme and substrate rather than an enzyme deficiency. Separating accessibility, localization and catalytic ability is the same reasoning needed to diagnose the different ways that a modern lysosome may fail.

3. Structure and membrane organization

A lysosome is bounded by a single membrane: one lipid bilayer, not one layer of lipid molecules. Unlike a mitochondrion, it does not possess an outer membrane and an inner respiratory membrane. Unlike the nucleus, it has no nuclear pore complex. It also lacks its own genome and translation machinery. The enzymes and membrane proteins needed for its functions are encoded in nuclear DNA, synthesized on cellular ribosomes and delivered through intracellular trafficking pathways.

In electron microscopy, lysosomes frequently appear as rounded or irregular membrane-bound profiles with heterogeneous contents. Many are a fraction of a micrometre across, although size and appearance vary markedly with cell type and cargo. A compartment digesting membranes may contain concentric membrane remnants; one processing protein-rich material can have a dense granular appearance; a recently formed hybrid may contain relatively recognizable material. A thin section is only a slice through a three-dimensional structure. Shape, darkness and size alone cannot identify a lysosome reliably. Evidence of an acidic lumen and lysosomal protein markers strengthens the identification.

The lumen contains soluble hydrolases and partially digested cargo, while the limiting membrane contains distinct integral membrane proteins. These include V-type ATPases that transport protons, proteins that control the passage of ions and small organic solutes, fusion machinery, and heavily glycosylated lysosome-associated membrane proteins such as LAMP1 and LAMP2. Many carbohydrate chains of these proteins project into the lumen. Together with other membrane properties, this carbohydrate-rich surface helps protect the boundary against enzymes operating nearby. It is not an indestructible shield: lysosomal membrane components themselves are eventually turned over.

The membrane's orientation follows the rule learned in vesicular traffic. A protein domain facing the ER lumen remains on the noncytosolic side as membranes bud and fuse; at the lysosome it faces the lysosomal lumen. Cytosolic domains remain accessible to cytoplasmic sorting and fusion proteins. This explains how a membrane protein can possess a protective carbohydrate-rich lumenal domain and a short cytosolic region that carries sorting information. The carbohydrate chains and the cytosolic sorting signal need not contact one another to perform complementary functions.

In a typical nucleated animal cell, multiple lysosomes interact with an extensive endosomal system. Some move toward or away from the nucleus along cytoskeletal tracks; some undergo transient or more complete fusion and subsequent fission. A lysosome is therefore defined primarily by its biochemical identity and functional behavior, not by a fixed spherical shape. Its single membrane must be selective enough to contain acid and enzymes but dynamic enough to permit substrate delivery, solute export and membrane remodeling.

4. How the lysosome establishes and maintains acidity

The cytosol of a typical mammalian cell is approximately neutral, with a pH near 7.2, whereas a functioning lysosome commonly maintains a lumen near pH 4.5–5.0. These values are representative, not rigid definitions for every cell and every lysosome. pH describes the negative base-ten logarithm of hydrogen ion activity. A decrease of one pH unit corresponds to a tenfold increase in proton activity. Since a lysosome contains hundreds of times greater proton activity than the cytosol, passive diffusion of protons from cytosol into lumen cannot establish this difference on its own.

The V-type H⁺-ATPase solves the energetic problem. Its cytosol-facing V1 sector uses the energy of ATP hydrolysis; its membrane-embedded V0 sector couples this energy to proton movement into the lumen. The pump is called an ATPase because it consumes ATP rather than producing ATP during its usual lysosomal function. The membrane location is essential: an enzyme that simply hydrolysed ATP in the cytoplasm would not create a proton gradient unless coupled to directional transmembrane transport. V-type pumps also acidify other compartments, including endosomes and plant vacuoles; they are not exclusive lysosomal markers.

An energetic complication arises as positively charged protons enter the lumen. In the absence of compensating movement of other ions, the lumen becomes more positive relative to the cytosol. This electrical difference opposes further movement of protons inward. Only a small imbalance in ion numbers is needed to charge a biological membrane. Continued acidification is supported by counterion pathways, such as anion entry and/or cation exit, which reduce the electrical opposition. The lysosomal ClC-7 protein is a chloride/proton exchanger rather than a simple chloride channel; its contribution to ion composition and acidification cannot always be inferred from the behavior of an ideal passive chloride pore. Different ion pathways and cell types can show different effects when one transporter is disrupted.

The lysosomal pH at steady state is not an equilibrium with the cytosol. Proton entry through the pump is balanced by proton leakage, transport coupled to other substrates, buffering reactions and changing cargo. If ATP is depleted or the V-ATPase is inhibited, proton entry decreases, but existing acidity may persist temporarily because of buffering and restricted permeability. The pH subsequently tends to rise if outward proton loss continues. It is therefore incorrect to predict that ATP depletion instantly makes every lysosome exactly neutral.

Acidification is needed at more than one point in the delivery pathway. In endosomes, an acidic lumen helps some receptor–ligand complexes dissociate; in late endocytic compartments it permits activation and efficient action of many hydrolases. The degree and timing of acidification are consequently related to both sorting and digestion. A defect in the pump may produce a broad degradation phenotype even when every hydrolase gene is normal. This is one reason that low degradation activity in intact cells should not automatically be attributed to a mutation in the enzyme being assayed.

5. Quantitative reasoning: proton gradients, energy and buffering

The logarithmic nature of pH is particularly important for examination problems. Approximating activity by concentration in a simplified dilute solution, [H⁺] = 10−pH mol L−1. For a cytosol at pH 7.2 and a lysosomal lumen at pH 4.8, the ratio [H⁺]lumen/[H⁺]cytosol equals 10−4.8/10−7.2 = 102.4 ≈ 251. The lumen contains approximately 251 times the free proton concentration of the cytosol in this model. Subtracting the pH values gives a logarithmic difference, not the concentration ratio.

The work of moving one mole of protons from cytosol to lumen includes chemical and electrical terms. The chemical potential of a dilute solute increases by RT ln(Clumen/Ccytosol) on transport toward the more concentrated solution. The electrical contribution for a mole of monovalent positive ions is F(ψlumen − ψcytosol). Adding gives ΔG = 2.303 RT(pHcytosol − pHlumen) + FΔψ. R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1, T is absolute temperature, F = 96,485 C mol−1, and Δψ is defined as lumen minus cytosol in volts. This sign convention must be stated: a positive lumen makes proton transport inward more costly.

At 310 K, a pH difference of 2.4 requires approximately 2.303 × 8.314 × 310 × 2.4 = 14.25 kJ/mol H⁺ of chemical work. If the lumen is 20 mV more positive than cytosol, electrical work is 96,485 × 0.020 = 1.93 kJ/mol. Total uphill work is 16.18 kJ/mol H⁺. If an illustrative pump transports three protons per ATP, the minimum work per mole of ATP cycles under those assumed conditions is 48.5 kJ. A cellular ATP hydrolysis free energy of −55 kJ/mol would make coupling thermodynamically possible under ideal conditions. The assumed stoichiometry and voltage are teaching values, not universal lysosomal constants. Real rates and coupling efficiency also depend on the enzyme and the cell.

A second useful distinction is free proton concentration versus the total amount of acid needed to change pH. A spherical lysosome of radius 0.25 μm has volume (4/3)πr³ ≈ 0.06545 μm³, or 6.545 × 10−17 L, because 1 μm³ = 10−15 L. At pH 4.8 its free proton concentration, in the dilute approximation, is 1.585 × 10−5 M. Multiplying by volume and Avogadro's constant gives an average of roughly 625 free protons. This surprisingly small estimate does not imply that pumping a few hundred protons into an initially neutral organelle is sufficient in reality. Proteins, weak acids and other buffer groups bind and release protons; proton exchange and cargo turnover also continue.

Suppose a lysosome has an effective buffer capacity of 20 mmol L−1 per pH unit, taken constant over a small interval. To lower pH by 0.5 requires approximately 10 mmol/L of net proton equivalents, not merely the change in the free proton concentration. For a volume of 6.545 × 10−17 L, that is about 6.545 × 10−19 mol, or 3.94 × 105 proton equivalents. The buffer model is approximate and assumes unchanged volume and capacity, but it explains how many more protons may need to be transported than are free at any instant.

The combination of a logarithmic concentration scale, membrane voltage, buffering and a continuously working pump explains why pH experiments must measure the organelle's internal environment rather than infer it from the nominal number of pump molecules.

6. Hydrolases: the chemical machinery of intracellular digestion

The lysosome contains a diverse collection of acid hydrolases. Each recognizes particular structures and catalyses a particular type of cleavage. Proteases such as cathepsins attack proteins; nucleases attack phosphodiester linkages in nucleic acids; glycosidases act on carbohydrate chains; lipases cleave suitable lipid ester bonds; phosphatases and sulfatases remove phosphate or sulfate groups from specific substrates. More than fifty types of degradative enzymes have been characterized in mammalian lysosomal systems, but their precise complement and abundance depend on cell type.

Why does the cell need so many enzymes? A complex glycolipid, for example, may contain a hydrophobic backbone and a carbohydrate head group with several different sugars. Cleaving one bond may produce an intermediate that is the substrate of a second enzyme. An enzyme that acts efficiently on a terminal sugar may be unable to act until a more external sugar is removed. A failure at one step can therefore interrupt a sequence even if the other hydrolases are fully functional. The chemistry of the accumulated intermediate may reveal where the block occurs. Some lipid-degrading enzymes also require accessory proteins that help them access membrane-associated substrates.

Enzymes are not equally active at all pH values. The protonation of amino acid side chains influences charge, binding and catalysis; it can also change the substrate's chemical form or the structure of the enzyme. Consider a catalytic group that must be protonated and another that must be deprotonated. At very low pH both may be protonated; at very high pH both may be deprotonated. Only an intermediate range gives both the required chemical states in a substantial fraction of molecules. This is one general explanation for a pH-dependent activity maximum, although the detailed profile depends on the particular enzyme. It is not sufficient to assume that every lysosomal enzyme has an optimum at exactly pH 5.

As an illustrative calculation, a purified hydrolase releases 80 nmol of product per minute at pH 4.8 and 16 nmol per minute at pH 6.2, with substrate concentration, temperature and enzyme amount held constant. The measured rate at pH 6.2 is 20% of its value at pH 4.8, a fivefold reduction. This experiment supports pH dependence but does not establish which ionizable group causes it, and it does not give the full shape of the activity profile. If the intact cell has 20% activity, the similar numerical ratio alone does not prove that its lysosomes have reached pH 6.2; enzyme amount, substrate access and other conditions could differ.

Many lysosomal proteases are synthesized as inactive or less active precursors that are processed into more active forms after reaching suitable compartments. Precursor processing and acid-dependent catalysis provide additional control, but they are distinct events. A mature enzyme may still require acidic conditions for maximal activity, whereas a precursor might not become fully active merely because the environment becomes acidic. The specificity of lysosomal chemistry and the regulated delivery of enzymes together prevent most premature digestion along the secretory pathway.

7. Why lysosomes do not ordinarily digest the cell itself

A functioning lysosome maintains several levels of protection. The first is spatial separation: the limiting membrane keeps soluble lumenal enzymes away from most cytoplasmic macromolecules. The second is chemical specialization: many acid hydrolases operate far less efficiently at cytosolic pH than at lumenal pH. The third is a coordinated system of enzyme maturation and membrane trafficking. These factors reinforce one another but are not individually absolute safeguards.

The fact that acid hydrolases can be less active at neutral pH is sometimes simplified into the claim that all lysosomal enzymes are completely inactive outside the lysosome. That is false. Different enzymes have different activity profiles, and some cathepsins retain meaningful activity after release. Lysosomal membrane permeabilization can injure cells and contribute to cell-death pathways. The outcome depends on how much enzyme is released, how long it remains active, the condition of the cell and the ability to repair or remove damaged organelles. The historical nickname “suicide bag” therefore emphasizes a possible pathological consequence rather than the principal normal function.

A separate question is why the membrane itself survives despite being in contact with lipases and proteases. Membrane proteins may have extensive lumenal glycosylation, and the lipid composition and arrangement of the membrane influence enzyme access. Furthermore, membrane-associated substrates delivered for degradation are not necessarily presented in the same physical form as the limiting membrane. Intralumenal membrane fragments can be made accessible to degradative machinery while the outer boundary remains relatively protected. Continued delivery of membrane proteins and membrane turnover also help preserve compartment function.

Consider a lysosome with intact membrane but defective glycosidase. The lysosomal pH can be normal, and several other classes of enzyme can be effective, yet one substrate may accumulate. By contrast, a damaged limiting membrane can dissipate proton gradients and release many enzymes. Both defects could reduce digestion, but only the second directly removes the spatial protection of the cytosol. An experiment that measures total hydrolase activity after disruption cannot by itself distinguish these outcomes; localization or membrane integrity must also be measured.

This distinction is useful when reasoning about normal autophagy. The cell encloses selected parts of its cytoplasm in a membrane carrier and delivers that material to lysosomes. It is not equivalent to rupturing lysosomes and allowing enzymes to attack their surroundings. Controlled recycling generally supports cell survival, while extensive unregulated lysosomal damage may promote cell death. The regulatory decisions linking degradation and cell death are developed in Unit 2.D.

8. From ER and Golgi to lysosome: the enzyme delivery problem

A lysosomal hydrolase is generally synthesized on a ribosome associated with the rough endoplasmic reticulum after a signal sequence directs the translating protein into the ER. It enters the lumen, where it folds and may acquire an N-linked oligosaccharide. From the ER, it proceeds through the Golgi apparatus. At this point the cell faces a sorting problem: many secretory proteins are also in the Golgi lumen, yet the hydrolase should normally remain within the endolysosomal system instead of being released outside the cell.

In many mammalian soluble lysosomal enzymes, an address is created on selected mannose residues of N-linked carbohydrate chains. In an early Golgi compartment, GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase recognizes structural determinants of the folded hydrolase and transfers a phospho-N-acetylglucosamine group to suitable mannose residues. A second enzyme removes the covering N-acetylglucosamine, exposing mannose-6-phosphate (M6P). This is a modified sugar residue, not a phosphate added directly to an amino acid. The fact that the folded protein contributes to recognition helps explain why other glycoproteins bearing mannose are not indiscriminately labelled for lysosomal delivery.

At the trans-Golgi network, transmembrane M6P receptors recognize the tagged enzymes on their lumenal side. Their cytoplasmic regions interact with sorting machinery; clathrin-associated transport carriers commonly deliver the receptor-bound cargo to endosomes. The receptor spans the membrane because it must connect the cargo in the lumen with packaging machinery on the cytosolic face. The basic coat and fusion mechanisms were introduced in 2.A.10; the important issue here is how a soluble hydrolytic enzyme acquires an organelle-specific destination.

In the more acidic endosome the receptor releases much of its cargo. The receptor is retrieved for repeated use, usually back toward the Golgi network; some receptor populations also cycle through the plasma membrane. The released enzyme remains in the degradative route and is delivered to late endocytic compartments, where further maturation and processing may occur. The lysosome does not manufacture the hydrolase, and it is misleading to picture a mature lysosome simply budding as a complete digestive organelle directly from a Golgi stack.

The M6P pathway is prominent but not universal. Some lysosomal soluble proteins use M6P-independent receptors; glucocerebrosidase, for example, can be delivered through LIMP-2. Many integral lysosomal membrane proteins use cytosolic amino-acid sorting motifs rather than the lumenal M6P recognition route. Therefore a defect in the M6P system disproportionately misroutes many soluble hydrolases without necessarily removing every lysosomal protein or destroying the organelle membrane itself.

The predictions of this mechanism can be tested. If an enzyme is made normally but not given the correct carbohydrate address, one may find excessive activity in the extracellular medium and reduced activity within lysosomes. If receptor recycling is blocked, initial delivery may continue from existing receptor pools but decline during repeated rounds of cargo sorting. If the endosomal pH is changed, dissociation and recycling can be disturbed without changing the initial glycan tag. The same final degradation defect can thus be traced to different positions in the delivery pathway.

9. Endosomal maturation and the dynamic lysosome

The Golgi pathway supplies the enzymes, but material for digestion also enters from other sources. An early endosome is a sorting compartment that receives much newly internalized material from the plasma membrane. Many surface receptors and membrane lipids must be returned to the cell surface instead of destroyed. Other cargo must be delivered to the degradative system. Sorting is therefore a decision about the fate of a molecule, not merely a change in the position of a vesicle.

Early endosomes are usually less acidic than mature lysosomes. As endosomal compartments mature, their membrane composition, associated proteins, transport properties and internal contents change. A shift from Rab5-associated early endosomal identity toward Rab7-associated late endosomal identity is a useful standard example. This transition reflects a coordinated remodeling of the organelle. The early endosome is not merely a small lysosome that has not yet grown large; it possesses functions and sorting pathways that are not identical to those of the terminal degradative system.

A particular topological problem arises for membrane proteins destined for degradation. The cytoplasmic domain of a receptor in the limiting endosome membrane faces the cytosol, where soluble lumenal hydrolases cannot reach it. During inward budding of portions of endosomal membrane, selected proteins become incorporated into intralumenal vesicles. The former cytosolic domains now face the interior of these small vesicles. When the vesicle membrane is subsequently degraded, the domains become accessible to lumenal enzymes. An endosome rich in intralumenal vesicles is called a multivesicular body. The sorting machinery helps separate proteins destined for destruction from those that should be returned to the cell surface.

Late endosomes deliver cargo to lysosomes by interactions and fusion events, often forming hybrid endolysosomal compartments in which substantial degradation occurs. As digestion proceeds, lysosomal identity can be re-established through membrane retrieval and fission. The precise labels used for individual intermediates vary among textbooks and experimental definitions. The most robust understanding is functional: an interconnected set of compartments performs sorting, acidification, digestion and membrane recycling.

Older descriptions distinguish primary lysosomes, containing enzymes but not yet substantial digestive cargo, from secondary lysosomes formed when digestive compartments encounter cargo. A heterolysosome processes external material and an autolysosome processes autophagic material. A residual body may retain incompletely degraded substances. These terms remain useful in conventional examination questions, but the modern maturation-and-fusion model is more accurate than a rigid sequence in which every lysosome has an identical lifetime. A mature lysosome can participate in repeated encounters with incoming material.

The fate of a receptor illustrates the biological importance. A receptor required for continual nutrient uptake may repeatedly cycle between surface and endosome. A receptor whose signaling must cease may instead enter intralumenal vesicles and be degraded. Blocking lysosomal digestion might preserve the second receptor without restoring it to the cell surface: retention of protein and functional recycling are not equivalent. Understanding that distinction prepares us to follow the LDL pathway and later signaling examples.

10. Heterophagy: digestion of material acquired from outside

Heterophagy refers to lysosomal degradation of material originating outside the cell. It can follow phagocytosis of large particles or other forms of endocytosis of fluid, macromolecules and receptor-bound ligands. Not all extracellular uptake ends in degradation: some molecules are transferred across a cell, some receptors recycle, and some ligands remain intact or escape the degradative route. The term heterophagy describes the destination of material that is actually directed to intracellular digestion.

Phagocytosis is particularly important in macrophages and other professional phagocytes. After recognition of a suitable particle, the plasma membrane and actin cytoskeleton cooperate to surround it. Closure produces an intracellular phagosome with the particle inside. The phagosome is initially separated from the cytosol and does not immediately contain the full collection of mature lysosomal enzymes. During its maturation it interacts with early and late endocytic compartments, becomes acidified and acquires digestive components. Fusion or content exchange with lysosomes generates a phagolysosome in which much particle breakdown takes place.

The macrophage illustrates why both delivery and enzyme activity matter. Imagine that a bacterium is engulfed normally, but phagosome maturation fails. It may remain within a membrane-bound vacuole that lacks adequate hydrolytic enzymes, acidity or other antimicrobial activities. Counting intracellular bacteria immediately after uptake would show successful phagocytosis, yet their subsequent clearance could be impaired. Some microorganisms exploit aspects of this distinction by interfering with maturation or tolerating the environment. The lysosome is therefore an important contributor to cellular defense, but successful uptake does not guarantee successful destruction.

Fluid-phase endocytosis can deliver soluble extracellular macromolecules to the same system. Receptor-mediated endocytosis provides a more selective example. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) binds an LDL receptor at the plasma membrane. After internalization, acidic endosomal conditions favor dissociation of LDL from its receptor. Receptor molecules usually return toward the surface, while LDL travels along the degradative pathway. In lysosomal compartments, its proteins are degraded and lysosomal acid lipase hydrolyses cholesteryl esters, releasing free cholesterol and fatty acids. Cholesterol must then leave the compartment for use in membranes and regulation of cellular cholesterol balance.

If an LDL receptor cannot bind LDL, uptake is defective and little cargo reaches the lysosome. If lysosomal acid lipase is missing, uptake can be normal but ester hydrolysis is defective. If cholesterol export from the endolysosomal system is impaired, hydrolysis may occur but cholesterol can accumulate inside. These are three different defects along the same pathway. A single observation of reduced cholesterol availability outside the lysosome cannot locate the block; tracking both precursor and product can.

Digestion of extracellular proteins also supplies peptides for certain antigen-presentation processes in immune cells. The key connection is that endosomal and lysosomal proteolysis can produce useful fragments instead of simply eliminating proteins. The molecular details of antigen loading and immune recognition belong to Unit 4. Here the important principle is that lysosomal processing has biological consequences beyond waste disposal.

11. Autophagic delivery: digestion of intracellular material

The lysosome must also process parts of the cell's own cytoplasm. Proteins may become aggregated, organelles may lose function, and nutrient scarcity may require the reuse of existing macromolecules. Large cytoplasmic structures cannot be threaded through a simple membrane solute transporter. Autophagic pathways solve the problem by delivering cytoplasmic material to the lysosomal lumen in a controlled fashion.

Macroautophagy begins with the development of a cup-shaped isolation membrane, called a phagophore, around cytoplasmic cargo. The membrane expands and closes to form a double-membrane autophagosome. The cytoplasmic material lies inside the inner membrane. The outer autophagosomal membrane subsequently fuses with a lysosome or late endocytic compartment; the inner membrane and enclosed cargo enter a degradative lumen. Their breakdown releases small molecules that may be reused. The double membrane of the autophagosome should never be confused with the single limiting membrane of the lysosome. After fusion, the inner autophagosomal membrane is cargo for digestion.

Autophagy can include relatively nonselective uptake of cytoplasm or selective delivery of particular structures. Mitophagy is selective autophagic removal of mitochondria, and is relevant because a mitochondrion with impaired function may be more harmful than useful. Other selective pathways help remove aggregates or damaged cellular components. These examples demonstrate cargo specificity without requiring the complete signaling and regulatory networks, which are covered in Unit 2.D. Autophagy is generally a normal maintenance and adaptive process, not a synonym for apoptosis.

Two other routes are often distinguished from macroautophagy. In microautophagy, material is incorporated by deformation or invagination of a lysosomal or related degradative membrane. Chaperone-mediated autophagy in mammalian cells can deliver certain soluble proteins to the lysosomal membrane, where recognition involving chaperones and LAMP2A allows translocation of unfolded substrate proteins. The latter route does not deliver a whole mitochondrion through the membrane and does not require a double-membrane autophagosome for that cargo. The three routes have the same broad endpoint but different physical delivery mechanisms.

Lysosomal function is essential to completing autophagy. A cell may form many autophagosomes without successfully digesting their contents. If formation increases and degradation also increases, the number visible at one instant might remain unchanged. Conversely, if fusion or degradation stops, carriers accumulate even if their formation rate has not changed. The quantity of autophagosomes at a snapshot is therefore not equivalent to the rate of autophagic recycling. This distinction becomes especially important when interpreting experiments using fluorescent markers or inhibitors.

In a nutrient-limited cell, successful recycling can provide amino acids and other precursors for vital metabolic reactions and protein synthesis. If lysosomal hydrolysis or product export fails, the cell may possess considerable internal material yet have difficulty accessing its reusable constituents. Autophagy can supply material to lysosomes, but it cannot overcome every defect in the downstream degradation or export step. This is why it is useful to consider the lysosome as part of a pathway rather than as an independent waste container.

12. From digestion products to cellular resources

Hydrolysis is not the last step in lysosomal function. A useful digestion product is of little benefit to the rest of the cell if it remains trapped in the lysosomal lumen. The same membrane that originally protected the cell from enzymes now forms a barrier to many of the products of those enzymes. A molecule such as an amino acid is small compared with a protein, but at physiological pH it carries ionizable groups and cannot necessarily diffuse freely through a phospholipid bilayer. Selective transport pathways are required.

Amino acids obtained by protein digestion can return to the cytosol and enter the general amino acid pool. Sugars released from degradable carbohydrate structures can be used by metabolic pathways or as biosynthetic precursors. Nucleic acid breakdown yields smaller derivatives that can be salvaged or metabolized further. Free cholesterol obtained from lipoprotein digestion is moved out of the endolysosomal compartment by a coordinated export process involving NPC2 and NPC1. Not every product follows an identical mechanism, and it is incorrect to picture all small products crossing the membrane freely by simple diffusion.

The process can be understood as a material balance. Let S be the quantity of a digestible polymer in the lumen. If I is the rate of substrate delivery and D is its rate of digestion, then dS/dt = I − D, when other removal routes are negligible. Let P represent the corresponding product, with an assumed one-to-one conversion for simplicity. If E is the rate of product export, dP/dt = D − E. At steady state both pools remain constant, so I = D = E. This does not mean that there is no traffic. Substrate can enter and product leave continuously at equal rates.

Suppose a compartment receives 12 arbitrary substrate equivalents per hour. A defect reduces digestive capacity to 7 equivalents per hour and export can handle 20 equivalents per hour. The substrate accumulates at 5 equivalents per hour while those rates remain constant; the capacity to export product is not limiting. In another cell digestion can handle all 12 equivalents, but export is limited to 8. Product now accumulates at 4 equivalents per hour. The two situations may both produce storage, but the chemical identity of the stored material differs.

Real lysosomes are not perfectly stirred reactors with fixed rates. Digestion rate often depends on substrate availability and enzyme kinetics; export can saturate; accumulating substances may alter membrane properties and further impair the organelle. If a simple first-order degradation model D = kS is reasonable over a limited range, steady-state substrate is I/k. A twofold decrease in k doubles this steady-state amount if input remains constant. When input exceeds the maximum capacity of a saturable enzyme system, no finite steady state is predicted by that simplified balance. These models are useful precisely because they identify assumptions; they are not meant to replace the complexity of real organelles.

Recycling helps couple degradation to cellular needs. Lysosomal surfaces also participate in nutrient sensing, and returned amino acids support metabolism and biosynthesis. Detailed regulatory signaling is outside the present chapter; the main lesson is that cellular digestion, solute transport and metabolic reuse are connected stages of one system.

13. Residual material, specialized lysosomes and cellular differences

Digestion is rarely equivalent to complete disappearance of all cargo. Some substances resist lysosomal enzymes, and some chemically modified cellular constituents cannot be efficiently broken down. Residual bodies may retain such material after much of the accessible substrate has been removed. In long-lived cells, heterogeneous autofluorescent material called lipofuscin can accumulate in lysosomal compartments as a consequence of incomplete degradation of cellular components. Its presence does not necessarily mean that every hydrolase is inactive; some material may be intrinsically difficult to degrade.

Residual material can have different fates in different cells. Some is retained for extended periods, some may be removed by regulated membrane traffic, and some cells can release lysosomal contents through lysosomal exocytosis. Exocytosis requires fusion of the limiting membrane with the plasma membrane and is distinct from leakage through a damaged membrane into the cytosol. One physiological context is membrane repair, in which calcium-dependent recruitment and fusion of lysosome-related compartments can contribute to restoring the surface. Secreted enzymes encounter a different chemical environment from that inside lysosomes, so their extracellular activity need not equal their lumenal activity.

Cell types differ according to the work they perform. A macrophage continually encounters particles and requires a powerful degradative endolysosomal system. Long-lived neurons require ongoing disposal of damaged proteins and organelles because dilution by cell division is limited. Hepatocytes process diverse incoming macromolecules and cellular components. A mature mammalian erythrocyte, by contrast, has lost its nucleus and most organelles, including lysosomes, during differentiation. Therefore the statement that every animal cell contains numerous lysosomes has an important exception.

Some specialized cells possess lysosome-related organelles whose components and acidic environment overlap with the ordinary lysosomal system but whose primary function is specialized. Cytotoxic lymphocytes have secretory granules containing molecules released toward target cells, and melanosomes in pigment-producing cells are specialized compartments in a related biogenetic family. The examples demonstrate how an endolysosomal pathway can be modified to support other cellular functions. They do not justify calling all secretory vesicles lysosomes, or all acidic compartments degradative organelles.

The abundance of lysosomes in a cell should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Increased lysosomal volume or enzyme content can reflect a greater physiological demand, an adaptation to stress, or accumulation caused by defective degradation. An image alone cannot distinguish increased useful capacity from a backlog of undigested material. A functional readout and an appropriate comparison are required.

14. Storage disorders: testing the mechanism with biological failures

Lysosomal storage disorders demonstrate the consequences of interrupting a particular step in the digestion-and-recycling system. Material is constantly delivered to lysosomes. If the enzyme needed to cleave a particular linkage is defective, its substrate or an intermediate can accumulate even though other lysosomal reactions continue. Accumulation can enlarge organelles and interfere with normal cellular function; the most affected tissues often reflect where the substrate is particularly abundant, where degradation demand is high, or where cells are unusually sensitive to the resulting disturbance. The following examples illustrate molecular principles rather than clinical diagnosis or treatment.

In Tay–Sachs disease, deficiency of the alpha subunit of beta-hexosaminidase A impairs degradation of GM2 ganglioside. GM2 is a glycolipid particularly relevant to neural membranes. Its breakdown requires a specific enzyme and an activator protein that helps present the membrane-associated substrate. A defect at that step cannot be corrected simply by increasing the activity of an unrelated lysosomal protease. The example illustrates substrate specificity and the importance of accessory proteins for lipid degradation.

In Gaucher disease, deficiency of lysosomal acid beta-glucosidase, also called glucocerebrosidase, impairs the breakdown of glucosylceramide. Macrophages involved in turnover of cellular membranes can accumulate lipid-rich material. The enzyme and substrate differ from Tay–Sachs disease even though both are disorders of sphingolipid degradation. A comparison of accumulated substrates, rather than the general label “lipid storage,” is essential in an examination problem.

Pompe disease is caused by deficiency of lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase, which normally degrades glycogen delivered to lysosomes. This is distinct from the cytosolic pathways of glycogen metabolism that utilize glycogen phosphorylase and debranching enzyme. Both systems act on glycogen, but they are in different compartments and employ different chemistry. Normal cytosolic glycogen metabolism therefore does not guarantee normal lysosomal glycogen degradation. Muscle, including cardiac muscle, can be especially affected.

In acid sphingomyelinase deficiency, historically called Niemann–Pick disease types A and B, the breakdown of sphingomyelin is impaired. Niemann–Pick disease type C is mechanistically different: it results from defective NPC1 or NPC2-dependent lipid trafficking, leading to accumulation of unesterified cholesterol and other lipids in late endosomes and lysosomes. In type C, hydrolysis of a cholesterol ester can occur but redistribution of the released cholesterol is impaired. Similar historical disease names must not be mistaken for identical molecular defects.

Hurler syndrome, one form of mucopolysaccharidosis type I, results from deficiency of alpha-L-iduronidase, affecting degradation of glycosaminoglycans such as dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate. Glycosaminoglycans consist of repeating sugar units with particular modifications. Their complete degradation requires a sequence of hydrolase and sulfatase reactions; a missing activity can leave partially degraded material even if upstream cleavage proceeds.

I-cell disease (mucolipidosis II) is an especially informative contrast. A defect in the GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase system prevents appropriate mannose-6-phosphate tagging of many soluble hydrolases. Instead of accumulating in their normal lysosomal destination, many are secreted and can show elevated extracellular activities, while intracellular lysosomal activities are reduced. Multiple substrates may therefore accumulate together. The observation that enzymes are present but mislocalized distinguishes a targeting disorder from a deficiency of only one catalytic activity. Exceptions to M6P targeting mean that not all lysosomal proteins are lost.

An examination question can therefore distinguish four levels of failure. A specific enzyme deficiency affects a defined chemical transformation; a targeting defect mislocalizes multiple enzymes; a pH-maintenance defect changes the activity of many otherwise normal acid-dependent reactions; and a membrane export defect traps certain products after hydrolysis. The appropriate experiment must determine which step has failed. These disorders make the lysosomal pathway a natural example of how cellular location and biochemical function are inseparable.

15. Interpreting experiments on lysosomal function

A strong experimental answer must specify which part of the pathway was measured. Consider immunofluorescence for LAMP1. A punctate signal indicates compartments rich in this membrane protein, often late endosomes and lysosomes. It does not demonstrate that their lumen is acidic, that their enzymes are active or that material is successfully leaving them. A marker identifies a property of the compartment; a functional assay is needed to establish digestion. Combining LAMP localization with a pH indicator and a degradation reporter can separate these questions.

Acidotropic fluorescent dyes accumulate in acidic compartments. They are useful for showing where acidified structures occur, but fluorescence intensity also depends on dye concentration, compartment volume, uptake and optical conditions. A larger or more numerous set of acidic compartments can yield a stronger signal without each lumen being more acidic. A calibrated ratiometric pH reporter uses two signal measurements, or a pH-sensitive and a reference signal, to reduce the dependence on probe amount. Its response must be calibrated for the relevant pH range before quantitative interpretation.

A pulse–chase experiment can distinguish uptake from degradation. In a pulse, cells encounter a labelled substrate for a defined interval; unbound extracellular substrate is then removed and the chase follows the labelled material already internalized. If two groups take up equal amounts but one retains intact substrate much longer, the difference is downstream of initial uptake, provided the labels and measurement distinguish intact substrate from fragments. Additional localization can test whether the substrate reaches the lysosomal system. A failure of delivery would be expected to leave intact material outside the appropriate degradative compartment, whereas a hydrolase defect might leave intact material within it.

For a hypothetical simplified experiment, wild-type cells internalize 100 units of labelled substrate; after 60 minutes 20 intact units remain. Mutant cells also internalize 100 units but retain 80 intact units. Thus wild type clears 80% and mutant clears 20% of the original intact material over the same interval. This observation alone establishes reduced disappearance of intact substrate, not necessarily reduced catalytic activity. Suppose the mutant substrate colocalizes with LAMP1, its lumenal pH is normal, and a purified fraction has a selective deficiency of the hydrolase for that substrate. The combined observations strongly support a digestive enzyme defect. If instead multiple hydrolases are normal when assayed at optimal pH but the mutant lumen measures pH 6.3, impaired acidification becomes a more plausible explanation.

Bafilomycin A1 inhibits V-type ATPases and is commonly used to interfere with lysosomal acidification in cell biological experiments. However, a perturbation of lysosomal pH can also affect cargo trafficking, enzyme maturation and autophagic flux. A substrate accumulating after drug treatment cannot by itself establish direct inhibition of a particular hydrolase. Likewise, weak bases can accumulate in acidic compartments and alter their internal environment, but their effects need not be confined to one target or one organelle. Proper comparisons include untreated cells, relevant enzyme assays and measurements of cargo delivery.

A final control concerns autophagic flux. If the number of autophagosomes rises after an intervention, it is tempting to conclude that autophagy has been activated. However, number reflects both formation and clearance. Comparing marker turnover or cargo degradation in the presence and absence of a downstream lysosomal block helps discriminate these possibilities. The conclusion should describe the measured step and state remaining alternatives. This is the practical difference between a descriptive Part B answer and an analytical Part C answer.

16. Distinguishing lysosomes from neighboring organelles

The Golgi apparatus and lysosome are connected by transport but specialize in different tasks. The Golgi consists of polarized stacks of flattened cisternae and associated networks that modify and distribute proteins and lipids. The lysosome is a single-membrane degradative compartment supplied with enzymes and substrates by trafficking. Many Golgi enzymes participate in biosynthesis and modification of carbohydrate chains; lysosomal hydrolases generally dismantle macromolecules. Both can maintain pH values below the cytosol, so acidity alone does not identify a lysosome.

Plant central vacuoles and fungal vacuoles offer closer functional analogies. A lytic vacuole contains hydrolases and can perform degradation related to lysosomal function. A central plant vacuole also has major roles in ion storage, water balance and turgor, and can occupy much of the cell's volume. The tonoplast is the vacuolar limiting membrane. In contrast to the common animal-cell arrangement of many smaller degradative compartments, plant vacuoles can combine degradative and storage functions on a very large scale, depending on tissue and developmental stage. Not every plant vacuole has exactly the same functions.

Peroxisomes are also single-membrane compartments, but their characteristic chemistry is oxidation rather than acid hydrolysis. Oxidases can produce hydrogen peroxide, and catalase converts peroxide into water and oxygen or participates in peroxide-dependent reactions. Peroxisomes import many proteins directly from the cytosol through targeting systems distinct from the conventional lysosomal M6P pathway. Thus acid phosphatase and catalase are useful traditional biochemical markers for different compartments, although no isolated marker measurement proves absolute purity of a fraction.

Mitochondria have a double-membrane architecture and use the inner membrane for respiratory energy conversion. Their proton gradient is normally produced by electron transport and is harnessed for ATP synthesis; the lysosome normally consumes ATP to pump protons into its lumen. Both use electrochemical gradients, but their physiological purposes and membrane locations are different. The ATP hydrolysis-driven V-type pump is not simply the mitochondrial ATP synthase operating in its ordinary physiological direction.

The proteasome is not a membrane-bound organelle. It degrades many individual cytosolic and nuclear proteins, often after their targeting by ubiquitin, using a proteolytic core and, in the 26S system, ATP-dependent regulatory components. Lysosomal pathways are particularly suited to extracellular material, membrane cargo, large aggregates and organelles delivered through vesicular mechanisms or autophagy. The systems overlap in protein quality control, but they solve different physical delivery problems. In particular, a whole damaged mitochondrion cannot be passed intact through the narrow proteasome core.

Together, these comparisons reveal an organizing principle of cell biology: the membrane count, chemical environment, protein delivery route and biochemical function form a coherent set of properties. Knowing only that an organelle is membrane-bound is inadequate to predict which enzymes it contains or how it uses energy. The next chapter on vacuoles extends this idea to storage and osmoregulation.

17. Integrating the pathway and diagnosing a defect

Follow a newly synthesized acid hydrolase from its source to its final function. It enters the ER, obtains appropriate processing and sorting information in the Golgi, is packaged toward the endosomal system, is released from the targeting receptor, and reaches an acidic digestive compartment. Independently, an LDL particle from the surface or an autophagic cargo from the cytoplasm is delivered into the same degradative network. The enzyme can only hydrolyse its substrate when these two routes converge in a suitable chemical environment. Finally, products must be transported out for reuse.

The pathway supplies a strategy for unfamiliar examination problems. Start by asking whether cargo has entered the cell or autophagic carrier. Next determine whether the cargo has reached a late endosome or lysosome. Then evaluate whether the compartment maintains normal pH and whether the enzyme is present and catalytically active under appropriate assay conditions. Finally determine whether products form and whether they exit. A defect in any of these steps can cause an accumulation phenotype, but the accompanying experimental findings differ.

A useful way to test this strategy is to imagine two mutations. Mutation A removes the signal for ER entry of a particular soluble hydrolase. The protein may be made but not enter the secretory pathway, so it cannot receive the usual Golgi targeting tag. Mutation B removes only the carbohydrate tagging activity for a set of otherwise folded hydrolases. Those proteins may still enter the ER and pass through Golgi but fail in later targeting. Both mutations lower lysosomal enzyme activity, yet extracellular secretion would be a much more direct prediction for B than for A. The distinction follows from membrane topology and the order of the secretory pathway.

In a second pair of cells, one has defective degradation of an endocytosed membrane receptor; the other has normal degradation but an impaired recycling pathway for another receptor. The first may retain a receptor in intralumenal vesicles, unavailable for signaling or reuse. The second may have reduced cell-surface receptor even if overall receptor abundance has not yet changed. Measuring total protein, surface localization and degradation separately prevents an ambiguous conclusion. The function of the lysosomal system is thus connected to the regulation of membrane composition as well as to the recovery of nutrients.

The core ideas can be consolidated in five causal statements. Compartmentalization separates enzymes from the cytosol. ATP-dependent proton transport establishes the favorable chemical environment. Endosomal sorting and autophagic delivery determine which materials encounter the hydrolases. Substrate-specific hydrolysis transforms complex molecules into smaller products. Selective transport and compartment remodeling allow the products and machinery to be reused. Failure of any statement's mechanism yields experimentally distinguishable consequences. The next step is to examine how related compartments, especially vacuoles, use membrane gradients and lumenal chemistry for storage and homeostasis as well as degradation.

18. Part B — Conceptual questions and assertion–reason

The following questions are original practice questions, not reproduced examination papers.

Question 1

Which property most directly explains why a lysosome can contain active proteases without normally digesting cytosolic proteins?

A. Its own ribosomes; B. A selectively permeable limiting membrane; C. Its double membrane; D. Absence of ATP

Answer and explanation

B. The membrane physically separates enzyme and cytosolic substrate. A and C are false for lysosomes, and D would compromise acidification.

Question 2

Which protein directly supplies energy for lysosomal acidification?

A. V-type H⁺-ATPase; B. Catalase; C. Mitochondrial ATP synthase in normal synthesis mode; D. Acid phosphatase

Answer and explanation

A. ATP hydrolysis is coupled to inward proton movement. Catalase handles peroxide, ATP synthase ordinarily makes ATP in mitochondria, and acid phosphatase hydrolyses phosphate esters.

Question 3

Where is the M6P sorting signal recognized for transport of many soluble lysosomal hydrolases?

A. Nuclear pore; B. Trans-Golgi network; C. Mitochondrial matrix; D. Proteasome

Answer and explanation

B. M6P receptors recognize lumenal tagged glycans at the TGN. The other structures are not the ordinary sorting location for this pathway.

Question 4

Which statement about the autophagosome is correct?

A. It is a single-membrane mature lysosome; B. Its double membrane encloses cytoplasmic cargo; C. It originates only from external bacteria; D. It is a peroxisome

Answer and explanation

B. Macroautophagy encloses intracellular cargo within two membranes; the outer membrane participates in fusion. A confuses the carrier and digestive compartment, C describes external cargo, D describes an oxidative organelle.

Question 5

Which disorder most directly illustrates failure of lysosomal targeting of multiple enzymes?

A. Pompe; B. Tay–Sachs; C. I-cell disease; D. Gaucher

Answer and explanation

C. Defective GlcNAc-1-phosphotransferase impairs tagging of many hydrolases. A, B and D are specific enzymatic deficiencies.

Question 6

Which pair is correctly matched?

A. Pompe — GM2; B. Gaucher — glucosylceramide; C. Tay–Sachs — glycogen; D. Niemann–Pick C — primary acid sphingomyelinase deficiency

Answer and explanation

B. Pompe stores lysosomal glycogen, Tay–Sachs stores GM2, and NPC involves cholesterol trafficking, unlike acid sphingomyelinase deficiency.

Question 7

Which observation alone establishes lysosomal hydrolysis of a particular cargo?

A. LAMP1-positive puncta; B. Acidotropic dye signal; C. Direct measurement of appropriate cleavage products from the cargo within a verified lysosomal compartment; D. Increased organelle diameter

Answer and explanation

C. Product formation with verified localization tests the specific reaction. A indicates identity, B acidity-related dye accumulation, and D morphology, not digestion.

Question 8

A receptor is sorted into an intralumenal vesicle. What is the fate of its cytosolic tail initially?

A. It faces the interior of the intralumenal vesicle; B. It immediately crosses into the nucleus; C. It stays exposed to the bulk cytosol; D. It becomes an M6P glycan

Answer and explanation

A. Inward budding preserves bilayer topology while enclosing the former cytosolic side within the new vesicle. Subsequent membrane breakdown exposes the tail to hydrolases. B, C and D do not follow from this topology.

Question 9 — Assertion–reason

Assertion: V-ATPase inhibition can lower lysosomal degradation. Reason: Many lysosomal hydrolases require an acidic environment for efficient catalysis.

A. Both true; reason explains assertion. B. Both true; reason does not explain assertion. C. Assertion true, reason false. D. Assertion false, reason true.

Answer and explanation

A. Inhibition reduces acidification, which can reduce acid-dependent catalytic activity. Additional effects on trafficking may also contribute.

Question 10 — Assertion–reason

Assertion: An increase in autophagosome number always proves increased autophagic flux. Reason: Autophagosome abundance depends on both formation and clearance.

A. Both true and explanatory. B. Both true but unrelated. C. Assertion true, reason false. D. Assertion false, reason true.

Answer and explanation

D. A clearance defect can increase abundance while completed flux falls. Formation rate and turnover must be distinguished.

Question 11

Which compartment has the closest broad degradative functional analogy to an animal lysosome?

A. Plant lytic vacuole; B. Peroxisome; C. Nucleolus; D. Mitochondrial intermembrane space

Answer and explanation

A. Lytic vacuoles have acid hydrolases and degradative functions. B specializes in oxidative chemistry, C in ribosome biogenesis, and D in mitochondrial architecture and energy-linked processes.

Question 12

Which claim about acid hydrolases released into the cytosol is most accurate?

A. Every enzyme instantly becomes inactive; B. Every enzyme becomes more active; C. Many have reduced activity, but leakage may still cause injury; D. Leakage is identical to physiological autophagy.

Answer and explanation

C. The pH shift often reduces enzyme activity but does not ensure zero activity. A and B overgeneralize; D confuses membrane damage with regulated delivery.

19. Part C — Analytical, numerical and experimental problems

All numerical values and experimental datasets in this practice set are illustrative. Use the assumptions stated in each question.

Problem 1 — Proton gradient

The cytosol is at pH 7.1 and the lumen at pH 4.6. What is the lumen-to-cytosol free proton concentration ratio in the ideal concentration approximation?

A. 2.5; B. 31.6; C. 316; D. 0.00316

Answer and explanation

C. The ratio is 10^(7.1 − 4.6) = 10^2.5 ≈ 316. A is the pH difference; B corresponds to 1.5 pH units; D reverses the ratio.

Problem 2 — Chemical and electrical work

At 310 K a lysosome has cytosolic pH 7.2, lumenal pH 5.0 and a lumen-positive potential of 30 mV. Calculate uphill proton transport work per mole, using R = 8.314 and F = 96,485.

A. 10.16; B. 13.06; C. 15.95; D. −15.95 kJ/mol

Answer and explanation

C. Chemical work = 2.303 × 8.314 × 310 × 2.2 = 13.06 kJ/mol; electrical work = 96,485 × 0.030 = 2.89 kJ/mol; sum ≈ 15.95. A subtracts voltage, B omits voltage and D reverses the direction.

Problem 3 — Latency

An organelle fraction yields 25 units before membrane disruption and 125 after disruption, assayed identically. What percentage of measured activity was latent?

A. 20%; B. 80%; C. 100%; D. 500%

Answer and explanation

B. (125 − 25)/125 × 100 = 80%. A is accessible activity. C assumes none was accessible. D is a ratio of total to initial activity expressed as a percentage, not latency.

Problem 4 — Buffering

A lysosome has volume 1 × 10⁻¹⁶ L and constant buffer capacity 10 mmol L⁻¹ pH⁻¹ over the interval. How many net proton equivalents are needed for a decrease of 0.4 pH units? Use Nₐ = 6.022 × 10²³.

A. 2.41 × 10⁵; B. 2.41 × 10²; C. 6.02 × 10⁷; D. 4 × 10⁻¹⁹ molecules

Answer and explanation

A. 0.010 × 0.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 4 × 10⁻¹⁹ mol, then multiply by Avogadro's constant to get 2.41 × 10⁵ equivalents. B is a factor of 1,000 smaller; C is unrelated; D has moles incorrectly labeled as molecules.

Problem 5 — Pump versus channel

A sealed model vesicle has a functional V-ATPase but negligible counterion permeability. ATP is added outside on the V1-facing side. Why might acidification stop before the chemically predicted gradient is reached?

A. ATP makes the lumen negative; B. Proton entry creates a lumen-positive voltage opposing further entry; C. Protons are uncharged; D. Hydrolases consume all ATP directly.

Answer and explanation

B. Inward movement of positive charge creates electrical work against further transport. A reverses the sign, C is false, and D is not the specified mechanism. Counterion pathways can relieve the voltage.

Problem 6 — Locating a targeting failure

Fibroblasts show reduced lysosomal activity of several glycosidases, increased activities in extracellular medium, and normal enzyme activity when purified at optimum pH. Which defect best explains the results?

A. M6P-tagging defect; B. Loss of all ribosomes; C. Acid alpha-glucosidase deficiency alone; D. Lack of extracellular substrate

Answer and explanation

A. Multiple active enzymes are produced but misdirected. B would reduce synthesis; C affects one primary activity; D does not explain systematic mislocalization.

Problem 7 — Separating uptake from digestion

Both control and mutant internalize 150 units of labelled intact protein. After one hour, controls retain 30 and mutants retain 90. Calculate the fraction cleared and infer what remains uncertain.

Answer and explanation

Control clears (150 − 30)/150 = 80%; mutant clears (150 − 90)/150 = 40%. Uptake is comparable, but a delivery, digestion or other clearance defect could explain retention. Localization, pH and enzyme activity measurements are needed before identifying the exact step.

Problem 8 — Substrate balance

Substrate enters at 18 equivalents/h; hydrolysis proceeds at 11 equivalents/h and export has spare capacity. How much extra intact substrate accumulates in four hours at constant rates?

A. 7; B. 28; C. 44; D. 72 equivalents

Answer and explanation

B. Net accumulation = (18 − 11) × 4 = 28 equivalents. A is an hourly rate, C total digestion and D total delivery.

Problem 9 — Product versus precursor

A substrate arrives at 10 units/h and is completely digested, but only six product equivalents/h are exported. Assuming one-to-one conversion and no other routes, what happens over five hours?

A. 20 units of substrate accumulate; B. 20 product equivalents accumulate; C. 50 product equivalents accumulate; D. Nothing accumulates

Answer and explanation

B. dP/dt = 10 − 6 = 4 units/h, so 20 product equivalents accumulate. Substrate digestion equals delivery. C ignores export and D ignores the bottleneck.

Problem 10 — First-order model

A substrate enters at 24 nmol/h and follows D = kS with k = 0.6 h⁻¹. A mutation lowers k to 0.2 h⁻¹. Predict the steady-state substrate pools.

A. 40 and 120 nmol; B. 14.4 and 4.8 nmol; C. 120 and 40 nmol; D. 24 and 24 nmol

Answer and explanation

A. At steady state I = kS, so S = 24/0.6 = 40 nmol and 24/0.2 = 120 nmol. B multiplies instead of divides, C reverses the effect, D ignores degradation kinetics.

Problem 11 — Autophagosome abundance

Cell A forms 12 autophagosomes/h and clears 12/h. Cell B forms 12/h and clears 3/h. Initially both have the same number. Assuming fixed rates, what happens after two hours?

A. Both unchanged; B. A unchanged, B gains 18; C. A gains 24, B gains 6; D. B loses 18

Answer and explanation

B. Net rates are zero and nine per hour, respectively. B gains 18 despite no increase in formation. Thus more autophagosomes can indicate impaired clearance, not increased completed flux.

Problem 12 — pH versus catalytic deficiency

Two mutants retain the same intact cargo. X has lumen pH 4.8 and a selectively deficient hydrolase when assayed at optimal pH. Y has lumen pH 6.4 and normal purified hydrolase activity. Which interpretation is supported?

A. Both must lack the same enzyme; B. X suggests catalytic deficiency; Y suggests impaired lumenal environment; C. Y must lack all lysosomal membranes; D. X proves defective uptake

Answer and explanation

B. Independent assays separate enzyme capacity and environment. A ignores the activity result; C overstates pH evidence; D does not follow from a selective enzyme deficiency.

Problem 13 — LDL cargo tracing

A cell internalizes LDL normally and generates free cholesterol inside late endosomes and lysosomes, but cytosolic redistribution is reduced. Which stage is most directly implicated?

A. LDL binding; B. Ester cleavage; C. Post-hydrolysis cholesterol transport; D. Nuclear import

Answer and explanation

C. Uptake and product formation establish that A and B can occur. The defect is downstream, consistent with a cholesterol trafficking problem such as NPC1/NPC2 dysfunction. D has no direct explanatory role.

Problem 14 — Marker interpretation

A treatment doubles the total fluorescence of an acidotropic dye and doubles the volume of LAMP1-positive compartments. Can the experiment alone establish a lower lumen pH?

Answer and explanation

No. Increased compartment volume can change total dye signal without a change in pH. Probe loading, optical factors and pH dependence must be controlled. A calibrated ratiometric measure within compartments would provide stronger evidence.
