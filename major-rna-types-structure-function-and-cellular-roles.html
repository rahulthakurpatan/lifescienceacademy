1. One chemical language, many cellular jobs

A cell must use its hereditary information without losing the ability to pass that information to its descendants. DNA provides a comparatively stable record, but the record alone cannot carry out most of the work of the cell. A sequence specifying an enzyme must be made available to a molecular machine that assembles amino acids. That machine must distinguish the meaning of one triplet from another, bring the appropriate amino acids into a common reaction chamber and form peptide bonds. Before the machine itself can function, several of its components have to be synthesized, folded and assembled. RNA participates in each of these tasks. Some RNAs act as readable copies of instructions. Other RNAs are permanent or reusable parts of a machine, and still others recognize particular molecular targets so that those targets can be processed. The major RNA classes represent different solutions to these problems, rather than different versions of the same chemical substance.

Suppose that a bacterial cell produces two proteins in response to a nutritional change. It does not need to construct two new varieties of ribosome, one dedicated to each protein. It can make two different messages and use its existing ribosomes to interpret them. The messages are messenger RNAs, or mRNAs. A ribosome has its own RNAs, known as ribosomal RNAs or rRNAs. An rRNA does not normally carry the instructions for making the protein being synthesized. Its nucleotide sequence specifies the shape and chemical properties of a working part of the ribosome. Transfer RNA, abbreviated tRNA, provides the physical connection between the message and an amino acid. When the cell stops producing one message and starts producing another, the rRNAs and tRNAs can continue to serve. This separation between replaceable information and reusable machinery is a central organizational advantage of gene expression.

A similar distinction operates in the eukaryotic nucleus. A newly transcribed precursor mRNA may contain introns that must be removed. Small nuclear RNAs, or snRNAs, help identify the relevant portions of the precursor and support the reactions that join exons. Their sequences are not being decoded into a chain of amino acids during that operation. They act through their own structures and their ability to pair with another RNA. In the nucleolus, small nucleolar RNAs, or snoRNAs, guide chemical changes to selected nucleotides of ribosomal RNA and help with aspects of its maturation. Here again the information in an RNA sequence acts directly, without first being converted into a polypeptide. This makes it important to distinguish expression of an RNA gene from translation of an RNA molecule. Both a protein-coding gene and a tRNA gene can be expressed through transcription, but only the former has a protein product as its usual endpoint.

The same nucleotide alphabet underlies all these molecules. An RNA molecule contains ribose sugars, phosphate groups and nitrogenous bases, most commonly adenine, guanine, cytosine and uracil. There is no chemical rule that an RNA containing these bases must become a message. Instead, the order of nucleotides can determine a set of codons, a stable fold, a pattern recognized by a protein or a surface able to contact another nucleic acid. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. An mRNA can contain structured regions that influence its use, and a tRNA uses a short sequence to recognize the message. The distinction between classes depends primarily on what the whole molecule does in its biological setting.

One useful first question when encountering an unfamiliar RNA is therefore what happens to it after it is made. A transcript that serves as a template for a ribosome and specifies a polypeptide is an mRNA. A folded RNA retained in a ribosomal subunit is an rRNA. A small molecule bearing an anticodon and a terminal amino acid attachment site is a tRNA. An RNA whose recognition sequence aligns an intron or guides a modification is a functional RNA acting at the level of RNA processing. These criteria are much more reliable than a rule based only on length or abundance. Although many tRNAs are short and many mRNAs are long, size alone cannot determine the biological role of an RNA. A short mRNA and a longer noncoding RNA are perfectly possible.

2. Why RNA can fold, recognize and transmit information

Every nucleotide contains a base connected to the first carbon of a sugar. Ribose has hydroxyl groups at both the second and third carbons. DNA uses deoxyribose, which lacks the hydroxyl group at the second carbon. An RNA chain connects successive sugars through phosphodiester bonds involving their third and fifth carbons. This connectivity gives the chain a chemical direction, conventionally described from its 5′ end to its 3′ end. These terms refer to sugar positions and not to the number of bases in a segment. The orientation remains meaningful when the RNA folds back on itself, when two RNAs pair or when a protein attaches to one end. In a diagram, therefore, the ends should be labelled before reasoning about sequence recognition.

The ribose 2′ hydroxyl group helps explain both the vulnerability and the versatility of RNA. Under alkaline conditions it can be deprotonated and attack the adjacent phosphate within the same chain. Cleavage may generate a cyclic phosphate intermediate and a free hydroxyl terminus. This internal reaction is unavailable to ordinary DNA because its corresponding sugar position lacks that group. Consequently, the two nucleic acids can behave very differently in chemical stability tests even though their phosphate backbones appear similar. RNA is not necessarily short-lived in a living cell, however. A tightly folded RNA protected by proteins can persist far longer than an unprotected message. Chemical susceptibility and biological lifetime are related but are not interchangeable properties.

The bases make it possible for RNA to recognize nucleic acid sequences. Adenine generally pairs with uracil, and guanine with cytosine, through complementary hydrogen-bonding arrangements. Guanine and uracil also form a common noncanonical pair in RNA. Such a pair has a different geometry from the usual Watson–Crick pairs, but it can still be accommodated in many helical contexts. Adjacent bases interact through stacking, and these interactions contribute substantially to helix stability. It is not sufficient to count hydrogen bonds and assume that the RNA with the greatest total must always form the most stable structure. The sequence context, stacking, ionic conditions and structural constraints all contribute to the free energy.

When complementary stretches are present in one chain, the molecule can form a stem by pairing those stretches in opposite directions. A connecting segment at the end of a stem can remain unpaired, creating a hairpin loop. A nucleotide or short region protruding from one side of a helix forms a bulge. If both sides contain unpaired regions within a helix, the feature is called an internal loop. Multiple helices can meet at a junction. These apparently irregular features are not simply defects in a perfect helix. Their exposed bases can participate in sequence recognition, and their geometry can orient distant portions of the RNA. A loop in a tRNA, for example, exposes a triplet that must interact with a message, whereas loops in an rRNA help create surfaces for contacts inside a ribosome.

RNA secondary structure describes patterns of local and longer-range base pairing, without requiring a complete three-dimensional arrangement. Tertiary structure describes how those paired and unpaired regions occupy space relative to one another. A set of secondary-structure elements may assemble into a compact structure through stacking between stems, contacts between distant loops and interactions involving sugars and phosphates. A tRNA cloverleaf is a useful secondary-structure map, but the molecule in a ribosome has a different three-dimensional outline. A drawing of four arms in a plane cannot tell us which surfaces face a ribosomal protein or where the amino acid attachment point lies relative to the anticodon. These questions require the tertiary fold.

The negative charge of the phosphate backbone creates a physical obstacle to folding. Bringing several RNA segments into close proximity increases unfavorable electrostatic interactions unless the charges are screened or otherwise stabilized. Monovalent cations and divalent cations, including magnesium, can reduce this penalty. Magnesium can also occupy particular positions in structured RNAs and contribute to reaction chemistry in certain RNA-containing enzymes. The effects of a salt change are not identical for all RNAs. Some stems remain stable under conditions in which the more delicate tertiary contacts are lost. Accordingly, an experiment may reveal that base pairing has persisted even though a biological function dependent on the complete fold is impaired.

Consider the illustrative sequence 5′-GCGAAACGC-3′. The first three nucleotides can pair antiparallel with the last three, leaving the central AAA stretch in a loop. Position 1 pairs with position 9, position 2 with position 8, and position 3 with position 7. The sequence does not have to join its ends by a covalent bond to make a hairpin. If the guanine at position 1 is replaced by adenine, the resulting A–C mismatch may destabilize the outer pair. If position 9 is additionally changed from cytosine to uracil, an A–U pair becomes possible again. This is a compensatory mutation: the second sequence change repairs the potential interaction even though the initial sequence is not restored. Restoration of the fold can provide evidence that a specific paired contact matters. It does not guarantee the original thermodynamic stability because the identity and stacking of the terminal pair have changed.

Such reasoning is especially useful when interpreting a conserved RNA alignment. Suppose that corresponding paired positions are G–C in one species, A–U in another and C–G in a third. The conservation of pairing despite sequence variation supports a structural requirement. In contrast, if the same sequence is strongly conserved regardless of its possible pairing partner, it may be necessary for a sequence-specific contact. Comparative evidence must be interpreted with awareness of shared ancestry and the small number of independent changes in a limited alignment. A single paired substitution is suggestive, not proof that two nucleotides directly contact one another. A functional mutation test and structural measurement can complement the comparative inference.

3. From a gene to a working RNA or ribonucleoprotein

The production of an RNA starts with a polymerase, but the identity of the polymerase does not by itself establish the RNA's eventual function. In a typical eukaryotic nucleus, RNA polymerase II produces precursor mRNAs and many noncoding transcripts, including several spliceosomal snRNAs. RNA polymerase I produces the large ribosomal precursor from which the principal large and small subunit rRNAs are formed. RNA polymerase III transcribes tRNA genes, 5S rRNA genes, U6 snRNA genes and various other small RNA genes. There are taxon-specific exceptions and additional organellar or plant polymerases. The usual assignments are useful because they connect the upstream synthesis machinery to the type of RNA that becomes available. They must not be turned into a rule that polymerase II always makes protein-coding messages.

In bacteria, a single principal multisubunit RNA polymerase transcribes genes for messages and functional RNAs, with initiation specificity influenced by promoter recognition. A bacterial ribosomal RNA operon may yield a precursor that includes multiple rRNA sequences and, in some organisms, tRNA sequences. Processing separates the individual RNA products. This differs from a polycistronic mRNA even though both start with multiple functional elements in a single transcription unit. In a polycistronic message, distinct coding regions usually serve as templates for separate polypeptides. In an rRNA precursor, distinct regions become working RNA molecules. Calling every multigene transcript polycistronic without asking about the fate of its products can therefore obscure the underlying biology.

A nascent RNA can begin to acquire its three-dimensional structure while additional nucleotides are being synthesized. The sequence at the 5′ end is available before the sequence at the 3′ end, so early contacts may form before their eventual partners emerge. Protein binding and processing can stabilize or alter these contacts. Some arrangements of an RNA therefore reflect the route through which the molecule was formed as well as the eventual balance of free energies. A drawing that shows a mature fold does not establish that the molecule reached that fold in one step. For an examination question about a mutant, it is important to distinguish failure of the final structure from failure of an intermediate necessary for reaching that structure.

Many cellular RNAs function as ribonucleoproteins, or RNPs. An RNP contains RNA associated with protein through noncovalent interactions, although individual components can have covalent modifications of their own. RNA-binding proteins may contact a particular sequence, a double-stranded region, a loop or the shape and charge pattern of an entire fold. Their binding can protect an RNA, help it fold, position a target or recruit a processing activity. The RNA can make an equally essential contribution to the complex. In a guide RNP the protein may provide an enzymatic activity while the RNA determines which target sequence is recognized. In other complexes, RNA is itself responsible for an important part of the reaction chemistry. The ribosome illustrates this latter case. It is an RNP, yet it cannot be understood as a protein enzyme with an inert RNA support.

It is useful to think of RNP formation as the acquisition of a biological state rather than an inevitable consequence of transcription. Imagine a gene for an RNA guide that is transcribed normally but has a mutation in a sequence required for binding its protein partner. The RNA may be detectable by a complementary probe. Nevertheless, the functional guide complex could be absent because the transcript has failed to assemble. Conversely, a mutation in an assembly protein may reduce the abundance of mature RNA because an unprotected intermediate is degraded. Reduced RNA abundance would not by itself show that transcription had slowed. When evaluating the result of a mutation, one must separate synthesis, folding, assembly, stability and function instead of treating them as a single event.

4. Evidence for a replaceable messenger

The discovery of messenger RNA resolved a basic uncertainty about the relationship between genes and ribosomes. If the ribosome were specific for an individual protein, switching the type of protein synthesized would require a corresponding change in the population of ribosomes. A different possibility was that ribosomes were comparatively stable machines that could receive new instructions. This latter model predicted that a cell could redirect protein synthesis through a newly made RNA even while most ribosomes remained old. Bacteriophage infection provided a valuable situation for distinguishing the models because the host begins making products specified by the infecting viral genome.

In 1961 Sydney Brenner, François Jacob and Matthew Meselson published evidence for a transient RNA intermediate. The strategy used bacterial ribosomes distinguished by isotopic density and radioactive labelling of newly synthesized RNA after phage infection. Newly made RNA became associated with ribosomes formed before the change in gene expression. The experimental logic depended on being able to distinguish the age of the ribosome from that of the RNA associated with it. Had the new RNA been confined to newly synthesized, specially programmed ribosomes, the pattern of association would have been different. The observed association supported the existence of a replaceable message carried to an already existing protein synthesis apparatus.

The two kinds of labels in this reasoning answer different questions. An isotope-dependent density difference identifies a population of ribosomal particles under suitable centrifugation conditions. A pulse of radioactive precursor allows newly formed RNA to be detected. Merely finding radioactivity in a ribosome-containing fraction would be ambiguous if all ribosomes were formed during the pulse. The old-ribosome distinction provides the crucial context. One must also avoid interpreting the newly associated RNA as a permanent structural component of the old ribosome simply because the materials sediment together. The biological result is informative because the change in expression, the labelling history and the association of new RNA with existing particles agree with the messenger model.

A complementary question is whether an RNA sequence can actually direct the formation of a particular amino acid sequence. Cell-free translation experiments made it possible to examine this with defined RNA substrates. In the classic 1961 poly(U) experiment by Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei, a synthetic RNA made of uracil residues directed incorporation of phenylalanine into a polypeptide in a cell-free system. This revealed a correspondence between the UUU triplet and phenylalanine. The significance for RNA classification is that a message has an experimentally recognizable template function. The experiment does not imply that natural messages generally consist of repeated identical codons, nor does it establish the whole genetic code on its own. It shows how changing the nucleotide instructions can change the identity of the translated product without constructing a new kind of ribosome.

These discoveries also suggest a powerful general design for Part C questions. Hold one component of a molecular system constant while varying another. If the ribosomal machinery is unchanged and replacing the supplied RNA changes the product sequence, the supplied RNA carries information relevant to the product. If the RNA message is unchanged but changing a transfer RNA changes the amino acid delivered, the effect lies at the adaptor stage. If neither message nor adaptor can be used after a defect in a ribosomal structural component, the lesion affects the working machine. A sequence of such comparisons can separate the different roles even if all three molecules are chemically recognizable as RNA.

5. Architecture of a protein-coding message

A typical mature eukaryotic nuclear mRNA has a 5′ untranslated region, a coding region and a 3′ untranslated region. Many such transcripts also carry a 5′ cap and a 3′ poly(A) tail. The untranslated regions are transcribed portions of the message that lie outside its main translated coding region. They can contain recognition sites for RNA-binding proteins and structural features that affect the efficiency or location of protein synthesis. Thus untranslated does not mean functionless. A nucleotide substitution in a region outside the main coding sequence can change the quantity of protein produced while leaving the amino acid sequence of that protein unchanged. This distinguishes effects on expression from changes to the protein's primary structure.

The coding region of an ordinary message is read in successive groups of three nucleotides, known as codons. A start codon establishes the main reading frame in context; a stop codon provides a termination signal and normally does not contribute an amino acid to the chain. The first nucleotide of the mRNA is not necessarily the first nucleotide of the start codon. For example, if transcription begins eighty nucleotides before the start codon, those eighty nucleotides can form part of the 5′ untranslated region of the mature message, subject to the transcript's actual processing pattern. A gene map using +1 to label transcription initiation therefore describes a different event from a translation diagram marking the start of the coding sequence.

The cap is chemically distinct from the first transcribed nucleotide. A modified guanosine is attached in an unusual 5′ to 5′ triphosphate arrangement to the RNA end. This creates a recognizable terminal structure and influences protection and interactions with binding proteins. The cap is not a codon and it does not become an amino acid. Likewise the usual eukaryotic poly(A) tail is an added run of adenosines at the processed 3′ end rather than an extension of the translated coding sequence. The detailed enzymes that install these features belong to RNA end processing. Here the key structural principle is that a message's physical length includes regions and terminal additions that are not translated into the main protein.

Consider a mature RNA with a 120-nucleotide 5′ untranslated region, a 903-nucleotide open reading frame from start codon through stop codon inclusive, a 250-nucleotide 3′ untranslated region and a poly(A) tail of 90 nucleotides. The count of ordinary residues is 120 + 903 + 250 + 90 = 1,363, excluding the separate cap guanosine. The open reading frame contains 903 / 3 = 301 triplets. Under the standard assumptions of one terminal stop codon, no recoding and no premature termination, the chain initially contains 300 amino acid residues. Counting the cap or the tail as part of the coding sequence would produce the wrong answer. Removing an initiator methionine or other residues during protein maturation could also change the final mature protein length, but that is a later process.

There are important natural departures from this common architecture. Replication-dependent histone mRNAs in many animals are notable examples of nuclear messages that terminate in a specialized stem-loop-containing region rather than the conventional long poly(A) tail. Bacterial messages generally lack the typical eukaryotic mRNA cap and long stabilizing poly(A) tail, although bacteria can make other terminal modifications and add short tails in different biological contexts. Some eukaryotic and viral mRNAs use alternative initiation strategies. Consequently, a cap or a poly(A) tail can be useful evidence about a particular transcript, but neither is a universal defining property of all mRNAs. The defining property remains that the molecule functions as a template for protein synthesis.

An mRNA may be actively translated by several ribosomes at the same time. The ribosomes are attached at different locations and each reads the same nucleotide sequence along its own trajectory. A group of ribosomes associated with one message is called a polysome or polyribosome. This arrangement makes it possible for a single transcript to support production of multiple protein molecules without making one transcript for each product. A polysome is not an arrangement of multiple separate mRNAs around one ribosome. In the common model, the mRNA is the shared thread and the ribosomes are the repeated machines. The polysome concept also illustrates the distinction between template copy number and protein output. A change in the rate at which ribosomes initiate can alter the output from the same number of messages.

6. Bacterial, archaeal and eukaryotic messages in their cellular setting

One of the most useful comparisons between bacterial and eukaryotic gene expression concerns the number of distinct coding regions in a mature RNA. A bacterial operon may contain several adjacent genes controlled by a common promoter. If one transcript includes all their coding sequences, and each of these sequences is translated separately, the transcript is called polycistronic. The term cistron was originally developed through genetic analysis of functional units. In this setting it is used to distinguish the separate protein-coding units on the same RNA. The physical continuity of the message allows the cell to coordinate production of proteins involved in a common process without requiring a separate transcription initiation event for every component. The ribosomes translating the RNA still need to recognize the appropriate start and stop signals for individual coding regions.

Imagine a bacterial message with coding regions A, B and C arranged in that order. The transcript is a single molecular object, yet it can give rise to three distinct polypeptide products. Translation of B normally need not extend the polypeptide made from A. Each region has its own reading frame and termination context, and internal initiation or translational coupling can influence how efficiently the downstream regions are used. Even if all three coding regions occur once on every message, the amount of each protein produced need not be equal. The number of copies of a coding region determines the number of templates available for that region, but initiation frequencies, local structures and other conditions can alter how often each template is used.

In typical animal and plant nuclear mRNAs, one main coding region is translated. Such a message is described as monocistronic. A common eukaryotic strategy involves separate RNA molecules for different proteins, even where expression of the corresponding genes is coordinated. This provides multiple opportunities for transcript-specific control through the untranslated regions and interactions with RNA-binding proteins. Monocistronic organization does not imply that a message has no other possible short open reading frames. Upstream open reading frames can influence initiation at the principal coding region, and certain messages produce more than one product by alternative mechanisms. The comparison between bacterial and eukaryotic messages is a broad organizing principle, not a prohibition against exceptions.

There are also bacterial monocistronic messages. It would be incorrect to identify an organism simply by finding a transcript that produces one polypeptide. Conversely, some eukaryotic organisms make long primary transcripts that are subsequently separated into individual mature mRNAs. The existence of a multigene precursor in those organisms does not demonstrate that the final mature RNAs are polycistronic. A mature transcript map and knowledge of how it is translated are more informative than the number of genes initially copied by one polymerase. Viruses supply further examples of unusual coding arrangements, but their diversity should not be taken as the standard model for cellular mRNA organization.

A second difference concerns the physical separation of transcription from translation. Bacteria have no membrane-enclosed nucleus. Ribosomes can begin translating a bacterial message while it is still being synthesized, provided that the relevant region has emerged and is accessible. The transcript does not generally have to traverse a nuclear envelope before reaching the protein synthesis machinery. In eukaryotes, nuclear transcription and cytoplasmic translation are normally separated. The nuclear pre-mRNA has to attain an appropriate processing and RNP assembly state and be transported out of the nucleus before ordinary cytoplasmic translation. The existence of this boundary provides an opportunity to examine whether the transcript has matured correctly.

The distinction is particularly useful in interpreting an abnormal RNA population. Suppose a nuclear fraction contains a high proportion of transcripts with retained introns, whereas the cytoplasm has a much lower proportion. This pattern is compatible with selective export or nuclear retention of some incompletely processed molecules. It cannot be explained simply by saying that the polymerase makes less RNA. One would need information about synthesis rates, processing, export and stability to assign the cause. In bacterial systems, the absence of nuclear export does not eliminate RNA quality control, but the route between production and use is organized differently.

Archaea provide a valuable reminder that the absence of a nucleus and the organization of the expression apparatus are different evolutionary features. Archaeal cells lack a conventional nucleus, as bacteria do, but many of their transcription components are related to eukaryotic counterparts. Archaeal messages can be monocistronic or polycistronic. Some are leaderless, with very little or no 5′ untranslated region before the initiation region. The same general template–adaptor–ribosome relationship is preserved even though the ways of initiating expression and organizing transcripts vary. This is why grouping bacteria and archaea as prokaryotes is appropriate for certain cellular comparisons but not for every molecular feature.

Organelles offer a further example of the distinction. Mitochondria and plastids retain their own genetic systems to varying degrees. Their transcripts are made within the organelle, and their messages are used by organellar ribosomes. Their RNA processing, gene organization and terminal structures cannot be inferred simply from the rules for nuclear mRNA. An organellar genome is part of a eukaryotic cell, yet several features of its expression apparatus reflect its bacterial ancestry, while others have diverged substantially. The nuclear compartment and the organellar compartments must therefore be identified explicitly in comparisons of polymerase products and RNA locations.

7. RNA abundance, lifetime and the interpretation of expression

RNA abundance is a property of both production and removal. The number of molecules of one kind in a cell does not reveal how quickly they were made unless their lifetime is known. A message that is synthesized rapidly but degraded rapidly can have the same abundance as a message produced slowly and preserved for a long period. This distinction is biologically important because the lifetime of a message affects the speed with which its protein output can respond to changes in transcription. A stable structural RNA and a labile regulatory message can serve different functions even when their chemical composition is broadly similar.

A simple quantitative model helps explain the distinction. Let M represent the expected number of molecules of a particular RNA in a cell, s its constant synthesis rate in molecules per minute and k its first-order loss constant in inverse minutes. The rate of change is dM/dt = s − kM. The loss term expresses the assumption that each molecule has the same constant probability of being removed per unit time. A steady state is reached when the mean rate of synthesis balances the mean rate of removal, giving M = s/k. At this state, molecules continue to enter and leave the population. Steady abundance does not mean that transcription and degradation have stopped.

If new synthesis stops and the same first-order removal law remains valid, the equation becomes dM/dt = −kM. Separation of variables and integration gives ln(M/M0) = −kt, where M0 is the abundance at the moment synthesis ceases. Rearrangement gives M = M0 exp(−kt). To obtain the half-life, substitute M/M0 = 1/2. It follows that t1/2 = ln(2)/k. The half-life is expressed in minutes when k is measured in inverse minutes. For an RNA whose half-life is ten minutes, k is approximately 0.0693 per minute. After thirty minutes without synthesis, three half-lives have elapsed and one eighth of the original abundance is expected to remain.

Suppose a cell synthesizes a particular RNA at 60 molecules per minute and its mean half-life is ten minutes under a specified condition. In the idealized model its steady abundance is 60/0.0693, or approximately 866 molecules. If an RNA-binding protein stabilizes the RNA sufficiently to double its half-life without changing transcription, the new first-order loss constant is approximately 0.0347 per minute. The new steady abundance is approximately 1,731 molecules. The same doubling could have been achieved by doubling the synthesis rate and leaving the loss constant unchanged. A measurement of abundance alone cannot distinguish these explanations. A measurement of newly synthesized RNA or a separate lifetime estimate is needed.

This model has limitations that matter when comparing RNA classes. A precursor may be lost from a particular assay because it matures into another RNA rather than because it is degraded. An assembled ribosomal RNA may have a different probability of turnover from an unassembled one. During growth, dilution by cell division can contribute to the effective loss rate of molecules measured per cell or per unit biomass. Multiple compartments or subpopulations with different lifetimes may produce a curve that is not a single exponential. Transcription can also change in response to an experimental perturbation. The equation is useful for reasoning with explicitly stated assumptions; it does not establish that every RNA has a characteristic universal half-life.

In many growing cells, rRNA constitutes a large part of the mass of cellular RNA, and tRNAs are also present in substantial amounts. Messenger RNA is commonly a relatively small part of total RNA mass, but proportions depend strongly on organism, growth conditions, cell type, extraction and the definition of the measured pool. An important conceptual distinction is the difference between molecule count and mass. A large rRNA contains many more nucleotide residues than a single tRNA, so equal numbers of the two molecules do not contribute equal mass. Statements about percentage of total RNA usually describe mass or an assay signal rather than the fraction of molecules.

As an illustrative calculation, consider 1,000 tRNA molecules of average length 75 nucleotides and 100 long RNA molecules of average length 1,500 nucleotides. The short class contains approximately 75,000 residues and the long class contains 150,000 residues. If average residue masses and detection efficiencies are similar, the long class accounts for two thirds of their combined nucleotide mass even though it accounts for only one eleventh of the molecule count. A student should identify which quantity a question measures before deciding which RNA population is more abundant. This issue also arises in sequencing and gel analysis, where the relationship between the measured signal and number of original molecules depends on the method.

8. Ribosomal RNA as the structural core of a molecular machine

A ribosome must solve a problem that a message alone cannot solve. The nucleotide sequence specifies an order of amino acids, but it does not physically align amino groups and carboxyl groups to form a peptide bond. The ribosome contains an extended arrangement of RNA and protein that positions the message, adaptor molecules and their attached chemical groups. Its RNAs occupy central positions in that organization. The small ribosomal subunit supports recognition of the message and matching of the correct adaptor. The large subunit contains the region in which peptide bonds are formed. The two subunits interact to coordinate these tasks but have distinguishable structural contributions.

In the standard bacterial example, the small subunit contains 16S rRNA together with proteins. The large subunit contains 23S and 5S rRNAs together with proteins. The small and large subunits are conventionally called 30S and 50S, and their assembled form is called a 70S ribosome. These labels refer to sedimentation behavior. In a typical eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosome, the small subunit is 40S and contains 18S rRNA. The large subunit is 60S and contains 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNAs in the conventional mammalian description. The assembled cytoplasmic ribosome is 80S. Some eukaryotes use a different size designation for the homolog of mammalian 28S rRNA, such as 25S in budding yeast. The purpose of the conventional names is to identify homologous components, not to assert identical lengths in all species.

The rRNA of a small subunit is not equivalent to an independent small ribosome. The 16S rRNA is only one constituent of a bacterial 30S particle. Similarly 23S is an RNA component within the large subunit, not the sedimentation label for the entire subunit. This distinction is frequently obscured by the reuse of the letter S in both contexts. A particle's sedimentation behavior reflects its entire physical structure, whereas the rRNA names refer to individual molecules characterized historically by sedimentation. A mutation in a 16S rRNA gene may therefore affect the assembly or function of a 30S subunit, but it is not a mutation in a hypothetical gene encoding the whole 30S particle.

Why do the numerical labels not add up? The sedimentation coefficient s is defined as the sedimentation velocity v divided by the applied centrifugal acceleration a. Its SI unit is seconds, and one Svedberg, abbreviated S, equals 10⁻¹³ seconds. At a given angular velocity ω and radial distance r, a = ω²r. In a simplified steady sedimentation model, the driving force on a particle depends on its mass corrected for buoyancy, and the frictional force is proportional to its velocity. Thus s is proportional to buoyant mass divided by a friction coefficient. Both mass and friction can change when separate molecular particles assemble. This means that sedimentation coefficients cannot be added as if they were molecular weights.

For an idealized particle in a centrifuge, suppose the acceleration at the location of interest is 10⁶ metres per second squared. A 70S particle has s = 70 × 10⁻¹³ seconds, so its local sedimentation speed in the simple model is v = sa = 7 × 10⁻⁶ metres per second, or seven micrometres per second. A 50S particle under the same conditions would have a speed of five micrometres per second. These values are illustrative and omit diffusion, changing radius, solvent corrections and other complications of actual sedimentation analysis. They make the unit physically meaningful and explain why 30S plus 50S can produce a 70S particle rather than an 80S particle.

The small subunit rRNA folds to create surfaces that organize interactions between mRNA and tRNA. A prominent function of the bacterial 16S rRNA is participation in the decoding region, where the structure of a codon–anticodon interaction is assessed. Its 3′ region also contains a sequence complementary to the Shine–Dalgarno element in many bacterial messages. Base pairing between these RNA elements can assist in positioning an initiation region. Not every bacterial gene uses a recognizable Shine–Dalgarno sequence, and this recognition principle should not be applied as a universal description of eukaryotic cytoplasmic initiation. The common lesson is that the sequence of a ribosomal RNA can directly contribute to recognition of a message.

In a hypothetical reporter experiment, replacing several bases in a bacterial ribosome-binding sequence reduces protein output while the message concentration remains unchanged. One explanation is that the RNA no longer forms the expected recognition interaction with the small subunit. Restoring the potential pairing through a compatible alteration in the interacting rRNA region would strengthen that interpretation if translation recovered. A well-designed comparison would also examine whether the altered rRNA can assemble into a small subunit and whether it supports expression of control messages. Otherwise a failure to recover protein synthesis might reflect a general defect in the ribosome rather than the recognition contact being tested. This is a conceptual experimental inference, not a claim that every possible rRNA alteration is biologically tolerated.

The large subunit is organized around rRNA domains that bring the amino acid attachment regions of tRNAs into suitable positions. In bacteria the peptidyl transferase center is formed predominantly by 23S rRNA; its eukaryotic cytoplasmic counterpart lies in the homologous large-subunit rRNA. It is important to distinguish a catalytic contribution from a structural one, but in a folded RNA these contributions can be intimately related. A precise RNA arrangement can create the environment in which substrates approach in a productive orientation. The existence of a catalytic RNA center does not remove the need for ribosomal proteins, which are required for normal assembly, stability and function of the intact cellular apparatus.

9. Experimental evidence for the RNA nature of the ribosomal core

For much of the development of molecular biology, the fact that ribosomes contained large amounts of RNA did not settle whether RNA was directly responsible for peptide bond formation. RNA might have been a passive framework supporting a conventional protein enzyme, or it might have formed a functional active center. Distinguishing these possibilities required evidence about the location of substrates and the identities of the molecular groups around them. Biochemical studies established that the large subunit contains the peptidyl transferase activity, while structural studies made it possible to identify the components surrounding the reaction site.

In 2000, work by Nenad Ban and colleagues provided a high-resolution structure of the large ribosomal subunit of an archaeon. A companion study by Poul Nissen and colleagues examined the relationship of substrate analogs to its peptidyl transferase center. The arrangements placed the relevant substrates in a region surrounded by conserved rRNA rather than by the protein side chains expected for a conventional protein active site. Together with biochemical evidence, these findings strongly supported the conclusion that the ribosome is an RNA-based catalyst. The result changed the way scientists described ribosomal RNA: it is not merely an abundant structural constituent but an essential participant in the chemistry of protein synthesis.

Structural observations have a particular strength and limitation. They can reveal proximity and geometry at a resolution sufficient to discriminate RNA from protein components. However, a static structure does not by itself reveal the complete sequence of transient events during catalysis. Nor can all effects of a substitution be assigned to direct participation in chemistry. A mutation can distort local geometry, disrupt the folding of a domain or prevent formation of a stable particle. Experimental interpretation therefore benefits from combining structural data with activity measurements and controls for assembly. The conclusion that RNA dominates the catalytic center is stronger than a claim that every conserved nucleotide in the center has an identical chemical role.

A classic examination scenario is a pair of assembled ribosomal preparations containing different substitutions. Preparation X has normal rRNA content and normal association of the two subunits but supports very little peptide bond formation. Preparation Y has little assembled large subunit and also produces very little peptide. Both show reduced protein synthesis, but the simplest immediate explanations differ. The defect in X may affect the functional center or substrate positioning, while the defect in Y may primarily affect assembly or stability. It would be premature to assign both substitutions directly to catalytic chemistry. The comparison teaches that a shared physiological endpoint does not establish a shared molecular mechanism.

Evidence for rRNA sequence conservation also has evolutionary significance. The main tasks of decoding and peptide bond formation must occur in diverse cells. Regions of rRNA important for these functions are often conserved in sequence or structure, while other regions tolerate greater variation. Small-subunit rRNA sequences are consequently useful for comparing the evolutionary relationships of organisms, particularly bacterial and archaeal 16S rRNA. A conserved stretch can be used as a reference across groups, and differences in other stretches provide information for classification. Sequence similarity is evidence to interpret using appropriate evolutionary methods; a single rRNA sequence comparison does not fully describe the organism's genome, physiology or pathogenic potential.

The presence of an RNA catalyst in an essential cellular machine is also compatible with hypotheses that RNA played a more central role at early stages of life's evolution. Modern ribosomes demonstrate that RNA can participate directly in fundamental chemical reactions, but they do not constitute a preserved recording of the first living system. The RNA world is a historical evolutionary hypothesis supported by several lines of evidence, not an experimentally observed ancient organism. The distinction between present-day function and historical inference prevents the student from confusing plausible evolutionary explanations with direct evidence about the past.

10. The nucleolus and the production of ribosomal RNA

The requirement for a large supply of ribosomes has consequences for the organization of the genome. A growing cell must produce enough ribosomal RNA to assemble new particles, and a single copy of an rRNA gene may not provide enough transcriptional capacity. Eukaryotic genomes commonly contain repeated ribosomal DNA units, many of which can be transcribed in the nucleolus. The nucleolus is a nuclear region organized around active rRNA synthesis, processing and ribosomal particle assembly. It is not surrounded by a lipid membrane. Its identity arises from the concentration of relevant macromolecules and their interactions, rather than from an organelle membrane that encloses a fixed collection of enzymes.

In the mammalian example, polymerase I transcribes a large precursor conventionally designated 47S, which is processed through intermediate forms often including 45S. The precursor contains the sequences that ultimately become 18S, 5.8S and 28S rRNAs, separated and flanked by transcribed spacer sequences. Cleavage and trimming remove spacers while the future mature domains fold, acquire modifications and associate with proteins. A single precursor can therefore provide one molecule of each of these three mature rRNAs if processing is complete. The 5S rRNA is produced separately, normally by polymerase III. It joins the developing large subunit through a distinct route. The observation that 5S rRNA is part of the large subunit does not imply that it is cut from the large polymerase I precursor.

In bacteria, the corresponding common pattern is a ribosomal operon containing sequences for 16S, 23S and 5S rRNAs, often accompanied by additional spacer sequences and sometimes tRNA genes. The operon produces an RNA precursor that must be processed. It is possible for the precursor to contain sequences belonging to two different classes of functional RNA because the final products are separated by maturation. It would be misleading to infer that a precursor containing a tRNA segment can be used as an intact transfer RNA. Its boundaries, terminal structure and fold may not yet be appropriate for the adaptor function. Similar reasoning applies to the ribosomal segments: detecting their sequences is not equivalent to detecting assembled ribosomes.

The production of rRNA and the formation of ribosomal particles must be coordinated with the supply of ribosomal proteins. In a eukaryotic cell, the proteins are synthesized on cytoplasmic ribosomes and imported into the nucleus, where they can associate with newly produced rRNAs and assembly factors. Particles resembling immature small and large subunits are formed and subsequently exported. Additional maturation can occur after export. The major location of a molecule's synthesis is therefore not necessarily its major site of function. A mature cytoplasmic ribosome contains rRNAs that were transcribed and processed in the nucleus, proteins synthesized in the cytoplasm and components assembled through interactions spanning both compartments.

This distinction can be tested with labelled RNA precursors. If a short pulse labels newly synthesized rRNA, one may initially detect label in larger precursor molecules and later in mature rRNA bands as processing proceeds. If a particular cleavage step is impaired, an upstream precursor may accumulate while a downstream product decreases. Such a pattern is consistent with defective maturation, but it is not sufficient by itself to name the responsible nuclease or guide. Additional information from the positions of probe-binding sites is helpful. A probe directed against a transcribed spacer can identify precursor RNA containing that spacer, whereas a probe in the mature 18S sequence can detect both the mature rRNA and its precursors. The different probes answer different questions about the same sequence of events.

Consider an idealized cell that produces 200 complete precursor molecules per minute. If all are successfully processed into one 18S, one 5.8S and one 28S molecule, the production rate of each mature RNA is 200 molecules per minute, before losses. Suppose that only 150 mature 18S molecules per minute become available for productive assembly, whereas the large-subunit rRNAs and 5S remain available at 200 molecules per minute. Under the assumption that there are no stored pools and that every functional ribosome requires one mature 18S, the long-term rate of producing complete ribosomes cannot exceed 150 per minute. The surplus large-subunit material does not compensate for the shortage of small-subunit rRNA. The result illustrates a general principle of assembling a multicomponent complex: the limiting component determines the maximum number of complete machines.

11. Transfer RNA: the physical adaptor between two chemical languages

The message and the amino acids use different alphabets. A codon consists of three nucleotides, whereas an amino acid is recognized through chemical features such as an amino group, a carboxyl group and a side chain. There is no general direct chemical correspondence that allows every amino acid to recognize its own codon on an mRNA. A molecular adaptor provides a solution. One region of the adaptor can participate in nucleotide sequence recognition while another region can carry an amino acid. Transfer RNA performs this function in cellular protein synthesis. Its structure enables a relationship between a nucleotide triplet and a chemically dissimilar molecule without requiring the message itself to bind and distinguish all the amino acid side chains.

This arrangement implies two independent recognition problems. A tRNA must be associated with the correct amino acid, and it must interact appropriately with a codon when it enters the decoding apparatus. These two functions are related by the structure and identity of the tRNA, but they are not identical. The enzyme that attaches an amino acid to a tRNA recognizes features of the tRNA and of its amino acid substrate. The ribosome primarily examines the interaction between the message and the anticodon, in the context of the translating apparatus. A tRNA bearing an inappropriate amino acid can therefore create an error even if the codon–anticodon interaction is correct. The detailed charging enzymes and fidelity mechanisms belong to the subsequent translation chapter; the structural separation of recognition sites explains why those mechanisms are needed.

Early protein synthesis experiments distinguished a relatively small soluble RNA fraction from larger ribosomal RNA. The soluble molecules could become associated with amino acids and contribute to transfer of these amino acids into growing polypeptides. This evidence supported the adaptor hypothesis, but understanding the adaptor required a sequence and a structural model. In 1965 Robert Holley and colleagues reported the nucleotide sequence of yeast alanine tRNA. Their work made it possible to relate a defined primary sequence to a pattern of internal complementary segments and ultimately to a cloverleaf representation. The achievement was important not just because it supplied a long sequence of letters, but because it connected the letters to the physical requirements of an RNA adaptor.

Many mature cytoplasmic tRNAs contain approximately 75 to 90 nucleotides, although some are shorter or longer. The cloverleaf description summarizes their conventional secondary structure. An acceptor stem joins residues near the 5′ and 3′ ends of the chain. The D arm contains a stem and loop often enriched in dihydrouridine modifications. The anticodon arm includes a loop in which three residues form the anticodon. The T arm is traditionally named for its TΨC motif, referring to ribothymidine, pseudouridine and cytidine in common examples. A variable region occurs between the anticodon and T arms and differs in length among different tRNAs. The names describe structural positions and characteristic features rather than immutable nucleotide compositions shared by every tRNA in every lineage.

The acceptor stem and the 3′ terminal region deserve particular attention. The final three residues of the mature amino acid-accepting tRNA are conventionally CCA. In some organisms and genes these residues are encoded by the tRNA gene; in others they are supplied or completed after transcription by a CCA-adding enzyme. The terminal adenosine provides a ribose hydroxyl group through which an amino acid can be linked by an ester bond. Depending on the charging enzyme and subsequent rearrangements, the linkage can involve the 2′ or 3′ hydroxyl of that ribose. The CCA sequence is a common structural feature of mature functional tRNAs, not the sequence that distinguishes one amino acid from another. A student should therefore not confuse the universal attachment region with the variable anticodon region.

The discriminator base is situated just before CCA at the conventional position 73. Its identity can contribute to recognition of a tRNA by the correct aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase. Other identity elements may occur in the acceptor stem, the anticodon region or elsewhere. The importance of a particular element varies with the tRNA class and organism. For example, a characteristic base pair in an acceptor stem can provide an important identity signal even though it lies far from the anticodon. This makes tRNA recognition more sophisticated than an enzyme merely reading the anticodon and choosing an amino acid. There are biological situations in which altering the anticodon need not change the principal charging identity of the molecule.

The anticodon is usually positioned at residues 34, 35 and 36 in the standard tRNA numbering convention. The first of these when written from 5′ to 3′, position 34, pairs with the third position of the codon in the ordinary antiparallel alignment. It is often called the wobble position because particular bases and modifications there can permit recognition of more than one synonymous codon. The central and remaining position of the anticodon pair with the other codon positions. The relation is easiest to establish by writing the two sequences with directions rather than memorizing an isolated triplet. For the mRNA codon 5′-GCU-3′, an exactly Watson–Crick complementary anticodon is 3′-CGA-5′, or 5′-AGC-3′ when written in the usual sequence direction.

An anticodon is not a codon. It identifies a potential recognition partner through complementarity, and the orientation of that complementarity matters. A question that gives the anticodon 5′-CAU-3′, for instance, is describing the reverse complement of the codon 5′-AUG-3′. Simply replacing each letter with its complement while keeping the order of the printed sequence would produce the wrong orientation. It is also essential to state whether the question assumes standard Watson–Crick pairing or permits wobble. The existence of tRNAs that recognize several codons does not mean that anticodon–codon pairing is arbitrary. The accepted recognition patterns depend on physical geometry and nucleotide modifications.

12. Three-dimensional tRNA structure and biological consequences

The familiar cloverleaf drawing can be misleading if it is interpreted literally as the shape of the entire molecule in space. The paired regions form helices, and interactions between distant parts of the chain arrange those helices into a compact three-dimensional structure. In the canonical tRNA fold, the acceptor stem and T arm stack approximately along one axis, while the D and anticodon arms form the other main axis. Contacts between the D and T loops help form the elbow of the molecule. The result resembles a bent L rather than a flat leaf. The anticodon and amino acid attachment site lie near opposite ends of the structure, an arrangement appropriate for contacting the separate regions of a ribosome that recognize the message and organize peptide formation.

The folded geometry gives functional meaning to sequences far from either of these terminal regions. A mutation in a D-loop residue could leave the anticodon triplet and CCA sequence unchanged but disrupt tertiary contacts. The molecule might still possess the correct parts on paper while failing to place them in the appropriate spatial relationship. It might also be less stable or interact inefficiently with binding partners. This is a general lesson about functional RNA: conservation of a sequence motif is not sufficient to establish the integrity of the whole molecule. It is necessary to distinguish the presence of a recognition sequence from the availability of that sequence in a working conformation.

The nucleotides in mature tRNAs are unusually rich in chemical modifications. Common examples include pseudouridine, dihydrouridine, methylated bases and inosine in particular tRNA contexts. Pseudouridine is an isomer of uridine with a different linkage of the base to the sugar, and its chemical properties can influence local structure. Dihydrouridine changes the properties of the uracil ring and is often associated with flexible structural regions. Inosine can arise from deamination of adenosine in certain tRNAs and can expand recognition possibilities at suitable anticodon positions. These examples illustrate that a sequence written only with the four ordinary RNA letters is a useful starting description but may not capture the full chemistry of the mature molecule.

The functions of modified residues depend on their positions. A modification near the anticodon may influence the geometry of base pairing or the maintenance of a reading frame. A modification within the folded core may contribute to stability or recognition by another molecule. The same type of chemical change can therefore have different consequences in different RNAs. It is inappropriate to conclude that all tRNA modifications simply increase stability or that all modifications broaden codon recognition. Instead, one should ask which nucleotide is modified, which interactions are affected and what evidence establishes the functional outcome.

There are important departures from the canonical tRNA shape. Some organellar tRNAs, particularly those in certain mitochondrial genomes, have reduced or unusual arms. They operate with corresponding adaptations in the molecular apparatus. Their existence illustrates that the standard cloverleaf is a broadly conserved solution rather than a rigid definition of every functional tRNA. A structure-based classification should look for the combination of an amino acid attachment region, decoding function and compatible molecular partners. A diagram of an unusual tRNA should not be rejected solely because one loop differs from the common bacterial or cytoplasmic form.

The distinction between initiator and elongator tRNAs provides another example of how function depends on identity elements in addition to codon recognition. In bacterial protein synthesis, the initiator tRNA for methionine carries a specially modified amino acid, formylmethionine, in the ordinary initiation pathway. Eukaryotic cytoplasmic initiation ordinarily uses an initiator methionine tRNA that is not formylated. A separate elongator methionine tRNA supplies methionine at internal codons. The two tRNA populations can recognize the same AUG triplet but have structural features and interacting proteins that direct them to different stages of protein synthesis. The anticodon alone cannot tell which stage a tRNA is adapted to serve.

A set of tRNAs associated with the same amino acid can contain several anticodon variants. Such tRNAs are known as isoacceptors when they accept the same amino acid and differ in anticodon or related characteristics. The relationship between codon number and tRNA number is consequently not one of simple equality. Several codons can be synonymous, and some individual tRNAs can recognize multiple codons. Conversely, different tRNA genes or sequence variants may contribute to the same decoding task. A count of tRNA genes in a genome is therefore not a direct count of amino acid species or codon types. Detailed codon assignments and wobble rules are developed in the translation sequence, but the reason for this non-equivalence already follows from the structure and adaptor function of tRNA.

13. Formation and maintenance of the mature tRNA pool

A tRNA gene commonly produces a precursor whose ends must be processed. The 5′ leader can be removed by RNase P, and additional activities trim the 3′ end. If the CCA sequence is not completely provided by the gene, it is added by an appropriate enzyme. Selected tRNAs have introns that are removed by pathways distinct from the ordinary spliceosomal maturation of nuclear pre-mRNA. Base modifications and folding further contribute to the functional product. A mature tRNA can therefore differ chemically and in its boundaries from the original transcript, even where its basic nucleotide identity derives from the same gene.

RNase P is an instructive example of a processing activity whose identity must not be confused with the identity of its substrate. In many bacteria, the RNA component of RNase P provides catalytic activity and acts with a protein partner in the cellular complex. The tRNA precursor is the substrate of this reaction. Finding a catalytic RNA associated with production of tRNA does not make every tRNA a ribozyme. In some organisms and cellular compartments there are protein-only forms of RNase P, emphasizing that a conserved processing requirement can be met by different molecular arrangements. The full comparison of RNA catalysis is developed in the dedicated ribozyme node; here the important distinction is between the mature adaptor and the machinery that produces it.

After maturation, nuclear-encoded eukaryotic tRNAs must reach the cellular locations where they function. Many are exported to the cytoplasm through selective pathways that recognize properties of mature or suitably processed molecules. Their distribution is not simply determined by the diffusion of small molecules across an unrestricted boundary. An export defect can therefore leave the overall cellular amount of a tRNA relatively unchanged while reducing the usable cytoplasmic pool. In some eukaryotes tRNAs also undergo regulated transport in the opposite direction. Location is part of function, and an RNA that has the correct sequence and structure may still be ineffective if it is in the wrong compartment.

The usable pool has another important property: not all mature tRNA molecules necessarily carry amino acids at a given moment. The charged fraction depends on attachment, utilization and other processes that influence the availability of amino acids and the molecular machinery. Imagine that an assay detects 1,000 molecules of a particular mature tRNA, but only 250 are aminoacylated. The charged fraction is 25 percent. A perturbation that raises the total abundance to 1,500 molecules while lowering the charged fraction to 10 percent would yield 150 charged molecules. Thus total tRNA abundance can rise while the immediate supply of amino acid-bearing adaptors decreases. This distinction is useful when interpreting the effect of nutrient stress on protein synthesis.

A mutation that damages CCA formation can reduce amino acid attachment without necessarily reducing transcription of the tRNA gene. A mutation that disrupts an important tertiary contact could increase the fraction of transcripts degraded before reaching the mature pool. A modification defect might allow production of stable mature molecules that are inefficient in decoding. These are mechanistically distinct failure points. Experiments that measure RNA quantity alone do not discriminate among them. Measurements of mature boundaries, folding, aminoacylation and translation output are complementary because they test different requirements. This is a useful general framework for reasoning about functional RNA defects without automatically assigning every abnormality to transcription.

14. Small nuclear RNAs: recognizing the boundaries of a message

A nuclear precursor mRNA in an organism with spliceosomal introns presents a particularly interesting recognition problem. The molecule contains both the sequences destined to remain in the mature message and sequences that must be removed. The correct boundaries cannot usually be determined by counting a fixed number of residues from one end of the transcript. Introns vary in length, and the signals marking their boundaries are relatively short. A molecular apparatus must combine recognition of several features, place appropriate parts of the precursor near one another and perform reactions that preserve the intended exon connections. Small nuclear RNAs are essential components of the major apparatus responsible for this task.

The name small nuclear RNA describes a group of relatively short RNAs associated with nuclear functions. The spliceosomal examples are named U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6 in the major spliceosome. Each has characteristic structural features and associates with proteins to form one or more small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particles, usually abbreviated snRNPs. A snRNA and its corresponding snRNP are not exactly the same thing. The RNA is one component; the particle includes its associated proteins. This distinction is important when examining an experiment that removes proteins from a cellular extract. The presence of an intact snRNA does not necessarily mean that an intact functional snRNP remains.

The roles of these RNAs can be understood as changes in molecular partnerships rather than as five independent enzymes acting sequentially. At an early stage of recognition, U1 snRNA can form base pairs with a region near a typical 5′ splice site. This creates an initial association between the precursor and the recognition apparatus. The complementarity is not the sole source of splice-site specificity because proteins and other parts of the precursor contribute to the decision. Nevertheless, the possibility of RNA–RNA pairing explains how a short sequence in one molecule can identify a region in a much longer RNA. A change that reduces the quality of this interaction can impair recognition without directly changing the chemistry of the eventual exon-joining reaction.

U2 snRNA interacts with the region around the branch site of the intron. The interaction helps present the branch-point adenosine in a geometry suitable for the first reaction. The branch adenosine need not simply disappear inside a continuous perfectly paired duplex. Its reactive ribose hydroxyl must be positioned in the assembly. This is an example of the functional significance of an unpaired residue within a structured RNA interaction. A loop, bulge or exposed nucleotide can be essential precisely because it is not hidden in a regular stem. The structure is designed to make a selected chemical group available.

The U4, U5 and U6 particles participate in rearrangements that convert the early recognition apparatus into an active spliceosome. U4 is associated with U6 through RNA base pairing before catalytic activation, and this association restrains U6 from forming certain interactions required in the active state. During activation, U4 is released and U6 can establish new relationships with the intron and U2. U5 contributes to positioning the exon ends through interactions involving an exposed region of its RNA. The working complex therefore has to change its network of RNA contacts. A mutation that locks an initially useful contact in place can prevent progression to the next stage even when the early complex forms normally.

U2 and U6 form an RNA-centered catalytic arrangement in the activated spliceosome. Their contributions help organize reactive groups and metal-dependent chemistry. The presence of proteins throughout the spliceosome should not obscure the roles of these RNAs in the working center. At the same time, the conclusion is not that each isolated snRNA can remove an intron on its own. Spliceosomal catalysis depends on the assembled and correctly rearranged complex. This is similar in principle to the catalytic RNA organization of the ribosome: the RNA-based center is a property of a molecular system, not an assertion that all protein components are dispensable.

The two reactions of ordinary spliceosomal splicing have already been treated as part of pre-mRNA processing. Here their relevance is the way RNA interactions support the choice and positioning of reacting groups. The branch-point ribose hydroxyl attacks the phosphate at the 5′ splice site, producing a branched intron-containing intermediate and an upstream exon with an available 3′ hydroxyl. That hydroxyl then participates in the reaction at the 3′ splice site, joining the exons. The characteristic lariat is associated with the intron, not a permanent part of the joined mature exons. A failure to make the lariat and a failure to join exons after lariat formation can be distinguished experimentally because they affect different intermediates.

ATP-dependent remodeling activities contribute to the establishment and rearrangement of spliceosomal contacts. This should not be confused with the energetics of the transesterification reactions themselves. The exchange of phosphodiester linkages does not require a new nucleotide to be polymerized at every junction. Energy consumption in the complete pathway supports assembly, structural transitions and directionality of the molecular cycle. A mutant that affects ATP-dependent remodeling may therefore accumulate a recognizable intermediate even when the RNA groups capable of participating in bond exchange are chemically unaltered. Exam reasoning becomes clearer when the requirement for making a bond is distinguished from the requirement for reorganizing the machinery that makes it.

Not every intron in every organism is removed by the major spliceosome. Some eukaryotes have a minor spliceosome that uses alternative small nuclear RNA components for a particular intron class, and other RNA processing reactions use different systems. The standard U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6 set is a model for the major spliceosome. Ordinary bacteria do not use the canonical nuclear spliceosomal pathway as the general mechanism for processing their messages. Some bacterial and organellar introns can be removed by other mechanisms. Finding an intron does not by itself establish that a U1-containing nuclear particle is involved.

15. Testing the functions of small nuclear RNAs

RNA–RNA recognition offers predictions that can be examined experimentally. Imagine that a sequence near the 5′ splice site is altered so that it no longer pairs well with a complementary region in U1 snRNA. A reduction in splicing could result from the disruption of this interaction, but it could also result from the removal of a protein-binding site or an unrelated change in folding. If a second mutation in U1 restores potential complementarity to the altered precursor, selective restoration of splicing would support a direct recognition role. The most informative experiment compares all four combinations of normal and altered precursor with normal and altered U1. The pattern of interactions matters more than the effect of either mutation alone.

For example, in a hypothetical reporter system the fraction of RNA correctly spliced is 90 percent for normal precursor with normal U1, 12 percent for mutant precursor with normal U1, 16 percent for normal precursor with mutant U1 and 75 percent for the combination of complementary mutants. The two single changes impair processing while the compatible double change partly restores it. The pattern supports a requirement for an interaction involving the two tested regions. It is still not evidence that no protein contacts these sequences or that the restored interaction exactly reproduces the original structure. The partial restoration may reflect changes in stacking, protein recognition or competing molecular interactions.

The same principle can help distinguish a recognition defect from an assembly defect. A mutation in a U1 region responsible for association with a protein partner may reduce the amount of functional U1 particle in the extract. In that case, restoring potential base pairing to a precursor need not restore splicing because the mutant RNA may not be present in a usable complex. Measuring the amount of U1 RNA, the amount associated with its normal protein partners and its association with the precursor would provide different pieces of information. The presence of the RNA in the cell is a prerequisite for its action but not proof of its proper assembly.

An RNA detected in the nucleus is not automatically a spliceosomal RNA. Localization alone is particularly uninformative in the nucleus because the compartment contains many precursors, newly produced functional RNAs, RNP assembly intermediates and molecules awaiting export. A candidate snRNA can be characterized through its association with a particular RNP, its effects on splicing after specific perturbation and evidence for interactions with recognized splicing substrates. The name of the class becomes meaningful when localization agrees with these functional properties. Conversely, the transient presence of a spliceosomal RNA outside its principal working compartment during aspects of biogenesis does not invalidate its classification.

The transcriptional origin of spliceosomal RNAs further illustrates the difference between a gene's transcription and its eventual function. In vertebrates, major spliceosomal U1, U2, U4 and U5 RNAs are usually polymerase II products, whereas U6 is a polymerase III product. Their maturation routes and protein associations differ in important respects. An intervention that selectively impairs one nuclear polymerase can therefore affect the components of the same working apparatus differently. It cannot be assumed that all RNA molecules in a particular complex must have been produced by the same polymerase. This principle also applies to the rRNAs in a eukaryotic ribosome.

Consider a hypothetical short exposure to a selective inhibitor of polymerase III. Newly synthesized U6 decreases while synthesis of U1 remains relatively unaffected. The amount of mature U6 may initially change very little if existing particles are stable. A defect in spliceosome function may arise only after the pool of usable U6 declines. The result would be consistent with the usual polymerase assignments, but a long-term reduction in other RNAs could reflect indirect cellular stress or secondary responses. Short-term synthesis measurements and later functional measurements answer related but distinct questions. The time of observation is therefore an essential variable in interpreting such experiments.

16. Small nucleolar RNAs: guiding modification and maturation

The production of ribosomal RNA presents a different recognition problem. A long rRNA precursor contains many potential reaction sites, only some of which require particular chemical modifications. A modifying enzyme could recognize each site using an independently evolved protein surface, but an adaptable guide RNA system offers another solution. An RNA guide can bind a complementary region of a target molecule while associated proteins perform a particular class of reaction. Changing the guide sequence can change the target without necessarily changing the enzyme's basic chemical activity. Small nucleolar RNAs exemplify this division of labor.

Many snoRNAs are concentrated in the nucleolus, where much of eukaryotic rRNA processing and assembly takes place. They function in snoRNPs containing RNA and associated proteins. The two best-known guide families are called box C/D snoRNAs and box H/ACA snoRNAs. Their names refer to characteristic conserved sequence motifs and associated structural arrangements. Their target-recognition regions differ from one guide to another, while conserved structural features contribute to the assembly of the appropriate type of RNP. The distinction between a conserved assembly motif and a variable guide region is central to understanding why a common reaction system can act at many selected locations.

Box C/D guide snoRNPs commonly direct 2′-O-methylation of selected ribose groups. The methyl group is added to the oxygen of a ribose hydroxyl, rather than replacing the entire nucleotide or changing the Watson–Crick identity of the base. The associated methyltransferase, fibrillarin in typical eukaryotic complexes, supplies the catalytic activity and uses a methyl donor. The guide RNA aligns the target with the modifying apparatus. An ordinary nucleotide sequence readout may therefore be unchanged even though the ribose chemistry at a specific position has been altered. Detecting the sequence of a target RNA and detecting its modification state are different analytical tasks.

A common structural rule is that the target nucleotide to be methylated pairs with the guide residue five nucleotides upstream of the D or D′ box. This relationship provides a way of positioning the target relative to the RNP, rather than requiring every modified rRNA site to contain the same nucleotide sequence. Consider a simplified guide segment with ten residues numbered 1 through 10 followed immediately by a D box. Counting backwards from the beginning of the box makes residue 10 the first upstream residue, residue 9 the second and residue 6 the fifth. The target paired to residue 6 is the predicted modification site in the canonical guide model. The counting convention is important; merely selecting the fifth nucleotide from the start of the guide segment would give a different result.

The actual formation of a guide–target duplex is antiparallel, as with other RNA base-pairing interactions. A sequence written 5′ to 3′ on the guide cannot be aligned in the same direction as a complementary target written 5′ to 3′. If the target sequence is written in its conventional direction, the predicted guide partner must be found by reversing and complementing the appropriate stretch. The geometry of the complex matters in addition to complementarity, so the canonical positioning rule cannot by itself prove that a computationally predicted interaction is used in living cells. The presence of a suitable sequence match and a properly positioned motif supplies a hypothesis to test.

Box H/ACA snoRNPs commonly direct pseudouridylation. Pseudouridine is an isomer of uridine, in which the uracil base is connected to the ribose through a carbon–carbon linkage rather than the ordinary nitrogen–carbon glycosidic linkage of uridine. The nucleotide remains part of the same RNA chain; it is not removed and replaced by a newly polymerized sequence. This conversion changes chemical and structural properties, including opportunities for interactions involving the base. In a typical eukaryotic H/ACA snoRNP, dyskerin or its homolog provides the catalytic activity. The guide RNA supplies a structural pocket that aligns an appropriate uridine with the reaction center.

The H/ACA family has a different guide arrangement from the C/D family. A typical eukaryotic example contains hairpin regions separated by a hinge and followed by a tail. Portions of a hairpin can pair with sequences on either side of the target region. The selected uridine is exposed in the guide pocket rather than incorporated into an uninterrupted duplex across the entire site. This geometry helps make the target accessible for modification. Thus a partially unpaired segment is again functionally meaningful. It would be incorrect to assume that the strongest possible continuous duplex is always the most useful guide arrangement. A reaction requires the target to be positioned and chemically accessible, not simply bound as tightly as possible.

Chemical modification can affect how an rRNA folds, interacts with proteins and functions within the ribosome. The contribution of any particular modification depends on its location. Loss of a single modification may produce a small, condition-dependent phenotype, whereas loss of multiple modifications or a modification important for a central interaction may have larger consequences. The mere abundance of modified residues in rRNA does not imply that each individually has an identical functional importance. A quantitative comparison of assembled particles and their activity is needed to distinguish defects in maturation from changes in the performance of particles that still assemble.

Not every snoRNA is a modification guide. U3 snoRNA, for example, is a well-established participant in early pre-rRNA processing and small-subunit biogenesis. It helps organize interactions required for maturation of the region that becomes 18S rRNA. Treating all box C/D-associated molecules as simple site-specific methylation guides would miss this important exception. Likewise some guides act on other RNA targets, and not every cellular modification depends on a small RNA guide. The standard C/D and H/ACA activities are strong classification clues but should be understood in the context of actual RNP function.

17. Comparing snRNAs and snoRNAs through target and output

The names small nuclear and small nucleolar RNA are similar, but the common examination models emphasize different cellular tasks. The major spliceosomal snRNAs act with a precursor messenger RNA and support removal of introns and joining of exons. Many snoRNAs act with a ribosomal precursor and guide modifications or maturation. Both classes use sequence and structure to recognize other molecules, and both frequently function with proteins. These shared properties do not make their molecular outputs interchangeable. An exon junction is not a ribose methylation, and a pseudouridylated rRNA is not a spliced protein-coding message.

Suppose a mutation removes an essential guide segment from a box C/D snoRNA but leaves its RNP assembly motifs intact. The mutant molecule might still associate with fibrillarin-containing proteins while failing to recognize the intended target. An assay of the target's primary sequence might show no change, yet a modification-specific assay could reveal a loss at the expected position. If assembly motifs are disrupted instead, the RNA itself might be destabilized and several aspects of guide function could be lost. In either case, the immediate molecular defect differs from a mutation in U1 that interferes with a particular splice-site interaction.

A hypothetical experiment compares cells lacking one specific modification guide with matched control cells. In the control, a target rRNA position is modified in 85 percent of mature molecules, whereas the mutant retains modification in 15 percent. The absolute decrease is 70 percentage points, and the relative decrease is approximately 82.4 percent of the control value. If total rRNA abundance remains similar in both conditions, the result is compatible with a modification defect rather than a large loss of mature rRNA. It does not yet prove that the guide is the only possible route to the modification. Residual signal could reflect incomplete depletion, an alternative modifying mechanism or a measurement artifact.

To test whether a phenotype depends on targeting, one could compare reintroduction of the normal guide with reintroduction of a variant unable to recognize the relevant target region, while controlling for abundance and RNP formation of the reintroduced molecules. Selective restoration of modification by the normal guide would strengthen the functional interpretation. If both variants are present at normal amounts but only the correctly targeting RNA restores activity, the difference is difficult to explain by a nonspecific increase in RNA concentration alone. Such comparisons are valuable for RNA biology because sequence complementarity generates precise predictions about molecular interactions.

Another class of small RNAs, called small Cajal body-specific RNAs or scaRNAs, helps illustrate the limits of a strict localization-based classification. Many guide chemical modifications of spliceosomal snRNAs in nuclear bodies associated with RNP maturation. Their targets are not simply the pre-rRNAs typically associated with nucleolar modification guides. Related guide-RNP principles therefore operate in distinct subnuclear locations and can support the production of other functional RNA complexes. A cell's RNA processing apparatus includes RNAs that help prepare other RNAs for their own work. This hierarchical relationship connects the major RNA classes in a way that a list of independent definitions cannot capture.

The functional relationships also predict indirect effects. A defect in maturation of a spliceosomal RNA may compromise splicing of many messages; a defect in a ribosomal RNA maturation guide may compromise the production or performance of ribosomal particles. Both can ultimately lower the synthesis of particular proteins. Measuring only a change in protein output would not discriminate between them. Exam problems often provide more immediate readouts such as exon junctions, precursor accumulation, site-specific modification or subunit abundance. The most informative evidence is the observation closest to the proposed molecular function.

18. Additional functional RNA species and the limits of simple classification

The five RNA classes considered so far are essential landmarks, but they do not exhaust cellular RNA diversity. A particularly instructive example of a guide RNA outside the ordinary spliceosomal and nucleolar systems is found in the mitochondria of certain kinetoplastid protists. Some of their primary transcripts must undergo insertion or deletion of uridines to produce appropriate mature RNA sequences. Guide RNAs provide sequence information that helps define where these changes occur. The guide and the target transcript are distinct molecules; their complementary regions assist in organizing the editing process. The resulting mRNA can then perform its template function. It is the editing machinery, rather than a ribosome reading the guide as a conventional protein-coding message, that uses the information supplied by the guide.

Comparison with snoRNA-mediated modification is useful. In the mitochondrial editing example, the order or number of nucleotide residues in the edited transcript changes. In a canonical C/D snoRNA-directed methylation, a chemical group is added to the ribose at a selected residue without changing the basic nucleotide sequence. Both pathways may use complementary recognition and associated proteins, yet their outputs differ. A comparison of DNA sequence with mature RNA sequence could reveal the insertion or deletion of uridines, whereas methylation may require a measurement sensitive to the altered chemistry. The method needed to detect an RNA-dependent change follows from the nature of that change.

The signal recognition particle, or SRP, provides an example in which RNA participates in the localization of another molecular complex. In a typical mammalian cytoplasmic system, 7SL RNA associates with proteins in SRP. The particle can recognize an emerging signal sequence of an appropriate nascent protein and help direct the ribosome-associated complex toward the endoplasmic reticulum. Bacteria also possess related SRP systems containing RNA, with differences in composition. The RNA here helps organize a targeting complex. It does not carry the instructions for the nascent protein being recognized; those instructions are supplied by the separate mRNA. This relationship distinguishes an RNA involved in the localization of protein synthesis from an RNA that is itself the message for that protein.

The bacterial transfer-messenger RNA, known as tmRNA, shows that biological categories need not be perfectly exclusive. If a ribosome reaches the end of a truncated message without an appropriate stop signal, it may become trapped in an incomplete translation event. In many bacteria, tmRNA participates in rescuing such ribosomes with the help of associated proteins. A part of tmRNA resembles tRNA and can accept an amino acid, while another part supplies a short translated reading frame. The switch permits completion of a tagged product and release of the machinery. The tag can contribute to recognition of the incomplete protein for degradation. Experiments reported by Keiler and colleagues in 1996 helped establish this connection between a special RNA, protein tagging and surveillance of defective messages. The example is included to illustrate multifunctionality, not to replace the later treatment of translation and degradation.

Some additional functional RNAs act chiefly in regulating gene expression rather than constituting the principal message, adaptor or ribosomal core. MicroRNAs and small interfering RNAs can guide protein complexes toward particular RNA targets. Longer noncoding RNAs can participate in varied interactions and cellular processes. These classes are taken up separately because their diversity and regulatory consequences deserve their own treatment. For present purposes, one should recognize that an RNA may supply information directly through complementarity or through a binding surface. Calling the RNA noncoding does not imply that it lacks an encoded sequence or biological information. It means that the relevant established role is not primarily to serve as a template for a protein product.

19. Identifying RNA classes from experimental readouts

The chemical similarity of cellular RNAs means that an investigator must choose observations that distinguish their functions. A denaturing RNA gel can separate molecules according to their electrophoretic behavior, which under suitable conditions is related to length. It can reveal a prominent rRNA population or smaller RNA bands. However, different classes can overlap in size. A short band cannot be identified uniquely as a tRNA simply by comparing it with a length marker. A native gel can reveal differences in folding or association, but these differences can alter migration in ways that are not directly proportional to nucleotide number. A good interpretation separates the question of length from the question of structural or molecular state.

A Northern blot adds sequence specificity by detecting an RNA with a complementary probe. If a probe corresponds to a region retained in a mature RNA and also present in its precursor, both can be detected when the region is accessible under the assay conditions. A second probe against a removed spacer or intron can help identify an intermediate. A mature rRNA band and a larger precursor band may therefore represent successive forms of the same sequence rather than two independently transcribed genes. Similarly, a probe against a particular exon can detect several RNA isoforms, while a probe spanning a particular exon junction can provide information about a particular processed form.

A fractionation experiment asks a different question. Molecules can be separated according to the compartments or molecular particles with which they are associated. If an RNA is found primarily with isolated ribosomal subunits and its sequence corresponds to a known ribosomal RNA, the combination supports its classification. If an RNA is detected with translating ribosomes, however, it might instead be the message or a participating adaptor. An association with ribosomes alone does not prove that the RNA is a structural rRNA. The persistence of the association, the identity of the isolated particle, the sequence and the functional behavior provide additional evidence.

In a hypothetical experiment, one RNA of approximately 80 nucleotides associates transiently with translating particles and can carry an amino acid. Another RNA of about 1,500 nucleotides remains associated with an isolated bacterial small subunit after the removal of ordinary translating messages. A third RNA of several hundred nucleotides varies in abundance with nutrient conditions and directs the formation of a defined polypeptide in an appropriate translation system. The combination of observations identifies the first RNA as a transfer RNA, the second as a plausible 16S ribosomal RNA and the third as a messenger RNA. The classification uses functional evidence along with size rather than treating size as sufficient.

A more complex comparison involves changes in protein output. Suppose four independently perturbed cell populations each produce less of a reporter protein. In the first, the abundance of mature reporter mRNA falls, while its translation efficiency is unchanged. In the second, the message is present normally but the charged pool of a relevant tRNA is reduced. In the third, mature ribosomal subunits are scarce and many different messages are translated poorly. In the fourth, a precursor reporter RNA with an unremoved intron accumulates while correctly joined reporter RNA decreases. These observations indicate different points of failure along the information-processing system. The fourth result suggests a processing defect; the other data implicate, respectively, availability of message, availability of an amino acid-bearing adaptor and availability of the decoding machine. Further measurements would be needed to identify the precise molecular lesions in each case.

These principles are also relevant to biotechnology and comparative biology. A ribosomal RNA probe can help identify bacteria in a mixed population because the probe recognizes a nucleotide sequence rather than a protein product. Its specificity must be evaluated against related sequences and the abundance of the target organism. Differences between bacterial and eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomal architecture also help explain why certain inhibitors of protein synthesis preferentially affect bacterial ribosomes, although organellar similarities and other factors can complicate selectivity. In plants, the existence of protein-only RNase P provides a useful example of how the same essential tRNA maturation reaction can be maintained despite a different molecular apparatus. None of these applications requires treating RNA class identity as a substitute for experimental evidence. They illustrate how a molecule's structure and cellular context determine the biological conclusions that can be drawn from it.

20. A connected view of RNA function

The major RNA classes can now be located in one coherent causal model. Nuclear polymerase II creates many pre-mRNAs that are processed with the help of spliceosomal RNAs. Many mature messages are transported into the cytoplasm in association with proteins. Ribosomal precursor RNAs are synthesized and processed with the aid of nucleolar guide complexes, while tRNA precursors acquire the structures and chemical properties required for amino acid transfer. The mature components converge during cytoplasmic protein synthesis. The message supplies the nucleotide instructions, the adaptors connect those instructions to amino acids and the ribosomal RNAs help create the machinery that decodes and joins the substrates.

The relationship is not entirely linear. Functional ribosomes are needed to produce many of the proteins that assist the maturation of new RNA molecules. Proteins required for assembling spliceosomal or nucleolar particles themselves originate from mRNAs whose maturation may depend on existing RNA processing complexes. A living cell inherits machinery and continuously renews it. This mutual dependence is a feature of an already functioning cellular system rather than a contradiction that requires one of its essential components to arise spontaneously in each generation. Studying the components separately helps identify their roles, while the integrated view explains why a lesion in one component can influence many downstream outputs.

This also explains why the three main questions about an RNA must be kept distinct. Where and how is it made? What structure and molecular partners allow it to work? What changes when its specific function is lost? An RNA can be transcribed in one compartment and function in another; its primary sequence may remain unchanged while maturation alters its chemistry; and its absence can affect a protein that the RNA does not encode. With these distinctions established, the wider noncoding RNA classes can be studied as molecules whose biological activities arise from their own interactions, before turning to catalytic and ligand-responsive RNAs in the next parts of the curriculum.

  1. Original sequence was restored exactly
  2. The paired contact is important
  3. All RNA folding is protein-independent
  4. Every A–U and G–C pair has identical energy
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B — The paired contact is important. A compatible second mutation restores pairing without restoring the original letters. This supports a structural role, not equal free energies or independence from protein.

46. A bacterial reporter's message abundance is normal but its Shine–Dalgarno variant makes little protein. A compatible 16S variant partly rescues expression, and control messages still translate. What is supported?

  1. A sequence-specific RNA recognition contribution
  2. Increased transcription of reporter
  3. A change in amino acid charging identity
  4. Loss of the whole 50S population
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A — A sequence-specific RNA recognition contribution. Rescue through matching interacting regions with preserved controls supports message recognition. Normal RNA and control translation oppose general transcription or ribosome-loss explanations.

47. A newly labelled RNA band contains both an 18S sequence and a spacer. During a chase, spacer signal falls and mature 18S signal rises. Which interpretation fits best?

  1. The spacer is added to mature 18S
  2. The larger RNA can be a precursor undergoing processing
  3. 18S is translated into the spacer
  4. 5S is necessarily derived from the same precursor
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B — The larger RNA can be a precursor undergoing processing. Spacer removal plus mature product appearance supports maturation. It does not show 5S co-transcription or translation of rRNA.

48. A Pol I-specific perturbation reduces newly synthesized large pre-rRNA but not newly synthesized 5S. Why?

  1. 5S is typically a Pol III product
  2. 5S is a Pol I protein
  3. Pol II produces all rRNAs
  4. 5S has no gene
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A — 5S is typically a Pol III product. Separate polymerase origin explains the acute difference. It does not guarantee the later abundance of assembled 5S-containing particles.

49. Normal U1 with a wild-type substrate gives 90% splicing, mutant U1 with wild-type substrate 10%, normal U1 with mutant substrate 15%, and both compatible mutants 80%. Which inference is justified?

  1. No direct RNA recognition can occur
  2. The paired regions likely contribute to recognition
  3. All spliceosomal proteins are dispensable
  4. The mutant precursor is not transcribed
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B — The paired regions likely contribute to recognition. Reciprocal loss and selective double-mutant rescue are evidence for a specific interaction; protein contributions and other structural effects remain possible.

50. A spliceosome perturbation permits accumulation of a lariat–downstream-exon intermediate but prevents joined exons. Which stage is most immediately affected?

  1. Transcription initiation
  2. First transesterification only
  3. Progression to or completion of the second reaction
  4. Amino acid attachment to tRNA
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C — Progression to or completion of the second reaction. Lariat formation means the first reaction can occur. Joined-exon loss points to subsequent positioning or second reaction, not necessarily a defect in a particular named factor.

51. A C/D guide is abundant and associates with its core proteins, but its target loses methylation following a guide-sequence substitution. Which control best supports a targeting defect?

  1. Compare only DNA mass
  2. Restore the complementary target–guide relationship and test modification
  3. Count all total RNA bands without sequence specificity
  4. Measure an unrelated protein only
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B — Restore the complementary target–guide relationship and test modification. Restoring a specific pairing tests the proposed interaction directly. Bulk abundance and unrelated protein readouts cannot distinguish target recognition.

52. A mature rRNA sequence is unchanged, yet a target uridine no longer has its usual isomeric form. Which class of guide is a plausible direct contributor?

  1. H/ACA snoRNA
  2. Ordinary mRNA
  3. U1
  4. A mitochondrial mRNA coding for a ribosomal protein
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A — H/ACA snoRNA. Canonical H/ACA guides pseudouridylation. Sequence retention is compatible with changed chemistry, not necessarily an editing-induced sequence alteration.

53. After removal of a protein partner, an snRNA remains detectable but splicing activity is lost. Which conclusion is warranted?

  1. The RNA was never synthesized
  2. The RNA's presence alone is insufficient for an active RNP
  3. Every snRNA is an mRNA
  4. The DNA sequence must have changed
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B — The RNA's presence alone is insufficient for an active RNP. The RNA can persist without its working protein assembly; functional loss does not prove failed synthesis or mutation.

54. An RNA of 80 nt is detected by a gel. Which observation provides the strongest additional evidence that it is tRNA?

  1. It migrates as a short band
  2. It contains uracil
  3. It accepts an amino acid and shows anticodon-dependent decoding activity
  4. It is extracted from a cell
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C — It accepts an amino acid and shows anticodon-dependent decoding activity. The combined adaptor functions discriminate tRNA; length, base composition and cellular origin alone do not.

55. A mutant displays equal whole-cell tRNA concentration but increased nuclear tRNA and reduced cytoplasmic tRNA. Which immediate explanation fits?

  1. A possible export or localization defect
  2. Certain abolition of all transcription
  3. An increased number of ribosomes proven
  4. Loss of every tRNA gene
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A — A possible export or localization defect. Redistribution with similar overall abundance is compatible with transport or retention. Other mechanisms require additional evidence.

Essential formula sheet

For first-order removal: dM/dt = s − kM; M at steady state = s/k; M(t) = M0 exp(−kt) when synthesis stops; t½ = ln(2)/k. For a sedimenting particle: v = sω²r; one Svedberg = 10⁻¹³ seconds. A coding region of L nucleotides including one stop codon ordinarily encodes L/3 − 1 amino acid residues. Charged tRNA count = total mature tRNA count × charged fraction. Approximate RNA mass contribution is proportional to molecule count × mean nucleotide length when mean residue mass is similar.

Common misconceptions and examination traps

RNA is always unstructured: Single-stranded RNA can form extensive intramolecular helices and tertiary interactions.

All RNAs encode proteins: Functional RNAs such as rRNA and tRNA act as RNA molecules.

Every mRNA has a cap and poly(A) tail: Common eukaryotic architecture has important exceptions, including replication-dependent histone messages.

Transcription +1 equals translation start: They mark separate events; the 5′ UTR can separate them.

Pol II makes only mRNA: Pol II also produces functional noncoding RNAs, including several snRNAs.

30S + 50S = 80S: Sedimentation coefficients depend on mass, buoyancy and friction, so they are not additive.

5S comes from the mammalian large Pol I precursor: 5S is usually a separately synthesized Pol III product.

Cloverleaf is the tertiary shape: It is the secondary-structure representation; the canonical folded tRNA is L-shaped.

The anticodon carries amino acids: The terminal adenosine of the 3′ CCA accepts the amino acid; the anticodon recognizes the codon.

The anticodon alone specifies charging identity: Other identity elements, including acceptor-stem features and discriminator base, can contribute.

All snoRNAs are modification guides: U3 is an important processing-associated snoRNA.

C/D and H/ACA are interchangeable: C/D commonly guides 2′-O-methylation; H/ACA commonly guides pseudouridylation.

A nuclear RNA must function only in the nucleus: Nuclear-encoded rRNAs and tRNAs often function in the cytoplasm.

RNA detection establishes its function: Sequence, assembly, location, modification and direct functional assays provide distinct evidence.

Active recall

What are the three principal RNA classes directly involved in translation?

mRNA, tRNA and rRNA.

Why can a single RNA strand make a hairpin?

Complementary parts of the strand can pair antiparallel and leave a connecting loop.

What did new RNA association with old ribosomes support?

A replaceable message directing a reusable translation apparatus.

Which rRNAs arise from the typical mammalian Pol I large precursor?

18S, 5.8S and 28S.

Which tRNA end carries an amino acid and which part reads a codon?

3′ terminal A of CCA; the anticodon.

What anticodon written 5′ to 3′ matches 5′-ACG-3′ exactly?

5′-CGU-3′.

Which snRNAs recognize the early splice site and branch region?

U1 and U2 respectively.

Which snoRNA guide family positions a target ribose for methylation?

Box C/D.

What determines whether an RNP-associated RNA is active?

Proper folding, compatible partners, assembly, localization and any necessary maturation.

Why can protein output fall without a drop in total mRNA?

Translation efficiency, adaptor availability, ribosome function or localization may change.
