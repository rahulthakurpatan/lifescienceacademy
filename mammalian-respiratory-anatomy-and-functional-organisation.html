1. The biological problem: joining an atmosphere to a circulation

A mammal cannot deliver atmospheric oxygen directly to every living cell. Most of its cells are separated from the outside environment by many layers of tissue. Diffusion is effective across small distances but cannot sustain the oxygen demand of a large active animal over the distance from the skin to the centre of the body. The solution is to combine bulk movement of air, a large internal exchange surface, bulk movement of blood and local molecular diffusion. Each process solves a different part of the transport problem. A failure of one process cannot always be compensated by increasing another.

Ventilation refers to the movement of air between the atmosphere and the gas spaces of the lung. Perfusion refers to the movement of blood through the vascular network supplying those spaces. External gas exchange takes place when oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the partition between alveolar gas and blood. Internal gas exchange takes place when these molecules move between systemic blood and tissues. Cellular respiration refers to the metabolic reactions that release useful chemical energy from substrates, with oxygen serving as the final electron acceptor during aerobic mitochondrial electron transport. A lung does not manufacture oxygen, and ventilation alone is not cellular respiration. The sequence of processes links the respiratory system to the cardiovascular system and ultimately to metabolism.

A useful way to understand mammalian respiratory anatomy is to follow two travellers: an oxygen molecule beginning outside the body and an erythrocyte returning from the systemic tissues. The oxygen molecule passes through airways that become progressively smaller, enters a gas exchange space, dissolves in its liquid lining, crosses an epithelial cell and a vascular endothelial cell, and reaches the erythrocyte. The erythrocyte approaches the same region through the right side of the heart and pulmonary arteries. The two routes meet across a microscopic barrier but their bulk fluids remain separated. Carbon dioxide can cross the same barrier in the reverse direction when its partial pressure is higher in incoming blood than in alveolar gas.

The architecture must satisfy competing needs. A mechanically robust tube is suitable for bringing outside air into the thorax, but its thick wall is unsuitable for rapid exchange. A fragile sheet of flattened cells is suitable for exchange but poorly suited to resisting mechanical loads without connective tissue support. A liquid film protects cells against drying, yet an excessively thick liquid layer increases diffusion distance and an unmodified air–water interface generates substantial surface tension. Different respiratory regions solve these trade-offs by using different tissues. This is why a microscopic section is more informative when interpreted as part of an integrated organ than when studied as an isolated list of cell types.

Human anatomy provides a convenient reference for the general mammalian arrangement. The exact number of lobes and branching generations differs between species and even between regions of an individual lung. Do not treat the human three-right-lobe and two-left-lobe pattern as a defining feature of Mammalia. The combination of branching conducting passages, alveolar exchange surfaces, a closely associated capillary network and a pleural relationship with the thoracic wall is much more informative for comparative physiology.

Why subdivision of the respiratory surface matters

The exchange surface must be large in relation to the amount of metabolically active tissue it supplies. Imagine a simple geometric model in which an organ contains one spherical gas cavity of radius R. Its surface area is 4πR², and its volume is 4πR³/3. Now redistribute the same gas volume into N equal smaller spherical cavities. Conservation of gas volume implies that each new radius is R divided by the cube root of N. The combined area becomes the original area multiplied by the cube root of N. Thus subdivision into 1,000 equal spheres would increase the surface area tenfold at the same total enclosed gas volume. The example is not an anatomical model of the real lung, because actual alveoli share walls, are open to ducts, and are not identical spheres. Its purpose is to isolate the geometrical benefit of subdivision.

Such geometry alone would be insufficient if the resulting internal surfaces were remote from the blood supply. An invagination of tissue creates area, but the area contributes to exchange only when a suitable transport pathway reaches it. The branching airway tree delivers fresh gas to a large collection of surfaces, while pulmonary vessels bring blood near them. A lung thus consists of at least three coordinated components: an accessible gas volume, its bounding tissue, and a vascular distribution network. Enlarging one component without adequate development of the others may not provide a corresponding improvement in function.

Subdivision also introduces costs. Small cavities with wet surfaces require mechanical stabilisation. Each surface consists of living cells that need nutrients, protection and renewal. The thinness necessary for rapid diffusion makes these surfaces more vulnerable to pressure, inflammation and accumulation of tissue fluid. A huge exchange area must also fit into a thorax that is able to change volume repeatedly. Surfactant, extracellular matrix and the arrangement of pleural membranes can be understood as adaptations that allow the geometrical advantage of subdivision to be realised without making the exchange surface prohibitively difficult to maintain.

An isolated organ may have a very large area but may not be able to supply a mammal with enough oxygen if the available gas is not renewed or if the transported blood is insufficient. This is analogous to a large interface between two streams that are not being replenished: exchange gradually reduces the driving difference. Gas and blood need not move in the same pattern, but both streams must maintain conditions that permit continued transfer. The anatomical organisation of the lungs is therefore meaningful only in the context of a ventilating system and a functioning circulation.

2. Upper respiratory tract: preparing inspired air

Air usually enters through the external nares and passes into the nasal cavity. The nasal vestibule is the short entry region containing relatively coarse hairs that intercept some large airborne particles. Deeper within the nasal cavity, projecting bony structures called conchae increase contact between moving air and the mucosal surface. They create passages whose geometry brings inspired air close to blood vessels and moist epithelium. A well-perfused mucosa helps transfer heat to incoming cold air, while evaporation from the airway surface adds water vapour. These functions are particularly valuable because the delicate gas exchange surface is adapted to work in a warm, moist internal environment.

The nasal lining is not uniform. Most of the cavity is covered by respiratory mucosa, whereas a specialised region near its roof contains olfactory sensory epithelium. The latter detects airborne chemicals and is therefore a sensory rather than a primary conditioning surface. At the entrance, stratified epithelium offers protection against abrasion. In the respiratory region, secretory cells and ciliated epithelial cells cooperate to remove material that has deposited on the surface. The particles do not need to be absorbed into blood in order to be removed from inspired air; surface trapping and transport can divert them to the digestive tract or outside the body.

Air moves through the pharynx after leaving the nasal cavity. The nasopharynx normally conducts nasal air, the oropharynx is shared with the alimentary pathway, and the laryngopharynx leads towards the larynx and oesophagus. Breathing through the mouth bypasses much of the nasal conditioning apparatus, although additional conditioning continues in the lower respiratory passages. The pharynx illustrates a fundamental design challenge in land vertebrates: a single anterior region must accommodate both food and air without routinely directing solid or liquid material into the lungs.

The larynx protects the entrance to the trachea and is also involved in sound production. Cartilaginous structures maintain the passage while intrinsic muscles alter the position and tension of the vocal folds. During swallowing, elevation of the larynx, closure of the vocal folds and movement of the epiglottis cooperate to protect the airway. The epiglottis is therefore not a solitary valve responsible for all protection. The coordination of several structural and neural events explains why failure of swallowing coordination can allow material to enter the lower airway even if the epiglottis itself is present.

Anatomical conventions differ over whether the larynx belongs to the upper or lower respiratory tract. This distinction is mainly a convention of location and is not identical to the functional boundary between the conducting and respiratory zones. The larynx remains a conducting structure under either convention because it lacks alveolar gas exchange surfaces. An exam question asking for a functional boundary must therefore be answered using the presence or absence of alveoli rather than a memorised upper-versus-lower classification.

The surface as a heat and water exchanger

Air conditioning involves two coupled exchanges. Heat moves between an incoming airstream and the mucosal tissue whenever they differ in temperature. Water enters the gas phase when the water vapour pressure in the air is below the equilibrium vapour pressure near the moist surface. Both processes are influenced by the time available for contact, the surface geometry and the properties of the tissue. A passage with a small surface in contact with a large volume of fast moving dry air is less effective at conditioning than a passage whose folds increase contact without excessively obstructing the lumen. Nasal conchae provide such a folded arrangement. Their vascular supply can replenish heat transferred to the gas, and surface secretions help replace water lost by evaporation.

During expiration, warm moist gas moves in the opposite direction. In favourable conditions the cooler proximal mucosa can recover some heat and water from the exhaled gas. The extent of recovery depends on ambient conditions, breathing pattern and species. Mammals living in dry environments can show particularly effective conservation of water through specialised nasal geometry and physiological regulation. The nose therefore participates in both protection of distal tissues and conservation of the animal's resources. The benefits are not free: evaporation costs water, heat transfer can alter the thermal balance of the body, and nasal passages add resistance to airflow. The anatomy represents a compromise among these demands.

A student can analyse the difference between warming and humidification by considering an inspired gas initially containing very little water vapour. Warming that gas without adding water changes the maximum amount of water it could contain, but does not supply the missing molecules. Conversely, adding water by evaporation requires a source of liquid and energy for the phase change. The two processes often occur together in a living airway but are conceptually distinct. A temperature measurement alone cannot establish how completely a gas has been humidified. Both temperature and moisture need to be measured if an experiment aims to distinguish the processes.

Local tissue swelling can alter airway geometry independently of the state of the distal lung. The mucosa has a rich vascular supply; changes in vascular filling and interstitial fluid affect the open cross section available to airflow. Nasal resistance can therefore rise without a cartilage fracture, a pulmonary capillary defect or a change in the number of alveoli. This is an example of a structural component whose dimensions vary over short time scales. The outline of a bony cavity by itself does not define the dimensions of the actual air passage during life.

The pharynx presents another kind of variable geometry. Unlike the cartilaginous trachea, portions of the pharyngeal passage are bounded predominantly by soft tissues and muscle. Their configuration changes during swallowing, vocalisation and breathing. The lumen must remain sufficiently open during inspiration while still allowing its temporary closure or diversion of material during swallowing. Changes in the activity of muscles supporting these soft tissues can affect airway patency without directly damaging alveolar cells. This illustrates why an anatomical diagram of a respiratory passage should be read with attention to the type of support at each level, not just the names of the structures.

3. Trachea, bronchi and bronchioles

The trachea conducts air from the larynx to the main bronchi. Its wall contains incomplete rings of hyaline cartilage, with an open posterior region bridged by connective tissue and trachealis smooth muscle. Cartilage resists deformation and helps preserve the lumen during pressure changes. The posterior region lies next to the oesophagus, and its flexible construction permits some accommodation of a swallowed bolus. The arrangement provides mechanical support without making the tube an entirely rigid cylinder.

At the carina the trachea divides into right and left main bronchi. In an adult human the right main bronchus is generally wider, shorter and more vertical than the left, a relationship that helps explain why aspirated material may preferentially enter the right side, although position and individual anatomy matter. Each main bronchus enters a lung and supplies lobar bronchi, which divide into segmental bronchi. Further subdivision produces progressively smaller intrapulmonary bronchi. Cartilage changes from tracheal rings to irregular supporting plates in intrapulmonary bronchi. Submucosal glands secrete material onto the epithelial surface; circumferential or spiral smooth muscle layers influence lumen dimensions.

Bronchioles differ from bronchi principally in their lack of cartilage and submucosal glands. The epithelial lining becomes shorter as the passages narrow, changing towards simple columnar and cuboidal epithelium. Ciliated cells persist into smaller conducting passages, while nonciliated club cells become prominent in many bronchioles. Club cells participate in epithelial protection, secretion and repair and can metabolise certain inhaled chemicals. They are distinct from the type II cells of the alveolar epithelium even though both populations have protective secretory functions.

The absence of cartilage does not imply that a small airway is unsupported. Airways are embedded in elastic lung tissue, and the surrounding parenchyma exerts outward traction on their walls. As lung volume changes, tissue geometry changes and the airway may change calibre even without active smooth muscle contraction. This connection between an airway and the surrounding tissue helps explain why loss of elastic structures can alter airflow as well as reduce exchange area. It also explains why isolated airway measurements may differ from measurements made in an intact inflated lung.

The terminal bronchiole is the last wholly conducting passage. Distal to it, respiratory bronchioles have alveoli interrupting portions of their walls. Alveolar ducts are passages whose walls are largely formed by openings into alveoli. Alveolar sacs are clusters of alveoli opening into a common space. These terms describe a continuous three-dimensional branching system rather than a succession of isolated bags joined in a perfectly regular chain. The pulmonary acinus is the region supplied by a terminal bronchiole, encompassing respiratory bronchioles and more distal alveolated structures. By identifying an acinus, a student can connect a single conducting pathway to the many exchange surfaces it supplies.

Branching creates parallel airflow paths. Even when individual distal tubes have very small lumina, their combined cross-sectional area can be much larger than that of the trachea. This is a geometrical distinction between the resistance or area of one path and the aggregate properties of the entire network. For example, if 64 ideal equal branches each have a radius one-quarter of the original tube, the combined area is 64 times one-sixteenth, or four times the original area. At an identical volume flow rate through the two levels, average velocity would be one-quarter of its original value. Real lungs have asymmetric branches and variable flow, but the example illustrates why the smallest individual radius does not necessarily identify the region of greatest total airway resistance. The full mechanics of flow belong to the next chapter.

From a branching plan to a three-dimensional distribution network

The purpose of the bronchial tree is to distribute gas to a three-dimensional volume while keeping the size of the conducting space within practical limits. A single central tube could bring gas into the thorax but could not place it near every exchange surface. Repeated branching makes many distal surfaces accessible through short local paths. The same principle occurs in vascular trees, although blood travels in closed endothelial tubes and airways terminate in a network of gas-filled spaces. The two trees must develop and remain closely coordinated so that gas and blood approach the same microscopic region.

One can describe the branching tree in terms of orders or generations. In a simplified model the trachea is assigned generation zero, each bifurcation adds one generation, and the number of branches would double at each successive level if every branch divided symmetrically. Such a model is useful in calculations, but it is not a map of an individual human lung. Actual branches differ in length, angle and radius; some paths reach their terminal conducting airways after fewer bifurcations than others. A textbook illustration that shows an exact number of generations should be interpreted as a model or average description rather than an invariant anatomical count.

The amount of air temporarily occupying the conducting tree is determined by the volumes of its individual passages. For a uniform cylindrical segment, lumen volume is its cross-sectional area multiplied by its length. A branching tree includes many segments, so its total gas volume is the sum of all those volumes. Increasing the number of branches does not necessarily increase total conducting volume without limit because the radii and lengths of individual segments decrease distally. Conversely, decreasing the dimensions of every tube indiscriminately would impair access to gas exchange regions. Efficient distribution requires both a suitable volume of conducting gas and an acceptable opposition to its movement.

Airway epithelium, basement membrane, supporting connective tissue, smooth muscle and external attachments should be distinguished when interpreting a wall section. The lumen is the central space; epithelium is the cellular lining, not the entire wall. A lumen can narrow when smooth muscle contracts without epithelial cells changing their identity. Inflammation or accumulation of secretions can narrow the same lumen by different mechanisms. A cross section of a narrowed airway cannot by itself reveal which mechanism was responsible unless the wall and luminal contents are also examined.

Smooth muscle is distributed along many parts of the airway tree and is especially important in bronchioles, where there is no rigid cartilaginous skeleton. Its fibres are arranged so that changes in tension alter airway calibre. Autonomic signalling and locally released mediators can influence that tone; a brief link to such regulation is enough here, because control mechanisms are developed in later nodes. The structural point is that an airway wall contains living contractile tissue, so its radius is a physiological variable rather than a constant dimension in a pipe network.

A bronchial obstruction also has a spatial consequence. A proximal obstruction can isolate a much larger downstream territory than an obstruction of an individual terminal bronchiole. This follows from the hierarchy of the tree: proximal branches are common routes to many acini. The amount of impaired tissue depends on the position of the blockage and the availability of collateral pathways, not simply on the diameter of the object causing it. An exam diagram that marks an obstruction on a branch should be analysed by tracing all downstream descendants of that branch.

Recognising the gross structure through sections and casts

The tracheal cartilage skeleton, bronchial cartilage plates and unciliated elements of bronchioles can be compared using both transverse and longitudinal sections. A transverse section shows the distribution of support around a lumen, whereas a longitudinal section shows how these supporting elements repeat or change along the passage. A tracheal ring is incomplete posteriorly, but individual sections may not intersect the ring at the same level. Similarly, a bronchial section may pass between supporting plates. Identification from a single image must therefore use multiple independent features rather than requiring that a visible fragment of cartilage be present in every section.

A careful histological interpretation proceeds from the lumen outward. First identify whether the lining consists of flattened, cuboidal, tall or apparently multilayered cells. Next examine the connective tissue beneath it for glandular structures and determine whether a contractile smooth muscle layer is visible. Finally ask whether the wall contains cartilage or is instead continuous with alveolated parenchyma. In a respiratory bronchiole, alternating stretches of relatively continuous bronchiolar epithelium and openings into alveoli provide particularly useful evidence. An alveolar duct is more distal and has numerous openings, giving the wall a much less continuous appearance.

The position of a vessel relative to a bronchus can also guide interpretation. Pulmonary arterial branches are often seen alongside airways and supply blood to the distal exchange network. Pulmonary veins frequently lie in different tissue planes, allowing collection of blood across adjacent territories. In histological sections, distinguishing an airway from an artery does not depend on whether its lumen contains visible red blood cells. Blood can be lost during preparation, and collapsed lumina may have distorted shapes. An airway has an epithelial lining of the appropriate regional type and may have cartilage or glandular features; a vascular lumen is lined by endothelium and has its own characteristic wall organisation.

A cast of the bronchial tree records the shapes of the pathways through which the casting material travels. It can help demonstrate branching patterns and relative dimensions. However, a cast does not preserve ciliary structures, epithelial cell identity, the thin fluid film or muscle tone at the time of normal breathing. Nor is the volume recorded by a cast necessarily identical to the volume of the tree at all living lung volumes. Comparing a cast with tissue sections allows the learner to reconstruct the relationship between the space accessible to air and the cellular wall that regulates its accessibility.

4. Conducting zone and respiratory zone: reading tissue by function

The conducting zone is a distribution system extending from the entry passages to terminal bronchioles. It moves air, conditions it and intercepts foreign material. Its lumen contains air but lacks specialised alveolar surfaces for substantial oxygen transfer into the pulmonary circulation. The respiratory zone begins with the appearance of alveoli along respiratory bronchioles and continues through alveolar ducts and sacs. It is possible for a passage to conduct gas and also participate in gas exchange: respiratory bronchioles are the transition between these functions.

A section of tracheal mucosa often shows nuclei at multiple heights, giving the impression of several cell layers. The term pseudostratified indicates that these cells contact the basement membrane although not all extend to the luminal surface. Ciliated cells expose motile cilia to the airway lumen. Goblet cells secrete mucins that contribute to mucus. Basal cells can act as epithelial progenitors in proximal airways. These features contrast with true stratified squamous epithelium, whose multiple layers provide protection against physical abrasion. A flattened cell in an alveolus should not be mistaken for one of the surface cells in a stratified squamous epithelium.

As a conducting airway becomes smaller, a very thick epithelial layer and large mucus-producing apparatus would occupy an increasing fraction of the cross section. Distal passages therefore employ lower epithelia and rely on a different balance of surface secretion, ciliary activity and cellular defence. The gas exchange surface itself must be free of a thick moving mucus blanket. An alveolus is instead lined by a thin liquid layer and surfactant, and particulate material that reaches it may be removed by macrophages.

A histological comparison can be made without memorising exact measurements. In a section with cartilage and a ciliated pseudostratified epithelium, identify a proximal conducting airway before deciding whether it is trachea or bronchus. If no cartilage or submucosal glands are present and the lumen is lined by cuboidal cells, a bronchiole is plausible. If alveoli interrupt that bronchiolar wall, it is a respiratory bronchiole rather than a terminal bronchiole. If the observed tissue is a network of extremely thin septa surrounding air spaces, it is likely to represent alveolated parenchyma. An apparent absence of cartilage in a very small sample of an airway does not independently establish the identity of the complete structure.

Anatomical dead space describes the volume of conducting passages not directly involved in gas exchange. This does not mean these passages are biologically useless. If the conducting system were removed, the lungs would lose their distribution, conditioning and protection functions. The trade-off becomes important when breaths are shallow: gas moved in and out of conducting space does not fully refresh distal exchange spaces. The quantitative treatment of dead space belongs to Node 7.C.04; here the central idea is that a structure may be essential for gas exchange without itself being a gas exchange membrane.

The acinus as the interface between transport modes

Airflow through a large airway is described as bulk transport because many gas molecules are displaced together. Molecular diffusion occurs within gases too, including in the distal air spaces, where concentration differences can drive local mixing even when average bulk velocities are low. There is no abrupt physical switch at the first alveolus that turns convection off and diffusion on. Instead, their relative importance changes along the pathway. The anatomical distinction between conducting and respiratory zones is based on the presence of alveoli, while the physical distinction between bulk transport and diffusion depends on the distance, velocity and concentration gradient.

The acinus is an informative unit because its gas supply enters through a common terminal bronchiole. Consider two acini receiving equal volumes of fresh gas, but one contains more exchange surface than the other. Their gas supply may be equal although their potential diffusion capacities differ. Alternatively, two acini may have similar surface areas but different ventilation because their upstream passages differ in resistance. The organisation of an acinus therefore clarifies why the number of alveoli, the quantity of gas delivered and the amount of blood perfusing them cannot be used interchangeably.

Within an acinus, the openings of alveoli interrupt the duct-like structures. A histological slice can cut through a duct at an angle, producing several apparent disconnected air spaces even though they are connected in three dimensions. Tissue sectioning is useful because it reveals wall composition, but it does not automatically provide a faithful picture of connectivity. Serial sections, three-dimensional reconstruction or careful interpretation of airway casts can help identify spatial relationships. A cast can demonstrate lumen geometry while losing much of the information about epithelial cell types and tissue composition. The choice of method follows the biological question.

The walls of alveolated passages do not have the continuous arrangement of cartilaginous and glandular elements found proximally. Instead, structural support is distributed through alveolar entrance rings, septal matrix and the surrounding parenchyma. In a diagram, the boundary of an alveolar duct should therefore not be drawn as a solid thick tube with sealed alveoli glued to its outside. Openings into gas exchange spaces form much of the duct wall. This arrangement maximises exposed surface but imposes a requirement for mechanical integration with neighbouring tissue.

The total cross-sectional area of the distal conducting and transitional airways can be considered separately from the enormous gas exchange surface area. The former is measured across pathways through which air moves; the latter follows the surfaces of epithelial sheets facing gas spaces. They have the same dimensions of length squared, but refer to entirely different geometrical measurements. It is incorrect to infer the total alveolar exchange area directly from the cross section of a bundle of bronchioles. This distinction also helps avoid confusing average airflow velocity with flux across the blood–air barrier.

5. Mucociliary clearance, epithelial defence and macrophages

An inhaled particle encounters a series of defences. Large particles may be intercepted in the nose, particles in conducting passages can become embedded in mucus, and very small particles that reach distal air spaces encounter a different defence system. Deposition depends on particle properties, airway geometry and airflow. Impaction is important where a particle with momentum fails to follow a turning airstream; sedimentation can occur under gravity; diffusion becomes significant for extremely small particles. These mechanisms describe how particles approach surfaces, not how the body subsequently removes them.

Mucus is a hydrated material containing mucins and other components. Its physical properties allow particles to become trapped without normally blocking airflow. Beneath or within the surface layers lies a hydrated periciliary environment that permits ciliary movement. Motile cilia contain microtubule arrangements and dynein motors that generate repeated bends. Coordinated strokes move mucus towards the pharynx, generally upward from lower airways and posteriorly from the nasal cavity. Material reaching the pharynx can be swallowed or expelled. A cilium is a mechanical component of clearance, not an immune cell that engulfs particles.

The epithelial surface must maintain water and ion balance. Inadequate hydration can make clearance inefficient even when cilia are structurally intact; conversely, defects of ciliary motility can impair clearance even if mucus hydration is adequate. Cystic fibrosis and primary ciliary dyskinesia illustrate these two distinct classes of mechanism. In cystic fibrosis, abnormal CFTR-dependent ion transport contributes to dehydrated or otherwise abnormal airway secretions and impaired clearance. In primary ciliary dyskinesia, defective motile cilia impair the directional transport of secretions. Neither example requires the assumption that all respiratory symptoms originate in the alveolar diffusion barrier.

Coughing supplements the mucociliary system by generating rapid expiratory airflow that can move secretions and foreign material towards larger airways. It is particularly useful when secretions accumulate or an irritant activates airway sensory receptors. Cough is not a substitute for continuous microscopic transport along every small airway. A comparison of the two mechanisms reveals how an intermittent whole-organ response can reinforce a continuously operating epithelial process.

Alveolar macrophages occupy the gas exchange region, where a thick mucus layer would interfere with exchange. They recognise and ingest particulate material, microorganisms and cellular debris. Following uptake, material may be degraded or transported towards clearance pathways; some material persists, particularly if it cannot readily be degraded. Macrophages also release signalling molecules and coordinate immune responses. Strong inflammation can be protective against infection but can also damage the thin epithelial and endothelial partition. Thus alveolar defence must balance removal of harmful material against preservation of an exceptionally delicate exchange surface.

An experiment comparing clearance mechanisms should measure more than the amount of deposited tracer. A fluorescent particle that remains at the initial site may reflect poor ciliary motion, abnormal mucus properties, impaired macrophage clearance or different initial deposition. Control the deposition conditions, identify the compartment where the particles are found and measure movement over time. Comparing ciliary beat frequency with net directional transport can reveal whether beating cilia are effectively moving the surface material. This distinction between an isolated cellular activity and a physiological outcome is essential in experimental interpretation.

Experimental distinctions in airway protection

The mucociliary system is best understood as a coupled motor and material system. Motile cilia generate forces using ATP-dependent dynein activity, but the net movement of mucus depends on the pattern of strokes, coordination between neighbouring cells and the mechanical properties of the material being transported. If a cilium beats repeatedly without a directional effective stroke, a high frequency alone may fail to produce net transport. If a normal ciliary motor acts against mucus that is too adherent or poorly hydrated, the same motor activity may produce less movement. This explains why several experimental readouts are needed to understand a clearance defect.

In a hypothetical experiment, investigators compare three epithelial preparations. In the first, both ciliary motion and mucus transport are normal. In the second, the cilia move slowly and the surface liquid resembles the control. In the third, the cilia beat at approximately the control frequency but tracer transport is slow and the secretion is concentrated. The second pattern supports a motor defect as one explanation; the third suggests a problem with the material being moved or with the coordination of beating. Neither observation is conclusive by itself. Restoring hydration while measuring transport, or examining beat direction and waveform, would provide additional evidence. A change that rescues movement under controlled conditions can help establish which component limits clearance.

Cellular defence also involves the epithelial boundary itself. Intercellular junctions help prevent unrestricted movement of substances between cells, while membrane transport systems regulate the composition of the fluid contacting their apical surfaces. The basal side of an epithelial cell faces tissue and its blood supply, whereas the apical side faces the airway lumen. The polarity of transport processes matters because it permits directional movement of solutes and water. An ion channel located on the wrong side of a polarised epithelium may not support the same net transepithelial transport even if its molecular conductance remains normal. This principle connects respiratory physiology with membrane transport studied in earlier units.

Alveolar macrophage activity can be studied using labelled particles, but a fluorescent signal inside a cell must be distinguished from a signal merely attached to its outer membrane. Microscopy at sufficient resolution or a method that distinguishes internal and external fluorescence can address that issue. If macrophages ingest particles normally but degrade them poorly, intracellular signal may persist; if uptake itself is defective, much of the material may remain extracellular. Both conditions could produce impaired clearance measured at the whole-tissue level. The anatomical location of the tracer and its change over time clarify which step is affected.

Inflammation changes defence and exchange simultaneously. Recruitment of additional immune cells may help eliminate pathogens, while released mediators and tissue injury can disrupt endothelial or epithelial barriers. If permeability rises, fluid can accumulate in compartments that normally contain only a thin liquid layer. This link between immune defence and diffusion does not mean that inflammation is always harmful. Its effects depend on the balance between pathogen clearance, preservation of tissue structure and resolution of the response. The structure–function question is whether defence can operate without excessively increasing the thickness or reducing the accessibility of the exchange partition.

Direct evidence for the hydrated clearance surface

The airway lining was traditionally represented using a relatively watery region around cilia beneath an overlying gel of mucus. This simple description captures the need for cilia to move beneath a mobile trapping layer, but it does not explain why the upper mucus normally remains separated from the cell surface when water is redistributed. Research by Brian Button and colleagues, published in Science in 2012, investigated the organisation and physical behaviour of these surface layers. Their work supported a model in which densely associated surface molecules form a brush-like periciliary compartment that resists intrusion by the overlying mucus.

The biological implication of this model is that water distribution is not governed solely by the depth of a freely flowing liquid pool. Interactions among macromolecules, hydration and osmotic forces help maintain an environment in which cilia can function. If the mucus becomes sufficiently concentrated, its physical interaction with the periciliary compartment changes and transport may be impaired. This helps explain how an alteration in epithelial ion and water transport can produce a respiratory defect even when ciliary proteins have not been directly damaged. The model refines, rather than removes, the fundamental concept of cooperation between motile cells and a hydrated surface.

The experimental logic is particularly useful for examination reasoning. To understand an epithelial transport failure, measure both the motion of the motor structures and the state of the material they move. If water is redistributed experimentally, observe whether the periciliary environment remains accessible to the cilia and whether particles continue to move. A study of physical properties can provide evidence for a causal mechanism independently of a measurement of infection frequency. Conversely, an association between concentrated secretions and infection would not by itself identify the specific physical defect responsible.

No single epithelial defence mechanism acts equally well throughout the organ. The nose intercepts a fraction of incoming material, large conducting passages remove deposited particles through mucus and cilia, and distal macrophages cope with material that reaches alveoli. These defences are arranged in series along the inhaled path but also operate concurrently. An intact upstream filter reduces the challenge faced by downstream cells. A downstream macrophage defect may therefore be difficult to detect under low exposure conditions but become important when large amounts of particulate material reach the distal surface.

The route by which material leaves a region is also important. A particle transported to the pharynx has been cleared from the lower airway even if it is subsequently swallowed rather than expelled. A particle taken up by a macrophage is no longer freely suspended in the alveolar lining fluid, but uptake does not establish that the particle has been degraded or removed from the organ. Similarly, a dissolved irritant can cross an epithelial boundary without behaving like an inert particle. These distinctions make clearance a sequence of transport and cellular processing rather than a single event described by the disappearance of a visible particle.

6. Lungs, lobes, hilum and pleural organisation

The lungs occupy much of the thorax on either side of the mediastinum. The apex extends superiorly and the base rests against the diaphragm. In humans the right lung has superior, middle and inferior lobes, separated by horizontal and oblique fissures. The left lung has superior and inferior lobes separated by an oblique fissure and accommodates the heart through its medial contour, including the cardiac notch. The lingula is a part of the left superior lobe rather than a separate middle lobe. These gross features help organise bronchi and vessels but are not themselves the sites of molecular diffusion.

The hilum is the region of the medial surface at which structures enter or leave the lung. The root consists of structures connecting the organ with the mediastinum, including the main bronchus, pulmonary arteries and veins, bronchial vessels, nerves and lymphatics. Bronchi and pulmonary arterial branches generally travel together within the lung, whereas pulmonary veins often occupy planes between segments. This organisation helps collect oxygenated blood from neighbouring regions. A bronchopulmonary segment is supplied by a segmental bronchus and an associated pulmonary arterial branch. Segmental organisation provides a useful spatial unit but should not be confused with the microscopic acinus.

The pleura consists of two continuous serous membrane layers. Visceral pleura covers the lung surface and follows fissures. Parietal pleura lines the inner chest wall, diaphragm and mediastinal surfaces. The layers are continuous around the root of the lung. Between them is a potential space containing a thin film of pleural fluid. In normal physiology this is not an air-filled chamber. The fluid permits the surfaces to slide as the thoracic wall and diaphragm move, while close apposition and the pressure relationship maintain mechanical coupling between the lung and chest wall.

The lung has an inward tendency to recoil and the chest wall has an outward tendency at ordinary resting lung volumes. The opposing forces help establish a pleural pressure that is normally below atmospheric pressure during quiet spontaneous breathing. Transpulmonary pressure is defined as alveolar pressure minus pleural pressure. A positive transpulmonary pressure distends the lung. The meaning of this quantity is not equivalent to air flowing into the lung: even when alveolar and atmospheric pressures are equal and there is no net airflow, a positive distending pressure may maintain lung expansion. Detailed pressure changes during breathing are developed in the next node.

When air enters the pleural space, the normal mechanical coupling can be impaired. The lung may recoil inward and the chest wall may move towards its own equilibrium configuration. The term pneumothorax describes air within the pleural space; it does not mean that the normal alveoli suddenly contain air for the first time. Fluid accumulation in the pleural space can also interfere with lung expansion, but pleural fluid is anatomically distinct from fluid within alveolar air spaces or the alveolar interstitium. These distinctions allow students to predict which component of respiration is initially affected by a structural disturbance.

A simple lung–chest wall model consists of an elastic membrane connected to a movable boundary by a sealed fluid interface. It helps explain coupling but is not an exact model of the rib cage, diaphragm or pleural pressures. The important point is that visceral pleura is attached to lung tissue and parietal pleura follows the thoracic boundary. Sliding does not imply that the two surfaces become widely separated during inspiration. Mechanical transmission and low-friction movement are compatible functions of the same interface.

Compartment boundaries and the mechanics of an intact thorax

The pleural membranes are lined by mesothelial cells, which differ from both the respiratory epithelium and vascular endothelium. Mesothelium forms the smooth facing surfaces of serous cavities and contributes to the maintenance of their liquid environment. The parietal and visceral layers are continuous around the lung root, but their attachments differ. The parietal layer follows the chest wall and diaphragm; the visceral layer follows the surface of the lung. This anatomical difference explains why the two surfaces move in relation to different underlying structures while remaining closely apposed.

Pleural fluid turnover is an important aspect of the interface. Fluid is continuously formed and removed, with lymphatic drainage particularly important for maintaining a small volume. The interface remains thin because formation and removal are balanced under normal circumstances. If fluid formation exceeds drainage, the volume in the pleural compartment can rise. Such a change can alter the mechanical environment without initially increasing the microscopic thickness of the alveolar septum. The term effusion identifies accumulation in a particular compartment rather than a direct measurement of gas exchange.

The distinction among the pleural, interstitial and alveolar spaces is easiest to grasp by imagining a tracer confined to one of them. A tracer in the pleural film lies outside the visceral surface of the lung; it is not ordinarily in direct contact with alveolar gas. A tracer in septal interstitium lies between cellular and extracellular elements of the parenchyma. A tracer in the alveolar lumen lies on the air-facing side of the respiratory epithelium. Leakage of a tracer across a boundary provides information about permeability or injury, but the relevant boundary must be named precisely. In an exam, a question asking about a gas diffusion barrier is generally concerned with the alveolar–capillary partition, not the pleural membrane.

One can use a simple numerical example to clarify pressure references. Suppose atmospheric pressure is defined as zero on a gauge scale, alveolar pressure is zero and pleural pressure is −5 cm of water. The difference between alveolar and pleural pressure is +5 cm of water. A positive distending pressure is therefore possible at a moment when there is no pressure difference driving flow between the atmosphere and the alveolus. If pleural pressure changes to −2 cm of water while alveolar pressure remains zero, the distending difference falls to +2 cm of water. These are illustrative gauge pressures, not universal resting values. They demonstrate the importance of distinguishing a pressure difference across the lung wall from a pressure difference along an airway.

Because pressures are measured relative to a reference, a negative gauge pressure does not imply a physically negative absolute pressure. The atmosphere itself exerts a large absolute pressure; a pleural pressure below that value is negative only relative to the chosen reference. A student who subtracts pressures expressed using different references may obtain a meaningless transpulmonary value. The calculation must use consistent units and a common reference. The full pressure cycle belongs to the mechanics node, but understanding the compartments and pressure definitions is a prerequisite for that topic.

Spatial localisation of pleural disturbances

The right and left lungs normally have separate pleural compartments. This arrangement in humans helps localise certain mechanical disturbances. A loss of normal pressure coupling on one side need not immediately imply identical loss on the other side, because the two pleural spaces are anatomically separated. Nevertheless, both lungs are parts of a shared thoracic and cardiovascular system, and a sufficiently severe alteration can affect whole-body function. The principle is separation with physiological interaction, rather than complete independence.

The pleura reflects around the margins of the lungs and forms recesses where the potential extent of the pleural cavity exceeds the position of the lung at some phases of breathing. These recesses permit changes in lung shape as the thoracic boundary and diaphragm move. Their existence illustrates why the pleural membrane is not simply a taut sheet fixed to an unchanging lung outline. Parietal pleura follows the walls of the compartment, whereas visceral pleura follows the expanding and recoiling organ. A spatial drawing at a single lung volume cannot describe all the relative movements that occur during a respiratory cycle.

The normal pleural liquid film is maintained through exchange with local vessels and drainage, particularly through lymphatic openings associated with the parietal layer. A pressure or permeability change in the supplying vessels can alter liquid entry; a reduction in effective lymphatic drainage can alter removal. These changes can lead to similar accumulation of fluid despite different initiating mechanisms. Consequently the amount of pleural liquid is an outcome of a balance, not a direct measure of only one process. This balance can be reasoned about using the same mass-conservation principle applied to other physiological compartments: accumulation equals entry minus removal over the interval considered.

Suppose, as a teaching example, a compartment receives 2 mL of fluid per hour and drains 2 mL per hour. Its volume remains approximately constant. If entry rises to 3 mL per hour while drainage remains fixed for four hours, the volume increases by 4 mL. If entry remains unchanged but drainage falls to 1 mL per hour, the same increase results. These deliberately simplified rates are not measurements of normal pleural turnover. Real lymphatic removal can respond to changes in volume and pressure, so a constant-rate model cannot predict indefinite accumulation. The example distinguishes two mechanisms producing an identical endpoint.

In contrast to pleural fluid, liquid in alveolar air spaces directly alters the medium through which gas approaches the epithelial barrier. Interstitial liquid influences septal geometry, and excess pleural liquid primarily alters the mechanical environment and available expansion. These distinctions are anatomical rather than diagnostic rules: an actual physiological disturbance may affect more than one compartment. A question that describes accumulation must therefore be read for the location of the fluid before conclusions are drawn about the immediate mechanism.

7. Alveolar architecture, pneumocytes and septal support

Under a microscope an alveolus is an air space bounded by thin walls shared with neighbouring alveoli. The shared walls are called interalveolar septa. A septum includes alveolar epithelium on its air-facing surfaces, connective tissue and capillaries. The capillaries form an extensive network, so a large fraction of the epithelial surface lies very close to blood. The alveoli are not independent spherical balloons surrounded by a thick outer shell; they are interconnected air spaces within a mechanically integrated tissue scaffold.

Type I pneumocytes are exceptionally thin, flattened epithelial cells. Each has a nucleus and a small thicker region containing organelles, with broad attenuated extensions that cover much of the alveolar surface. This geometry reduces the distance between alveolar gas and capillary blood while retaining a continuous epithelial boundary. Although type I cells cover most of the surface, their fraction of the total number of epithelial cells is not equivalent to their fraction of surface area. Counting nuclei in a micrograph is not the same as estimating surface coverage.

Type II pneumocytes are more cuboidal and are often found at septal junctions. They contain specialised secretory organelles called lamellar bodies that store surfactant lipids and associated hydrophobic proteins. They can proliferate and contribute to replacement of damaged type I and type II epithelial cells. This regenerative role is especially important because the extensive thin cytoplasmic processes of type I cells are mechanically and chemically vulnerable. Injury and repair must restore both the continuity of the epithelial barrier and the flattened geometry required for efficient gas exchange.

The interstitium contains extracellular matrix components including collagen and elastic fibres. Elastin contributes to reversible deformation and recoil, while collagen provides tensile restraint, especially as tissue is stretched. Fibroblasts maintain and remodel this matrix. Capillary endothelial cells form the blood-facing surface of the septum. Their location brings blood into close contact with air but prevents direct mixing under normal conditions. Lymphatic drainage and vascular and epithelial fluid transport help keep the interstitial and alveolar fluid compartments within appropriate limits.

A small alveolar pore, often called a pore of Kohn, can connect adjacent alveolar air spaces. Such connections can permit collateral movement of gas and other material. They do not replace the normal branching airway system, and their presence does not imply that every alveolus receives equal ventilation. A local airway obstruction, altered tissue compliance or uneven ventilation can produce regional differences despite these connections. The geometry of septa, neighbouring air spaces and their shared mechanical support must therefore be considered together.

A useful prediction follows from the division of labour between cell types. Selective loss of type I cells can disrupt the thin barrier even if surfactant synthesis initially remains intact. Selective dysfunction of type II cells can impair surfactant homeostasis and later epithelial repair, even if many type I cells are initially present. Macrophage dysfunction primarily impairs clearance and immune regulation, although secondary inflammation can then affect the barrier. The final physiological consequences can converge, but the initiating cellular mechanisms differ.

Cell geometry, repair and the meaning of a surface-area measurement

A simple squamous epithelium is formed by flattened cells arranged in a single layer. Flattening allows the cytoplasm to extend over a large surface with relatively little thickness. The type I pneumocyte is an extreme example of this geometry. Its thin extensions may be difficult to identify separately in conventional light microscopy, particularly when the epithelial and endothelial components of a septum are closely apposed. It is easier to identify a cell nucleus or a cuboidal type II cell than to reconstruct the complete outline of an attenuated type I cell from one two-dimensional section. This difference in visibility can bias intuitive estimates of which cell type occupies the greatest area.

An illustrative numerical model makes the distinction explicit. Suppose a tissue contains 40 cells of one class, each exposing 20 arbitrary area units, and 60 cells of another class, each exposing 2 area units. The first class contributes 800 area units and the second contributes 120. Although the first class represents only 40 percent of cell number, it accounts for about 87 percent of the combined exposed area. The figures are invented for arithmetic teaching, not histological measurements of the lung. The relevant inference is that an area fraction is a weighted measure: both the number of cells and their average exposed areas matter. This explains why statements about the predominance of type I pneumocytes must specify whether they refer to area or number.

The alveolar epithelium is not simply a passive membrane whose cells can be discarded once the organ is formed. It must survive repetitive stretch, maintain cell–cell junctions and restore barrier continuity after injury. Type II cells can proliferate and contribute to epithelial regeneration, including production of differentiated type I cells. An early proliferative response and a fully restored exchange surface are different stages of repair. A thick layer of replacement cells can protect a damaged region before a thin functional barrier has been reconstructed. Increased cell proliferation should therefore not be equated automatically with increased gas exchange efficiency.

An experiment designed to test the contribution of type II cells to repair might label a population of these cells before an injury and then examine whether labelled descendants acquire type I cell morphology and appropriate markers. A valid interpretation requires a label specific enough to identify the original population, controls for unwanted labelling of other cells and observations at several time points. Finding a marker in a cell at one time point does not establish its ancestry. The principle of following labelled descendants is the connection to developmental lineage tracing; detailed lineage technology is outside this chapter's scope.

The cell types of the septum form a functional community. Type I cells provide the air-facing diffusion surface; type II cells supply surface-active material and regenerative capacity; endothelial cells maintain the vascular boundary; fibroblasts help maintain the extracellular scaffold; macrophages contribute to clearance and immune signalling. Damage to any component can indirectly affect others. For instance, inflammation triggered by a persistent particle can affect vascular permeability even though the particle first reached the air-facing compartment. A useful analysis identifies the initial site of disturbance and then traces how it alters other components, rather than describing all abnormal lungs as examples of an identical cellular defect.

Counting alveoli and measuring a complex organ

It is natural to ask how many alveoli a human lung contains. This seemingly simple question became a problem in quantitative anatomy because an alveolus is not a detached spherical object that can be lifted out and counted. It is an opening or space within a continuous network. A histological section can intersect the same alveolus more than once, and differently sized or oriented structures have different probabilities of appearing in a given plane. Counting every circular profile on one slide therefore does not yield the number of alveoli in the organ.

In 2004, Matthias Ochs and colleagues reported estimates of human alveolar number using a sampling-based stereological approach. Their study established that alveolar number varies substantially between individuals and is related to lung size. This is more instructive than treating one rounded textbook number as a universal count. A biological measurement has a definition, a sampling method and a range of variation. The authors' approach was designed to estimate three-dimensional structure from sampled tissue without requiring every alveolus to be represented as an identical geometrical sphere. It therefore addresses some of the biases of simple profile counting.

At the examination level, the central principle of stereology is that representative sampling and appropriate counting rules can estimate properties of a three-dimensional object using sections. If a larger structure is more likely to intersect a randomly chosen plane, an uncorrected profile count can overrepresent large structures. If a structure intersects several serial sections, it may be counted multiple times. An appropriate design accounts for such probabilities and repeated intersections. The technical mathematical development of stereology is not required to understand why the original question is harder than counting independent particles in a suspension.

Alveolar number also cannot fully determine alveolar area. Consider two idealised lungs with the same number of alveoli but different average alveolar dimensions. Their surface areas could differ. Conversely, similar exchange area might arise through different combinations of number and size. Because surface shape, inflation state and shared septa also matter, even number and mean diameter may not uniquely specify the true exchange surface. This explains the importance of measuring area and thickness separately when the question concerns diffusion.

The repair capacity of alveolar epithelium has also been supported by direct experimental evidence. In 2013, Christina Barkauskas and colleagues used lineage analysis in living animal models, injury and repair experiments, and cultures of selected cells to investigate the contribution of type II alveolar cells. Their evidence supported the role of type II cells as progenitors that can maintain and repair alveolar epithelium, producing descendants with type I characteristics. The use of several complementary approaches matters because cells that can differentiate in culture are not necessarily the same populations that perform repair in an intact organ. Lineage evidence connects the initial labelled cells with their later descendants.

The conclusion does not require claiming that a single cell population accounts for every form of lung repair in all species or every injury model. Experimental injury conditions, tissue context and the specificity of the lineage marker limit the interpretation. For syllabus purposes the important consequence is that type II cells are not merely secretory factories. They also contribute to renewal of the epithelium, making them essential for long-term preservation of the alveolar exchange surface.

8. Surfactant and the stability of wet exchange surfaces

The alveolar epithelial surface is covered by a very thin aqueous lining. Water molecules attract one another and an air–water interface tends to minimise its area. This tendency is described by surface tension, a force per unit length with SI units newtons per metre. In a lung, surface tension contributes to the inward recoil of the air spaces and therefore affects the effort required to expand them. It is necessary to distinguish surface tension from tissue elastic recoil: both contribute to lung mechanics, but they originate in different physical structures.

Pulmonary surfactant is a mixture of lipids and proteins produced largely by type II alveolar epithelial cells. Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine is an important surface-active phospholipid. Like other phospholipids it is amphipathic: its polar head interacts with water, while its hydrophobic chains avoid water. Surfactant molecules accumulate at the interface between alveolar lining liquid and air, where their orientation alters the interactions that would otherwise occur between water molecules. The functional film changes dynamically as the surface area varies during breathing.

Surfactant proteins have different principal roles. SP-B and SP-C are hydrophobic proteins that help lipid films spread and function at the interface. SP-A and SP-D are hydrophilic collectins with important roles in host defence and surfactant homeostasis. It is misleading to assign all surfactant-associated proteins an identical action. In particular, a defect in a surface-active film protein need not initially resemble a defect predominantly affecting immune recognition, even though both may ultimately harm respiratory function.

Type II cells synthesise and process surfactant components using their secretory machinery. Lipids and hydrophobic proteins are assembled in lamellar bodies and released by exocytosis into the alveolar lining fluid. Material then spreads at the interface and forms a dynamic reservoir of surface-active molecules. Some surfactant components are taken up and recycled by type II cells; macrophages also contribute to turnover. Production, secretion, film organisation and removal are distinct steps, so a normal rate of lipid synthesis does not guarantee a normal functional film.

When an ideal spherical air space is surrounded by a single liquid interface, the pressure difference attributable to surface tension is modelled as two times the surface tension divided by the radius. For a fixed surface tension, the smaller ideal sphere has a greater pressure difference. This provides a useful explanation of why a surface tension-lowering system is advantageous in a lung containing many small air spaces. However, actual alveoli share septa, have non-spherical shapes and are mechanically interconnected; their pressure relations cannot be predicted from isolated spherical bubbles alone. Surfactant also has area-dependent behaviour, so surface tension is not a fixed number throughout the breathing cycle. The mechanical model and its assumptions will be treated more fully in 7.C.03.

In a simplified teaching calculation, take a radius of 100 micrometres and surface tension of 0.025 N/m. Converting the radius to 0.0001 m gives a pressure difference of 2 × 0.025 / 0.0001 = 500 Pa. If surface tension falls to 0.005 N/m at the same assumed radius, the predicted difference becomes 100 Pa. These numbers are hypothetical values for the model, not measured alveolar pressures. The fivefold decrease follows directly from the proportionality to surface tension at fixed radius. It illustrates why surfactant can reduce the distending pressure required to counter surface forces without changing the amount of oxygen in the atmosphere.

The historical study by Mary Ellen Avery and Jere Mead in 1959 examined the surface properties of lung material in relation to atelectasis and hyaline membrane disease. Their findings helped connect impaired surface activity with the failure of immature lungs to remain adequately expanded. The scientific value of measuring a material's surface properties was that it connected a physical property of the lung lining with whole-organ behaviour. Such comparisons alone do not isolate every molecular cause, because lung maturation changes many properties together. Subsequent understanding of surfactant composition, type II cell secretion and film behaviour supplies the mechanistic connection. The original paper is identified in the references.

Prematurity provides an important biological example of this relationship. An immature surfactant system can increase the tendency of exchange spaces to collapse, reduce effective exchange area and increase the work required for ventilation. This is not primarily a defect of haemoglobin's oxygen-binding sites. A normal amount of haemoglobin cannot fully compensate for a severely reduced effective alveolar surface. Conversely, even abundant surfactant cannot correct oxygen delivery when the principal defect is absence of pulmonary blood flow.

The interfacial film is not a second epithelial membrane

A phospholipid bilayer separates the interior of a cell or organelle from an aqueous environment on either side. The air-facing surfactant film has a different physical setting: air contacts the surface of an aqueous layer. Its amphipathic molecules can orient with hydrophilic regions towards water and hydrophobic regions towards the gas. The film is dynamic and associated with additional lipid structures beneath the interface. It is therefore misleading to draw it as an ordinary static cell membrane enclosing the alveolar lumen. It sits outside the epithelial cells and modifies the properties of an interface that would otherwise have relatively high surface tension.

The amount of material present and the area over which it is spread both influence film behaviour. When the interface is compressed, surface-active molecules can become more closely packed, and the film can achieve particularly low surface tension. During expansion, the interface requires redistribution or additional material to maintain its properties. Film behaviour during repeated compression and expansion depends on lipid composition, associated proteins and the availability of surface-active material. A measurement obtained at a single surface area does not completely describe performance over a breathing cycle. An experiment should consider how the tension changes as area changes, rather than reporting only the equilibrium tension of a bulk liquid sample.

The secretion and recycling cycle protects against progressive loss of surface-active material. The type II cell packages components, releases them into the lining liquid, and can recover a portion of the extracellular material. Some material is processed through macrophages. These processes are not necessarily affected in the same way by injury. A defect in lamellar body formation can impair delivery of surfactant despite synthesis of lipid precursors. A defect in extracellular film organisation can impair surface activity despite normal secretion. Impaired removal may lead to accumulation of material rather than a simple absence of it. Measuring total lung phospholipid alone cannot distinguish all these mechanisms.

A hypothetical experiment compares two preparations containing equal phospholipid mass. Preparation X has intact lipid–protein film organisation; preparation Y contains altered film-associated proteins. Under the same cyclic changes in interface area, Y maintains a higher minimum surface tension than X. The experiment supports the proposition that quantity of lipid is insufficient to explain film function. A further comparison restoring the missing protein, while keeping lipid mass and test conditions constant, would help evaluate whether that protein contributes causally. The result would not on its own establish the effect of the alteration on blood flow or the complete physiology of a living lung.

The Avery and Mead investigation is historically important because it examined a physical property associated with failure of expansion in immature lungs, rather than treating the clinical appearance of the lung as a sufficient explanation. The finding fits a causal sequence in which poor surface activity increases the forces opposing inflation, reduces stability and contributes to inadequate ventilation of exchange surfaces. A proper historical interpretation distinguishes this experimentally supported association from later knowledge of specific surfactant proteins, lipid trafficking and epithelial development. Modern molecular explanations should not be attributed retrospectively to the original study.

A distinction between alveolar liquid and surface tension is also necessary. Surface tension is a property of an interface; fluid thickness is the distance occupied by a liquid layer. A thin film can have high surface tension if it lacks appropriate surface-active molecules. A thick liquid layer can still possess surfactant at its interface. Consequently, a defect in film function and an increase in diffusion distance are separable processes, although they can coexist. This distinction allows the student to interpret a problem in which the film is altered without fluid accumulation, or fluid accumulates while surfactant production is initially preserved.

A changing air–liquid interface in the first breaths

Before birth the developing mammalian lungs are fluid-filled rather than functioning as the principal site of oxygen exchange. Oxygen reaches the fetus through the placenta. At the transition to air breathing, liquid must be cleared from air spaces and the alveolar interface must support repeated expansion and contraction. The maturation of type II cells and their surfactant system is therefore important before the first effective pulmonary breaths. The distinction between fluid clearance and surface-active film function remains important during this transition: removing excess fluid makes room for gas, while surfactant changes the physical forces at the emerging air–liquid boundary.

During the first inflations, formation and expansion of gas interfaces require mechanical work. A poorly surface-active film increases the opposition associated with those interfaces. Once gas occupies a space, maintaining an adequate opening during subsequent expiration is another aspect of the same structural-functional problem. The work required is not predicted simply by the mass of the lung or by the concentration of haemoglobin in the blood. Lung tissue mechanics, fluid distribution, surfactant activity and thoracic pressure all contribute. The newborn transition reveals how several components described separately in anatomical sections become essential at the same time.

The surface-active film should not be imagined as a substance that removes all surface forces. Physiological surface tension varies with interface area and film composition. Tissue fibres also contribute to recoil. Consequently normal surfactant does not make the lung permanently expanded in the absence of an appropriate pressure difference. It lowers an important component of the forces opposing expansion and contributes to stability while the pressure environment continues to change. This distinction is a useful foundation for understanding pressure–volume behaviour in the next chapter.

A comparison of two hypothetical samples can make this interaction clear. In sample A, extracellular elastic fibres have been degraded, but surfactant composition remains normal. In sample B, tissue fibres remain largely intact, but surface-active film function is defective. The first change alters tissue recoil and structural support; the second primarily increases interfacial forces. Both influence the pressure required to deform the lung, but they act through different physical mechanisms. The direction of a particular whole-organ pressure–volume change cannot be attributed to surfactant alone without information about tissue structure and the measurement conditions.

The normal function of surfactant is therefore best understood in relation to alveolar anatomy rather than as an isolated list of lipid and protein components. Its place at the air–liquid interface distinguishes it from connective tissue elements. Its origin in type II cells connects cellular secretion to organ mechanics. Its continuous turnover links metabolic activity and clearance to the maintenance of the exchange surface. Its impairment can reduce effective surface area without first destroying the septa. These relationships explain why a relatively small population of cuboidal epithelial cells can have a major influence over the function of an entire alveolated region.

Interpreting the original film-balance experiment

Avery and Mead compared lung extracts from infants with and without hyaline membrane disease using an apparatus in which an interfacial film could be compressed and expanded. A suspended wettable plate provided a measure of the force exerted by the interface. Their comparison found that material from the affected infants failed to achieve the low minimum tensions characteristic of sufficiently mature comparison lungs. They also examined potential influences of sample concentration and post-mortem storage. The measurements were made on extracted material rather than directly in the living alveolus, so the evidence supported a deficiency in surface activity without providing a direct measurement of the pressure within individual alveoli. This distinction is important when evaluating the historical study as evidence for the surface tension mechanism rather than as a complete modern model of neonatal respiratory physiology.

9. The blood–air barrier and diffusion geometry

Oxygen in the alveolar gas must enter the liquid lining before moving through cellular and extracellular layers. On the thin side of the blood–air barrier, the path includes alveolar lining liquid, type I cell cytoplasm, the closely apposed or fused epithelial and endothelial basal laminae, capillary endothelial cytoplasm and plasma. Oxygen then crosses the erythrocyte membrane and enters the cell, where haemoglobin binds it. Carbon dioxide travels in the opposite net direction under ordinary exchange conditions. These paths do not require an open channel connecting alveolar air directly to blood.

The epithelial and endothelial layers are distinct cellular boundaries. Each has its own plasma membranes, and their basement membranes are extracellular structures rather than additional cell layers. Where the basal laminae lie close together, diffusion distance is minimised. Other regions of the septum have more interstitial tissue and serve mechanical and cellular functions. Electron microscopy reveals this organisation more clearly than a light micrograph, which may resolve the alveolar wall but not the individual membranes making up the thinnest barrier.

A simple diffusion relationship states that transfer rate is proportional to area and the difference in gas partial pressure, and inversely proportional to effective diffusion distance, for a homogeneous barrier with constant gas-dependent permeability. The relationship is useful for reasoning about anatomy. If the available area is halved while thickness and gradient remain constant, the predicted transfer rate is halved. If thickness doubles while area and gradient remain constant, the predicted rate is likewise halved. If both alterations occur simultaneously, the predicted rate is one-quarter of the original. Actual oxygen transfer also depends on blood flow and binding, and a diseased lung does not necessarily preserve the other variables.

Pulmonary oedema may increase the thickness of the diffusion path and, if alveoli fill with liquid, reduce ventilated exchange area. Fibrosis can remodel the interstitium and increase effective diffusion distance as well as altering mechanical properties. Destruction of alveolar septa, as occurs in emphysema, reduces the area of exchange and changes elastic support of airways. These conditions are useful physiological contrasts. They should not be reduced to a single universal explanation, since tissue injury often changes several variables at once.

One can distinguish structural and functional measures of surface area. Morphometry estimates geometric surface area from tissue images using appropriate sampling and corrections. Functional exchange depends on whether that surface is ventilated, perfused and accessible for diffusion. An experiment reporting a large anatomical area does not prove that every part of the surface contributes equally during exercise or disease. A gas-transfer measurement, in contrast, reflects combined effects of barrier properties, perfused capillary blood and interactions with the transported gas. A structural observation and a whole-organ functional measurement answer related but different questions.

The very thin membrane presents a biological challenge because it must withstand repeated mechanical deformation and prevent inappropriate fluid movement into air spaces. Cell junctions, extracellular matrix, epithelial ion transport and vascular regulation all contribute to preserving this barrier. Epithelial sodium absorption, for example, supports removal of excess liquid by establishing osmotic gradients. The maintenance of a thin liquid layer is therefore an actively regulated property of living tissue, not simply the absence of blood leakage. The details of partial-pressure calculations and whole-lung diffusion capacity are reserved for the gas exchange chapter.

What a diffusion barrier measurement can and cannot establish

The simplified diffusion relationship can be expressed more carefully using a membrane permeability coefficient for a gas. If the concentration difference across a homogeneous planar layer is denoted by ΔC, its thickness by T and the gas diffusion coefficient by D, the magnitude of the molar transfer rate is D A ΔC/T. When D is measured in square metres per second, A in square metres, ΔC in moles per cubic metre and T in metres, the resulting unit is moles per second. For a dissolved gas whose concentration is proportional to its partial pressure at equilibrium, a solubility factor relates ΔC to ΔP. This is why physiological versions of the diffusion relationship often use partial pressure rather than dissolved concentration.

The derivation presumes a steady gradient across a uniform layer, a constant diffusion coefficient and a well-defined area. The actual exchange partition contains several layers whose transport properties differ. In a simplified sequence of layers, each contributes resistance to transfer; a particularly thick or poorly permeable layer can account for a large fraction of the total resistance. Adding a new layer cannot be assumed to have the same effect as increasing the thickness of an existing layer by the same physical distance, because their diffusion coefficients and gas solubilities may differ. This is important when comparing gas diffusion through alveolar liquid, cell cytoplasm and extracellular matrix.

Consider a conceptual two-layer model in which each layer has an equal baseline diffusion resistance. The total resistance is two arbitrary units. If one layer's resistance doubles while the other remains constant, the new total is three units. At the same driving difference, the transfer becomes two-thirds of its initial value rather than one-half. The arithmetic follows because resistances of sequential layers add, and transfer is inversely proportional to total resistance. These are hypothetical resistance values, not direct measurements of alveolar membranes. They show why knowing the fractional contribution of a changed layer is necessary to predict the overall effect.

When blood receives oxygen, haemoglobin binding helps maintain a gradient by limiting the rise of dissolved oxygen concentration for a given amount of oxygen entering blood. This is a useful bridge to respiratory pigments, but the membrane and the binding reaction must not be confused. A red blood cell can have a large capacity to bind oxygen while the diffusion barrier remains damaged. Conversely, the structural membrane can be extremely thin while reduced blood haemoglobin concentration limits the oxygen content that the blood can ultimately carry. Distinguish the rate of transfer across the partition from the final amount transported in the systemic circulation.

Morphometric measurements of barrier thickness require suitable sampling because the thickness varies across a septum. Selecting only the thinnest visible points would underestimate the variation in the complete interface; selecting mostly regions near nuclei or connective tissue would overrepresent thicker areas. The biologically effective resistance is not necessarily predicted by a simple arithmetic mean of all measured thicknesses. Exchange pathways with small diffusion resistance may carry disproportionately large fluxes, provided they are accessible to gas and blood. The essential examination principle is that local geometry and parallel routes influence the relationship between a structural average and a physiological measurement.

10. Pulmonary and bronchial circulations

The pulmonary circuit begins at the right ventricle. Blood flows through the pulmonary trunk and right and left pulmonary arteries, then through successively smaller arterial branches to capillary networks adjacent to alveoli. Oxygenated blood leaves these networks through venules and pulmonary veins and reaches the left atrium. The systemic circuit then distributes it to tissues through the left ventricle. The terms artery and vein describe direction of blood flow relative to the heart, not oxygen content. Pulmonary arteries normally carry relatively deoxygenated blood after birth, and pulmonary veins normally carry oxygen-rich blood.

In the steady state, the pulmonary circuit receives approximately the entire output of the right ventricle. It therefore accommodates a large flow despite relatively low vascular pressures. This property follows from the low resistance of a highly branching, distensible vascular network. In simple haemodynamic terms, mean flow equals pressure difference divided by resistance. Because pulmonary and systemic circulations are connected in series, their average flows must closely match in the long run, but their driving pressure differences are very different. Similar flow at a smaller pressure difference implies a lower resistance.

Consider illustrative values of pulmonary arterial mean pressure 15 mmHg, left atrial pressure 5 mmHg and cardiac output 5 L/min. The pressure difference across the pulmonary circulation is 10 mmHg, giving a resistance of 10 divided by 5, or 2 mmHg·min/L. These are illustrative values rather than a diagnostic threshold or a measurement from a particular person. The calculation clarifies that a low-pressure circulation is not necessarily a low-flow circulation. Increasing vascular resistance at fixed flow would require a greater pressure difference. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure can promote filtration of liquid into surrounding tissue, illustrating why the vascular environment matters for a short air–blood diffusion path.

Capillaries lie within alveolar septa, and their effective cross sections and perfusion can change with pressure and lung expansion. Additional vascular pathways may become perfused as cardiac output rises, while already perfused vessels may distend. Recruitment and distension can help the lung accommodate increased flow without a proportionate increase in vascular resistance. The local pressure environment includes both vascular pressures and surrounding alveolar or tissue pressures. The consequences of regional ventilation and perfusion differences are developed in 7.C.05.

The bronchial circulation provides systemic arterial blood to conducting airways, supporting connective tissue and other lung structures. It usually originates from branches associated with the thoracic aorta, with anatomical variation. Some bronchial venous drainage returns through systemic veins, while some enters the pulmonary venous circulation. The latter contributes to the small normal mixing of blood with different oxygen contents before it reaches the systemic arterial circuit. It is incorrect to assume that every vessel in the lung belongs to the pulmonary circuit or that the pulmonary arteries are the principal systemic nutrient supply to all conducting structures.

A distinction between the two circulations can be demonstrated conceptually by following a vascular tracer. Injection into the pulmonary artery first labels the exchange vascular network and subsequently pulmonary veins. Injection into bronchial arterial branches initially labels airway-associated systemic vascular territories. The routes can communicate through vascular connections, so perfect segregation cannot be assumed. Tissue sampling, time of observation and tracer properties affect the interpretation. Mapping vessels therefore requires both knowledge of their origins and evidence of their actual distribution.

Vascular arrangement, fluid balance and the cost of high flow

The pulmonary capillary bed is embedded in the alveolar septum rather than located in a separate organ downstream of the exchange surface. This short spatial relationship is critical: blood approaching the lung through a large artery does not exchange substantially with alveolar air until it reaches the appropriate microscopic vascular channels. The capillary arrangement creates numerous routes through which blood can pass in close contact with thin epithelial regions. Although the capillaries form a connected network rather than a set of isolated straight cylinders, considering several parallel pathways helps explain the low resistance of the whole bed.

In an idealised network of identical resistances connected in parallel, each path experiences the same pressure difference. The total flow is the sum of the flows through individual paths. If four paths each have a resistance of eight arbitrary units, each carries a flow proportional to the pressure difference divided by eight. The four together carry four times that flow; their equivalent resistance is two units. Closing three routes would raise the equivalent resistance back to eight. Actual pulmonary networks are more complex, with pressure-dependent vessel dimensions, interconnected pathways and variations in surrounding tissue forces. Nevertheless, the model explains why access to multiple perfused channels can reduce the resistance experienced by total pulmonary flow.

Distension and recruitment can alter the available exchange geometry as well as the haemodynamic resistance. A previously poorly perfused capillary region may begin carrying more blood, while expansion of a vessel's lumen can affect its flow and its contact with surrounding tissue. A rise in pulmonary blood flow therefore need not be accommodated entirely by a proportional rise in arterial pressure. The response depends on the initial state of the network and how much additional vascular capacity is available. At very high flows or in altered vascular tissue, the pressure response can differ from that predicted by a fixed-resistance model. These properties help connect the structure of the lung with cardiovascular adjustments during exercise.

Keeping capillary pressures relatively low is particularly advantageous because the partition across which gas moves is thin. Fluid moves through vascular boundaries in response to hydrostatic and osmotic forces, and lymphatic drainage removes much of the fluid that enters lung interstitium. If the vascular pressure environment or permeability changes sufficiently, filtration may exceed the ability of the tissue to remove excess fluid. The interstitial compartment can enlarge and liquid may reach alveolar spaces. The resulting impairment is not simply a consequence of increased blood volume; it depends on the balance of fluid entry, removal and barrier integrity.

Pulmonary and bronchial vessels should also be distinguished when reasoning about the metabolic needs of airway walls. A proximal bronchus contains epithelial cells, smooth muscle, glands and connective tissue that need a blood supply. These tissues are not automatically oxygenated adequately by exposure of the airway lumen to inspired air. The bronchial arteries provide a systemic nutritional supply to many of these structures. Within the distal lung, vascular connections and diffusion contribute to a more complex pattern of tissue oxygenation. The basic distinction remains that the pulmonary circuit is organised primarily for gas exchange with circulating blood, whereas the bronchial circulation is organised principally for the nutrition of supporting tissues.

The normal connection of some bronchial venous drainage to pulmonary veins introduces a small mixing component into arterial blood. Mixing must be analysed using oxygen content rather than merely averaging partial pressures, because haemoglobin binding is nonlinear. The quantitative treatment belongs to the oxygen transport and ventilation–perfusion chapters. Here the anatomical consequence is enough: blood reaching the left atrium is not necessarily composed exclusively of blood that has just crossed fully ventilated alveolar capillaries. The structure of the dual blood supply provides a physiological example of why the idealised pulmonary circuit is a useful model but not a perfectly isolated pathway.

11. Evidence-based integration and reasoning

The organisation of the lung is established by evidence collected at several scales. Dissection and anatomical imaging reveal relationships between lobes, airways and vascular roots. Histological staining reveals epithelial and connective tissue organisation. Electron microscopy reveals type I cell extensions, basement membranes, capillary endothelium and lamellar bodies within type II cells. A structural image is a snapshot, and sample preparation can change the dimensions of air spaces or distort delicate barriers. Comparisons must therefore control inflation state and preparation conditions rather than treating all images as direct measurements of the living lung.

Functional tests provide complementary evidence. A directional clearance experiment can follow movement of labelled material along a conducting airway. A surface film experiment can compare tension as a surfactant-containing interface is expanded and compressed. A tracer in the vascular circulation can establish the distribution of perfused channels. The principle of each test is to change or observe a defined variable while holding other relevant conditions comparable. The resulting data should be interpreted in terms of the tissue compartment and physical process being studied.

A useful example is a hypothetical set of lung tissue preparations having identical gas pressure gradients. Preparation A has normal septal area and thickness. Preparation B retains the same area but has twice the barrier thickness. Preparation C retains normal thickness but only half of the exchange area. Both B and C have one-half of the simplified diffusion conductance of A. Preparation D has both changes and one-quarter of the conductance. The experiment illustrates that a single functional measurement need not uniquely identify a structural defect. Histological or morphometric evidence can distinguish mechanisms that otherwise produce the same transfer rate.

A second comparison separates mechanical access to the exchange surface from the surface itself. In one preparation, the conducting airway supplying a region is obstructed but its alveolar walls and capillary network are intact. In another, airway access is normal but many alveolar septa are destroyed. Both may show poor transfer of oxygen, yet the first problem is failure to renew gas in an anatomically preserved region while the second involves loss of the available partition. An intervention that merely restores airflow could improve the first preparation more directly than the second. These are reasoning examples, not clinical treatment recommendations.

At the organ level, airflow, pleural mechanics, exchange geometry, surface stability and blood supply are mutually dependent. A perfectly thin alveolar epithelium is ineffective if no gas reaches its surface. A patent bronchial tree cannot oxygenate blood if perfusion fails. A well-perfused capillary network cannot maintain efficient transfer if the alveolar lining becomes a thick fluid layer. The major task of the next chapter is to explain how thoracic muscle activity, pressures, lung elastic properties and airway resistance determine whether inspired air reaches this carefully organised interface.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Arteries always carry oxygenated blood.

Correction: Arteries carry blood away from the heart; the pulmonary artery normally carries deoxygenated blood. Reasoning trap: The common systemic example is incorrectly generalised.

2. Terminal bronchioles exchange gas.

Correction: Alveoli first interrupt the walls of respiratory bronchioles. Reasoning trap: The similar names conceal a functional boundary.

3. Every airway contains cartilage.

Correction: Bronchioles lack cartilage and rely on tissue support and smooth muscle. Reasoning trap: A structural feature of the trachea is extrapolated distally.

4. Alveolar epithelium is ciliated pseudostratified epithelium.

Correction: The exchange surface is dominated by flattened type I cells. Reasoning trap: The epithelia of conducting and exchange zones are confused.

5. Type II cells cover most alveolar area.

Correction: Type I cells cover most area; type II cells secrete surfactant and support repair. Reasoning trap: Cell number and surface coverage are different quantities.

6. Pleural space is normally full of air.

Correction: It is a potential space containing a thin liquid film. Reasoning trap: The pleural and alveolar compartments are confused.

7. Pleural fluid and alveolar fluid are interchangeable terms.

Correction: They occupy separate compartments with different functions. Reasoning trap: The lung is treated as one undivided space.

8. Surfactant increases surface tension.

Correction: Surfactant lowers surface tension at the air–liquid interface. Reasoning trap: The effect of a surface-active molecule is reversed.

9. SP-A and SP-B have identical roles.

Correction: SP-A participates importantly in defence and homeostasis; SP-B supports film function. Reasoning trap: Shared names obscure distinct protein functions.

10. Macrophages beat cilia.

Correction: Macrophages phagocytose material; ciliated epithelial cells move mucus. Reasoning trap: Different modes of airway defence are combined.

11. A low-pressure circulation must carry little blood.

Correction: Flow also depends on resistance; pulmonary flow is large at low pressure difference. Reasoning trap: Pressure is mistaken for flow.

12. Bronchial arteries and pulmonary arteries are synonyms.

Correction: Bronchial arteries provide systemic nutritional supply; pulmonary arteries deliver blood for gas exchange. Reasoning trap: All vessels in a lung are assumed to serve the same role.

13. Alveoli are isolated spherical bubbles.

Correction: They share septa and mechanical support; the spherical model is an approximation. Reasoning trap: A simplified equation is mistaken for literal anatomy.

14. A larger anatomical exchange area always means greater transfer.

Correction: Functional transfer requires ventilation, perfusion and suitable gradients. Reasoning trap: Geometrical area is confused with effective exchange area.

15. A thicker barrier improves oxygen diffusion.

Correction: At fixed area and gradient, greater diffusion distance reduces transfer. Reasoning trap: Mechanical strength is confused with transport efficiency.

Original examination practice

1. Part B — Where does the respiratory zone begin?

Answer and explanation

Respiratory bronchioles. They contain alveoli in portions of their walls; terminal bronchioles are conducting only.

2. Part B — Which epithelial cell produces pulmonary surfactant?

Answer and explanation

Type II pneumocyte. Lamellar bodies store surfactant material for secretion. Type I cells primarily form the thin exchange surface.

3. Part B — Which pulmonary vessel carries blood away from the right ventricle?

Answer and explanation

Pulmonary artery via the pulmonary trunk. Arteries are defined by the direction of flow relative to the heart.

4. Part B — What separates visceral and parietal pleura?

Answer and explanation

A potential space containing a thin film of fluid. This film allows sliding; the space is not normally air-filled.

5. Part B — Which cell primarily engulfs particles in alveoli?

Answer and explanation

Alveolar macrophage. It phagocytoses particles; alveoli do not have a mucociliary blanket.

6. Part B — What feature distinguishes a bronchiole from a bronchus?

Answer and explanation

Absence of cartilage and submucosal glands. Bronchioles are supported by surrounding parenchyma and have smooth muscle.

7. Part B — Which two surfactant proteins are principally hydrophobic and promote lipid film function?

Answer and explanation

SP-B and SP-C. SP-A and SP-D are hydrophilic collectins with important defence roles.

8. Part B — What is the last wholly conducting airway?

Answer and explanation

Terminal bronchiole. Respiratory bronchioles have alveoli in their walls.

9. Part B — Which lung has a middle lobe in typical adult human anatomy?

Answer and explanation

Right lung. The left lingula is part of the superior lobe, not a separate middle lobe.

10. Part B — What tissue lines the pulmonary capillary lumen?

Answer and explanation

Capillary endothelium. It is distinct from the alveolar epithelial lining even where the basal laminae are closely apposed.

11. Part C — A preparation retains half the exchange area and has twice the barrier thickness. Predict its relative transfer rate if all other factors remain constant.

Answer and explanation

One quarter. In the simplified diffusion model, relative transfer is (1/2)/2 = 1/4. Equal pressure gradient and permeability are assumptions.

12. Part C — A tracer deposited on conducting airways remains stationary even though cilia appear structurally intact. Name two additional measurements that distinguish causes.

Answer and explanation

Measure beat function and airway surface hydration or mucus transport. Structural appearance does not establish motility; normal beating may also fail to move abnormal mucus.

13. Part C — Pulmonary blood flow is 5 L/min and pressure drop 10 mmHg. What happens to the drop at fixed flow if resistance doubles?

Answer and explanation

It doubles to 20 mmHg. Initially R = 2 mmHg·min/L; doubling R to 4 at fixed Q gives 20 mmHg.

14. Part C — A type II cell defect abolishes normal film secretion while type I area initially remains unchanged. Predict the most immediate structural-functional consequence.

Answer and explanation

Greater surface tension and reduced alveolar stability. The primary defect affects surface forces rather than initial geometric thickness, though secondary collapse can reduce effective area.

15. Part C — Two lungs have identical anatomical exchange area. One has no blood flow through half its capillary network. Are they functionally equivalent?

Answer and explanation

No. An anatomical surface without sufficient perfusion cannot deliver oxygen effectively to circulating blood.

16. Part C — A group measures twice as many epithelial nuclei of one type as another and concludes that it covers twice the area. Is the inference justified?

Answer and explanation

No. Type I cells have wide thin extensions; cell counts cannot establish surface area without measurements of cellular geometry.

17. Part C — An ideal branch network contains 64 tubes of radius one-quarter of the parent. What is aggregate area relative to the parent?

Answer and explanation

Four times. Each area is one-sixteenth of the parent; 64/16 = 4. This does not by itself prove a fourfold flow.

18. Part C — Air enters the pleural cavity while alveolar surfactant remains normal. Identify the primary disturbance.

Answer and explanation

Impaired mechanical coupling between thoracic wall and lung. The lung can recoil despite intact film chemistry; the pleural space is not the exchange lumen.

19. Part C — A selective macrophage defect increases persistent alveolar particles but surfactant secretion is unchanged. Which compartment is primarily impaired?

Answer and explanation

Alveolar immune clearance. The data separate initial phagocytic impairment from the surface film and proximal ciliary apparatus.

20. Part C — An airway section lacks cartilage and has cuboidal epithelium, but alveolar openings interrupt its wall. Identify the structure.

Answer and explanation

Respiratory bronchiole. Absence of cartilage supports bronchiolar identity; alveolar openings identify the transition to the respiratory zone.

21. Part B — Which structure is lined by mesothelium: trachea, pleural membrane, alveolar epithelium or bronchial lumen?

Answer and explanation

Pleural membrane. Mesothelium covers serous surfaces; airways have epithelial linings and the alveolar air-facing layer contains pneumocytes.

22. Part B — Identify the anatomical unit distal to a terminal bronchiole.

Answer and explanation

Pulmonary acinus. It comprises respiratory bronchioles and distal alveolated structures supplied by the terminal bronchiole.

23. Part B — Which structure contains lamellar bodies?

Answer and explanation

Type II pneumocyte. These organelles store and release lipid-rich surfactant material.

24. Part B — Which epithelial cells become prominent in bronchioles and participate in protection and repair?

Answer and explanation

Club cells. They are nonciliated secretory bronchiolar cells, distinct from alveolar type II cells.

25. Part B — What defines the carina?

Answer and explanation

The ridge at the tracheal bifurcation. It marks division into the main bronchi, not division into alveolar sacs.

26. Part B — Which layer directly covers the external lung surface?

Answer and explanation

Visceral pleura. Parietal pleura lines thoracic boundaries and is continuous with visceral pleura around the root.

27. Part B — What is the principal surface-active lipid commonly associated with pulmonary surfactant?

Answer and explanation

Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine. It is a phospholipid capable of lowering air–liquid interfacial tension; haemoglobin and collagen are not surfactant lipids.

28. Part B — What is the predominant direction of mucociliary clearance from the tracheobronchial tree?

Answer and explanation

Towards the pharynx. Coordinated ciliary activity moves mucus proximally, rather than delivering it into alveoli.

29. Part B — Which pulmonary structure contains the capillary network adjacent to alveolar epithelium?

Answer and explanation

Interalveolar septa. These shared walls contain epithelium, connective tissue and capillaries.

30. Part B — Which term describes the volume of air in conducting passages not directly exchanging gas?

Answer and explanation

Anatomical dead space. It is a functional gas volume, not a statement that the conducting passages have no biological role.

31. Part B — What distinguishes a pulmonary venule from a pulmonary arteriole in the exchange circuit?

Answer and explanation

A venule drains the capillary network towards pulmonary veins; an arteriole supplies it. Vessel naming refers to flow direction rather than oxygen content.

32. Part B — What feature allows the tracheal wall adjacent to the oesophagus to deform more readily?

Answer and explanation

Incomplete cartilage rings with a posterior membranous wall and trachealis. A completely rigid circumferential skeleton would reduce flexibility.

33. Part B — What does pseudostratified mean in a respiratory epithelium?

Answer and explanation

Nuclei occur at different heights, but the cells contact the basal lamina. The appearance of multiple layers does not make it a true stratified epithelium.

34. Part B — Are pleural fluid and surfactant secreted into the same compartment?

Answer and explanation

No. Pleural liquid occupies the pleural interface; surfactant is released into the alveolar lining fluid.

35. Part B — What defines an alveolar pore of Kohn?

Answer and explanation

A connection between adjacent alveolar air spaces. It is not a channel connecting capillary blood with alveolar gas.

36. Part C — A scientist obtains two tissue samples. One has a thin squamous lining and dense capillary network; the other has cartilage and a pseudostratified ciliated lining. Which is better suited to gas transfer and why?

Answer and explanation

The first. It has the short diffusion distance and vascular proximity characteristic of exchange tissue; the second specialises in conduction.

37. Part C — Two preparations have identical surface tension and radius 100 and 200 micrometres in an ideal spherical model. Compare their pressure differences.

Answer and explanation

The smaller model sphere has twice the pressure difference. ΔP = 2γ/r, so doubling radius halves pressure at fixed tension. Real alveoli are mechanically interconnected.

38. Part C — A group applies a treatment that restores normal ciliary beat frequency but tracer movement remains poor. What alternative mechanism should be investigated?

Answer and explanation

Abnormal mucus hydration or rheology, or poor beat coordination. Frequency alone does not establish effective directional transport; examine the material and waveform.

39. Part C — A respiratory bronchiole supplies three equal downstream regions. One is no longer ventilated, while perfusion and the other two regions are unchanged. What fraction of that local anatomical exchange area remains accessible to fresh gas in this simplified model?

Answer and explanation

Two-thirds. Only two of three equal territories remain accessible. This is not necessarily two-thirds of whole-lung oxygen transfer.

40. Part C — Pulmonary flow is 4 L/min at a pressure difference of 8 mmHg. It becomes 6 L/min at 9 mmHg. Compute and compare effective resistances.

Answer and explanation

2 and 1.5 mmHg·min/L. R = ΔP/Q. The calculated fall is compatible with recruitment or distension, but does not prove either uniquely.

41. Part C — A fluorescent particle signal remains associated with macrophages after washing. Why is that not proof of ingestion?

Answer and explanation

Material may still be attached to the external membrane. Use a method distinguishing extracellular from intracellular fluorescence and suitable controls before inferring phagocytosis.

42. Part C — A tissue preparation has equal sequential layer resistances of 1 and 1 units. One rises to 3 units. Calculate the relative transfer at fixed driving force.

Answer and explanation

One-half. The original total is 2; new total 4. Relative rate is 2/4; sequential layer resistances add.

43. Part C — At atmospheric and alveolar gauge pressure zero, pleural pressure changes from −6 to −3 cm water. What is the change in transpulmonary pressure?

Answer and explanation

It decreases from +6 to +3 cm water. Ptrans = Palv − Ppl. At both instants there is no atmospheric-to-alveolar pressure gradient.

44. Part C — Two samples have identical lipid content, but one cannot achieve low surface tension during compression. Which claim is disproved?

Answer and explanation

That lipid quantity alone determines surfactant performance. Composition, protein-associated film organisation and dynamics can differ despite equal lipid mass.

45. Part C — A lung has normal bronchial arterial blood flow but absent pulmonary arterial flow. Can systemic bronchial flow substitute for the normal exchange circuit?

Answer and explanation

No. Bronchial blood mainly supplies airway-associated tissues; it cannot replace the high-flow alveolar capillary route.

46. Part C — A micrograph shows more type II cell nuclei than type I cell nuclei. What extra observation is needed to determine which type covers more surface?

Answer and explanation

A measurement of exposed cell area or surface coverage. Nuclear counts do not capture the broad attenuated extensions of type I cells.

47. Part C — In an experiment on barrier injury, alveolar tracer appears in blood. What two components of the normal partition should be examined?

Answer and explanation

The alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial barriers, including their associated extracellular layers. Tracer movement could reflect abnormal permeability at either component or both; it is not evidence of a pleural defect by itself.

48. Part C — A lung develops extensive septal destruction and loses elastic attachments to bronchioles. Predict two distinct functional consequences.

Answer and explanation

Reduced exchange area and reduced airway support. The same structural loss affects diffusion geometry and airway patency by different mechanisms.

49. Part C — A region retains all its septa but its alveoli become liquid-filled. Why can its anatomical area remain constant while effective exchange falls?

Answer and explanation

Gas access decreases and diffusion through liquid becomes less favourable. Anatomical area is not the same as ventilated, functional area.

50. Part C — Investigators compare epithelial repair after injury and observe a transient increase in cuboidal cells, followed by restoration of thin squamous lining. Does the sequence alone establish lineage?

Answer and explanation

No. The observation is consistent with repair involving type II cells, but specific lineage evidence is needed to establish cell ancestry.

51. State SET / application — Assertion: The conducting zone is essential even though it contains anatomical dead space. Reason: It distributes, conditions and protects inspired gas before it reaches the exchange surface.

Answer and explanation

Both true; the reason explains the assertion. The term dead space describes lack of direct gas exchange, not absence of physiological function.

52. State SET / application — Assertion: The pulmonary artery is a vein because it carries oxygen-poor blood. Reason: Arteries and veins are named by flow direction relative to the heart.

Answer and explanation

Assertion false; reason true. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle, and therefore remains an artery.

53. State SET / application — Arrange from proximal to distal: alveolar duct, terminal bronchiole, lobar bronchus, respiratory bronchiole.

Answer and explanation

Lobar bronchus → terminal bronchiole → respiratory bronchiole → alveolar duct. The conducting tree precedes the alveolated transition and distal ducts.

54. State SET / application — An ideal spherical interface has γ = 0.010 N/m and r = 200 µm. Compute its Laplace pressure difference.

Answer and explanation

100 Pa. r = 0.0002 m; 2γ/r = 0.020/0.0002 = 100 Pa. The single-interface spherical model is assumed.

55. State SET / application — Which pair is correctly matched: type I–lamellar body secretion; type II–surfactant secretion; macrophage–ciliary beating; parietal pleura–alveolar epithelium?

Answer and explanation

Type II–surfactant secretion. The other options misassign structures or functions to different cells or compartments.

Revision and mastery

The conducting system conditions and distributes air; respiratory bronchioles mark the beginning of alveolar exchange. Pleural coupling allows the thorax to expand the lung. Type I cells minimise diffusion distance; type II cells produce surfactant and support epithelial renewal. Pulmonary vessels carry a high flow at low pressure, while bronchial vessels supply conducting tissues. Test mastery by explaining each connection without relying on a memorised list.

  1. Trace inspired air from external nares to alveoli.
  2. Explain the difference between anatomical and functional airway boundaries.
  3. Identify cartilage and epithelial changes from trachea to bronchiole.
  4. Explain how mucus hydration and cilia cooperate.
  5. Contrast ciliated cells and alveolar macrophages.
  6. Describe pleural coupling and the consequence of air entering the pleural space.
  7. Compare type I and type II pneumocytes.
  8. Explain why surfactant lowers the pressure required in an idealised air space.
  9. Trace oxygen across the blood–air barrier.
  10. Compare pulmonary and bronchial circulations.
  11. Explain why identical transfer rates do not identify identical tissue defects.

Mastery checklist

  • Trace both air and blood pathways without confusing vessels and airways.
  • Recognise conducting, transitional and alveolar tissue from structural evidence.
  • Distinguish pleural fluid, lining fluid and septal interstitial fluid.
  • Explain surface tension and the limits of an isolated sphere model.
  • Predict the consequences of area, thickness, perfusion or clearance changes and state assumptions.
  • Explain which evidence can discriminate two mechanisms yielding similar functional results.

Key quantitative relationships

Area of a circular lumen = πr²; transpulmonary pressure = alveolar minus pleural pressure; ideal spherical interfacial pressure difference = 2γ/r; simple diffusion transfer ∝ AΔP/T at fixed permeability; vascular resistance = pressure difference/flow. Apply consistent units and identify the idealisations before using any relationship.
