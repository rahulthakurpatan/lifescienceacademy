1. The physical problem of ventilating a lung

A mammalian lung exchanges oxygen and carbon dioxide with the blood, but the gas within its alveoli cannot be left permanently in contact with the same blood supply. Oxygen would be progressively removed and carbon dioxide would accumulate. Ventilation renews the gas available for diffusion by moving air between the environment and the respiratory tract. Unlike diffusion, which describes random molecular motion producing net movement down a concentration or partial-pressure gradient, ventilation is bulk flow: a mixture of gases moves together because of a difference in total pressure.

The distinction matters when an animal is able to expand its lungs but unable to exchange gases efficiently, or when the exchange membrane is intact but air cannot reach it. A mechanically normal respiratory system can still have a defect in blood flow or membrane diffusion. Conversely, a mechanically obstructed airway can prevent the replenishment of an otherwise functional gas-exchange surface. The mechanics of breathing is concerned principally with the physical processes that generate bulk flow. Later chapters examine the consequences of that flow for alveolar ventilation, oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide removal.

An isolated lung has no skeletal muscles within its alveolar walls that can pull each alveolus open on command. Instead, the thoracic cage and diaphragm act as a ventilatory pump, with the pleural membranes transmitting changes in the geometry of the thorax to the lungs. The conducting airways form the route along which air moves; the distal respiratory structures form a deformable gas reservoir. The system consequently resembles a bellows coupled to elastic tissue by a fluid interface, rather than a rigid pump. The analogy is limited because the lung is heterogeneous, vascularised, branched, covered by a dynamic surface film and subject to changing mechanical stresses.

Three mechanical demands must be met. First, the respiratory muscles must deform the lung and chest wall against their elastic properties. Second, they must create enough pressure difference to overcome resistance as gas moves through the airways. Third, under rapidly changing conditions, they must accelerate gas and tissues. The third contribution is relatively small in ordinary quiet breathing but becomes more relevant at high frequencies. The first two demands dominate many elementary experiments. Separating them is useful because an increase in resistance and an increase in stiffness may both make breathing difficult while producing different patterns of volume and airflow.

Imagine two expandable bags, each attached to a tube. The first has stiff walls and a wide tube; the second has flexible walls and a narrow tube. If air is delivered at a fixed rate, the first eventually needs a large pressure to maintain a large volume even after flow has stopped. The second needs extra pressure mainly while air is moving. This distinction anticipates static compliance and flow resistance. In a living lung the two properties interact: expansion can widen airways through tissue traction, and obstruction can alter the volume at which breathing occurs.

Pressure is force per unit area. The SI unit is the pascal, equal to one newton per square metre. Respiratory mechanics commonly uses centimetres of water because the relevant differences during quiet breathing are small. One centimetre of water corresponds to approximately 98.1 Pa. A pressure difference of only one centimetre of water is quite sufficient to generate appreciable respiratory flow through healthy airways. It is not necessary to generate a pressure difference comparable to the full atmospheric pressure.

It is essential to identify the pressure reference before assigning a sign. Atmospheric pressure may be approximately 760 mmHg at sea level, varying with weather and altitude. In a mechanical breathing diagram it is convenient to set the local atmosphere to zero, so that pressures below atmospheric are negative and pressures above it are positive. Negative gauge pressure does not mean physically negative absolute pressure. A pressure of minus five centimetres of water represents a small reduction from ambient absolute pressure. This distinction will be essential when Boyle's law is used, because that law requires absolute pressure.

2. Muscles, ribs and the mechanically coupled pleural surfaces

The diaphragm separates the thoracic and abdominal cavities. At rest it forms a dome, with muscular fibres extending towards its central tendon. Contraction shortens those fibres and usually moves the central tendon caudally, increasing thoracic volume. The abdominal contents are displaced and abdominal pressure generally rises. The lower ribs can also move outward because of the geometry of diaphragmatic attachment. Thus diaphragmatic contraction is not a simple vertical piston movement, even though the piston model captures an important component of its action.

The rib cage changes its anteroposterior and transverse dimensions. Elevation of the upper ribs and sternum increases the anteroposterior dimension; elevation of the lower ribs contributes to lateral expansion. These movements are often described as pump-handle and bucket-handle movements. They are geometric descriptions, not distinct types of muscle. External intercostal muscles assist inspiratory expansion and stabilise the intercostal spaces. Their mechanical effect depends on the orientation of the fibres, the rib level and the prevailing shape of the chest wall.

The diaphragm receives motor innervation from the phrenic nerves, arising mainly from cervical spinal segments C3 to C5. Intercostal nerves provide motor input to the intercostal muscles. These anatomical facts connect neural activation to the mechanical events of inspiration without requiring the detailed respiratory rhythm circuitry studied in the regulation chapter. A reduction in phrenic motor output can reduce diaphragmatic force while leaving airway calibre and lung elastic tissue initially unchanged. The resulting defect is a failure of pressure generation rather than an intrinsic loss of compliance.

During quiet inspiration, inspiratory muscles contract and do mechanical work. At the end of inspiration the newly expanded lung stores elastic energy, much of which is recovered during the following expiration. Quiet expiration is largely passive in a healthy resting adult: inspiratory activation declines, the lung recoils, and air leaves. This does not imply that no neural activity or muscular tension exists during expiration. Post-inspiratory activity, laryngeal behaviour and posture can influence the rate of emptying. The basic distinction is that active expiratory muscle contraction is not normally needed for ordinary tidal breathing.

When ventilation increases, accessory inspiratory muscles can assist by lifting or stabilising the thoracic cage. The sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles are representative examples. During active expiration the abdominal muscles raise abdominal pressure and push the diaphragm cranially, while the expiratory portions of the internal intercostals depress the ribs. Interosseous internal intercostal fibres commonly aid expiration; parasternal interchondral portions can assist inspiration. Treating every internal intercostal fibre as a uniform expiratory motor is therefore an oversimplification.

The visceral pleura lies against the lung and the parietal pleura lines the thoracic wall. A small quantity of pleural liquid separates the membranes. It lubricates their relative motion and transmits mechanical forces, allowing the lung to follow expansion of the thoracic enclosure. The two surfaces are not fused. They can slide as the chest wall changes shape, but their separation is normally strongly resisted by the mechanics of the closed pleural space. An everyday approximation is two wet glass plates that slide along one another more readily than they separate; the living pleural space is more complex because pressure, fluid turnover, geometry and tissue elasticity also matter.

If the pleural cavity communicates freely with the atmosphere, its pressure can approach atmospheric pressure. The normal distending pressure across the lung is reduced and the affected lung tends to recoil. The chest wall may move outward towards its own unstressed configuration. This situation, pneumothorax, reveals that the lung and chest wall normally exert opposing elastic forces. It is not principally a failure of oxygen diffusion through an alveolus, nor is it simply a hole in a conducting airway. The essential mechanical defect is disruption of the pressure relationship across the lung. The extent of collapse depends on the communication, geometry, adhesions and pressure conditions.

The normal pleural pressure is below atmospheric near resting lung volume, but not because the pleural cavity is a vacuum. The inward elastic tendency of the lung and the outward tendency of the chest wall establish a subatmospheric pressure in the fluid-containing pleural space. Its magnitude varies with position and gravity. Pleural pressure is generally more negative at the apex than the base of an upright lung, contributing to regional differences in resting expansion. A single pressure value in a textbook diagram is a representative simplification rather than the literal pressure everywhere.

3. Pressure differences through a complete quiet breath

Let Pao denote pressure at the airway opening, Palv alveolar pressure and Ppl pleural pressure. At an open mouth or nose, Pao is approximately the local atmospheric pressure, defined here as zero gauge. Transpulmonary pressure PL is Palv minus Ppl. It is the distending pressure across the lung as a whole. The pressure difference that drives bulk airflow, however, is Pao minus Palv for inspiratory flow. These are distinct differences across distinct physical structures.

At the end of quiet expiration, the airway is open and there is no net flow in the idealised model. Palv equals Pao, approximately zero. A representative pleural pressure is minus five centimetres of water, so PL equals zero minus negative five, or positive five centimetres of water. The lung is distended even though there is no airflow. The resting volume at this equilibrium is called functional residual capacity, whose measurement and subdivision into component volumes are considered in the next chapter.

Inspiratory muscle contraction enlarges the thoracic cavity. Pleural pressure becomes more negative, increasing the distending pressure on the lung. As lung volume begins to increase, alveolar pressure falls slightly below atmospheric pressure. Air flows inward down the resulting pressure gradient. A representative early inspiratory moment could have Palv equal to minus one centimetre of water and Ppl equal to minus seven, yielding a positive transpulmonary pressure of six. The negative alveolar pressure drives entry; the positive transpulmonary pressure maintains distension.

As inspiration proceeds, incoming air increases the number of gas molecules inside the expanding lung. Alveolar pressure therefore does not continue falling indefinitely. The relationship between volume change, gas entry and the changing muscular effort determines the instantaneous pressure. Near the end of inspiration the flow slows, and when it stops Palv again equals atmospheric pressure. The expanded lung still has a higher transpulmonary pressure than before inspiration, perhaps eight centimetres of water in an illustrative cycle. Equal alveolar and atmospheric pressures do not mean equal lung volumes at the two no-flow moments: pleural pressure differs.

Once inspiratory muscular activity declines, the distending effect of the thoracic muscles decreases and elastic recoil reduces lung volume. Compression of alveolar gas raises Palv slightly above atmospheric pressure, for example plus one centimetre of water during passive expiration. Air then flows outward. Pleural pressure becomes less negative than at the end of inspiration, but usually remains negative during quiet expiration. Positive alveolar pressure and negative pleural pressure can therefore coexist. Substituting these values into PL shows why the lung remains distended while air leaves.

At the end of expiration the respiratory system approaches its equilibrium resting volume. Flow ceases again and alveolar pressure returns to atmospheric pressure. In a normal resting cycle the end-expiratory lung does not empty. A substantial volume remains because the balance of elastic forces is reached at a volume greater than zero. The sequence is cyclic: muscle action changes pleural pressure, pleural pressure changes lung distension, lung volume changes create a small alveolar pressure deviation, and airflow reduces that deviation.

A pressure recording can therefore be interpreted without memorising a set of arbitrary signs. First ask where the pressure is measured. Next ask whether flow is inward, outward or absent. Finally subtract the relevant pressures. If an illustrative record shows Palv equal to plus two and Ppl equal to minus four, the transpulmonary pressure is plus six. If the airway opening is at atmospheric pressure, flow is outward. A positive PL does not specify the direction of airflow.

During a breath with an occluded airway, the relationship changes. An inspiratory effort may lower alveolar pressure substantially while no gas enters because the opening is blocked. The equality between alveolar and atmospheric pressure at zero flow therefore requires a patent airway and enough time for pressure equilibration. An occluded airway is a useful experimental counterexample to the common assumption that zero flow always implies zero alveolar gauge pressure. A pressure gradient may exist without flow when resistance is effectively infinite.

4. Boyle's law, an open airway and gas compressibility

Robert Boyle's seventeenth-century experiments established an inverse relationship between gas pressure and volume under controlled conditions. In a simple ideal gas model, PV equals nRT, where P is absolute pressure, V volume, n amount of gas in moles, R the gas constant and T absolute temperature. If the amount of gas and temperature are constant, nRT is constant. Thus P1V1 equals P2V2. The inverse relationship is nonlinear: doubling the volume halves the absolute pressure, not the gauge pressure.

The connection to breathing is most direct during a short interval in which lung volume changes before much gas has entered or left. If a sealed gas compartment is expanded isothermally, molecular collisions against its walls become less frequent per unit area and the pressure falls. If it is compressed, the pressure rises. In an intact open respiratory system, pressure changes drive flow which simultaneously changes the number of gas molecules. Boyle's law explains the direction of the initial tendency but cannot be used as though the entire tidal breath were an expansion of a fixed quantity of gas.

For a worked example, suppose a hypothetical sealed compartment contains three litres of air at an absolute pressure of 760 mmHg and its volume increases to 3.01 litres without a temperature change. The final absolute pressure is 760 times 3 divided by 3.01, approximately 757.48 mmHg. The gauge pressure relative to the original atmosphere is approximately minus 2.52 mmHg, or minus 3.43 centimetres of water. Even this small relative volume change creates an appreciable gauge-pressure difference in the closed system. The values are illustrative, not measurements of a normal breath.

Now consider mistakenly substituting a gauge pressure of zero into P1V1. The predicted final pressure would remain zero for any volume, implying no pressure change. The error arises because an ambient-referenced zero does not represent an absence of molecular collisions. Absolute pressure must be used in the gas equation. Gauge pressures, on the other hand, can be directly subtracted from one another to obtain pressure differences across anatomical structures, because the common atmospheric reference cancels.

During quiet breathing, alveolar gas is humidified and exchanges oxygen and carbon dioxide with pulmonary capillary blood. Temperature is approximately maintained near body temperature. These conditions do not make the ideal gas law useless, but they mean that the lung is not the sealed isothermal gas chamber of a classical experiment. The extent of gas compression, the flow resistance and the movement of the lung boundary must be considered together. Gas flow can be modelled with a pressure difference divided by resistance, whereas tissue deformation is modelled by compliance.

A bell-jar demonstration can illustrate the mechanism: an expandable bag connected to the atmosphere is housed within a chamber whose lower boundary can be displaced. Lowering that boundary increases the external chamber volume and reduces pressure surrounding the bag; the bag expands and air enters through its tube. The experiment establishes that changing the surrounding pressure can inflate a compliant gas reservoir. It does not reproduce the pleural liquid interface, alveolar surface tension, pulmonary circulation, rib mechanics or neural feedback. It should therefore be used to identify the direction of pressure changes, not to calculate physiological compliance from the properties of a rubber balloon.

5. Compliance and elastic recoil: separate lung and chest wall

Compliance measures how much volume changes per unit change in the distending pressure. Lung compliance CL equals delta V divided by delta PL. It may be expressed in litres per centimetre of water, or millilitres per centimetre of water. Compliance is a slope measured over a specified range or at a specified operating volume. The reciprocal, elastance E, expresses pressure change per unit volume change. High elastance means a stiff structure; high compliance means a readily expandable structure.

If an increase in transpulmonary pressure from five to eight centimetres of water produces a lung-volume increment of 0.6 litres, the mean compliance over that interval is 0.6 divided by three, or 0.2 L per centimetre of water. The corresponding elastance is five centimetres of water per litre. If a second lung expands by only 0.3 litres over the same interval, its compliance is 0.1 and its elastance is ten. The second lung requires twice the pressure increment to achieve the same volume increment within the assumed linear range.

Elastic recoil is the tendency of a deformed structure to return towards a lower-energy configuration. It is not synonymous with compliance. A compliant lung can expand easily and exhibit reduced restoring pressure at a given expansion, whereas a stiff lung can have a high restoring pressure. A rubber band analogy is often misleading because in everyday language a highly stretchable object is called elastic, while physiological elastance describes resistance to expansion. Use the measured pressure-volume slope to avoid the ambiguity.

The lung's elastic behaviour derives from the arrangement of its extracellular matrix and the surface forces at air-liquid interfaces. Elastin fibres support reversible deformation; collagen fibres increasingly bear load as tissue is stretched. At relatively high lung volume additional recruitment and straightening of collagen structures limit distension, so the pressure-volume curve becomes flatter when volume is on the vertical axis. At very low volumes, closure and reopening of lung units can also influence the slope. Compliance is therefore not a universal material constant for the lung.

The chest wall has its own resting configuration. At typical resting lung volume it tends to expand outward; at sufficiently high volumes it tends to recoil inward. Define chest-wall distending pressure Pcw as pleural pressure minus pressure at the body surface, in the idealised passive model. The total pressure across the passive respiratory system is Palv minus body-surface pressure. Adding PL and Pcw cancels Ppl. At a static equilibrium with airway and body surface exposed to the atmosphere, the total pressure is zero even though the individual lung and chest-wall pressures are not zero.

Because both components undergo approximately the same volume change, their pressure increments add. The combined compliance Crs satisfies one over Crs equals one over CL plus one over Ccw. If both components individually have compliance 0.2 L per centimetre of water, the reciprocal values are each five and their sum is ten; combined compliance is 0.1 L per centimetre of water. It is incorrect to add the two compliances directly as though the same pressure independently inflated two parallel reservoirs. For this coupled series mechanical load, the same volume change is associated with the sum of two pressure increments.

The equilibrium volume can shift when either structure changes its recoil curve. Loss of inward lung recoil shifts the balance towards a greater volume, provided the chest-wall curve is unchanged. Increased lung stiffness can shift the balance towards a smaller volume. A chest-wall mechanical limitation can likewise restrict expansion even when the lung's intrinsic pressure-volume curve remains normal. These predictions show why measurements of total respiratory-system compliance alone do not identify the anatomical source of stiffness.

6. Pressure-volume curves, hysteresis and measurement

A static pressure-volume curve is constructed by measuring volume at a series of equilibrated pressures. One can inflate an isolated lung in steps and record the pressure needed to sustain each volume after flow has ceased, then repeat during deflation. At a given transpulmonary pressure, the deflating lung generally has a larger volume than the inflating lung. The difference between the two paths is called hysteresis. The history of the mechanical system influences its present state.

On a plot with pressure on the horizontal axis and volume on the vertical axis, the local slope represents compliance. A steep segment corresponds to a large volume change for a small pressure change. If the axes are reversed, the slope instead represents elastance. Examination questions commonly exploit this reversal. The direction of traversal must also be recognised: inflation proceeds towards larger volumes, whereas deflation proceeds towards smaller volumes. A curve alone without axis labels or direction cannot support an unambiguous interpretation.

Several effects contribute to hysteresis. Lung recruitment means the opening of units that were previously closed or poorly aerated; derecruitment is the converse. Opening pressures can exceed closing pressures. Surface film behaviour also differs during expansion and compression. Tissue viscoelasticity contributes time-dependent deformation. The relative importance of each mechanism depends on volume range, prior inflation history, measurement conditions and the preparation. It is therefore incorrect to attribute every observed loop exclusively to airway resistance or to surfactant.

Dynamic measurements are performed during flow. The pressure required at a given instant includes both the elastic load and the pressure losses associated with flow. A measurement that does not distinguish flow-related pressure from distending pressure can yield an apparent compliance lower than static compliance. At higher breathing frequencies, regions with high resistance may have insufficient time to fill, causing frequency-dependent dynamic compliance. A static measurement after sufficient equilibration helps separate tissue stiffness from delayed filling.

An oesophageal balloon pressure recording can approximate pleural pressure changes in an intact animal. Combined with airflow and airway pressure, it allows estimation of transpulmonary pressure and breathing effort. The oesophageal pressure is not exactly the pleural pressure at every lung region. Posture, local surrounding structures, balloon positioning and calibration affect the relationship. A student should regard the measurement as a useful estimate with a defined domain of validity rather than as a perfect direct recording of all regional pressures.

In a controlled pressure-flow experiment, compare two hypothetical conditions with the same delivered volume of 0.5 L and the same constant flow of 0.5 L per second. In condition A the airway pressure peaks at 15 and falls to 10 centimetres of water during a no-flow hold. In condition B the peak is 20 but the plateau remains 10. If baseline pressure, muscle activity and other assumptions are matched, the extra peak pressure in condition B suggests a larger resistive pressure loss rather than an increase in static elastic load. Under the simple model, the resistances estimated from peak-minus-plateau divided by flow are ten and twenty centimetres of water seconds per litre. These numbers describe a hypothetical passive inflation test, not normal spontaneous breathing.

7. Surface tension, alveolar geometry and surfactant

A thin aqueous lining covers the alveolar epithelium. Water molecules attract one another through intermolecular forces, and creating new air-water interfacial area requires energy. Surface tension is the reversible work per unit new surface area under specified conditions, equivalently a force per unit length. Its SI unit is N per metre. A curved interface with surface tension creates a pressure difference. The smaller the radius of curvature, the larger the pressure difference for a given constant tension.

For an ideal spherical gas space surrounded by liquid with a single air-liquid interface, the Laplace relation is delta P equals 2T divided by r. It follows by balancing pressure work during expansion against the increase in interfacial energy: dV is 4 pi r squared dr and dA is 8 pi r dr, so delta P equals T dA/dV, giving 2T/r. This derivation assumes a spherical interface, uniform tension and a quasistatic displacement. A soap bubble with two interfaces requires a different factor, so four T divided by radius should not be substituted for the one-interface model.

Consider two ideal gas spaces with radii 100 and 200 micrometres and a constant surface tension of 0.025 N/m. The smaller space has a calculated pressure difference of 500 Pa, or approximately 5.10 centimetres of water. The larger has 250 Pa, approximately 2.55 centimetres of water. If the spaces communicate with otherwise identical external pressures and no supporting structures, gas tends to move from the higher-pressure smaller space towards the larger. This hypothetical result illustrates a destabilising tendency of constant surface tension.

Real alveoli are not isolated spherical balloons. Their septa are shared, their geometry is irregular, and a continuous tissue network distributes stresses. Pulmonary surfactant alters the tension at the air-liquid interface, and its surface behaviour depends on composition, area and history. The simple Laplace model is therefore a way of identifying how curvature and tension influence pressure, not an exact equation for predicting pressure differences between every pair of alveoli.

Type II alveolar epithelial cells produce pulmonary surfactant, a mixture enriched in phospholipids including dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine and containing specific proteins. Material is stored in lamellar bodies before release onto the alveolar surface. Hydrophilic portions interact with the aqueous lining, and hydrophobic portions favour orientation towards the gas phase. The interfacial film reduces the energetic cost of expanding air-liquid area compared with a clean aqueous interface. Surfactant-associated proteins contribute to film formation and stability; the immune roles of some surfactant proteins are distinct from their mechanical effects.

As an alveolar surface contracts, film components can become more densely packed and effective surface tension can fall substantially. This behaviour can counteract the effect of decreasing radius on the Laplace pressure. For an ideal example, if radius halves while tension also halves, the ratio T/r remains unchanged. The smaller space need not have a greater Laplace pressure in this model. Actual film behaviour is more complicated than a universal proportionality between radius and tension, and neighbouring tissue support further stabilises real alveoli.

The mechanical importance of the interface was demonstrated in classical experiments by Kurt von Neergaard in 1929. By comparing pressure-volume behaviour of an isolated lung expanded with gas and with liquid, he found that liquid filling substantially reduced the pressure needed for expansion. Removing the alveolar air-liquid interface reduced the surface contribution to recoil. A useful control is the same preparation or comparable tissue under matched volume and loading conditions; residual recoil in the liquid-filled lung shows that tissue elasticity still contributes. The finding does not imply that surface tension is the only determinant of lung mechanics.

In 1959, Mary Ellen Avery and Jere Mead reported evidence of deficient surface-active material in lung extracts from infants with hyaline membrane disease. The comparison linked the mechanical problem of neonatal lung expansion to the composition and behaviour of the lung surface film. Subsequent research established the importance of surfactant in reducing collapse and lowering inflation work. The history illustrates how an observation at the organ level, followed by measurement of interfacial properties, can identify a molecular contribution to physiology. The details of neonatal care are beyond the present mechanical scope.

Surfactant deficiency can decrease compliance, raise the pressure needed to recruit collapsed regions and increase the work of inflation. This does not mean that surfactant creates gas flow by acting as a respiratory muscle. The driving pressure gradient still results from mechanical deformation or external ventilation. Surfactant modifies the load against which that process occurs. Its contribution to alveolar stability and reduced surface forces connects the cell biology of type II pneumocytes with the organ-level mechanics of breathing.

8. Airway resistance, radius and flow patterns

The movement of gas through the respiratory passages encounters resistance. For a simple linear model, flow Q equals the pressure difference across the airway divided by resistance R. If Q is measured in litres per second and the pressure difference in centimetres of water, R has units of centimetres of water seconds per litre. This resistance is not identical to tissue elastance. A narrow airway can impose a large pressure loss during flow while contributing no pressure loss from viscous flow when the gas is static.

For steady, fully developed laminar flow of an incompressible Newtonian fluid through a straight rigid circular tube, Poiseuille's relation gives Q equals pi r to the fourth power times pressure difference divided by 8 viscosity times tube length. Resistance is therefore 8 viscosity times length divided by pi r to the fourth power. The formula demonstrates why small radius changes strongly affect resistance. Its assumptions are important: respiratory air is compressible, airways branch and deform, flow is pulsatile, and turbulent or developing flow can occur.

If the radius of an ideal tube decreases from two millimetres to one millimetre while length and viscosity are unchanged, the predicted resistance increases by a factor of sixteen. With the same pressure difference, flow becomes one sixteenth of its initial value. This does not mean that narrowing one bronchiole halves the radius of the entire respiratory system or increases total airway resistance sixteenfold. The affected airway belongs to a branched network, and the result depends on where and how extensively narrowing occurs.

Resistances in series add because the same flow passes through each segment and pressure losses accumulate. Parallel resistances combine reciprocally because separate pathways share a pressure difference and their flows add. The many small peripheral airways have an enormous collective cross-sectional area and provide numerous parallel routes. Although each small airway individually may have appreciable resistance, their combined resistance can be modest in a healthy lung. A substantial loss of parallel pathways or widespread small airway narrowing changes this relationship.

Laminar flow is organised into relatively smooth layers, with the highest velocity near the tube centre. Turbulent flow involves irregular fluctuations and enhanced mixing. The Reynolds number, defined as density times mean velocity times characteristic diameter divided by dynamic viscosity, is dimensionless and provides a guide to the relative roles of inertial and viscous forces. It does not establish a universal transition threshold for every branching, oscillating biological airway. Turbulence and flow separation are more likely in regions with high velocities, abrupt geometry changes or high flow during forced breathing.

Airway smooth muscle can change calibre. Parasympathetic cholinergic signalling generally favours bronchoconstriction, whereas beta-2 adrenergic receptor activation favours relaxation. Mucus and oedema may narrow the functional lumen without requiring smooth muscle contraction. Mechanical traction from surrounding parenchyma can enlarge intrapulmonary airways at higher lung volume. Therefore changes in airway resistance can arise from neural, cellular, secretory or tissue-level mechanisms. A measured resistance increase alone cannot identify which mechanism produced it.

In a simple teaching calculation, an inspiratory pressure difference of two centimetres of water and airway resistance of four centimetres of water seconds per litre give flow of 0.5 litres per second. If resistance doubles at the same pressure difference, flow falls to 0.25 litres per second. To maintain the original flow, the pressure difference must double under the linear model. In a real airway system, pressure loss can increase more than proportionally at higher flows, so an effective resistance calculated from a single pressure and flow pair is conditional on that measurement.

9. Why forced expiration can become flow-limited

During passive expiration, the inward elastic recoil of the respiratory system generates the pressure gradient that expels air. During a forced expiration the abdominal muscles and expiratory intercostals can make pleural pressure positive relative to atmospheric pressure. Alveolar pressure also rises because the alveolar gas is compressed by the surrounding tissues. It is tempting to conclude that every increase in expiratory effort must increase the speed at which air leaves the lungs. This conclusion would be valid for a rigid pipe with a fixed resistance, but some intrathoracic airways can deform under the very pressure that drives expiration.

Pressure inside an airway is not necessarily the pressure inside an alveolus. During expiration, pressure decreases along the conducting pathway from the alveolus towards the mouth. The pressure difference between the lumen and the tissue outside the airway is called its transmural pressure. Positive transmural pressure tends to maintain airway patency; negative transmural pressure tends to narrow a compliant airway. Cartilage and surrounding tissue traction resist narrowing, so the actual change in calibre depends on airway wall structure as well as pressure.

Take an illustrative forced breath at a fixed lung volume. Pleural pressure is plus 20 cm H2O, and lung elastic recoil contributes another 10 cm H2O, giving an alveolar pressure of plus 30. At the mouth pressure is approximately zero. Along the intrathoracic airway, imagine local luminal pressures of 28, 24, 20, 15 and 9 cm H2O. At the location with luminal pressure 20, luminal and pleural pressures are equal. This is the equal pressure point in the simplified model. Upstream, on the alveolar side, transmural pressure is positive. Downstream, towards the mouth, a portion of the intrathoracic pathway can experience negative transmural pressure.

An airway that becomes narrower as surrounding pressure rises undergoes dynamic compression. If expiratory muscle effort is increased enough at the same lung volume, alveolar pressure and pleural pressure can rise together. Suppose pleural pressure increases from 20 to 30 and elastic recoil remains 10, giving alveolar pressure 40. The pressure available to move gas from alveolus to the equal pressure point remains 10, because pressure at the equal pressure point is now 30. The additional muscular effort may produce more compression in the downstream segment rather than a proportionate increase in flow.

The fixed-volume qualification is fundamental. Elastic recoil depends on volume, and upstream resistance can also change as the lung empties. When lung volume is high, outward traction on intrapulmonary airways is generally stronger, and lung recoil pressure is larger. When lung volume decreases, both the reduction of recoil and narrowing of some airways can lower maximum expiratory flow. The decreasing flow observed during the descending portion of a forced expiratory flow-volume curve therefore cannot be explained simply by exhaustion of respiratory muscle force.

An experimental way to demonstrate flow limitation is to compare expiratory flow at the same lung volume during a set of expirations performed with graded efforts. At relatively low efforts, flow rises as pressure generation rises. Above a sufficient effort, the measured flow at that volume may approach a plateau even though expiratory pressure continues to increase. Repeating this comparison at different volumes reveals the dependence of the limiting flow on recoil and airway support. The effort-independent region is not necessarily the entire expiratory manoeuvre; the peak and early phase can remain effort dependent.

Consider another illustrative comparison. At a specified volume two lungs have similar upstream airway resistance, but one has lost much of its tissue recoil. If the equal-pressure-point model is applied with a stable upstream resistance of 2 cm H2O seconds per litre, a recoil pressure of 10 cm H2O would support an upstream flow of 5 L/s, whereas a recoil pressure of 4 would support 2 L/s. These numbers are not clinical estimates and the exact resistance and position of the limiting segment would change in a real lung. Their purpose is to show that high alveolar pressure generated by muscle is not equivalent to high elastic driving pressure available upstream of a flow-limiting segment.

The equal pressure point may move towards smaller, less mechanically supported airways if recoil decreases or pressure losses increase along the peripheral conducting pathway. Such an alteration contributes to premature compression during forced expiration in emphysema. Inflammation or smooth muscle narrowing can also increase upstream pressure losses even if tissue recoil is preserved. The two changes have different causes but may interact to produce a similar limitation of expiratory flow.

The simplified equal pressure point model treats surrounding pleural pressure as representative of the external pressure along the airway. Real airways experience local stresses and can narrow progressively over a region rather than at a mathematically sharp point. Flow limitation can also be analysed with more detailed physical models of pressure-wave propagation in collapsible tubes. For NET and SET purposes, the important conclusion is that the resistive pathway itself responds to transmural pressure, so a rigid-tube pressure-flow law cannot be extrapolated indefinitely. The model explains how a powerful muscular effort can coexist with reduced expiratory flow.

10. A mechanical equation for a ventilating respiratory system

The individual principles developed so far can be integrated into a simple equation of motion. Begin with the passive respiratory system at its equilibrium volume. Let v denote the increase in volume above that baseline, Q denote inward flow dv/dt, Ers denote respiratory-system elastance and Rrs denote an effective resistance. Define Pdrive as the net pressure supplied by the inspiratory muscles and any externally imposed pressure above the baseline. If inertial effects are neglected and the mechanical coefficients are approximately constant, then Pdrive = Ers v + Rrs Q. The volume term represents the pressure required to overcome elastic recoil, and the flow term represents pressure dissipated by resistance.

The definition of Pdrive requires careful attention to signs. During spontaneous inspiration it is convenient to define the pressure supplied by inspiratory muscles as a positive driving contribution, even though pleural pressure itself becomes more negative. During passive inflation by positive pressure at the airway opening, an external device supplies the driving pressure. An identical algebraic form for the combined load does not imply that the pleural pressure changes in the same direction under these two modes of inflation. In both cases a positive change in transpulmonary pressure can distend the lung.

Suppose Ers is 10 cm H2O/L and Rrs is 3 cm H2O seconds/L. At one instant during an illustrative inspiration, v equals 0.3 L and Q equals 0.4 L/s. The elastic pressure is 3 cm H2O. The resistive pressure is 1.2 cm H2O. The total required driving pressure is 4.2 cm H2O. At the same lung volume with zero flow, the resistive term disappears and the pressure requirement falls to 3. If flow reverses to minus 0.4 L/s while the volume remains 0.3 L, the resistive term is negative in this sign convention. It opposes the direction of outward flow just as it opposed inward flow during inspiration.

The equation reveals two independent ways to make the ventilatory load larger. An increase in Ers raises the elastic pressure needed to reach a specified volume, even at zero flow. An increase in Rrs raises the pressure needed to produce a specified flow, but the extra contribution disappears when flow stops. An experiment can distinguish them by measuring the pressure at two different flows and the same volume, then at two different volumes and zero flow. Merely observing a larger pressure during breathing does not reveal which term changed.

With non-negligible inertia, an additional term proportional to dQ/dt may be included. Inertance describes resistance to acceleration rather than to sustained movement. If airflow is large but steady, the acceleration term is zero; if airflow changes rapidly near the beginning of a breath, it may contribute even while the instantaneous flow is small. In most elementary tidal breathing problems, resistance and elastance are sufficient approximations, but that assumption should be stated rather than treated as a general physical law.

The equation of motion can also reveal a hidden source of error in measurements from spontaneously breathing animals. If airway opening pressure remains zero, an investigator might conclude that no driving pressure has been applied. In fact, the respiratory muscles supply the pressure through thoracic expansion. An estimate of pleural pressure and an independent estimate of passive chest-wall mechanics are needed to infer the muscular pressure. Measuring only mouth pressure during spontaneous breathing therefore cannot quantify the mechanical load.

Now consider an ideal experiment in which an external pressure step of 5 cm H2O is suddenly applied to a relaxed respiratory system. With Ers equal to 10, the final volume increase after equilibration is 0.5 L. At the first instant the volume has not yet changed, so the elastic term is zero and the initial flow is 5/Rrs. If Rrs is 5, initial flow is 1 L/s; if resistance doubles to 10, initial flow becomes 0.5 L/s. Both conditions eventually reach the same static volume provided the elastic load is identical and equilibration is allowed. Increasing resistance changes the speed of inflation without necessarily changing the final static volume.

This last observation gives rise to an important experimental distinction. A small observed volume after a brief pressure pulse might be due either to stiffness or to a slow approach to the equilibrium volume. Extending the duration of the pulse tests the alternatives. If the final equilibrated volume becomes normal, resistance or another time-dependent process may explain the original observation. If the volume remains reduced despite full equilibration, an increased elastic load is more plausible. This inference assumes no gas leak, recruitment change, active muscle contraction or drift in the applied pressure.

11. Time constants and the regional distribution of ventilation

A lung is not a single homogeneous elastic bag. It contains numerous regional units, each supplied by a branching airway and supported by local tissue. Nevertheless, the simplest resistance-compliance model provides a useful starting point for understanding why different regions fill at different rates. Write the equation of motion for passive inflation under a constant pressure step P as R dv/dt + v/C = P. Multiplying by C gives RC dv/dt + v = CP. The quantity RC has units of time because the pressure and volume units cancel, leaving seconds.

The solution for an initially uninflated incremental volume in this linear model is v(t) = CP[1 − exp(−t/RC)]. The final change CP is set by static compliance; the approach to that final value is set by the time constant tau = RC. One time constant produces approximately 63.2 percent of the final volume change. After two time constants the fraction is approximately 86.5 percent, after three it is 95.0 percent and after five it is 99.3 percent. These percentages are mathematical properties of a single exponential, not universal measured fractions for every lung.

During passive emptying back towards the same baseline, the corresponding solution is v(t) = v(0) exp(−t/RC). After one time constant about 36.8 percent of the original excess volume remains; after three about five percent remains. The direction of change differs, but the same product RC determines the timescale if compliance and resistance remain constant. This model excludes active expiratory contraction, changes of airway calibre during expiration, dynamic compression, recruitment and flow-dependent resistance.

As an example, consider a lung unit with C = 0.05 L/cm H2O and R = 4 cm H2O seconds/L. Its time constant is 0.2 s. A second unit with the same compliance but resistance 20 has a time constant of 1 s. Under an identical sustained pressure step, the two units would eventually reach equal volume changes. After an inspiratory interval of 0.4 s, however, the first has reached about 86.5 percent of its equilibrium change whereas the second has reached only about 33.0 percent. At short inspiratory times, air is therefore distributed preferentially to faster-filling regions even without differences in their static compliance.

A different pair of lung units might have the same resistance but different compliance. The unit with greater compliance has a larger equilibrium volume response to a pressure step, and also has a longer time constant. It may receive more volume at long inspiratory times but not a proportionately greater share during a short pulse. This illustrates why neither compliance nor resistance alone determines the distribution of dynamic ventilation. The driving pressure, duration of inflation and characteristics of the alternative pathways all contribute.

In a simple two-unit experiment, each unit is connected to a shared pressure source. One can insert a narrow tube into one branch while leaving the elastic reservoirs unchanged. If both units are observed after a long inflation, their volumes may be similar. If the duration is shortened, the volume of the obstructed branch falls relative to the other. Measuring only total volume would conceal the regional inequality. The experiment is a mechanical analogue of ventilation heterogeneity and explains why a globally acceptable volume can coexist with poorly ventilated regions.

The regional lung also experiences differences in initial expansion. In the upright position, gravitational effects on the pleural pressure distribution mean that apical alveolar regions are generally more distended at rest than basal regions. A region that begins on the flatter, high-volume part of its pressure-volume curve may expand less than a region operating on a steeper portion of its curve when subjected to a comparable pressure change. Under ordinary upright breathing this is an important reason for greater ventilation per unit resting volume in dependent regions. The pattern can change with posture, lung volume, airway closure and disease.

This regional explanation must not be confused with ventilation-perfusion matching. The distribution of blood flow is a separate variable, although gravity influences it as well. A unit can expand well yet receive little blood, or be supplied by blood yet fill slowly. The relation between regional mechanical properties and the matching of gas flow with blood flow is developed in 7.C.05. Here the conclusion is that even an apparently symmetrical pair of lungs can contain regions with different mechanical operating points and filling times.

Incomplete expiration can occur when the next inspiration begins before a slow compartment returns to its previous equilibrium. The retained volume raises the operating lung volume. In a simple passive system, the increased volume also means a residual elastic pressure at the end of expiration. The inspiratory muscles may need to overcome this residual pressure before new inward flow is generated. This phenomenon is often called dynamic hyperinflation when the end-expiratory volume rises as a result of incomplete emptying. It is especially relevant when expiratory resistance is elevated or the breathing cycle becomes short.

One should distinguish dynamic hyperinflation from a static shift of the resting volume. A loss of lung recoil can increase the equilibrium volume even if the subject waits sufficiently long for expiration to finish. In contrast, a rise in the operating volume caused by insufficient expiratory time is a time-dependent phenomenon. Both may coexist. Comparing the end-expiratory volume during rapid breathing with the volume reached after a sufficiently prolonged relaxed expiration helps distinguish them, subject to the assumptions of the measurement.

12. Mechanical work, elastic energy and dissipative losses

Mechanical work is energy transferred when a force acts through a displacement. In the respiratory system, pressure acting on the boundary of a gas compartment does work as volume changes. An infinitesimal mechanical work increment is pressure difference multiplied by volume increment. The pressure used must correspond to the chosen mechanical load; the volume change must have the sign and direction consistent with that load. The product of a pressure in pascals and a volume in cubic metres has the unit joule.

For the pressure and volume units used in respiratory physiology, 1 cm H2O times 1 L is approximately 0.0981 J. This conversion follows because 1 cm H2O is approximately 98.1 Pa and 1 L is 0.001 cubic metres. A pressure-volume diagram can therefore be used to measure energy from the appropriate area, provided the axes and reference pressures are known. The area underneath a pressure-volume curve, the area enclosed by a loop and the slope of a curve have different physical meanings.

For the linear elastic model with volume measured relative to the passive equilibrium, the incremental elastic pressure is Ers v. Integrating it from zero to VT gives elastic inflation work equal to one half Ers VT squared, or VT squared divided by twice the compliance. The factor one half occurs because elastic pressure increases progressively during inflation rather than remaining equal to its final value. If the pressure-volume curve is nonlinear, the corresponding integral must be evaluated using the actual curve. If the baseline includes an additional load, that load contributes separate work.

For a hypothetical system with Crs = 0.1 L/cm H2O and VT = 0.5 L, the elastic inflation work above equilibrium is 0.25 divided by 0.2, or 1.25 cm H2O L. This is approximately 0.123 J. If compliance falls to 0.05 while the same volume change is maintained, elastic work doubles to 2.5 cm H2O L, approximately 0.245 J. If tidal volume doubles while compliance is unchanged, the elastic work per inflation increases fourfold. This quadratic relationship is a key reason why large breaths can become mechanically expensive in stiff lungs.

The resistive pressure in a linear model is RQ. Mechanical power dissipated through resistance is pressure drop multiplied by flow, giving RQ squared. Because the square is nonnegative, the energy loss is positive during both inspiration and expiration. For a constant inspiratory flow of 0.5 L/s over one second with R = 4 cm H2O seconds/L, resistive inflation work is 4 times 0.25 times 1 = 1 cm H2O L, or approximately 0.098 J. This resistive work is not recovered as useful elastic energy during expiration.

At the same tidal volume of 0.5 L, extending the inspiratory time from one second to two seconds and maintaining a constant flow reduces flow from 0.5 to 0.25 L/s. With unchanged resistance the resistive inflation work becomes 4 times 0.0625 times 2, or 0.5 cm H2O L. The static elastic work remains the same because the final volume is unchanged. A slower inflation can therefore reduce resistive energy per breath while leaving elastic energy per breath unchanged. This is an idealised comparison that ignores the consequences of a longer breathing cycle for total ventilation.

During passive expiration, previously stored elastic energy drives gas outward and overcomes resistive losses. The muscular work of inspiration need not be repeated in the opposite direction in order for expiration to occur. During forced expiration, muscles can contribute additional work. The metabolic cost to respiratory muscles is not identical to the external mechanical work: muscle efficiency is less than one, isometric force production consumes energy without visible volume displacement, and muscle shortening speed and activation patterns affect energy requirements.

If one constructs a dynamic pressure-volume loop for a passively ventilated system, the pressure needed during inspiration is greater than the pressure at the same volume during expiration when flow is present and resistance is positive. The loop area represents net dissipated mechanical work over the cycle under appropriate assumptions. Static hysteresis can also contribute to an enclosed area. In an intact spontaneously breathing animal, a pressure-volume loop using oesophageal pressure and a passive chest-wall reference can be analysed to partition work into components. This is the basis of the Campbell diagram. A loop drawn with arbitrary pressure or without a chest-wall reference should not be interpreted as a precise measurement of total respiratory muscle work.

A subject with stiff lungs might produce a large muscular pressure but modest volume change. Another subject with severe airway obstruction might expend considerable energy maintaining airflow through narrow passages. In the first case the elastic pressure-volume component is increased; in the second, the resistive pressure-flow component is increased. Both subjects can experience a high mechanical load, but their pressure, flow and volume traces will differ. The distinction suggests why an experiment that measures pressure without measuring volume and flow cannot accurately characterise the nature of respiratory effort.

Breathing work per minute depends on both work per breath and the number of breaths performed. A pattern that reduces work per breath may not reduce work per minute if the frequency rises sufficiently. Furthermore, the conducting airway contains gas that is not directly participating in exchange at that moment. Very shallow breathing can increase the fraction of each breath devoted to renewing this conducting volume. For a fixed effective alveolar ventilation, changing breathing frequency changes the tidal volume that must be generated. The detailed calculation of alveolar ventilation belongs to the next node; the qualitative trade-off is crucial for understanding mechanical efficiency.

13. Mechanical patterns in obstruction, restriction and muscle weakness

An obstructive mechanical pattern arises when airflow is disproportionately limited by airway resistance or collapsibility. A restrictive mechanical pattern arises when the respiratory system cannot achieve its usual range of lung volumes. The two concepts describe different functional properties, not mutually exclusive causes of all respiratory difficulties. A physiological observation can suggest a mechanism but does not establish a specific clinical diagnosis.

Asthma provides a familiar model of variable airway narrowing. Smooth muscle contraction, changes in the airway wall and luminal secretions can reduce effective radius. Since resistance is highly sensitive to calibre, a substantial pressure gradient may be needed to sustain flow. The static compliance of the lung tissue need not be intrinsically reduced merely because the conducting airways are narrowed. If sufficient time is allowed at an imposed distending pressure, lung expansion may be closer to normal than a brief dynamic measurement suggests.

Emphysema offers a contrasting obstructive mechanism. Destruction of supporting lung tissue can reduce elastic recoil and increase static lung compliance. The lung becomes easier to inflate over some volume ranges, but its weakened recoil and reduced traction on intrapulmonary airways make expiration less effective. Higher compliance is therefore not invariably beneficial. The loss of support can increase dynamic compression, and the longer time constant associated with high compliance and resistance can promote incomplete expiration. Both effects tend to increase operating lung volume.

In pulmonary fibrosis, deposition and reorganisation of connective tissue can increase the elastic load of the lung. A specified increase in transpulmonary pressure yields a smaller volume change. In contrast to the loss of recoil in emphysema, increased stiffness can generate greater inward restoring pressure at a comparable lung expansion. Fibrosis often produces reduced lung volumes and a mechanically restrictive pattern. It would be inaccurate to assume that every fibrotic lung has low elastic recoil simply because its capacity to expand is reduced.

A restriction in chest-wall movement may reduce total respiratory-system compliance without an equivalent change in intrinsic lung compliance. A simplified laboratory analogue would be to place a flexible bag inside an enclosure whose walls are mechanically constrained. The bag could retain its original intrinsic pressure-volume curve while the pressure required to expand the whole arrangement increases. Comparing transpulmonary and total distending pressures helps locate the increased elastic load. This distinction is essential when interpreting measurements of respiratory-system compliance.

Respiratory muscle weakness is a third mechanism. In the ideal case, the passive elastic and resistive properties of the lung remain normal, but the pressure source cannot generate sufficient force. Both maximum inspiratory volume and expiratory flow may be reduced because the system is not adequately driven. A pressure-volume measurement during passive external inflation could reveal normal mechanical properties despite reduced voluntary inspiratory capacity. The experiment must control for leaks, active effort and measurement conditions before muscle weakness is inferred.

The representative patterns can be compared using a teaching model that holds all but one parameter constant. In an airway-resistance model, the difference between pressure during flow and after equilibration increases. In a lung-stiffness model, the static pressure-volume slope falls. In a loss-of-recoil model, the static slope may increase but the available passive expiratory driving pressure decreases. In a muscle-weakness model, the passive curves are unchanged but generated muscular pressures decrease. Actual biological conditions often combine more than one of these changes, and a single simple measurement may not distinguish them.

For example, suppose a student obtains a small measured tidal volume during a brief inspiratory effort. The observation is consistent with elevated resistance, reduced compliance, reduced muscle pressure or a combination. Extending the measurement until flow ceases, estimating transpulmonary pressure, comparing the pressure-flow relationship and evaluating muscular force can resolve different alternatives. The appropriate answer to a question with insufficient evidence is not to select the most familiar disease, but to state which mechanisms remain compatible with the observation and what additional measurement would discriminate among them.

Breathing patterns also reflect mechanical trade-offs. When the elastic load is high, smaller breaths can reduce the energy associated with each expansion. When airflow resistance is high, slower airflow and adequate expiratory time may reduce resistive losses and incomplete emptying. These are tendencies, not universal descriptions of every animal or person. Neural drive, gas exchange needs, fatigue and behavioural influences can alter the observed pattern. A pattern cannot be explained from mechanics alone if the demand for ventilation is changing simultaneously.

14. Separating muscle function, elastic load and pressure transmission

The ability of the diaphragm to generate a pressure change depends not only on its neural activation but also on its length, curvature, attachment to the thoracic cage and the pressure imposed by surrounding abdominal structures. Skeletal muscle force varies with the length at which the fibres begin contraction. In an intact respiratory pump, however, the relationship between fibre force and pleural pressure is further modified by geometry. A muscle may be activated strongly while generating a relatively small useful pressure change if its geometry or starting length is unfavourable.

At a higher lung volume, the diaphragm tends to be flatter and its fibres are often shorter. The effect of this change is especially relevant when the resting volume has increased because of loss of lung recoil or incomplete expiration. The inspiratory muscles must act from a different mechanical starting configuration. Consequently, a lung with high static compliance may coexist with a respiratory pump that generates less effective pressure for a given level of activation. This distinction between the passive load and the capacity of the motor system helps explain why the compliance of an isolated lung cannot by itself predict the ability of the whole animal to ventilate.

The abdominal compartment is not merely an empty space into which the diaphragm descends. Its contents exert mechanical forces against the diaphragm. Abdominal pressure usually increases during a normal diaphragmatic contraction, while thoracic pleural pressure decreases. The transdiaphragmatic pressure is the difference between abdominal pressure and pleural pressure. It can be estimated using gastric pressure as an approximation to abdominal pressure and oesophageal pressure as an approximation to pleural pressure. A rise in transdiaphragmatic pressure during inspiration supports the inference that diaphragmatic force is being transmitted between these compartments.

For a representative numerical example, suppose gastric pressure is plus three centimetres of water and oesophageal pressure is minus four at end expiration. The estimated transdiaphragmatic pressure is seven centimetres of water. During an inspiratory contraction the two pressures become plus eight and minus nine, respectively. The estimated transdiaphragmatic pressure becomes seventeen, an increase of ten. These hypothetical values demonstrate the importance of subtracting the pressures across the diaphragm rather than observing only one. They do not define a normal threshold for strength.

There are circumstances in which a change in pleural pressure is produced largely by muscles other than the diaphragm. An inspiratory effort generated by the rib cage can lower intrathoracic pressure and draw a weak or inactive diaphragm in an abnormal direction. Abdominal pressure responses and chest-wall motion help interpret the pattern, although recordings require attention to posture and coordination. In an experiment designed to isolate diaphragmatic function, recording thoracic and abdominal pressures together is more informative than recording either pressure alone. The remaining limitation is that the measured pressures are indirect measures of muscle force.

The diaphragm also illustrates the difference between a muscular action and the resulting motion of the boundary. During an occluded inspiratory effort, muscle fibres can contract and develop tension without a large change in lung volume. The pressure exerted on the enclosed gas and pleural system changes even though bulk airflow is absent. The subject consumes metabolic energy during the effort despite doing relatively little external pressure-volume work. A measurement of mechanical work based exclusively on gas displacement therefore cannot serve as an exact measurement of muscle energy expenditure.

Consider the mechanical effect of posture. In the supine position, the abdominal contents interact differently with the diaphragm than in the upright position. The resting lung volume is often lower, and the distribution of regional ventilation changes. This reflects both gravitational redistribution of tissue forces and a changed balance between the abdominal, chest-wall and lung elastic loads. A posture-dependent change in respiratory mechanics need not imply that alveolar surfactant composition or airway smooth-muscle signalling changed. A change in the boundary conditions can modify the behaviour of a system whose constituent tissues remain the same.

The geometry of the respiratory pump also provides a basis for distinguishing mammalian negative-pressure ventilation from other mechanical modes. In ordinary spontaneous mammalian inspiration, a reduction of surrounding pressure is transmitted to the lungs through the thorax. In an amphibian buccal pump, air can be pushed into the lungs by raising pressure upstream. Both mechanisms can create a positive pressure difference driving inward flow and can increase the distending pressure of the lung, but they use different motor arrangements. This comparison, established in the earlier comparative chapter, prevents the mistaken idea that an alveolar pressure below atmosphere is a universal requirement for inspiration in every vertebrate.

The respiratory and circulatory systems share the mechanical environment of the thorax. Changes in intrathoracic pressure influence the pressure surrounding the heart and thoracic blood vessels as well as the airways. During quiet spontaneous inspiration, the fall in pleural pressure and rise in abdominal pressure can favour the movement of systemic venous blood towards the thorax. The consequences for cardiac filling and output depend on the vascular and cardiac conditions and are not identical for every breath. This relationship connects respiratory mechanics to the vascular pressure gradients studied in 7.B without replacing the cardiovascular mechanisms with a single pressure rule.

15. A closer examination of surface films and lung stability

Surface tension is not equivalent to the viscosity of the liquid lining. Viscosity describes dissipation during relative motion of fluid layers, whereas surface tension describes the energetic cost of creating interfacial area. An aqueous liquid may have appreciable surface tension even if its viscosity is low. Pulmonary surfactant acts primarily by modifying the interfacial properties. Changing the viscosity of a lining liquid and changing its surface tension could produce different mechanical consequences even when both alterations affect the energy of breathing.

An intuitive way to distinguish these properties is to compare a droplet with a thin flowing film. A droplet tends towards a shape that minimises surface area for a given volume because interfacial energy is associated with area. A flowing film can resist deformation because of viscosity. In the ideal static spherical interface, the pressure difference follows the curvature and surface tension, even when the liquid is not flowing. A measurement at equilibrium can therefore detect a contribution of surface forces without requiring a viscous flow through the airways.

For a general curved interface with uniform tension, the pressure jump depends on both principal radii of curvature. The ideal Young-Laplace expression is delta P = T(1/R1 + 1/R2), where the radii carry signs according to the chosen curvature convention. The spherical model used earlier has two equal radii, giving 2T/r. A cylindrical interface with one infinite radius has only one curvature contribution. A flat interface has no curvature-generated pressure jump in this simplified description even though its surface tension may be nonzero. These comparisons reinforce why geometry matters when applying the formula.

An alveolar septum experiences stresses from its extracellular matrix, the capillary and epithelial structures, and neighbouring airspaces. One region expanding can exert traction on adjacent regions. This alveolar interdependence contributes to the mechanical stability of the parenchyma. An isolated small bubble connected to a large bubble does not share a septal network of this kind. Consequently, the instability predicted from the constant-tension bubble model does not establish that all smaller alveoli in a lung must collapse into larger ones. The model identifies a tendency that biological architecture and surfactant help oppose.

The composition of the surface film provides another layer of explanation. Saturated phospholipids such as dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine can form tightly packed interfacial structures at small surface areas. Other lipids and the hydrophobic surfactant-associated proteins facilitate adsorption, spreading and film behaviour during repeated area changes. A mechanically effective surfactant system must be capable not only of lowering tension during compression but also of re-forming and spreading across an expanding surface. A lipid film that performed well in a single compression experiment but could not spread during expansion would not necessarily perform well in a repeatedly ventilated lung.

The surface area of a lung increases and decreases during breathing, while the amount of material at the interface is dynamic. If the area expands rapidly, adsorption and redistribution of surfactant may not instantly reproduce the equilibrium surface concentration. During compression, rearrangement of film components and exchange with associated material can alter the relationship between area and tension. This history dependence helps explain why the pressure required to inflate a lung and the pressure observed during deflation are different. The exact surface behaviour cannot be inferred from a single measurement of bulk phospholipid concentration.

A simple surface-film experiment demonstrates the principle. In an interfacial trough, a surface-active preparation is placed at an air-liquid interface and movable barriers change the area available to the film. A balance measures the force associated with the interface, which is used to estimate tension. A matched aqueous control without the surface-active preparation establishes the baseline. If the preparation produces lower surface tension and the measured tension varies during area cycling, the observations support a dynamic interfacial mechanism. This general experimental principle, used in classical surfactant research, does not require knowledge of a specialised lipid-analysis protocol.

The interpretation of such a surface-film experiment must distinguish presence from sufficiency. A sample that reduces tension outside the body contains surface-active components, but the measurement alone does not demonstrate that those components reach the alveolar interface or remain effective throughout a breath in the living organism. Organ-level pressure-volume measurements provide complementary evidence. Conversely, an altered organ-level compliance curve can be produced by changes in connective tissue or recruitment even if surfactant is normal. A convincing explanation connects interfacial measurements to the mechanical behaviour of the lung while testing alternative explanations.

An important comparison involves lungs filled with air and with liquid. At the same volume, a liquid-filled isolated lung may require less distending pressure because the relevant air-liquid interface has been removed. This does not mean that the fluid chemically dissolves all elastic structures or eliminates recoil. The residual pressure-volume relationship reflects the mechanical response of the tissue and other contributions under the conditions of the preparation. Moreover, a liquid-filled lung is not a normally gas-ventilating organ. The experiment is an intervention that removes one contribution to recoil in order to reveal its importance, not a physiological alternative to air breathing.

The mechanical transition around birth provides a biologically meaningful application. Before birth, mammalian lungs contain liquid and do not serve as the principal site of oxygen uptake from air. As the newborn establishes air breathing, aeration introduces a large air-liquid interface while lung liquid is being cleared. A functional surfactant system helps lower the interfacial load and maintain aerated units between breaths. Initial opening can require a greater pressure than subsequent breaths because the mechanical history and aeration state differ. A premature lung with an immature surfactant system can therefore have difficulty establishing and maintaining stable expansion. This is an example of the developmental origin of a mechanical phenotype, not a discussion of treatment.

Surface forces also matter when considering fluid accumulation in the distal lung. Increasing the thickness or altering the composition of the lining can affect gas exchange and the mechanical behaviour of the interface, but the two effects should be distinguished. Increased diffusion distance primarily influences transfer of gases across the exchange barrier. Altered surface film behaviour or recruitment primarily changes the mechanical pressure-volume relationship. Both may occur in the same condition. A question that asks which experimental measurement identifies the mechanical consequence should therefore be answered using a pressure-volume or opening-pressure comparison rather than an oxygen concentration alone.

For an ideal numerical comparison, take a spherical gas space with radius 150 micrometres. If surface tension is 0.030 N/m, the pressure jump is 400 Pa. If an interfacial film reduces the tension to 0.006 N/m at the same radius, the pressure jump becomes 80 Pa, an eighty percent reduction. This is a comparison at fixed curvature. It cannot be directly converted into an eighty percent reduction in whole-lung inflation pressure because tissue elastance and geometry also contribute. The correct scientific inference is that the interfacial component has been reduced in the stipulated model.

16. Designing experiments to identify mechanical causes

An experimental observation is useful only when the variable being measured can be connected to the proposed mechanism. A measured decrease in tidal volume is an outcome; it is not itself a measurement of compliance, resistance or muscle force. To decide among these mechanisms, a student should identify the controlled variable, the measured response and the conditions that permit the response to be interpreted. The simple mechanical models developed in the preceding sections suggest experiments that change one physical quantity while holding another constant.

Suppose an isolated lung is connected to a calibrated pressure source and a flow sensor. The outside pressure is known and the airway opening is connected to the source. In the first experiment, small pressure increments are applied and enough time is allowed for each new volume to stabilise. Integrating the measured inflow gives the change in gas volume if there are no leaks and the volume calibration accounts for gas conditions. At the end of each hold the pressure loss associated with bulk flow has largely vanished. The resulting pressure-volume slope estimates static compliance in the relevant volume range.

A second experiment uses two different constant flows to reach the same volume. The faster inflation needs a larger pressure if resistance is appreciable. Under the linear model, subtracting the pressures at the same volume removes the elastic contribution and dividing by the flow difference estimates resistance. If the relation is nonlinear, repeating the measurement at several flows can reveal departures from the simple proportional law. This method distinguishes a resistance estimate from a compliance estimate without assuming that the peak airway pressure directly measures either one.

Consider an illustrative dataset obtained at a fixed incremental volume of 0.4 L. At zero flow the pressure required above baseline is 4 cm H2O. At flows of 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 L/s the measured pressures are 5, 6 and 7 cm H2O. The slope of pressure against flow is four centimetres of water seconds per litre. The intercept is four centimetres of water, representing the elastic pressure at that volume. If the elastic relation is linear from zero volume increment, elastance is 4/0.4 = 10 cm H2O/L and compliance is 0.1 L/cm H2O. This calculation separates two properties from one series of observations, under the explicitly stated assumptions.

Now replace the three nonzero-flow pressures with 5, 7 and 10 cm H2O, leaving the zero-flow pressure unchanged. The incremental pressure loss is one, three and six at the three flows. The ratio of pressure loss to flow is four, six and eight, respectively. A constant resistance cannot account for all observations. The rising ratio is compatible with flow-dependent losses, for example an increasing contribution of inertial or turbulent effects. It does not uniquely identify turbulence, because geometry could also change. The important analytical conclusion is that the linear resistance approximation fails over the whole flow range.

An inspiratory hold can provide another test. If gas is flowing inward at constant rate, the pressure at the airway opening reflects the elastic and resistive demands. When flow is interrupted while the volume remains approximately fixed, the flow-dependent pressure difference rapidly decreases. Further gradual pressure changes may occur because of stress relaxation or redistribution among lung regions. A plateau measurement should therefore be made under a defined equilibration condition. If a slow pressure decay persists, the system may not be represented adequately by a single resistance and a perfectly elastic compartment.

The interpretation of airway pressure requires an additional distinction between the lung and the chest wall in an intact animal. In a passive inflation experiment, an increase in total pressure for a given volume could reflect stiffening of either component. Simultaneous oesophageal pressure measurements help estimate the division of distending pressure. For example, if an increase in volume of 0.4 L is associated with a rise in airway pressure of 8 cm H2O and an estimated pleural pressure rise of 3 cm H2O during a no-flow hold, the transpulmonary pressure rises by five. The lung compliance estimate is 0.4/5 = 0.08 L/cm H2O, and the chest-wall compliance estimate is 0.4/3, about 0.133 L/cm H2O. The combined compliance is 0.4/8 = 0.05. The reciprocal rule gives the same result up to rounding.

A flow sensor itself needs calibration. A pneumotachograph estimates flow from a pressure difference across a known resistive element under specified conditions. If the calibration remains valid, integrating flow over time estimates volume change. An offset in the zero-flow baseline accumulates into an error in the integrated volume. A leak creates a difference between measured flow at the sensor and the actual change in lung gas volume. These are not minor technical details when the conclusion depends on the difference between a large and small compliance. A student should consider how the instrument's errors could mimic the biological change being investigated.

The pressure sensor also has a reference level. A constant zero offset in pleural pressure measurements can distort absolute estimates of transpulmonary pressure, although a stable offset may cancel when pressure changes are calculated. An offset in the pressure-flow relationship can masquerade as an elastic intercept. If two sensors use different reference pressures, subtracting their readings without correcting the references produces a spurious pressure difference. Calibration against a common reference and measurement at a known zero-flow condition help detect these errors.

Experiments with respiratory muscles require particular caution because the pressure source is then biologically variable. If an animal increases effort after resistance is raised, the resulting flow may remain nearly unchanged despite an altered pressure-flow relation. Without recording effort or pressure, a student might incorrectly conclude that the airway resistance had not changed. Controlled passive inflation is useful because the imposed pressure or flow can be standardised independently of neural drive. In a spontaneously breathing preparation, recording pressure, flow and muscle activity can instead reveal compensatory responses.

An especially informative experimental comparison would combine three interventions: mechanical narrowing of the inlet, an increase in elastic stiffness and a reduction of surface activity in otherwise similar model preparations. The narrowed inlet should mainly change the pressure loss during flow. Increased elastic stiffness should reduce the equilibrated volume response at a fixed pressure. Loss of surface activity should change the static expansion behaviour of an air-filled preparation, with recruitment and hysteresis potentially affected. Comparing air and liquid filling can help distinguish a surface contribution from a change in tissue mechanics, while matched preparation history and loading reduce alternative explanations.

These experiments show why no single curve answers every question. A pressure-time curve reveals the timing of mechanical events, a volume-pressure curve quantifies deformation, a flow-pressure curve characterises resistance, and a flow-volume curve can reveal the dependence of maximum flow on lung volume. Simultaneous recordings allow a stronger inference than isolated measurements. The same principle applies in cellular biology: the existence of a transport pathway, its capacity, and the driving force across it are different quantities. Mechanistic interpretation requires identifying each independently.

17. Integrating depth, frequency and mechanical cost

A respiratory system must provide enough effective ventilation while working within its mechanical capacity. It could in principle move a given volume of air per minute using relatively deep, infrequent breaths or smaller, more frequent breaths. These alternatives do not impose the same pressure and energy demands. Furthermore, the amount of fresh air that reaches exchange surfaces is smaller than the total inspired volume when part of each breath simply refills the conducting airways. The cost of a breathing pattern therefore cannot be compared using frequency alone.

To isolate the mechanical principle, assume a linear combined compliance and a fixed anatomical dead-space volume VD. If effective alveolar ventilation is to remain constant at VA litres per minute, the idealised relation is VA = f(VT − VD), where f is breaths per minute and VT is tidal volume in litres. Solving for VT gives VT = VA/f + VD. This equation is introduced here only to explain the mechanical trade-off; measurement and physiological dead space receive fuller treatment in 7.C.04. The assumptions include negligible changes in the relevant dead space and stable breathing from cycle to cycle.

With constant compliance, the elastic inflation work per minute in the linear model is f VT squared divided by twice the compliance. Replacing VT by VA/f + VD shows that at very low frequency each breath must be large, making the squared-volume elastic cost high. At very high frequency each breath still must exceed the dead-space volume, so total elastic work does not necessarily fall indefinitely as breaths become smaller. The derivation shows that the dead-space constraint changes the optimum even before the resistive load is considered.

For an illustrative calculation, suppose the target alveolar ventilation is 4.2 L/min, the anatomical dead space is 0.15 L and compliance is 0.1 L/cm H2O. At a frequency of 12 breaths/min, VT must be 0.5 L. The elastic inflation work is 1.25 cm H2O L per breath, or 15 cm H2O L/min. At a frequency of 20, the required VT is 0.36 L. The elastic work per breath becomes 0.648 and per minute becomes 12.96 cm H2O L/min. At a frequency of 40, VT becomes 0.255 L and the elastic work per minute is about 13.005. These numbers are hypothetical and do not prescribe an optimal breathing pattern for a living animal.

Resistive work adds another constraint. Assume, solely for an illustrative model, that each inspiration occupies a fraction k of the total breathing cycle and that inward flow is constant during that interval. Inspiratory duration in seconds is 60k/f. Constant flow is VT divided by that duration. Resistive inflation work per breath is R VT squared divided by inspiratory duration, and per minute it is R f squared VT squared divided by 60k. Substituting the alveolar ventilation relation gives R(VA + fVD) squared divided by 60k. With these assumptions, increasing frequency increases the resistive cost because total inspired volume per minute increases as the repeated dead-space volume rises.

Take the same illustrative breathing patterns, with R = 3 cm H2O seconds/L and k = 0.4. At 12 breaths/min, total inspired volume is 6 L/min and inspiratory resistive work per minute is 3 times 36 divided by 24, or 4.5 cm H2O L/min. At 20 breaths/min, total inspired volume is 7.2 L/min, giving 6.48. At 40, total inspired volume is 10.2 L/min, giving about 13.005. The sum of the model's elastic and inspiratory resistive contributions is 19.5, 19.44 and 26.01 cm H2O L/min for the three cases. The small difference between the first two is less important than the increasing penalty in the final case.

These comparisons explain why mechanical optimum is not a single universal breathing frequency. Reducing compliance raises the elastic term and can favour smaller expansions; increasing resistance raises the cost of flow and can favour patterns allowing slower inflation and more complete expiration. Changing the dead-space fraction changes the relation between total and effective ventilation. A living organism also has constraints on respiratory muscle pressure, gas exchange, airway closure, neural rhythm and metabolic demand. The idealised cost calculation is a way to reason about the direction of trade-offs, not a prescription or a complete model of breathing.

During exercise, the demand for oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide elimination increases. Both breathing depth and frequency may increase as the respiratory motor system attempts to meet that demand. A larger breath can initially make ventilation more efficient by reducing the dead-space fraction, but at high lung volumes additional expansion faces increasing elastance. High airflow also increases resistive losses. The respiratory muscles themselves require oxygen and energy. Mechanical limitations can therefore reduce the capacity to increase ventilation further even when neural drive continues to rise.

An examination problem may hold alveolar ventilation constant while comparing work, or instead hold minute ventilation constant. Those two constraints are different. For example, with a minute ventilation of six litres per minute, a frequency of twelve and tidal volume of 0.5 L gives an effective ventilation of 4.2 L/min under the stated 0.15 L dead-space assumption. A frequency of twenty and tidal volume of 0.3 L gives the same minute ventilation but only 3 L/min effective ventilation. The reduction in elastic work in the second pattern cannot be called an improvement in mechanical efficiency without taking the reduced effective ventilation into account.

Mechanical reasoning thus connects the pressure-generating pump, the deformable lung and the distribution of air with the physiological requirement for gas exchange. The next chapter will make the definitions and measurements of lung volumes and effective ventilation more precise. The present chapter establishes the causal sequence that makes those measurements meaningful: muscle force alters thoracic pressures, pressure gradients move air, elastic and surface forces determine the volume response, airway geometry determines flow resistance, and these properties together set the temporal and energetic limits of pulmonary ventilation.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Pressure below zero means a vacuum.

Correct concept: Gauge pressure is relative to ambient pressure; absolute pressure remains positive.

Reasoning strategy: Many figures omit the reference. In calculations use absolute pressure for gas laws and a shared gauge reference for differences.

2. Transpulmonary pressure and airflow-driving pressure are the same.

Correct concept: PL spans the lung; airflow depends on airway-opening minus alveolar pressure.

Reasoning strategy: Both are pressure differences, so exam questions may swap their endpoints. Write the two equations first.

3. Zero flow always means alveolar pressure is atmospheric.

Correct concept: Only a patent equilibrated airway supports that conclusion.

Reasoning strategy: The usual end-breath diagram hides the patent-airway assumption; occlusion supplies the counterexample.

4. The lung contracts actively to inhale.

Correct concept: Respiratory skeletal muscles change thoracic mechanics; the lung distends passively.

Reasoning strategy: The visible lung movement may be mistaken for its own motor. Trace the force from diaphragm through pleura.

5. Every expiration is driven by abdominal contraction.

Correct concept: Quiet expiration is mainly passive recoil; forced expiration recruits expiratory muscles.

Reasoning strategy: Forced breathing examples can be overgeneralised. Identify the mode before naming active muscles.

6. All internal intercostal fibres are expiratory.

Correct concept: Interosseous and parasternal interchondral portions can have different mechanical roles.

Reasoning strategy: Simplified muscle lists conceal orientation. Distinguish the fibre group and movement.

7. High compliance implies strong recoil.

Correct concept: High compliance means small pressure increment for a volume increment; elastance is its inverse.

Reasoning strategy: Everyday elasticity terminology is ambiguous. Use measured slope and pressure at matched volume.

8. Lung and chest-wall compliances add directly.

Correct concept: Their elastances add in the coupled passive system.

Reasoning strategy: The same volume change requires both pressure increments. Apply the reciprocal sum rather than parallel conductance.

9. A steeper pressure-volume curve always means high compliance.

Correct concept: It does when volume is vertical and pressure horizontal; reversed axes give elastance.

Reasoning strategy: Graphs may reverse axes. Read axis units before interpreting the slope.

10. Static hysteresis must be caused entirely by airway flow resistance.

Correct concept: Surface films, recruitment and tissue history can create hysteresis even with equilibrated points.

Reasoning strategy: The loop resembles a dynamic resistance loop. Check whether flow was present at the measurement points.

11. Laplace pressure for an alveolus is 4T/r.

Correct concept: The single-interface ideal sphere gives 2T/r; a soap bubble has two interfaces.

Reasoning strategy: Students memorise bubble formulas without identifying interfaces. Count the surfaces and state the geometry.

12. The smallest alveolus inevitably empties into a larger one.

Correct concept: Variable surfactant tension and shared septal support can stabilise real alveoli.

Reasoning strategy: Constant-tension isolated bubbles omit crucial biological mechanics. Test the assumption of equal T and no tissue support.

13. The narrowest individual bronchiole necessarily contributes the most total resistance.

Correct concept: Parallel arrangement and collective cross-sectional area determine equivalent resistance.

Reasoning strategy: Single-tube Poiseuille intuition ignores network structure. Calculate conductance across all parallel routes.

14. Poiseuille's fourth-power relationship is exact for every breathing pattern.

Correct concept: It is a rigid-tube laminar steady-flow model; actual airways are deformable and flow can be nonlinear.

Reasoning strategy: Questions may specify ideal flow or real high-flow breathing. State the model domain before applying the exponent.

15. Stronger forced expiration always gives proportionately higher flow.

Correct concept: Dynamic compression can produce effort-independent flow at fixed volume.

Reasoning strategy: A rigid-pipe assumption fails in collapsible intrathoracic airways. Compare recoil and local transmural pressure.

16. A long time constant always indicates high resistance alone.

Correct concept: τ = RC; increased compliance also lengthens the time constant.

Reasoning strategy: Slow filling is not uniquely diagnostic. Compare equilibrium volume at a common pressure to separate C and R.

17. The area within every pressure-volume loop equals total muscle energy expenditure.

Correct concept: The meaning depends on pressure reference, loop type and load; metabolic cost differs from mechanical work.

Reasoning strategy: A familiar area rule is easily misapplied. Identify the measured pressure and whether the cycle includes active muscle work.

18. Equal minute ventilation ensures equal fresh alveolar ventilation.

Correct concept: With nonzero dead space, depth and frequency alter fresh gas delivery.

Reasoning strategy: The product fVT omits conducting volume. Compare f(VT−VD) when the question holds minute ventilation fixed.

Original examination practice

These original questions assess concepts and reasoning; they are not reproduced previous-year examination questions. All numerical scenarios are illustrative.

CSIR-NET Part B

1. At end expiration in a relaxed adult, with patent airways and no flow, which pressure equals atmospheric pressure?

  1. Pleural pressure
  2. Alveolar pressure
  3. Transpulmonary pressure
  4. Abdominal pressure
Answer and explanation

B. Alveolar pressure. At equilibrium with a patent airway, alveolar pressure equals airway-opening pressure. Pleural pressure normally remains subatmospheric, so transpulmonary pressure is positive; abdominal pressure is not required to equal atmosphere.

2. Which muscle is the principal motor of quiet inspiration?

  1. Rectus abdominis
  2. Diaphragm
  3. Transversus abdominis
  4. Expiratory interosseous internal intercostals
Answer and explanation

B. Diaphragm. Diaphragmatic contraction increases thoracic volume. The abdominal muscles and expiratory intercostal fibres principally assist forced expiration, not ordinary quiet inspiration.

3. During quiet inspiration, which change most directly distends the lung?

  1. A fall in pleural pressure relative to alveolar pressure
  2. A rise in airway resistance
  3. A fall in transpulmonary pressure
  4. Active contraction of alveolar skeletal muscle
Answer and explanation

A. A fall in pleural pressure relative to alveolar pressure. Increasing Palv − Ppl increases lung-distending pressure. Resistance opposes flow; reduced distending pressure favours recoil. Alveoli have no skeletal muscle pump.

4. What is the primary cause of quiet expiration?

  1. Continued shortening of diaphragm
  2. Active abdominal contraction in every breath
  3. Elastic recoil after inspiratory effort declines
  4. Oxygen consumption by alveolar gas
Answer and explanation

C. Elastic recoil after inspiratory effort declines. Stored elastic energy drives passive outward airflow when inspiratory activation falls. Expiratory muscles may act during forced breathing but are not normally required during quiet expiration.

5. Which equation defines transpulmonary pressure?

  1. Pao − Palv
  2. Palv − Ppl
  3. Ppl − Palv
  4. Palv − Pao
Answer and explanation

B. Palv − Ppl. Transpulmonary pressure spans lung tissue from alveolar gas to pleural space. The first and fourth options describe pressure differences driving flow across the airway; the third reverses the sign.

6. Boyle's law requires which conditions for P1V1 = P2V2?

  1. Constant gauge pressure and temperature
  2. Constant amount of gas and temperature, using absolute pressure
  3. Constant volume only
  4. An open gas system with arbitrary inflow
Answer and explanation

B. Constant amount of gas and temperature, using absolute pressure. The ideal gas equation reduces to PV = constant only at fixed amount and temperature. Gauge pressure cannot be substituted for absolute pressure; airflow changes the amount of gas.

7. Which unit is suitable for lung compliance?

  1. cm H₂O/L
  2. L/s
  3. L/cm H₂O
  4. cm H₂O·s/L
Answer and explanation

C. L/cm H₂O. Compliance is volume change divided by pressure change. The first unit is elastance, the second flow, and the fourth resistance.

8. What is elastance?

  1. Inverse of compliance
  2. Product of flow and volume
  3. Pressure difference divided by flow
  4. Volume divided by flow
Answer and explanation

A. Inverse of compliance. Elastance is the incremental pressure change required per volume increment. The other quantities describe different mechanical or temporal relationships.

9. When volume is plotted on the vertical axis and transpulmonary pressure on the horizontal axis, what does slope represent?

  1. Work
  2. Resistance
  3. Compliance
  4. Airway cross-sectional area
Answer and explanation

C. Compliance. The slope is ΔV/ΔPL. Work requires a pressure-volume area; resistance relates pressure difference to flow.

10. In the usual static lung pressure-volume hysteresis loop, at a given intermediate transpulmonary pressure, which limb has greater volume?

  1. Inflation
  2. Deflation
  3. Both are necessarily identical
  4. Cannot differ in the absence of flow
Answer and explanation

B. Deflation. Recruitment, surface film behaviour and tissue mechanics can make the deflation volume greater than inflation volume at the same pressure, even in a static experiment.

11. Which cell is principally responsible for synthesis of pulmonary surfactant?

  1. Type I pneumocyte
  2. Type II pneumocyte
  3. Erythrocyte
  4. Alveolar macrophage
Answer and explanation

B. Type II pneumocyte. Type II epithelial cells produce and release surfactant. Type I cells form much of the thin exchange surface; macrophages contribute defence.

12. Which formula represents the Laplace pressure magnitude for one ideal spherical air-liquid interface?

  1. T/r²
  2. 4T/r
  3. 2T/r
  4. Tr
Answer and explanation

C. 2T/r. For two equal principal curvatures the pressure jump is 2T/r. A soap bubble with two interfaces gives a different factor; the other options are dimensionally or physically incorrect.

13. What is a major mechanical consequence of pulmonary surfactant?

  1. Increasing surface tension at all volumes
  2. Reducing the interfacial contribution to recoil
  3. Actively contracting the diaphragm
  4. Eliminating the need for an airway pressure gradient
Answer and explanation

B. Reducing the interfacial contribution to recoil. Surfactant lowers surface tension and helps stabilise aerated units. It changes the load, not the source of muscular pressure, and bulk flow still requires a pressure difference.

14. For ideal laminar flow in a rigid circular tube, resistance is proportional to which radius term?

  1. r²
  2. r⁴
  3. 1/r
  4. 1/r⁴
Answer and explanation

D. 1/r⁴. Poiseuille's model gives R = 8ηL/(πr⁴). Its fourth-power dependence is valid under the stated model conditions, not for arbitrary airway flow.

15. Why can healthy small peripheral airways have a relatively low combined resistance despite small individual radii?

  1. They have no viscous resistance
  2. They are connected only in series
  3. Many parallel pathways share flow
  4. Their luminal pressure always equals pleural pressure
Answer and explanation

C. Many parallel pathways share flow. Parallel branches increase total conductance. Individual small branches have resistance; adding many independent paths reduces the equivalent resistance.

16. What happens at the equal pressure point during forced expiration in the simplified model?

  1. Alveolar pressure equals atmosphere
  2. Local intraluminal airway pressure equals pleural pressure
  3. Flow always stops
  4. Transpulmonary pressure of the whole lung becomes zero
Answer and explanation

B. Local intraluminal airway pressure equals pleural pressure. The local airway transmural pressure is zero at that point. Flow can continue; alveolar-to-pleural pressure remains positive if lung recoil persists.

17. Which expression has units of time in a linear lung model?

  1. R/C
  2. C/R
  3. RC
  4. R + C
Answer and explanation

C. RC. Resistance in cm H₂O·s/L multiplied by compliance in L/cm H₂O leaves seconds. This product is the characteristic filling or emptying time.

18. Which expression describes elastic work of inflation from equilibrium in a linear system?

  1. VT/C
  2. VT²/(2C)
  3. RVT
  4. C/VT²
Answer and explanation

B. VT²/(2C). Integrating P = V/C over volume produces VT²/(2C). VT/C is final pressure rather than work.

19. Which representative mechanical change is expected in emphysema?

  1. Increased lung compliance with reduced recoil
  2. Reduced compliance with increased recoil in every case
  3. Absence of any airflow resistance
  4. Increased recoil caused by additional collagen
Answer and explanation

A. Increased lung compliance with reduced recoil. Loss of alveolar supporting tissue reduces recoil and may increase compliance. Expiratory flow can still be limited because airway support and recoil are reduced.

20. Which finding most directly supports an increased static elastic load in a controlled passive experiment?

  1. Increased flow at the same pressure difference
  2. Higher pressure at a fixed volume after equilibration
  3. Greater peak pressure with unchanged plateau at matched flow
  4. Longer expiratory time only
Answer and explanation

B. Higher pressure at a fixed volume after equilibration. The no-flow equilibrated pressure at a specified volume reflects the static load. Peak-minus-plateau changes instead suggest flow-related pressure losses; time alone is ambiguous.

CSIR-NET Part C

21. During an illustrative breath Palv = −1 cm H₂O and Ppl = −7 cm H₂O; the mouth is open to atmosphere. What follows?

  1. PL = −6 and air leaves
  2. PL = +6 and air enters
  3. PL = +8 and air leaves
  4. PL = −8 and no flow occurs
Answer and explanation

B. PL = +6 and air enters. PL = −1 − (−7) = +6 cm H₂O. Atmospheric gauge pressure is zero and exceeds alveolar pressure, so flow is inward. The other choices confuse pressure references or reverse subtraction.

22. At the end of inspiration Palv = 0 and Ppl = −8; at end expiration Palv = 0 and Ppl = −5. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Both lung volumes must be identical
  2. Distending pressure is greater at end inspiration despite zero flow at both instants
  3. Pleural pressure drives air directly through the airway
  4. Airflow must be outward at both instants
Answer and explanation

B. Distending pressure is greater at end inspiration despite zero flow at both instants. PL values are 8 and 5 cm H₂O. With patent airways the two no-flow instants have equal alveolar pressure but different distending pressures and normally different volumes.

23. A sealed isothermal gas chamber starts at 750 mmHg absolute and 2 L, then expands to 2.5 L. What is the new absolute pressure?

  1. 600 mmHg
  2. 937.5 mmHg
  3. −150 mmHg
  4. 0 mmHg
Answer and explanation

A. 600 mmHg. P2 = 750×2/2.5 = 600 mmHg. Relative to the original 750 mmHg environment the gauge pressure would be −150; the question asks absolute pressure.

24. A lung expands by 400 mL when PL rises by 4 cm H₂O. What is its mean compliance?

  1. 0.01 L/cm H₂O
  2. 0.1 L/cm H₂O
  3. 10 L/cm H₂O
  4. 1.6 L/cm H₂O
Answer and explanation

B. 0.1 L/cm H₂O. 400 mL = 0.4 L; 0.4/4 = 0.1 L/cm H₂O. Ten is the corresponding elastance in cm H₂O/L.

25. If lung compliance is 0.3 and chest-wall compliance is 0.15 L/cm H₂O, what is combined passive compliance?

  1. 0.45
  2. 0.20
  3. 0.10
  4. 0.15
Answer and explanation

C. 0.10. Reciprocal compliances add: 1/0.3 + 1/0.15 = 10. Crs = 0.10 L/cm H₂O, smaller than either component.

26. At a matched volume and constant flow of 0.5 L/s, peak pressure rises from 14 to 19 cm H₂O while no-flow plateau remains 9. What changed in the linear model?

  1. Resistance rises from 10 to 20 cm H₂O·s/L
  2. Compliance doubles
  3. Elastic pressure rises from 5 to 10
  4. Resistance remains 9
Answer and explanation

A. Resistance rises from 10 to 20 cm H₂O·s/L. The peak-minus-plateau differences are 5 and 10 cm H₂O; dividing by 0.5 gives 10 and 20. The matched plateau provides no evidence of changed static elastic pressure.

27. Two isolated preparations receive a pressure step of 4 cm H₂O. Both reach 0.4 L after a long hold, but B fills more slowly. Which inference is best?

  1. B has lower static compliance
  2. B may have higher resistance
  3. B must contain more collagen
  4. B has higher equilibrium volume
Answer and explanation

B. B may have higher resistance. Both static compliance estimates are 0.4/4 = 0.1. A slower approach with equal final volume is compatible with higher resistance or another time-dependent effect, not necessarily a static stiffness change.

28. One ideal airway narrows to 75% of original radius without other changes. What is the approximate new resistance relative to original?

  1. 0.75
  2. 1.33
  3. 1.78
  4. 3.16
Answer and explanation

D. 3.16. Rnew/Rold = (1/0.75)^4 = 3.16 approximately. The intermediate values do not account for the fourth-power dependence.

29. Four identical airway branches of resistance 8 cm H₂O·s/L are arranged in parallel. Two are closed completely. How does equivalent resistance change?

  1. 2 to 4
  2. 8 to 16
  3. 32 to 16
  4. 4 to 2
Answer and explanation

A. 2 to 4. For n equal parallel branches, Req = R/n. Four give 2 and two give 4 cm H₂O·s/L. The total resistance doubles, although the patent branches themselves have not narrowed.

30. In ideal spherical alveolar models, A has radius 100 μm and surface tension 0.010 N/m; B has radius 200 μm and tension 0.020 N/m. Compare Laplace pressure.

  1. A is twice B
  2. B is twice A
  3. Both equal 200 Pa
  4. Both equal 100 Pa
Answer and explanation

C. Both equal 200 Pa. A: 2×0.010/0.0001 = 200 Pa. B: 2×0.020/0.0002 = 200 Pa. The reduced tension compensates for smaller radius in the model.

31. At the same volume an isolated lung requires substantially less pressure when liquid-filled than when air-filled. What is the strongest inference?

  1. All recoil is caused by surface tension
  2. Air-liquid interfacial forces contribute to recoil
  3. The liquid eliminates collagen
  4. Gas exchange improves because compliance rises
Answer and explanation

B. Air-liquid interfacial forces contribute to recoil. Removing the air-liquid interface reduces one mechanical contribution, but residual recoil indicates tissue mechanics. The comparison does not test normal gas exchange.

32. A surfactant preparation reduces surface tension when compressed but fails to spread after expansion. Which interpretation is most reasonable?

  1. One low-tension measurement guarantees effective breathing
  2. Dynamic film renewal may be inadequate despite low tension during compression
  3. Surface tension and viscosity are identical
  4. The film directly activates phrenic motor neurons
Answer and explanation

B. Dynamic film renewal may be inadequate despite low tension during compression. A repeatedly cycling interface requires effective adsorption and redistribution as well as low tension during compression. A single state does not establish full physiological performance.

33. At fixed lung volume Ppl rises from +15 to +25 during stronger forced expiration. Recoil is +8 cm H₂O in both efforts. What happens to Palv − Ppl?

  1. Increases by 10
  2. Decreases by 10
  3. Remains 8
  4. Becomes zero
Answer and explanation

C. Remains 8. Alveolar pressures are +23 and +33; subtracting the matching pleural pressures gives +8 both times. Additional effort does not increase recoil at fixed volume.

34. During graded expirations, at a particular volume flow is 2, 4, 4.1 and 4.1 L/s for progressively greater effort. What is the best interpretation?

  1. Evidence consistent with expiratory flow limitation
  2. Compliance is proven zero
  3. All airways are fully rigid
  4. Respiratory muscles have stopped contracting
Answer and explanation

A. Evidence consistent with expiratory flow limitation. A plateau in flow despite increased effort at fixed volume is consistent with dynamic flow limitation. It is not proof of absent muscle activity or zero compliance.

35. Two units have compliance 0.1 L/cm H₂O and resistance 2 versus 8 cm H₂O·s/L. What are their time constants?

  1. 0.2 and 0.8 s
  2. 2 and 8 s
  3. 20 and 80 s
  4. 0.05 and 0.0125 s
Answer and explanation

A. 0.2 and 0.8 s. τ = RC; multiplying by 0.1 yields 0.2 and 0.8 s. The high-resistance compartment fills and empties more slowly in the model.

36. A compartment with τ = 0.5 s is allowed to empty passively for 1.5 s. What fraction of its initial excess volume remains?

  1. 95%
  2. 63%
  3. 37%
  4. Approximately 5%
Answer and explanation

D. Approximately 5%. The interval is three time constants, so v/v0 = exp(−3) ≈ 0.0498. About 95% has left, not remained.

37. A pressure step gives the same final volume to two units with τ = 0.2 and 1.0 s. After 0.2 s, which unit has filled more?

  1. The 1.0 s unit, because its time constant is larger
  2. The 0.2 s unit, about 63% versus 18%
  3. Both 63%
  4. Neither can fill until equilibrium
Answer and explanation

B. The 0.2 s unit, about 63% versus 18%. For the fast unit t/τ = 1 and fraction is 0.632. For the slow unit t/τ = 0.2 and fraction is 1 − e^−0.2 ≈ 0.181.

38. A lung has Ers = 12 cm H₂O/L and R = 5 cm H₂O·s/L. At v = 0.25 L and inward Q = 0.4 L/s, what is driving pressure in the linear model?

  1. 3
  2. 2
  3. 5
  4. 7.8
Answer and explanation

C. 5. Elastic term = 12×0.25 = 3; resistive term = 5×0.4 = 2; sum = 5 cm H₂O. The first two answers give only one contribution.

39. At fixed compliance, tidal volume increases from 0.4 to 0.6 L. What is the factor change in linear elastic work per breath?

  1. 1.5
  2. 2.25
  3. 0.67
  4. 3
Answer and explanation

B. 2.25. Elastic work is proportional to volume squared, so (0.6/0.4)² = 2.25. This is per inflation and does not account for changes in frequency.

40. A linear airway has R = 6 cm H₂O·s/L. A 0.6 L breath enters at constant flow in 2 s. What is resistive inspiratory work?

  1. 0.54 cm H₂O·L
  2. 1.08 cm H₂O·L
  3. 3.6 cm H₂O·L
  4. 0.18 cm H₂O·L
Answer and explanation

B. 1.08 cm H₂O·L. Flow = 0.3 L/s. Work = RQ²t = 6×0.09×2 = 1.08 cm H₂O·L. 0.54 would omit half the duration.

41. In a passive inflation test, ΔV = 0.5 L, airway pressure rises by 10 and estimated pleural pressure by 4 cm H₂O at zero flow. What is estimated lung compliance?

  1. 0.05
  2. 0.125
  3. 0.0833
  4. 0.5 L/cm H₂O
Answer and explanation

C. 0.0833. ΔPL = 10 − 4 = 6; CL = 0.5/6 ≈ 0.0833 L/cm H₂O. Total compliance is 0.05 and chest-wall compliance is 0.125.

42. A study reports gastric pressure +9 and oesophageal pressure −6 cm H₂O during an inspiratory effort. Estimated transdiaphragmatic pressure is:

  1. 3
  2. −15
  3. 15
  4. −3 cm H₂O
Answer and explanation

C. 15. Pdi ≈ Pgastric − Poesophageal = 9 − (−6) = 15 cm H₂O. The signs matter because pressures are recorded on opposite sides of the diaphragm.

43. At equal lung volume, a subject has high static compliance but low forced expiratory flow. Which mechanism coherently explains both?

  1. Greater recoil and wider airways
  2. Loss of elastic recoil and supporting traction
  3. Increased surfactant tension alone
  4. A perfectly rigid chest wall with increased recoil
Answer and explanation

B. Loss of elastic recoil and supporting traction. Loss of parenchymal recoil increases compliance while reducing passive expiratory driving pressure and airway support. The other mechanisms do not predict this combination as directly.

44. A passive system has pressure 3 at zero flow, 4 at 0.25 L/s, 6 at 0.5 L/s and 9 at 0.75 L/s at matched volume. What does the dataset show?

  1. Constant resistance of 4
  2. Increasing effective resistance with flow
  3. Compliance must fall with each flow
  4. No flow-dependent pressure loss
Answer and explanation

B. Increasing effective resistance with flow. Subtract the zero-flow pressure: losses 1, 3, 6. Dividing by flow gives 4, 6, 8 cm H₂O·s/L. A single fixed resistance is inconsistent; the mechanism of nonlinearity needs further evidence.

45. An animal maintains minute ventilation at 6 L/min. With dead space 0.15 L, breathing changes from 12×0.5 L to 20×0.3 L. How does ideal alveolar ventilation change?

  1. 4.2 to 3.0 L/min
  2. 6 to 6 L/min
  3. 3.0 to 4.2 L/min
  4. 4.2 to 6 L/min
Answer and explanation

A. 4.2 to 3.0 L/min. VA = f(VT−VD). Original = 12×0.35 = 4.2; new = 20×0.15 = 3.0. Equal minute ventilation does not imply equal delivery of fresh alveolar gas.

SET and integrated practice

46. Assertion: Airflow may be zero despite a nonzero alveolar-to-atmospheric pressure difference. Reason: An occluded airway can prevent gas movement.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion. An occlusion makes pathway resistance effectively very high, so pressure gradients can persist without bulk airflow. The usual equality of alveolar and atmospheric pressure at no flow assumes airway patency.

47. Assertion: Quiet inspiration increases transpulmonary pressure. Reason: Pleural pressure usually falls more than alveolar pressure during inspiration.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

A. Both true; reason explains assertion. PL = Palv−Ppl, so the greater fall in Ppl increases the difference. This applies to typical spontaneous inspiration, not necessarily every externally imposed mode.

48. Which statement combination is correct? I: Airway resistance can depend on lung volume. II: Static compliance is measured after flow has ceased. III: Static lung compliance and total respiratory-system compliance are always equal.

  1. I only
  2. II and III
  3. I and II
  4. I, II and III
Answer and explanation

C. I and II. I follows from parenchymal traction; II separates the elastic relation from bulk-flow pressure loss. III is false because chest-wall elastance contributes to total system elastance.

49. A researcher measures no-flow pressure-volume curves before and after a surfactant alteration. Which additional comparison best helps distinguish an interfacial change from tissue stiffness?

  1. Measure body temperature only
  2. Compare air-filled and liquid-filled preparations under matched loading
  3. Measure erythrocyte count only
  4. Measure one peak flow without pressure
Answer and explanation

B. Compare air-filled and liquid-filled preparations under matched loading. Liquid filling removes the relevant air-liquid interface and reveals residual tissue mechanics. Matched volume, inflation history and conditions are essential controls.

50. What is the best reason that an isolated lung compliance measurement cannot alone predict voluntary ventilation?

  1. It includes all neural and muscular pressure generation
  2. It ignores respiratory motor capacity, chest-wall load and flow resistance
  3. Lung volume cannot be measured in isolation
  4. Compliance is always zero outside the thorax
Answer and explanation

B. It ignores respiratory motor capacity, chest-wall load and flow resistance. The isolated passive measurement characterises one load. In the intact system neural and muscle force, chest-wall mechanics and resistance jointly determine airflow and volume.

51. A high-resistance compartment receives a brief inspiratory pressure pulse but never reaches its normal static volume. Which next experiment best tests whether compliance itself is altered?

  1. Shorten the pulse further
  2. Increase pressure and flow simultaneously without measuring them
  3. Hold the same pressure until equilibration, then compare volume
  4. Measure blood oxygen only
Answer and explanation

C. Hold the same pressure until equilibration, then compare volume. A sufficiently long controlled hold isolates the static volume response. If it recovers to the expected value, delayed filling rather than lower static compliance may explain the brief pulse result.

52. A model gives inflation work of 2.5 cm H₂O·L per breath. Approximately how much mechanical energy is this?

  1. 2.5 J
  2. 0.245 J
  3. 24.5 J
  4. 0.025 J
Answer and explanation

B. 0.245 J. 1 cm H₂O·L ≈ 0.0981 J. Multiplying by 2.5 gives about 0.245 J; this is mechanical work, not total muscular metabolic expenditure.

Revision and mastery

Mechanistic summary

Spontaneous inspiration begins with respiratory muscle activation. Thoracic expansion reduces pleural pressure, raises transpulmonary distending pressure and produces inward airflow while alveolar pressure is below airway-opening pressure. During quiet expiration, declining inspiratory effort permits elastic recoil. Surface tension contributes to recoil, while surfactant reduces the interfacial load and helps maintain stable aeration. Airway calibre, branching and collapsibility determine flow resistance. Elastic and resistive loads together govern the volume, timing and mechanical cost of breathing.
