1. The physical problem of ventilating a lung

A mammalian lung exchanges oxygen and carbon dioxide with the blood, but the gas within its alveoli cannot be left permanently in contact with the same blood supply. Oxygen would be progressively removed and carbon dioxide would accumulate. Ventilation renews the gas available for diffusion by moving air between the environment and the respiratory tract. Unlike diffusion, which describes random molecular motion producing net movement down a concentration or partial-pressure gradient, ventilation is bulk flow: a mixture of gases moves together because of a difference in total pressure.

The distinction matters when an animal is able to expand its lungs but unable to exchange gases efficiently, or when the exchange membrane is intact but air cannot reach it. A mechanically normal respiratory system can still have a defect in blood flow or membrane diffusion. Conversely, a mechanically obstructed airway can prevent the replenishment of an otherwise functional gas-exchange surface. The mechanics of breathing is concerned principally with the physical processes that generate bulk flow. Later chapters examine the consequences of that flow for alveolar ventilation, oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide removal.

An isolated lung has no skeletal muscles within its alveolar walls that can pull each alveolus open on command. Instead, the thoracic cage and diaphragm act as a ventilatory pump, with the pleural membranes transmitting changes in the geometry of the thorax to the lungs. The conducting airways form the route along which air moves; the distal respiratory structures form a deformable gas reservoir. The system consequently resembles a bellows coupled to elastic tissue by a fluid interface, rather than a rigid pump. The analogy is limited because the lung is heterogeneous, vascularised, branched, covered by a dynamic surface film and subject to changing mechanical stresses.

Three mechanical demands must be met. First, the respiratory muscles must deform the lung and chest wall against their elastic properties. Second, they must create enough pressure difference to overcome resistance as gas moves through the airways. Third, under rapidly changing conditions, they must accelerate gas and tissues. The third contribution is relatively small in ordinary quiet breathing but becomes more relevant at high frequencies. The first two demands dominate many elementary experiments. Separating them is useful because an increase in resistance and an increase in stiffness may both make breathing difficult while producing different patterns of volume and airflow.

Imagine two expandable bags, each attached to a tube. The first has stiff walls and a wide tube; the second has flexible walls and a narrow tube. If air is delivered at a fixed rate, the first eventually needs a large pressure to maintain a large volume even after flow has stopped. The second needs extra pressure mainly while air is moving. This distinction anticipates static compliance and flow resistance. In a living lung the two properties interact: expansion can widen airways through tissue traction, and obstruction can alter the volume at which breathing occurs.

Pressure is force per unit area. The SI unit is the pascal, equal to one newton per square metre. Respiratory mechanics commonly uses centimetres of water because the relevant differences during quiet breathing are small. One centimetre of water corresponds to approximately 98.1 Pa. A pressure difference of only one centimetre of water is quite sufficient to generate appreciable respiratory flow through healthy airways. It is not necessary to generate a pressure difference comparable to the full atmospheric pressure.

It is essential to identify the pressure reference before assigning a sign. Atmospheric pressure may be approximately 760 mmHg at sea level, varying with weather and altitude. In a mechanical breathing diagram it is convenient to set the local atmosphere to zero, so that pressures below atmospheric are negative and pressures above it are positive. Negative gauge pressure does not mean physically negative absolute pressure. A pressure of minus five centimetres of water represents a small reduction from ambient absolute pressure. This distinction will be essential when Boyle's law is used, because that law requires absolute pressure.

2. Muscles, ribs and the mechanically coupled pleural surfaces

The diaphragm separates the thoracic and abdominal cavities. At rest it forms a dome, with muscular fibres extending towards its central tendon. Contraction shortens those fibres and usually moves the central tendon caudally, increasing thoracic volume. The abdominal contents are displaced and abdominal pressure generally rises. The lower ribs can also move outward because of the geometry of diaphragmatic attachment. Thus diaphragmatic contraction is not a simple vertical piston movement, even though the piston model captures an important component of its action.

The rib cage changes its anteroposterior and transverse dimensions. Elevation of the upper ribs and sternum increases the anteroposterior dimension; elevation of the lower ribs contributes to lateral expansion. These movements are often described as pump-handle and bucket-handle movements. They are geometric descriptions, not distinct types of muscle. External intercostal muscles assist inspiratory expansion and stabilise the intercostal spaces. Their mechanical effect depends on the orientation of the fibres, the rib level and the prevailing shape of the chest wall.

The diaphragm receives motor innervation from the phrenic nerves, arising mainly from cervical spinal segments C3 to C5. Intercostal nerves provide motor input to the intercostal muscles. These anatomical facts connect neural activation to the mechanical events of inspiration without requiring the detailed respiratory rhythm circuitry studied in the regulation chapter. A reduction in phrenic motor output can reduce diaphragmatic force while leaving airway calibre and lung elastic tissue initially unchanged. The resulting defect is a failure of pressure generation rather than an intrinsic loss of compliance.

During quiet inspiration, inspiratory muscles contract and do mechanical work. At the end of inspiration the newly expanded lung stores elastic energy, much of which is recovered during the following expiration. Quiet expiration is largely passive in a healthy resting adult: inspiratory activation declines, the lung recoils, and air leaves. This does not imply that no neural activity or muscular tension exists during expiration. Post-inspiratory activity, laryngeal behaviour and posture can influence the rate of emptying. The basic distinction is that active expiratory muscle contraction is not normally needed for ordinary tidal breathing.

When ventilation increases, accessory inspiratory muscles can assist by lifting or stabilising the thoracic cage. The sternocleidomastoid and scalene muscles are representative examples. During active expiration the abdominal muscles raise abdominal pressure and push the diaphragm cranially, while the expiratory portions of the internal intercostals depress the ribs. Interosseous internal intercostal fibres commonly aid expiration; parasternal interchondral portions can assist inspiration. Treating every internal intercostal fibre as a uniform expiratory motor is therefore an oversimplification.

The visceral pleura lies against the lung and the parietal pleura lines the thoracic wall. A small quantity of pleural liquid separates the membranes. It lubricates their relative motion and transmits mechanical forces, allowing the lung to follow expansion of the thoracic enclosure. The two surfaces are not fused. They can slide as the chest wall changes shape, but their separation is normally strongly resisted by the mechanics of the closed pleural space. An everyday approximation is two wet glass plates that slide along one another more readily than they separate; the living pleural space is more complex because pressure, fluid turnover, geometry and tissue elasticity also matter.

If the pleural cavity communicates freely with the atmosphere, its pressure can approach atmospheric pressure. The normal distending pressure across the lung is reduced and the affected lung tends to recoil. The chest wall may move outward towards its own unstressed configuration. This situation, pneumothorax, reveals that the lung and chest wall normally exert opposing elastic forces. It is not principally a failure of oxygen diffusion through an alveolus, nor is it simply a hole in a conducting airway. The essential mechanical defect is disruption of the pressure relationship across the lung. The extent of collapse depends on the communication, geometry, adhesions and pressure conditions.

The normal pleural pressure is below atmospheric near resting lung volume, but not because the pleural cavity is a vacuum. The inward elastic tendency of the lung and the outward tendency of the chest wall establish a subatmospheric pressure in the fluid-containing pleural space. Its magnitude varies with position and gravity. Pleural pressure is generally more negative at the apex than the base of an upright lung, contributing to regional differences in resting expansion. A single pressure value in a textbook diagram is a representative simplification rather than the literal pressure everywhere.

3. Pressure differences through a complete quiet breath

Let Pao denote pressure at the airway opening, Palv alveolar pressure and Ppl pleural pressure. At an open mouth or nose, Pao is approximately the local atmospheric pressure, defined here as zero gauge. Transpulmonary pressure PL is Palv minus Ppl. It is the distending pressure across the lung as a whole. The pressure difference that drives bulk airflow, however, is Pao minus Palv for inspiratory flow. These are distinct differences across distinct physical structures.

At the end of quiet expiration, the airway is open and there is no net flow in the idealised model. Palv equals Pao, approximately zero. A representative pleural pressure is minus five centimetres of water, so PL equals zero minus negative five, or positive five centimetres of water. The lung is distended even though there is no airflow. The resting volume at this equilibrium is called functional residual capacity, whose measurement and subdivision into component volumes are considered in the next chapter.

Inspiratory muscle contraction enlarges the thoracic cavity. Pleural pressure becomes more negative, increasing the distending pressure on the lung. As lung volume begins to increase, alveolar pressure falls slightly below atmospheric pressure. Air flows inward down the resulting pressure gradient. A representative early inspiratory moment could have Palv equal to minus one centimetre of water and Ppl equal to minus seven, yielding a positive transpulmonary pressure of six. The negative alveolar pressure drives entry; the positive transpulmonary pressure maintains distension.

As inspiration proceeds, incoming air increases the number of gas molecules inside the expanding lung. Alveolar pressure therefore does not continue falling indefinitely. The relationship between volume change, gas entry and the changing muscular effort determines the instantaneous pressure. Near the end of inspiration the flow slows, and when it stops Palv again equals atmospheric pressure. The expanded lung still has a higher transpulmonary pressure than before inspiration, perhaps eight centimetres of water in an illustrative cycle. Equal alveolar and atmospheric pressures do not mean equal lung volumes at the two no-flow moments: pleural pressure differs.

Once inspiratory muscular activity declines, the distending effect of the thoracic muscles decreases and elastic recoil reduces lung volume. Compression of alveolar gas raises Palv slightly above atmospheric pressure, for example plus one centimetre of water during passive expiration. Air then flows outward. Pleural pressure becomes less negative than at the end of inspiration, but usually remains negative during quiet expiration. Positive alveolar pressure and negative pleural pressure can therefore coexist. Substituting these values into PL shows why the lung remains distended while air leaves.

At the end of expiration the respiratory system approaches its equilibrium resting volume. Flow ceases again and alveolar pressure returns to atmospheric pressure. In a normal resting cycle the end-expiratory lung does not empty. A substantial volume remains because the balance of elastic forces is reached at a volume greater than zero. The sequence is cyclic: muscle action changes pleural pressure, pleural pressure changes lung distension, lung volume changes create a small alveolar pressure deviation, and airflow reduces that deviation.

A pressure recording can therefore be interpreted without memorising a set of arbitrary signs. First ask where the pressure is measured. Next ask whether flow is inward, outward or absent. Finally subtract the relevant pressures. If an illustrative record shows Palv equal to plus two and Ppl equal to minus four, the transpulmonary pressure is plus six. If the airway opening is at atmospheric pressure, flow is outward. A positive PL does not specify the direction of airflow.

During a breath with an occluded airway, the relationship changes. An inspiratory effort may lower alveolar pressure substantially while no gas enters because the opening is blocked. The equality between alveolar and atmospheric pressure at zero flow therefore requires a patent airway and enough time for pressure equilibration. An occluded airway is a useful experimental counterexample to the common assumption that zero flow always implies zero alveolar gauge pressure. A pressure gradient may exist without flow when resistance is effectively infinite.

4. Boyle's law, an open airway and gas compressibility

Robert Boyle's seventeenth-century experiments established an inverse relationship between gas pressure and volume under controlled conditions. In a simple ideal gas model, PV equals nRT, where P is absolute pressure, V volume, n amount of gas in moles, R the gas constant and T absolute temperature. If the amount of gas and temperature are constant, nRT is constant. Thus P1V1 equals P2V2. The inverse relationship is nonlinear: doubling the volume halves the absolute pressure, not the gauge pressure.

The connection to breathing is most direct during a short interval in which lung volume changes before much gas has entered or left. If a sealed gas compartment is expanded isothermally, molecular collisions against its walls become less frequent per unit area and the pressure falls. If it is compressed, the pressure rises. In an intact open respiratory system, pressure changes drive flow which simultaneously changes the number of gas molecules. Boyle's law explains the direction of the initial tendency but cannot be used as though the entire tidal breath were an expansion of a fixed quantity of gas.

For a worked example, suppose a hypothetical sealed compartment contains three litres of air at an absolute pressure of 760 mmHg and its volume increases to 3.01 litres without a temperature change. The final absolute pressure is 760 times 3 divided by 3.01, approximately 757.48 mmHg. The gauge pressure relative to the original atmosphere is approximately minus 2.52 mmHg, or minus 3.43 centimetres of water. Even this small relative volume change creates an appreciable gauge-pressure difference in the closed system. The values are illustrative, not measurements of a normal breath.

Now consider mistakenly substituting a gauge pressure of zero into P1V1. The predicted final pressure would remain zero for any volume, implying no pressure change. The error arises because an ambient-referenced zero does not represent an absence of molecular collisions. Absolute pressure must be used in the gas equation. Gauge pressures, on the other hand, can be directly subtracted from one another to obtain pressure differences across anatomical structures, because the common atmospheric reference cancels.

During quiet breathing, alveolar gas is humidified and exchanges oxygen and carbon dioxide with pulmonary capillary blood. Temperature is approximately maintained near body temperature. These conditions do not make the ideal gas law useless, but they mean that the lung is not the sealed isothermal gas chamber of a classical experiment. The extent of gas compression, the flow resistance and the movement of the lung boundary must be considered together. Gas flow can be modelled with a pressure difference divided by resistance, whereas tissue deformation is modelled by compliance.

A bell-jar demonstration can illustrate the mechanism: an expandable bag connected to the atmosphere is housed within a chamber whose lower boundary can be displaced. Lowering that boundary increases the external chamber volume and reduces pressure surrounding the bag; the bag expands and air enters through its tube. The experiment establishes that changing the surrounding pressure can inflate a compliant gas reservoir. It does not reproduce the pleural liquid interface, alveolar surface tension, pulmonary circulation, rib mechanics or neural feedback. It should therefore be used to identify the direction of pressure changes, not to calculate physiological compliance from the properties of a rubber balloon.

5. Compliance and elastic recoil: separate lung and chest wall

Compliance measures how much volume changes per unit change in the distending pressure. Lung compliance CL equals delta V divided by delta PL. It may be expressed in litres per centimetre of water, or millilitres per centimetre of water. Compliance is a slope measured over a specified range or at a specified operating volume. The reciprocal, elastance E, expresses pressure change per unit volume change. High elastance means a stiff structure; high compliance means a readily expandable structure.

If an increase in transpulmonary pressure from five to eight centimetres of water produces a lung-volume increment of 0.6 litres, the mean compliance over that interval is 0.6 divided by three, or 0.2 L per centimetre of water. The corresponding elastance is five centimetres of water per litre. If a second lung expands by only 0.3 litres over the same interval, its compliance is 0.1 and its elastance is ten. The second lung requires twice the pressure increment to achieve the same volume increment within the assumed linear range.

Elastic recoil is the tendency of a deformed structure to return towards a lower-energy configuration. It is not synonymous with compliance. A compliant lung can expand easily and exhibit reduced restoring pressure at a given expansion, whereas a stiff lung can have a high restoring pressure. A rubber band analogy is often misleading because in everyday language a highly stretchable object is called elastic, while physiological elastance describes resistance to expansion. Use the measured pressure-volume slope to avoid the ambiguity.

The lung's elastic behaviour derives from the arrangement of its extracellular matrix and the surface forces at air-liquid interfaces. Elastin fibres support reversible deformation; collagen fibres increasingly bear load as tissue is stretched. At relatively high lung volume additional recruitment and straightening of collagen structures limit distension, so the pressure-volume curve becomes flatter when volume is on the vertical axis. At very low volumes, closure and reopening of lung units can also influence the slope. Compliance is therefore not a universal material constant for the lung.

The chest wall has its own resting configuration. At typical resting lung volume it tends to expand outward; at sufficiently high volumes it tends to recoil inward. Define chest-wall distending pressure Pcw as pleural pressure minus pressure at the body surface, in the idealised passive model. The total pressure across the passive respiratory system is Palv minus body-surface pressure. Adding PL and Pcw cancels Ppl. At a static equilibrium with airway and body surface exposed to the atmosphere, the total pressure is zero even though the individual lung and chest-wall pressures are not zero.

Because both components undergo approximately the same volume change, their pressure increments add. The combined compliance Crs satisfies one over Crs equals one over CL plus one over Ccw. If both components individually have compliance 0.2 L per centimetre of water, the reciprocal values are each five and their sum is ten; combined compliance is 0.1 L per centimetre of water. It is incorrect to add the two compliances directly as though the same pressure independently inflated two parallel reservoirs. For this coupled series mechanical load, the same volume change is associated with the sum of two pressure increments.

The equilibrium volume can shift when either structure changes its recoil curve. Loss of inward lung recoil shifts the balance towards a greater volume, provided the chest-wall curve is unchanged. Increased lung stiffness can shift the balance towards a smaller volume. A chest-wall mechanical limitation can likewise restrict expansion even when the lung's intrinsic pressure-volume curve remains normal. These predictions show why measurements of total respiratory-system compliance alone do not identify the anatomical source of stiffness.

6. Pressure-volume curves, hysteresis and measurement

A static pressure-volume curve is constructed by measuring volume at a series of equilibrated pressures. One can inflate an isolated lung in steps and record the pressure needed to sustain each volume after flow has ceased, then repeat during deflation. At a given transpulmonary pressure, the deflating lung generally has a larger volume than the inflating lung. The difference between the two paths is called hysteresis. The history of the mechanical system influences its present state.

On a plot with pressure on the horizontal axis and volume on the vertical axis, the local slope represents compliance. A steep segment corresponds to a large volume change for a small pressure change. If the axes are reversed, the slope instead represents elastance. Examination questions commonly exploit this reversal. The direction of traversal must also be recognised: inflation proceeds towards larger volumes, whereas deflation proceeds towards smaller volumes. A curve alone without axis labels or direction cannot support an unambiguous interpretation.

Several effects contribute to hysteresis. Lung recruitment means the opening of units that were previously closed or poorly aerated; derecruitment is the converse. Opening pressures can exceed closing pressures. Surface film behaviour also differs during expansion and compression. Tissue viscoelasticity contributes time-dependent deformation. The relative importance of each mechanism depends on volume range, prior inflation history, measurement conditions and the preparation. It is therefore incorrect to attribute every observed loop exclusively to airway resistance or to surfactant.

Dynamic measurements are performed during flow. The pressure required at a given instant includes both the elastic load and the pressure losses associated with flow. A measurement that does not distinguish flow-related pressure from distending pressure can yield an apparent compliance lower than static compliance. At higher breathing frequencies, regions with high resistance may have insufficient time to fill, causing frequency-dependent dynamic compliance. A static measurement after sufficient equilibration helps separate tissue stiffness from delayed filling.

An oesophageal balloon pressure recording can approximate pleural pressure changes in an intact animal. Combined with airflow and airway pressure, it allows estimation of transpulmonary pressure and breathing effort. The oesophageal pressure is not exactly the pleural pressure at every lung region. Posture, local surrounding structures, balloon positioning and calibration affect the relationship. A student should regard the measurement as a useful estimate with a defined domain of validity rather than as a perfect direct recording of all regional pressures.

In a controlled pressure-flow experiment, compare two hypothetical conditions with the same delivered volume of 0.5 L and the same constant flow of 0.5 L per second. In condition A the airway pressure peaks at 15 and falls to 10 centimetres of water during a no-flow hold. In condition B the peak is 20 but the plateau remains 10. If baseline pressure, muscle activity and other assumptions are matched, the extra peak pressure in condition B suggests a larger resistive pressure loss rather than an increase in static elastic load. Under the simple model, the resistances estimated from peak-minus-plateau divided by flow are ten and twenty centimetres of water seconds per litre. These numbers describe a hypothetical passive inflation test, not normal spontaneous breathing.

7. Surface tension, alveolar geometry and surfactant

A thin aqueous lining covers the alveolar epithelium. Water molecules attract one another through intermolecular forces, and creating new air-water interfacial area requires energy. Surface tension is the reversible work per unit new surface area under specified conditions, equivalently a force per unit length. Its SI unit is N per metre. A curved interface with surface tension creates a pressure difference. The smaller the radius of curvature, the larger the pressure difference for a given constant tension.

For an ideal spherical gas space surrounded by liquid with a single air-liquid interface, the Laplace relation is delta P equals 2T divided by r. It follows by balancing pressure work during expansion against the increase in interfacial energy: dV is 4 pi r squared dr and dA is 8 pi r dr, so delta P equals T dA/dV, giving 2T/r. This derivation assumes a spherical interface, uniform tension and a quasistatic displacement. A soap bubble with two interfaces requires a different factor, so four T divided by radius should not be substituted for the one-interface model.

Consider two ideal gas spaces with radii 100 and 200 micrometres and a constant surface tension of 0.025 N/m. The smaller space has a calculated pressure difference of 500 Pa, or approximately 5.10 centimetres of water. The larger has 250 Pa, approximately 2.55 centimetres of water. If the spaces communicate with otherwise identical external pressures and no supporting structures, gas tends to move from the higher-pressure smaller space towards the larger. This hypothetical result illustrates a destabilising tendency of constant surface tension.

Real alveoli are not isolated spherical balloons. Their septa are shared, their geometry is irregular, and a continuous tissue network distributes stresses. Pulmonary surfactant alters the tension at the air-liquid interface, and its surface behaviour depends on composition, area and history. The simple Laplace model is therefore a way of identifying how curvature and tension influence pressure, not an exact equation for predicting pressure differences between every pair of alveoli.

Type II alveolar epithelial cells produce pulmonary surfactant, a mixture enriched in phospholipids including dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine and containing specific proteins. Material is stored in lamellar bodies before release onto the alveolar surface. Hydrophilic portions interact with the aqueous lining, and hydrophobic portions favour orientation towards the gas phase. The interfacial film reduces the energetic cost of expanding air-liquid area compared with a clean aqueous interface. Surfactant-associated proteins contribute to film formation and stability; the immune roles of some surfactant proteins are distinct from their mechanical effects.

As an alveolar surface contracts, film components can become more densely packed and effective surface tension can fall substantially. This behaviour can counteract the effect of decreasing radius on the Laplace pressure. For an ideal example, if radius halves while tension also halves, the ratio T/r remains unchanged. The smaller space need not have a greater Laplace pressure in this model. Actual film behaviour is more complicated than a universal proportionality between radius and tension, and neighbouring tissue support further stabilises real alveoli.

The mechanical importance of the interface was demonstrated in classical experiments by Kurt von Neergaard in 1929. By comparing pressure-volume behaviour of an isolated lung expanded with gas and with liquid, he found that liquid filling substantially reduced the pressure needed for expansion. Removing the alveolar air-liquid interface reduced the surface contribution to recoil. A useful control is the same preparation or comparable tissue under matched volume and loading conditions; residual recoil in the liquid-filled lung shows that tissue elasticity still contributes. The finding does not imply that surface tension is the only determinant of lung mechanics.

In 1959, Mary Ellen Avery and Jere Mead reported evidence of deficient surface-active material in lung extracts from infants with hyaline membrane disease. The comparison linked the mechanical problem of neonatal lung expansion to the composition and behaviour of the lung surface film. Subsequent research established the importance of surfactant in reducing collapse and lowering inflation work. The history illustrates how an observation at the organ level, followed by measurement of interfacial properties, can identify a molecular contribution to physiology. The details of neonatal care are beyond the present mechanical scope.

Surfactant deficiency can decrease compliance, raise the pressure needed to recruit collapsed regions and increase the work of inflation. This does not mean that surfactant creates gas flow by acting as a respiratory muscle. The driving pressure gradient still results from mechanical deformation or external ventilation. Surfactant modifies the load against which that process occurs. Its contribution to alveolar stability and reduced surface forces connects the cell biology of type II pneumocytes with the organ-level mechanics of breathing.

8. Airway resistance, radius and flow patterns

The movement of gas through the respiratory passages encounters resistance. For a simple linear model, flow Q equals the pressure difference across the airway divided by resistance R. If Q is measured in litres per second and the pressure difference in centimetres of water, R has units of centimetres of water seconds per litre. This resistance is not identical to tissue elastance. A narrow airway can impose a large pressure loss during flow while contributing no pressure loss from viscous flow when the gas is static.

For steady, fully developed laminar flow of an incompressible Newtonian fluid through a straight rigid circular tube, Poiseuille's relation gives Q equals pi r to the fourth power times pressure difference divided by 8 viscosity times tube length. Resistance is therefore 8 viscosity times length divided by pi r to the fourth power. The formula demonstrates why small radius changes strongly affect resistance. Its assumptions are important: respiratory air is compressible, airways branch and deform, flow is pulsatile, and turbulent or developing flow can occur.

If the radius of an ideal tube decreases from two millimetres to one millimetre while length and viscosity are unchanged, the predicted resistance increases by a factor of sixteen. With the same pressure difference, flow becomes one sixteenth of its initial value. This does not mean that narrowing one bronchiole halves the radius of the entire respiratory system or increases total airway resistance sixteenfold. The affected airway belongs to a branched network, and the result depends on where and how extensively narrowing occurs.

Resistances in series add because the same flow passes through each segment and pressure losses accumulate. Parallel resistances combine reciprocally because separate pathways share a pressure difference and their flows add. The many small peripheral airways have an enormous collective cross-sectional area and provide numerous parallel routes. Although each small airway individually may have appreciable resistance, their combined resistance can be modest in a healthy lung. A substantial loss of parallel pathways or widespread small airway narrowing changes this relationship.

Laminar flow is organised into relatively smooth layers, with the highest velocity near the tube centre. Turbulent flow involves irregular fluctuations and enhanced mixing. The Reynolds number, defined as density times mean velocity times characteristic diameter divided by dynamic viscosity, is dimensionless and provides a guide to the relative roles of inertial and viscous forces. It does not establish a universal transition threshold for every branching, oscillating biological airway. Turbulence and flow separation are more likely in regions with high velocities, abrupt geometry changes or high flow during forced breathing.

Airway smooth muscle can change calibre. Parasympathetic cholinergic signalling generally favours bronchoconstriction, whereas beta-2 adrenergic receptor activation favours relaxation. Mucus and oedema may narrow the functional lumen without requiring smooth muscle contraction. Mechanical traction from surrounding parenchyma can enlarge intrapulmonary airways at higher lung volume. Therefore changes in airway resistance can arise from neural, cellular, secretory or tissue-level mechanisms. A measured resistance increase alone cannot identify which mechanism produced it.

In a simple teaching calculation, an inspiratory pressure difference of two centimetres of water and airway resistance of four centimetres of water seconds per litre give flow of 0.5 litres per second. If resistance doubles at the same pressure difference, flow falls to 0.25 litres per second. To maintain the original flow, the pressure difference must double under the linear model. In a real airway system, pressure loss can increase more than proportionally at higher flows, so an effective resistance calculated from a single pressure and flow pair is conditional on that measurement.
