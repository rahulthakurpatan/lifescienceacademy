1. Why a second division is indispensable

A fertilization event brings together two haploid chromosome sets. If each participating cell carried the same number of chromosome sets as the diploid parent, fertilization would double ploidy in every sexual generation. Meiosis prevents this accumulation by creating nuclei containing a single representative of each homologous chromosome pair. However, reduction of chromosome sets is only half of its task. Each chromosome must also be delivered with the appropriate number of DNA molecules. The first division achieves the reduction in sets; the second resolves the replicated chromosomes that remain.

Imagine an organism with two different chromosome types, A and B. A diploid precursor has one maternal and one paternal homolog of each type: A-maternal, A-paternal, B-maternal and B-paternal. These are four chromosomes. Before meiosis begins, each chromosome duplicates its DNA, but its two daughter DNA molecules remain physically connected as sister chromatids. The first meiotic division sends one homolog of A and one homolog of B to each daughter nucleus. Each nucleus is therefore haploid, even though each chromosome remains duplicated. Neither nucleus can become a typical final 1C meiotic product until its two chromatids per chromosome have been distributed.

Meiosis II solves this remaining problem by placing sister kinetochores in opposition, releasing the physical connections that hold the sisters together and transferring one chromatid of each replicated chromosome into each future nucleus. This is called an equational division because the number of chromosome sets per resulting nucleus does not change. What changes is the amount of nuclear DNA per nucleus. Compare this to meiosis I, a reductional division in which homologous chromosomes separate while sister chromatids normally remain associated.

The distinction also explains why meiosis is not simply mitosis performed twice. A normal mitotic division begins with replicated chromosomes, separates sister chromatids and preserves the original ploidy. Repeating such a process with a new S phase in between would yield four cells that are each still diploid. In a standard meiosis, a single premeiotic S phase is followed by two nuclear divisions of different chromosome behavior. Their combination produces four haploid nuclear products from one diploid precursor. Actual cell numbers and developmental fates can differ: a mammalian oocyte, for example, produces a large egg and polar bodies rather than four equally sized functional gametes.

To understand the second division, we will first identify exactly what is inherited from meiosis I, then ask how a spindle recognizes and separates the appropriate units, how cohesion is retained and released in the correct order, and how failure of either division changes the final chromosome distribution.

2. The vocabulary of chromosome and DNA counts

Three quantities must be kept separate. Ploidy describes the number of complete chromosome sets in a nucleus. In a normal diploid organism, 2n is the somatic chromosome number and n is the chromosome number of a complete haploid set. A chromosome type is represented twice in the diploid state and once in the haploid state. Ploidy is about homologous sets, not the number of DNA strands or the visual thickness of a chromosome.

The symbol C denotes the amount of DNA in one unreplicated haploid nuclear genome. A diploid G1 nucleus has 2C. After S phase the same nucleus contains 4C but remains diploid: its homologous sets have not changed. At the end of meiosis I, each daughter nucleus contains n chromosomes and 2C of DNA. The apparent paradox of a haploid nucleus with 2C disappears once we realize that it contains one homolog of each pair, and that homolog has two DNA molecules. At the end of meiosis II, each nucleus has n chromosomes and 1C. C-value is a reference amount of DNA; it is not a name for a cell-cycle phase.

For chromosome counting, use the conventional cytogenetic rule that a replicated chromosome with joined sisters is counted as one chromosome. Although there are replicated centromeric DNA sequences and two kinetochores, the sisters form one segregation unit until they disjoin. After the centromeric cohesion is released in anaphase II, each former chromatid is a separate daughter chromosome. A single undivided anaphase-II cell transiently contains 2n daughter chromosomes distributed into two groups of n. This is not the same as formation of a diploid daughter nucleus: the two groups are spatially segregating and each receives a single set. Do not describe this transient doubling of chromosome number as restoration of the diploid condition.

A chromatid contains one continuous double-stranded nuclear DNA molecule in the simplified model of a normal linear eukaryotic chromosome. One chromosome can therefore contain one or two such molecules depending on replication and separation. Before S phase, one may describe a chromosome as a single-chromatid chromosome; after separation the word chromatid is best replaced by daughter chromosome. Individual DNA strands are not counted as chromatids. If a question specifies DNA molecules, count one double helix per chromatid, not two polynucleotide chains. Mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA are excluded unless the question explicitly includes them.

As a first example, take a species with 2n = 12. Its premeiotic G1 nucleus contains 12 chromosomes and 12 nuclear DNA molecules, giving 2C. Following replication, the cell still contains 12 chromosomes but now 24 chromatids and 4C. Each metaphase-I cell has six bivalents, because a bivalent consists of two replicated homologs. Each meiosis-I daughter has six duplicated chromosomes, 12 chromatids and 2C. At metaphase II these same counts apply. After sister separation, count 12 daughter chromosomes in the whole undivided cell, but six at each pole. At the end of division, each of the four products has six chromosomes, six DNA molecules and 1C. A count of 12 at anaphase II cannot be used alone to infer that homologous pairs are present.

3. What meiosis I leaves behind

The second division is only possible because of the particular way chromosomes were arranged in the first. During meiotic prophase I, corresponding maternal and paternal chromosomes associate along their lengths. Two homologs with two chromatids each constitute a bivalent, also called a tetrad when attention is directed to the four chromatids. Homologs carry largely corresponding genetic loci but can have different alleles. Sisters were originally produced by replication of one homolog. The distinction between sister relationship and allelic identity becomes important after crossing over.

Crossing over during prophase I involves physical exchange of corresponding DNA segments between nonsister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. After the synaptonemal complex disassembles, the visible connections between homologs at crossover sites are called chiasmata. A chiasma is maintained by sister chromatid cohesion along the chromosome arms, especially distal to a crossover. The exchanged DNA segments themselves are not transient ropes tying homologs together; rather, completed exchange changes the continuity of chromatids, and the resulting topology together with cohesion makes the homologs behave as a connected bivalent. Without this connection, two homologs may fail to establish a stable orientation in many species.

At metaphase I, the paired sister kinetochores of each homolog act together toward one spindle pole while the corresponding homolog is connected toward the opposite pole. Spindle pulling forces are opposed by the chiasma-associated connections and cohesion. At the onset of anaphase I, loss of arm cohesion allows the chiasmata to resolve mechanically, so homologs move apart. Centromeric cohesion remains, preserving the pair of sister chromatids within each segregating homolog.

After telophase I, each new nucleus contains only one homologous chromosome from each pair. It does not generally contain an intact set solely from one parent: orientation of the different bivalents is independent in the usual model, and crossing over has changed segments of some chromosomes. This means that the blue or orange color used to represent a homolog in a simple diagram is usually a label for its centromere lineage, not a guarantee that every DNA segment on the chromatids came from that parent. If a crossover involved only one of two sisters, they may carry different alleles in the exchanged region. The sisters must still separate in meiosis II regardless of their sequence differences.

There is also a geometric inheritance. The sister kinetochores had to behave as a single directed unit at meiosis I, but in the second division they must become independently accessible to opposite poles. Retaining the first-division orientation would send both sister chromatids to the same side. Thus the second division requires a change in kinetochore geometry as well as a change in which cohesin connections can be removed. The two changes are related but not identical: changing attachment orientation does not itself cut cohesin, and cutting cohesin does not guarantee correct attachment.

4. Interkinesis and the suppression of a new S phase

Between the two meiotic divisions there is usually a brief transition called interkinesis. Depending on species and cell type, the nuclear envelope may re-form, chromosomes may partly decondense, or the second division may follow the first almost directly. None of these morphological changes is sufficient to identify an S phase. The defining feature of the standard interkinesis is that bulk chromosomal DNA replication does not occur. The replicated chromosomes that enter meiosis II are the same paired sisters produced in the premeiotic S phase.

Ordinary mitotic cycles have a mechanism to prevent a replication origin from initiating repeatedly in one S phase. A replication origin is a DNA region from which DNA synthesis can start. Licensing is the preparation of an origin for later initiation, including loading of the helicase machinery during a suitable low-CDK interval. Activation of licensed origins in S phase then uses kinase-dependent signals. High CDK activity during and after S phase ordinarily prevents new licensing, helping enforce one round of genome duplication per cycle.

Meiosis changes the normal alternation of replication and segregation. In many organisms, CDK activity decreases sufficiently to allow the exit from meiosis I and reorganization of the spindle, but it does not stay at the low, licensing-permissive state characteristic of a full G1 phase. Residual CDK activity and meiosis-specific regulatory mechanisms prevent a return to a replication-competent state; a rise in division-promoting CDK activity helps establish the second spindle. The detailed cyclins and licensing controls are species-dependent, and the absence of S phase should not be attributed to one identical regulator in all organisms. Some species use additional repression or inhibition of replication factors. The central logic is that exit from one division is permitted without resetting the cycle to begin another genome duplication.

Suppose a hypothetical mutant permits a complete extra S phase in both daughters of meiosis I. Each originally n, 2C daughter would gain another 2C of DNA, assuming all nuclear DNA is copied once. This would create a chromosome organization that does not fit the normal two-sister segregation system, and ordinary meiosis-II outcomes could not be assumed. A student should therefore avoid the tempting conclusion that another division automatically corrects the extra replication. The normal 1C products depend on the fixed initial supply of 4C DNA being partitioned into four groups without replenishment.

How could a cell biologist test the absence of a second S phase? A pulse of a labeled thymidine analogue can reveal synthesis of nuclear DNA. In a suitably staged population, label supplied during premeiotic S phase should be incorporated extensively. A pulse specifically between meiosis I and II should not show the genome-wide replication pattern of S phase. A positive control from replicating cells establishes that the label and detection method work. An unlabeled sample establishes background fluorescence. DNA-content measurements of staged nuclei provide independent evidence: 2C should be retained between divisions, followed by 1C per final nucleus. Limited DNA repair synthesis or mitochondrial replication is not evidence of a new genome-wide nuclear S phase. These distinctions prevent a small localized signal from being misinterpreted as an additional round of DNA duplication.

5. Prophase II: rebuilding a division apparatus

The second division begins with the reorganization of the haploid chromosome group inherited from meiosis I. In organisms that form a nuclear envelope at telophase I, it must be remodeled again to permit access by spindle microtubules in open meiosis. Chromosomes that partly decondensed during interkinesis become compact enough to move without excessive entanglement. In organisms whose chromosomes stay condensed, the apparent prophase II can be very short. A sequence of visible stage names is useful, but real cells do not necessarily pause at each sharply separated textbook stage.

A spindle is a dynamic arrangement of microtubules and associated proteins that positions chromosomes and separates them. Microtubules have a structural polarity: their plus ends are often the more dynamic ends, whereas their minus ends tend to be organized toward spindle poles. In a conventional animal spindle, pole-associated organizing structures help arrange the microtubules into two opposed arrays. In many flowering plant cells and mammalian oocytes, spindles can organize without the typical pair of centrosomes found in many somatic animal cells. The requirement for a bipolar spindle does not imply an absolute requirement for centrioles.

Each duplicated chromosome now presents its sister kinetochores in a configuration capable of attachment toward opposite poles. Kinetochores are multiprotein structures assembled on centromeric chromatin. Their role is to couple a chromosome to the dynamic microtubule ends and communicate whether the attachment process is progressing properly. They are not the same thing as centromeres, which are chromosome regions. Each of the two sister chromatids has its own kinetochore. Cohesion keeps the two chromatids associated until a later stage.

In open division, the stage during which microtubules gain access to the chromosomes and attachments mature is often called prometaphase II. Some textbooks combine it with prophase II, especially when the focus is on four main stages. The presence of a separate stage name does not change the molecular sequence. Chromosomes may first encounter microtubules laterally, become attached to their ends, and reposition as the attachments are remodeled. An unaligned chromosome in early meiosis II does not necessarily indicate failure; it may still be undergoing this search and capture process.

What should distinguish a prophase-II image from prophase I? In prophase I the characteristic process is association of homologs and formation of bivalents, with the five classical substages associated with pairing and recombination. In prophase II the cell already contains a reduced homolog set, and the remaining structures are duplicated individual chromosomes, not bivalents. There is no normal second round of homolog synapsis or crossing over. If the stage is inferred from a single photograph without chromosome counting or knowledge of the cell lineage, however, prophase II may be difficult to distinguish from mitotic prophase in a haploid cell. Morphology must be interpreted with its developmental context.

6. Metaphase II: attachment, tension and alignment

At metaphase II, each replicated chromosome generally aligns individually on the spindle equator. Imagine a dyad whose two sister kinetochores face opposite sides. If one kinetochore captures microtubules from the left pole and the other captures microtubules from the right, the sisters are bi-oriented. Because they are still physically held together, pulling in opposite directions generates tension across the paired centromeric region. Stable bipolar attachment provides a geometry in which the subsequent removal of cohesion will allow the two sisters to move to different poles.

Not all attachments that move a chromosome toward the center are correct. In a monotelic attachment only one sister kinetochore is attached to a pole; the other lacks the appropriate connection. In a syntelic attachment both sisters are attached toward the same pole, which would send both to that side if cohesion were released. In a merotelic attachment a single kinetochore binds microtubules from both poles. Merotely can be especially problematic because a kinetochore may be occupied and therefore not produce the same checkpoint signal as an unattached kinetochore. It can give rise to a lagging chromosome during anaphase.

Bi-orientation is established by repeated testing and correction. The Aurora family of kinases is part of a conserved error-correction system; in vertebrate meiotic cells the particular contributions of Aurora B and C depend on the cell type and division. Phosphorylation of attachment components can reduce the stability of improperly configured kinetochore–microtubule contacts, allowing another capture opportunity. Correct geometry and the resulting mechanical state generally stabilize proper contacts. Tension is an important feature of this process, but it is not a simple switch that detects every possible wrong configuration. An unattached kinetochore and an attached but geometrically incorrect kinetochore are not equivalent.

The difference between metaphase I and metaphase II is best understood at the level of the unit attached to the spindle. In I, homologs are oriented oppositely while sisters normally act together toward one pole. In II, the sister kinetochores of the remaining homolog face opposite poles. A bivalent may contain four chromatids, but it behaves as two linked homologous units. A metaphase-II chromosome contains two chromatids and behaves as two opposing sister units linked by retained cohesion. The same physical principle—opposed forces resisted by a temporary connection—is used at both stages with a different connection and a different pair of objects.

When a diagram shows several X-shaped chromosomes on a metaphase plate, count the chromosomes and ask whether they are arranged in homologous pairs. An X shape alone does not tell whether the image represents mitosis or meiosis II. Both have independently aligned replicated chromosomes and bipolar sister attachments. A known diploid chromosome count and the actual number present in the image may resolve the ambiguity. If a species has 2n = 8 and only four replicated chromosomes are seen in a normal secondary meiocyte, the number fits metaphase II. Four chromosomes could also appear in a haploid mitotic cell of that species, so biological context remains essential.

These four attachment patterns can be recognized by following microtubules from each pole to each kinetochore, rather than by counting lines in the figure. In a merotelic arrangement, the defining feature is that one kinetochore receives fibers from both poles; the other sister may also have its own attachment. The diagram includes that possibility. In an examination image, a central position alone cannot certify amphitelic attachment.

7. The molecular logic of two-step cohesion release

The two divisions could not be reliably separated in time if the physical association between sisters were removed everywhere at the first anaphase. The essential regulatory solution is to divide cohesion into two functional regions. Along chromosome arms, cohesion maintains the connection that allows chiasmata to resist spindle pulling during meiosis I. Around the centromeres, cohesion keeps sisters associated so that they remain a usable chromosome unit after the homologs have separated. The first division removes the former and preserves the latter; the second removes the remaining centromeric cohesion.

Cohesin is a protein complex that holds sister chromatids together after DNA replication. Its structural maintenance of chromosomes proteins, SMC1 and SMC3 or their meiotic variants, associate with a connecting kleisin subunit and additional regulatory components. The kleisin helps close the ring-like complex. Rec8 is a characteristic meiotic kleisin in many eukaryotes. Mitotic cohesin commonly uses a related kleisin such as Rad21/Scc1. The molecular details of cohesin architecture are not necessary to predict every exam outcome, but the importance of the kleisin is: cleavage of this component by separase disrupts the cohesive connection. Separase is a protease, an enzyme that cleaves proteins rather than DNA.

At the first metaphase-to-anaphase transition, separase activity is permitted by the cell-cycle regulatory machinery. Arm-associated Rec8 can be cleaved, allowing resolution of chiasma-supported links. If the same reaction occurred at centromeres at the same time, each homolog would lose its sister connection before meiosis II. This would remove the physical basis of the sister bi-orientation system described earlier. The protection of centromeric cohesin is therefore an active, spatially localized regulatory process rather than a simple delay in all separase activity.

A major protector is shugoshin, a family of proteins whose name reflects its guardian function. In several model systems, shugoshin recruits protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) to the pericentromeric region. A phosphatase removes phosphate groups from proteins. Meiotic Rec8 cleavage is promoted by phosphorylation in well-studied organisms. By maintaining Rec8 in a less phosphorylated, less cleavage-susceptible state, PP2A helps protect centromere-associated cohesion while arm cohesion is lost. This is a local protection of the substrate; it need not globally inhibit separase. A cell can have active separase at anaphase I and nevertheless retain centromeric cohesion.

The situation changes before or during anaphase II. The centromeric Rec8 must become susceptible to cleavage, and protection must cease to operate effectively at the relevant sites. The changed arrangement and tension of sister kinetochores can contribute to the spatial separation of protective machinery from its cohesin substrate in some systems; shugoshin localization and additional regulatory steps vary among organisms. Do not infer that all shugoshin molecules must be degraded in every species before anaphase II. The conserved functional requirement is the loss or overcoming of protection at the cohesion that must be released. When separase acts again, remaining protected cohesion can now be removed, permitting sisters to disjoin.

The model makes directional predictions. A mutant that eliminates centromeric protection may cause premature sister separation during or after meiosis I, even when homolog movement begins normally. A manipulation that extends protection to the chromosome arms may prevent homologs from separating correctly because the chiasma-associated connections persist. If centromeric protection persists abnormally into anaphase II, a normal spindle may pull against unbroken connections, leading to bridges or failure to partition sister chromatids. Finally, a loss of separase activity prevents normal cleavage of susceptible cohesin in either division. These distinct perturbations distinguish location of protection from timing of protease activation.

These predictions also explain why an apparently intact metaphase-II spindle cannot compensate for earlier cohesion loss. Two chromatids that are no longer connected cannot generate normal sister-pair tension even if their kinetochores are individually capable of attachment. Some may reach opposite poles by chance, but reliable segregation has been compromised. Conversely, a pair held together by excessively persistent cohesin may orient correctly yet fail to disjoin when the cell receives permission to enter anaphase. Physical linkage, spindle geometry and biochemical permission are three necessary but distinct aspects of fidelity.

8. Evidence for local protection: reasoning from mutants

The idea of a protected centromeric pool of cohesin arose from a real experimental problem: why does separase action during meiosis I not destroy all sister association? One approach was to use yeast, in which many meiotic cells can be observed, gene function can be changed and chromosomes or proteins can be labeled. The experiments establishing shugoshin function used the fact that a defect in chromosome behavior can reveal a required component of the segregation machinery.

Kitajima, Kawashima and Watanabe reported in 2004 that the fission yeast meiotic protein Sgo1 was required to maintain centromeric cohesion through the first division. In cells lacking the appropriate protection, cohesion did not persist normally to the second division. The key insight is the difference between a general cohesin assembly defect and a stage-specific failure of protection. If cohesion were never established, sister association would be absent before the first division. If association were established but then lost when anaphase I begins, the defect would instead implicate a process needed to retain cohesion during the first wave of cleavage. These possibilities require observations at more than one stage.

Consider a teaching reconstruction of how one could evaluate this distinction. Label a specific chromosome near its centromere, use a fluorescent marker for a cohesin component and compare normal cells with cells lacking a candidate protective protein. Score cells before anaphase I, after homolog segregation and before sister disjunction. A normal control should show a cohesin signal near the centromeres after arm signal has declined. A protection-deficient cell could show normal pre-anaphase association but a reduction of centromeric signal after the first division. A rescue control restoring the functional candidate gene would test whether the defect depends on that gene rather than on an unrelated genetic change. This is a conceptual experimental design, not a claim that the original paper used precisely this simplified reporter or obtained the numerical values below.

Subsequent work linked shugoshin with PP2A and the protection of cohesin. Association between the two proteins is not, on its own, sufficient evidence for causation. A stronger test interferes with the phosphatase or its recruitment while observing whether shugoshin localization and sister cohesion are retained. If the protector reaches the centromere but fails to maintain cohesion when phosphatase function is disrupted, the enzyme is implicated as a functional effector. The conclusion remains specific to the system studied; a phosphatase can have many substrates and cellular roles, so controls for indirect effects on the cell cycle matter.

For a hypothetical dataset, suppose 92 of 100 wild-type cells retain a paired centromere reporter after meiosis I, while 18 of 100 cells deficient in the protective factor retain it. In a rescue strain, 87 of 100 retain it. The fractions are 0.92, 0.18 and 0.87; the loss in the deficient strain relative to wild type is 74 percentage points, not 74 percent of the initial value. The relative reduction is (92 − 18)/92 = 0.804, or about 80.4%. The difference supports a strong association with the gene perturbation, and the rescue supports specificity. It does not establish that every reporter focus measures one cohesin ring, nor prove direct phosphorylation of Rec8. For the latter, biochemical or additional targeted experiments are required. The counts are illustrative, not published observations.

The evidence is particularly valuable for analytical questions because it allows mechanistic claims to be separated. An observed protein at centromeres does not prove that it protects cohesion, and a failure of segregation does not identify the protein responsible. Combining time-resolved observations, loss of function, rescue and biochemical evidence is what narrows a causal model. The principal references at the end of this chapter provide the original and review literature for the shugoshin–PP2A framework.

9. Meiotic checkpoints and the metaphase-to-anaphase decision

A spindle can take time to establish the appropriate connections. A cell that starts anaphase while a chromosome is still unconnected risks losing that chromosome. The spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) delays the metaphase-to-anaphase transition when kinetochore attachment is not adequate. This is a regulatory response, not a physical barrier holding chromosomes at the metaphase plate. It uses signals generated at kinetochores to restrain the activation of the chromosome separation machinery.

In the well-characterized checkpoint pathway, proteins including Mad and Bub family members participate in assembling an inhibitory complex with Cdc20. Cdc20 is an activator of the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), an E3 ubiquitin ligase. An E3 ligase helps attach ubiquitin to selected proteins, often marking them for degradation by the proteasome. While the checkpoint inhibitory complex restrains APC/C–Cdc20, the substrates that must be removed for anaphase remain relatively stable. Securin, an inhibitor of separase, is an important substrate. Mitotic or meiotic cyclins are also regulated by APC/C.

When the chromosome attachment state allows checkpoint signaling to diminish, APC/C–Cdc20 can promote securin destruction and appropriate cyclin turnover. Loss of securin releases separase from inhibition; changes in cyclin–CDK activity contribute to separase regulation and to exit from the division state. Active separase then cleaves susceptible cohesin. This sequence explains why a biochemical transition can occur rapidly after a period of apparently stable metaphase alignment. A molecular threshold is crossed, and irreversible degradation and cleavage make it difficult for the cell to reverse anaphase.

In meiosis I this permission triggers removal of cleavage-susceptible arm cohesion while centromeric cohesion is protected. In meiosis II it triggers release of the remaining centromeric connection. The checkpoint does not directly choose whether arms or centromeres separate; that choice depends on the state of the cohesion protection machinery and the geometry of the chromosomes. Similarly, the spindle assembly checkpoint is distinct from earlier meiotic surveillance systems that can delay prophase progression in response to incomplete synapsis or unrepaired recombination intermediates. These checkpoints address different sources of failure at different stages.

Consider three hypothetical cell populations. The first has defective kinetochore capture, so one chromosome retains an unattached kinetochore. If the SAC is intact, progression is expected to be delayed. The second lacks a functional SAC signal but has the same capture defect; it may enter anaphase despite the unattached chromosome, increasing missegregation. The third has normal attachment but nondegradable securin. It can satisfy attachment surveillance but still fail to activate separase appropriately, producing a different cause of metaphase-to-anaphase failure. The distinction is important: similar visible arrest phenotypes do not identify the same molecular lesion.

The SAC has limitations. Some geometrically incorrect attachments, notably merotely, may not maintain a strong unattached-kinetochore signal. Meiotic cells can differ in checkpoint stringency from mitotic cells. In mammalian oocytes, for example, a small number of misaligned chromosomes may not always delay anaphase as effectively as the textbook ideal suggests. Checkpoint satisfaction should therefore be interpreted as the disappearance of sufficient inhibitory signals, not a mathematical proof that all chromosomes are correctly connected.

A further regulatory arrest, distinct from an attachment error, occurs in many vertebrate eggs. In mammalian oocytes, meiosis commonly pauses at metaphase II until fertilization-associated signaling. Cytostatic factor activity, including stabilization of the APC/C inhibitor Emi2 in well-studied vertebrate systems, helps maintain the high division-promoting state. Fertilization-induced calcium signaling promotes release from this arrest, allowing APC/C activity and sister separation. This physiological pause is not a new S phase and does not imply defective kinetochores. It ensures that completion of the second division is coordinated with the reproductive event; detailed fertilization signaling belongs to developmental biology.

10. Anaphase II: changing a paired chromosome into two daughters

When separase removes the remaining cohesive connections, the two sister chromatids cease to behave as one chromosome. Each now has its own independent centromere–kinetochore unit and is counted as a daughter chromosome. Because the spindle was previously organized so that the kinetochores faced different poles, release of the physical connection allows the two daughters to travel in opposite directions. Separation is therefore not caused simply by the cell cutting chromosomes in half: the DNA molecules were already distinct, and the proteinaceous association between them is what is removed.

Anaphase movement combines more than one process. In anaphase A, chromosomes move toward spindle poles as the effective length of kinetochore microtubules decreases. Microtubule dynamics and kinetochore-associated coupling mechanisms allow shortening to be linked to chromosome displacement. In anaphase B, the distance between spindle poles increases through reorganization and sliding of spindle microtubules and, depending on cell type, forces acting on the poles. The relative contributions vary; either or both can help move the chromosome groups apart. These names refer to mechanical components of segregation, not to separate rounds of anaphase.

A useful distinction is between chromosome separation and poleward transport. If cohesion release is blocked, the chromosome can remain connected despite normal spindle forces. If microtubule function is disrupted after cohesion release, the chromatids may be physically independent but not travel accurately to their destinations. If a merotelic kinetochore remains connected toward two poles, one chromosome may lag between the two segregating sets. A lagging chromosome may eventually join one group, be excluded in a micronucleus, or be lost; a lag does not always produce the same final count pattern as classical nondisjunction.

The anaphase-II interval also produces a common counting trap. Before separation, a secondary meiocyte of a species with n = 5 contains five chromosomes, each having two chromatids. During anaphase II, after disjunction but before completion of cytokinesis, the same cell contains ten daughter chromosomes and ten DNA molecules. The amount of DNA has not increased; what changed was the number of independently countable segregation units. At each pole there are five chromosomes and 1C. If an examination asks for the chromosome number in each daughter cell after division, the correct answer is five, not ten. If it asks for the whole anaphase cell, ten is correct under the usual chromosome-counting convention.

Anaphase I differs at the same level of analysis. Each moving homolog still has two chromatids held near the centromere. Therefore the total number of chromosomes in the entire undivided meiosis-I cell remains 2n during homolog disjunction, rather than doubling to 4n. Per pole, the number is n with two DNA molecules per chromosome. A figure that shows X-shaped units moving apart at the first anaphase and single-chromatid units moving apart at the second is accurately communicating the distinction. In actual microscopy chromosome shapes are not always neat X and I symbols, so the behavior of the centromeres is a more dependable conceptual criterion.

11. Telophase II, cytokinesis and the meaning of four products

After the chromosomes have reached the appropriate pole regions, the spindle is disassembled or reorganized and the nuclear state is restored. In open meiosis, nuclear envelopes assemble around the separated chromosome groups. Chromosomes generally become less compact as the nuclei transition away from the division state. Declining division-promoting kinase activity permits the reversal of many phosphorylations associated with the M phase. This prepares the nuclear products for their subsequent cellular or developmental functions rather than for another obligatory division.

Cytokinesis is the division of cytoplasm. It is conceptually distinct from karyokinesis, the division of the nuclear chromosome complement. In many animal cells, actin and myosin form a contractile structure in the cell cortex that narrows the plasma membrane at a cleavage furrow. The position of the furrow is coordinated with the spindle so that the chromosome groups end up in separate cells. In plant cells, which have a rigid wall, new wall material is assembled in a developing cell plate, with microtubule-rich structures such as the phragmoplast helping guide vesicles to the appropriate region. The plate extends toward the parent wall to separate the daughter compartments.

Cytokinesis timing in meiosis is variable. In successive cytokinesis the cytoplasm divides after meiosis I and again after meiosis II. In simultaneous cytokinesis, particularly familiar in many plant meiocytes, the two nuclear divisions can be completed before the final partition into four products. In that situation a whole-cell DNA measurement after meiosis I might still include both 2C nuclei within one shared cytoplasm, giving 4C in total. A measurement per nucleus, however, still reads 2C. The classic table of meiotic DNA content assumes that the sampled unit is the specified nucleus or that cellular partition has occurred. It must not be applied blindly to any cell with multiple nuclei.

Furthermore, four nuclear products do not always correspond to four functional gametes. In animal spermatogenesis, the products of meiosis normally differentiate into spermatids and subsequently sperm cells. In mammalian oogenesis, unequal cytoplasmic divisions retain most cytoplasm in one developing egg while small polar bodies receive chromosome complements. Depending on whether the first polar body also divides, a literal count of visible final cell bodies may differ from four. In land plants, meiotic products are typically haploid spores, not directly gametes; gametes form later in the haploid generation by mitotic processes. These examples show why the phrase meiotic product is more general and more accurate than automatically calling every product a gamete.

If cytokinesis fails while chromosomes have separated correctly, a cell may contain two or more nuclei with normal haploid chromosome sets. That is not identical to a failure of chromosome segregation. Conversely, a cell may form four compartments but distribute the chromosomes incorrectly. Counting nuclei, measuring DNA per nucleus and identifying chromosome copies are complementary ways of examining meiotic outcomes. A simple question about the number of final cells must specify whether nuclear division, cytokinesis and later survival are all assumed to be normal.

12. Independent assortment and crossing over: different sources of variation

Meiosis contributes to genetic diversity through two distinct chromosome-level processes. Independent assortment arises because each homologous pair can generally orient in either of two directions at metaphase I, independently of the orientation of other pairs. If the maternal homolog of chromosome A moves left, the maternal homolog of chromosome B need not also move left. The relative orientations of the bivalents establish which centromere lineages are collected together in each meiosis-I product.

Crossing over instead changes the combination of DNA segments along an individual chromatid. Consider a chromosome bearing alleles A and B on one homolog and a and b on the other. A single reciprocal crossover between these loci involving two nonsister chromatids can produce Ab and aB chromatids, while the two uninvolved chromatids retain AB and ab. Meiosis II is where the sisters carrying these distinct segments are separated into different nuclear products. The exchange itself happened earlier in prophase I, not while the chromatids were moving at anaphase II. This is the reason sisters are not guaranteed to be genetically identical in a meiotic dyad even though they arose from replication.

If crossing over is ignored and every bivalent has two equally likely independent orientations, the possible combinations of homolog centromere origins for an individual product are 2 × 2 × ... repeated n times, giving 2^n. For a species with n = 4, there are 16 possible combinations across many meioses. This is a count of potential distinct origin patterns, not the number of cells produced by one meiotic event. One ordinary meiosis generates four products; without crossing over and with faithful sister separation, the two products descended from each first-division nucleus share the same origin pattern, giving two complementary patterns among those four cells.

For humans with n = 23, the idealized number is 2^23 = 8,388,608 possible combinations of parental homolog origins, before considering crossing over. To calculate the probability of a particular origin combination for a randomly chosen product under the stated assumptions, multiply n factors of 1/2, obtaining (1/2)^n. With n = 4, the probability is 1/16, or 6.25%. This does not mean all gametes are genetically unique or that independent assortment can distinguish homologs that carry identical sequences. It also ignores nonrandom segregation, differences in gamete viability and changes introduced by recombination. It describes the simplest independent orientation model.

Why not use 2^n for alleles at every gene? Genes on the same chromosome may be physically linked. Their alleles travel with the same chromatid unless a recombination event changes their association. Counting chromosome pairs is therefore not the same as counting independently segregating gene loci. A crossover between two loci may separate their alleles; a crossover elsewhere on that chromosome may not. Detailed linkage and recombination frequency analysis is studied later in inheritance biology. Here the essential reasoning is that the first division determines homolog assortment, while the second separates the chromatids that carry the resulting segment combinations.

13. Nondisjunction in meiosis I and meiosis II

Nondisjunction means that chromosome elements that should separate at a division fail to go to opposite daughter groups. To analyze its effect, trace one homologous pair in isolation while assuming that every other chromosome segregates normally. At the end, add the unaffected chromosomes to the count. This method is more reliable than remembering a pattern of numbers without understanding its origin.

In meiosis-I nondisjunction, the two replicated homologs go to the same pole rather than different poles. The other pole receives neither homolog of the affected chromosome type. For a species with haploid number n, the two first-division products therefore contain n + 1 and n − 1 chromosomes, respectively, still duplicated. When meiosis II proceeds normally, the first of those products passes one sister from each homolog to both of its daughter nuclei. Each of these final products carries two copies of the affected chromosome type, giving n + 1. The other first-division product passes no copy to either daughter; each becomes n − 1. Thus a single classical error in meiosis I produces two n + 1 and two n − 1 products, with no normal-count product among the four.

In meiosis-II nondisjunction, the first division correctly distributes the homologs, so both secondary meiocytes have n duplicated chromosomes. Now suppose that sisters of one chromosome fail to separate correctly in just one secondary meiocyte and both go to the same final nucleus. The affected division yields one n + 1 and one n − 1 product. The other secondary meiocyte divides normally, producing two n products. Therefore the expected pattern is n + 1, n − 1, n, n. The presence of two normal-count products is a clue to a single error affecting only one second division under this idealized model.

The reason the first error affects all four products is that it occurs upstream of the branching of both secondary meiocytes. The second error is downstream of that first branching and can therefore be confined to one lineage. This logic extends to less familiar cases. If the same chromosome type undergoes a separate nondisjunction event in both secondary meiocytes, all four products may again be abnormal, even though the first division was normal. Thus the four-product pattern diagnoses a stage only when the assumptions of a single classical event, normal segregation of other chromosomes and recovery of every product hold. Loss of a lagging chromosome may yield an n − 1 product without a compensating n + 1 product.

An extra chromosome gives an aneuploid product, meaning one whose chromosome number differs from the complete set by one or more individual chromosomes. Aneuploidy is different from polyploidy, which is a change in the number of complete chromosome sets. An n + 1 product is not diploid in a typical organism with many chromosomes. It has a haploid background with an extra copy of one chromosome type. If it participates in fertilization with a normal n gamete, the resulting zygote has 2n + 1, a trisomic complement for the affected chromosome. An n − 1 gamete produces a 2n − 1 monosomic zygote under the same assumptions. These arithmetic outcomes describe chromosome complements; they do not predict viability or clinical phenotype for every chromosome or species.

A cell-level example is trisomy 21 in humans, which can arise when a gamete carries an extra chromosome 21 and combines with a normal gamete. The resulting zygote may have 47 chromosomes rather than the usual 46. Other mechanisms, including chromosome rearrangements or postzygotic missegregation, can also produce abnormal chromosome-21 dosage. The chromosome count alone cannot establish the exact meiotic division responsible. One needs information about parental chromosome origin, informative genetic markers or the cellular history of the event. This is why mechanistic and diagnostic conclusions should never be made solely from the total count.

14. Using chromosome markers to infer the division of error

Chromosome count reveals dosage but not necessarily the identities of the copies. To infer how an extra chromosome arose, imagine a polymorphic marker near the centromere of a homologous pair, with allele M on one homolog and allele P on the other. Assume that no crossover occurs between the centromere and the marker and that the marker distinguishes the homologs. If both homologs fail to segregate at meiosis I and the second division is normal, an extra-copy product receives one chromatid descended from the M homolog and one from the P homolog. It will contain both marker alleles. This corresponds to a heterodisomic pattern at the informative marker: the two copies derive from different homologs.

If meiosis I is normal and sisters of the M homolog nondisjoin at meiosis II, the extra-copy product receives two chromatids descended from the same centromere lineage. The marker pattern is M and M, an isodisomic pattern at that marker. The corresponding missing-copy product has neither allele for the affected chromosome. The other meiosis-I lineage normally supplies one P copy to each of its two products. These are predicted patterns for a particular chromosome in one meiotic parent, not a claim that all chromosome pairs in an aneuploid organism have identical or different genetic origins.

The no-crossover assumption matters. A crossover between the centromere and a distal marker can give sister chromatids different alleles at the distal site. A meiosis-II error could then produce two different distal alleles despite retaining the same centromere lineage. Conversely, distal markers may show matching alleles in a meiosis-I error if exchanges alter the segment combinations. Centromere-proximal markers are therefore more informative for the classical distinction, though real interpretation requires informative markers and consideration of recombination and other complex mechanisms.

In a hypothetical tetrad analysis, a four-product set contains chromosome counts n + 1, n + 1, n − 1 and n − 1. Both extra-copy products show M and P at a marker close to the centromere. Under the single-error model this is consistent with homolog nondisjunction in meiosis I: two different centromere lineages traveled together. In another tetrad, counts are n + 1, n − 1, n and n. The extra-copy product shows P and P. This fits a sister disjunction error during meiosis II in the P-bearing secondary meiocyte. If the marker were uninformative, for example both homologs carried allele M, the genotype could not distinguish the two mechanisms. The counts would still provide evidence under the restricted single-error model.

There is an additional limitation. Premature sister chromatid separation in meiosis I can produce outcomes that do not fit the simple homolog-nondisjunction scheme. Some sisters may segregate independently at the first division and alter the starting chromosome configuration for the second. Abnormal cohesion, multipolar spindles, chromosome loss and other events can therefore create more complicated patterns. The purpose of the elementary models is to develop the capacity to trace units through divisions, not to pretend that every real aneuploid product has a uniquely determined history from one observation.

15. Quantitative analysis across both divisions

A useful way to solve DNA-content problems is to start with the conservation of nuclear DNA. After premeiotic replication, a precursor contains 4C. If there is no further replication and no nuclear DNA is lost, the sum over all four final nuclei must still be 4C. In normal division each receives an equal haploid genome, so the mean and individual amount are 4C/4 = 1C. The equal-amount conclusion depends on correct segregation; conservation alone guarantees only the sum. Different chromosomes can contain different amounts of DNA, so aneuploid products do not all differ from 1C by a common amount.

Example 1: A species has 2n = 18 and an unreplicated haploid nuclear genome of 3 pg. Thus n = 9 and 1C = 3 pg. The premeiotic cell after S contains 18 chromosomes, 36 DNA molecules and 12 pg (4 × 3 pg). Each secondary meiocyte contains nine duplicated chromosomes, 18 DNA molecules and 6 pg. At anaphase II before cytokinesis, each secondary cell contains 18 daughter chromosomes and still 6 pg. At each pole, nine daughter chromosomes carry 3 pg. The four final nuclei collectively contain 36 DNA molecules and 12 pg, matching the post-S precursor. Ploidy is not inferred from the mass of DNA alone.

Example 2: A cell at metaphase II is reported to contain 14 DNA molecules and 7 pg DNA. Assuming an ordinary diploid precursor and no abnormalities, each chromosome consists of two chromatids, so n = 14/2 = 7 and 2n = 14. Its DNA content at this stage is 2C, so C = 7/2 = 3.5 pg. The original post-S precursor contained 28 DNA molecules and 4C = 14 pg. Each final product has seven DNA molecules and 3.5 pg. Notice that the number 14 describes different quantities in different contexts: chromosomal DNA molecules in a secondary meiocyte and chromosome number in the original diploid precursor.

Example 3: In a species with n = 6, the normal haploid nuclear genome weighs 2.4 pg, and one particular unreplicated chromosome accounts for 0.35 pg of this mass. A classical meiosis-I nondisjunction of this chromosome produces two n + 1 and two n − 1 nuclei. The extra-copy nuclei each contain 7 chromosomes and 2.4 + 0.35 = 2.75 pg. The missing-copy nuclei each contain 5 chromosomes and 2.4 − 0.35 = 2.05 pg. Their total DNA content is 2 × 2.75 + 2 × 2.05 = 9.60 pg, equal to 4 × 2.4 pg. We used the actual mass of the affected chromosome, not the average 2.4/6 = 0.4 pg, because chromosome sizes are unequal.

Example 4: Suppose 200 independent meiotic precursors are observed and each completes four recoverable products. Among them, 12 undergo one classical meiosis-I nondisjunction and 20 undergo one classical meiosis-II nondisjunction in one secondary cell. Assume these groups do not overlap and all other meioses are normal. There are 800 products. The first error group contributes 12 × 4 = 48 abnormal products, and the second contributes 20 × 2 = 40. Total abnormal products = 88, a fraction 88/800 = 0.11, or 11%. There are 44 extra-copy and 44 missing-copy products under the symmetric model. The fraction of meioses affected is (12 + 20)/200 = 16%, which differs from the fraction of abnormal products. The distinction between event rate and product rate is necessary whenever one error can affect multiple descendants.

Example 5: In an asynchronous population, 50 measured nuclei are at 4C, 120 at 2C and 230 at 1C. A total of 400 nuclei are measured, giving fractions 12.5%, 30% and 57.5%. The 2C group cannot be labeled as metaphase II exclusively without further data: a premeiotic G1 diploid nucleus also contains 2C, as does a nucleus after meiosis I. Chromosome counts or stage-specific markers can distinguish these populations. The fraction of cells in a phase cannot automatically be converted to the fraction of meiotic time spent there because nuclear divisions multiply the number of sampled nuclei and different cell types may be represented. The example is hypothetical and illustrates an inference limit rather than a measured distribution.

A systematic solution always specifies the observation unit (whole cell, nucleus, pole or final product), then separates chromosome count from DNA molecule count and from DNA mass. For an error analysis, draw the affected homolog pair first. Mark every chromatid individually if crossing over matters, and only then add the unaffected chromosome types. Test the result against conservation when the scenario excludes chromosome loss. This method is more general than memorizing a collection of stage-specific numbers.

Example 6: A 2n = 10 meiocyte has one bivalent with a single crossover between two heterozygous loci and the remaining bivalents show no exchange. A microscope reveals five independently aligned dyads in each of two secondary nuclei. If both second divisions proceed normally, the four products each contain five chromosomes. At the exchanged loci, two products carry parental allele associations and two carry recombinant associations, so the recombinant fraction in this particular tetrad is 2/4 = 50%. This is not a population recombination-frequency estimate, since the example conditions on a crossover in every observed tetrad. Without that condition, many tetrads may contain only parental associations. The count of chromosome sets remains unchanged regardless of the exchange.

16. Integrating mitosis, meiosis I and meiosis II

Mitosis and meiosis II use a related segregation principle: sister kinetochores attach to opposite poles and sister cohesion is removed so that one copy of each replicated chromosome reaches each daughter nucleus. In a normal diploid mitosis, the parent after replication has 2n chromosomes and 4C DNA, and each daughter has 2n and 2C. In meiosis II, the starting nucleus is normally haploid with n duplicated chromosomes and 2C DNA, and each product has n and 1C. Both are equational divisions in terms of ploidy, but their starting chromosome complements differ. A haploid cell can also undergo mitosis, which is why haploidy does not define meiosis II.

Meiosis I is the distinctive reductional division. It relies on homolog associations, crossover-supported connections in typical chiasmate meiosis and co-orientation of sister kinetochores. At anaphase I, the homologs disjoin; the chromatids of each homolog remain associated near the centromere. Its normal starting complement is 2n, 4C and the product is n, 2C. The division is reductional even though the movement of DNA divides the total DNA amount by two, just as in the other nuclear divisions. Reduction of ploidy and halving of DNA mass are not interchangeable statements.

The usual rules describe chiasmate meiosis in diploid eukaryotes. Some organisms use achiasmate mechanisms for particular sexes or chromosome pairs. For example, male Drosophila meiosis normally occurs without crossing over, but homolog association and segregation are still possible through alternative mechanisms. The absence of crossovers in this system does not imply absence of meiosis I or absence of homolog segregation. Certain species also have unusual orders of chromosome segregation, such as inverted meiosis in some holocentric chromosomes. These are exceptions to the standard centromere-focused model, not the default assumptions for ordinary textbook calculations. Detailed adaptations are outside the present scope.

Age-dependent loss of cohesion in mammalian oocytes provides a biological reason to distinguish the mechanical and temporal components of regulation. In many mammals, oocytes establish meiotic cohesion during fetal development and may remain arrested for extended periods before completing division. Deterioration of cohesion can weaken chiasma-associated connections and disrupt sister arrangements. Spindle organization and checkpoint regulation also contribute to the risk of chromosome errors. This is a multifactorial phenomenon; it would be misleading to attribute every age-associated aneuploidy to one protein or one division. The example connects molecular integrity with the reliability of chromosome transmission over time.

A final method for interpreting images is to locate the segregation units, determine their relationship, and inspect what is connected. Paired homologs arranged as bivalents identify meiosis I in the standard case. Independently aligned dyads with opposing sister kinetochores fit mitosis or meiosis II. Daughter chromosome groups without sister connections fit anaphase of an equational division, but a single image may not distinguish which. Counts relative to known ploidy, staging of neighboring cells or chromosome-specific labels supply the missing information. A convincing interpretation uses more than one visual cue.

Meiosis illustrates how successful reproduction depends on a controlled order of cellular processes: one replication, pairing and homolog separation, preservation and release of sister cohesion, then final partition. Cells that fail such processes may arrest, repair damage, missegregate chromosomes or be eliminated. The next chapter concerns regulated cell elimination, apoptosis, a different cellular process whose purpose and molecular mechanisms should not be confused with the completion of meiosis.

17. Part B — Conceptual practice

1. Which feature makes meiosis II equational?

A. Homolog pairing begins again
B. Ploidy per daughter nucleus is maintained
C. Nuclear DNA replicates
D. Each daughter retains both sister chromatids

Answer and reasoning

B. A haploid nucleus gives two haploid nuclei. A describes prophase I; C is excluded between divisions; D reverses the outcome of sister separation.

2. A replicated chromosome in metaphase II contains how many double-stranded nuclear DNA molecules?

A. One
B. Two
C. Four
D. It depends on ploidy

Answer and reasoning

B. Each of the two chromatids contains one DNA double helix. A applies after disjunction; C counts individual strands rather than DNA molecules; D confuses chromosome structure with chromosome-set number.

3. Which pair should be bi-oriented at normal metaphase II?

A. Homologous chromosomes
B. Sister kinetochores
C. Two chromosome arms of the same chromatid
D. Two unrelated chromosomes

Answer and reasoning

B. Opposite-pole sister attachments direct disjunction. A is characteristic of meiosis I; C and D are not the paired segregation units.

4. Shugoshin-associated PP2A primarily protects which structure during the first division?

A. All chromosome arms equally
B. Nuclear envelope
C. Centromeric meiotic cohesion
D. DNA replication origins

Answer and reasoning

C. Local protection maintains cohesion while arm linkage is removed. A would inhibit normal homolog separation; B and D do not describe the relevant substrate.

5. Which protein directly cleaves the susceptible meiotic kleisin Rec8?

A. Separase
B. Securin
C. Shugoshin
D. Cdc20

Answer and reasoning

A. Separase is the protease; securin inhibits it, shugoshin protects selected cohesion, and Cdc20 activates APC/C rather than cleaving Rec8.

6. Assertion: Normal meiosis II follows meiosis I without a new S phase. Reason: The chromosomes inherited from meiosis I still contain two sister chromatids.

A. Both true, reason explains assertion
B. Both true, reason unrelated
C. Assertion true, reason false
D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and reasoning

A. The already replicated chromosome supply allows the second segregation without copying the genome again. B ignores that rationale; C and D contradict the normal sequence.

7. A cell at metaphase II contains n chromosomes and 2C DNA. After sister disjunction, before cytokinesis, what is the DNA content of the whole cell?

A. 1C
B. 2C
C. 4C
D. Zero

Answer and reasoning

B. DNA is redistributed, not created or destroyed. A is per pole, C would require additional DNA, and D is incompatible with intact chromosome groups.

8. Which statement about crossover is correct?

A. It normally occurs during anaphase II
B. It can make sister chromatids different at a distal marker
C. It requires all four chromatids to exchange in every event
D. It is identical to independent assortment

Answer and reasoning

B. A nonsister exchange in prophase I may alter one sister. A assigns the wrong stage, C is false for a single reciprocal exchange, and D confuses segment exchange with orientation.

9. Which event is most directly responsible for sister disjunction after checkpoint satisfaction?

A. Securin degradation followed by separase activation
B. New DNA synthesis
C. Synaptonemal complex formation
D. Permanent activation of centromeric protection

Answer and reasoning

A. The regulatory cascade permits susceptible cohesion cleavage. B and C belong to earlier stages; D would oppose separation.

10. Which statement is accurate for flowering plants?

A. All meiotic products are directly gametes
B. Meiosis generally yields spores
C. Meiosis II always requires centrioles
D. Cell plates never form in meiocytes

Answer and reasoning

B. The haploid generation forms gametes later. A confuses the generations; C incorrectly universalizes centrosome requirements; D excludes normal plant cytokinesis.

18. Part C — Experimental and quantitative reasoning

1. An animal species has 2n = 20. Count chromosomes and DNA molecules in one secondary meiocyte at metaphase II and the entire anaphase-II cell before cytokinesis.

A. 10/20 then 20/20
B. 20/40 then 40/40
C. 10/10 then 10/10
D. 10/20 then 20/40

Answer and reasoning

A. n = 10. At metaphase II ten replicated chromosomes contain twenty DNA molecules. At anaphase II each chromatid is a daughter chromosome, so there are twenty chromosomes and the same twenty molecules. B uses the original diploid complement; C misses replication; D invents a second S phase.

2. The haploid genome is 5 pg. A secondary meiocyte has a normal chromosome complement. What are the DNA masses at metaphase II, per anaphase-II pole, and all four final nuclei combined?

A. 5, 5, 10 pg
B. 10, 5, 20 pg
C. 20, 10, 40 pg
D. 10, 10, 40 pg

Answer and reasoning

B. Metaphase II = 2C = 10 pg; each pole = 1C = 5 pg; four nuclei = 20 pg. A confuses pre- and post-disjunction, C assumes a diploid secondary cell, D fails to partition the DNA.

3. A mutant permits normal Rec8 loading before meiosis I, but after anaphase I the centromeric Rec8 signal disappears prematurely. Which mechanism is most directly implicated?

A. Loss of centromeric cohesion protection
B. Failure of premeiotic DNA replication
C. Failure to degrade all securin
D. A second round of homolog synapsis

Answer and reasoning

A. Initial cohesion exists, but it is not preserved through the first wave of cleavage. B would change initial replication/loading; C would inhibit separase rather than favor loss; D does not account for stage-specific Rec8 disappearance.

4. A phosphatase is artificially recruited along chromosome arms during meiosis I and makes arm Rec8 resistant to cleavage. What is the immediate predicted consequence?

A. Homolog disjunction fails or is impaired
B. Homolog disjunction is accelerated
C. Normal centromeric cleavage in I
D. DNA replication resumes

Answer and reasoning

A. Arm cohesion maintains chiasma-associated linkage. Retention resists homolog separation even if the spindle pulls. B reverses the effect, C is not implied, and D concerns licensing rather than cohesion.

5. A cell has one unattached kinetochore and a defective SAC. Which combination is most plausible?

A. APC/C remains necessarily inhibited indefinitely
B. Premature anaphase and elevated missegregation risk
C. Guaranteed correction of attachment
D. New crossover between sisters

Answer and reasoning

B. Loss of checkpoint restraint permits anaphase before all attachments are ready. A assumes an intact checkpoint; C confuses error correction with checkpoint loss; D is unrelated.

6. A chromosome lies near the equator but one kinetochore attaches toward both spindle poles. Which outcome is most consistent?

A. Merotelic attachment can cause anaphase lagging
B. Normal homolog synapsis
C. Guaranteed arrest by every checkpoint
D. The chromosome already contains two homologs

Answer and reasoning

A. Opposing connections at a single kinetochore can resist poleward movement. B and D refer to homolog pairing; C is too absolute because occupied kinetochores may not sustain a strong checkpoint signal.

7. Four products from one meiosis of a species with n = 8 have chromosome numbers 9, 9, 7, 7. Under a single classical nondisjunction event, which stage is implicated?

A. Meiosis I
B. Meiosis II in one secondary cell
C. Normal meiosis
D. Normal S phase

Answer and reasoning

A. Both homologs enter one lineage and none the other, making all four products abnormal. B gives 9, 7, 8, 8; C gives four eights; D changes DNA quantity, not the final homolog distribution.

8. In a separate tetrad, counts are 9, 7, 8, 8. The chromosome present twice in the nine-chromosome product has two identical informative centromere-proximal alleles. No recombination occurred between marker and centromere. Which inference is best?

A. Classical meiosis-II nondisjunction in one secondary cell
B. Classical meiosis-I nondisjunction
C. Two independent normal meioses
D. Proof of failed cytokinesis

Answer and reasoning

A. Two sisters from one centromere lineage entered one product; the unaffected lineage generated two normal-count products. B predicts four abnormal products and distinct marker lineages; C does not explain the tetrad; D is not implied by chromosome identity.

9. A species has n = 5. A normal product has 4 pg DNA, and the chromosome involved in an error accounts for 0.8 pg. What masses are expected in the four products after classical nondisjunction in one meiosis-II cell?

A. 4.8, 3.2, 4, 4 pg
B. 4.8, 4.8, 3.2, 3.2 pg
C. 8, 0, 4, 4 pg
D. 4.8, 4, 4, 4 pg

Answer and reasoning

A. Only one second division is affected; one product gains 0.8 pg and one loses 0.8 pg, while two are normal. Total = 16 pg. B is a first-division error, C assumes the whole set missegregates, D violates conservation without extra DNA synthesis.

10. Of 60 meioses, 6 undergo single meiosis-I nondisjunction and 8 single meiosis-II nondisjunction (one secondary cell). All products survive. What fraction of products has abnormal counts?

A. 14/60
B. 40/240
C. 14/240
D. 56/240

Answer and reasoning

B. There are 240 products. Six first-division errors contribute 24 and eight second-division errors contribute 16, yielding 40/240 = 1/6, or about 16.7%. A is affected meioses; C counts events as products; D assumes four abnormalities for both types.

11. A species has six independently orienting homolog pairs. Ignore crossing over. What is the probability of one specified origin pattern among random products?

A. 1/6
B. 1/12
C. 1/64
D. 1/4

Answer and reasoning

C. Six independent binary choices give 2^6 = 64 patterns, with probability 1/64. A and B do not multiply the choices; D confuses the four products of one meiosis with the total space of origin combinations.

12. In the hypothetical graph of reporter retention, wild type has 92% paired reporters and mutant has 18%. Which conclusion is justified?

A. Relative reduction is 74% exactly
B. Difference is 74 percentage points and the result supports a protection defect
C. Exactly 74 cohesin complexes were lost per cell
D. The data alone prove direct Rec8 dephosphorylation

Answer and reasoning

B. The subtraction gives 74 points; the relative reduction is 74/92 ≈ 80.4%. A confuses relative and absolute change; C assigns molecular stoichiometry to a cell fraction; D overstates the causal specificity.

13. A diploid meiocyte completes both nuclear divisions without cytokinesis. Four separate nuclei remain in a single cytoplasm. Which reading is expected if nuclear DNA is measured per nucleus and in the entire cell?

A. 1C per nucleus, 4C in whole cell
B. 4C per nucleus, 16C whole
C. 2C per nucleus, 8C whole
D. 1C whole cell

Answer and reasoning

A. Four normal haploid nuclei each contain 1C. Whole-cell nuclear DNA is their sum. B and C invent extra DNA; D mistakes the sampled unit.

14. Two cells contain six X-shaped chromosomes at an equatorial plate with opposing sister attachments. One is a haploid mitotic cell; the other is a normal secondary meiocyte with n = 6. Which observation alone distinguishes them?

A. X shape
B. Six replicated chromosomes
C. Opposed sister attachments
D. None of these; lineage or stage-specific context is required

Answer and reasoning

D. All three features are shared by the two stated cells. A, B and C are not diagnostic in this comparison.
