1. Why a lipid boundary needs proteins

Imagine two artificial vesicles containing the same lipids and enclosing the same volume of water. Both prevent most hydrated ions from crossing their hydrophobic interior. Now introduce a glucose carrier, a potassium channel and a receptor into only one vesicle. Its boundary acquires the ability to exchange selected solutes and recognize a chemical signal without losing its basic bilayer organization. A lipid bilayer establishes separation, but membrane proteins make that separation useful to the cell. They allow two compartments to maintain different compositions while remaining connected in precisely controlled ways.

The molecular difficulty is that proteins are normally studied in water, whereas the centre of a lipid bilayer is an oil-like environment. A protein that bridges these surroundings must satisfy two different chemical environments simultaneously. Its lipid-facing region needs to remain compatible with fatty acyl chains, while its exposed parts must interact with water, ions, other proteins or carbohydrates. A transporter has an additional challenge: the substance it transports may itself be hydrophilic, so the transporter must provide a suitable environment inside a hydrophobic barrier. It solves this problem by the spatial arrangement of ordinary amino acid side chains and the folding of the polar peptide backbone, not by using a special kind of peptide bond.

Recall three prerequisites. A polypeptide has an amino (N) terminus and a carboxyl (C) terminus; hydrogen bonds stabilize many arrangements of the polar backbone; and hydrophobic side chains tend to avoid exposure to water. The bilayer consists of two leaflets with aqueous interfaces separated by a hydrocarbon core. Its cytosolic and noncytosolic faces are chemically and functionally distinct. Membrane topology describes the arrangement of a protein's segments with respect to these two faces. This differs from its complete three-dimensional fold, which includes the detailed geometry of the protein, and from its functional classification. Two proteins with the same number of crossings can have opposite orientations or quite different activities.

We shall establish the types of attachment, explain the structural solutions for crossing a bilayer, and then determine how a cell assigns a protein to particular sides. The final sections develop the methods that allow these ideas to be tested quantitatively. Throughout, distinguish an observation from an interpretation: a hydrophobic sequence, a protected peptide fragment or a recovering fluorescence signal each reveals a different property.

2. The evidence that proteins are embedded in a dynamic membrane

Early membrane models treated proteins as continuous layers coating a lipid film. This was a plausible proposal when the bilayer was considered primarily an insulating sheet. It became difficult to reconcile with the observation that some proteins remained membrane-associated after surface interactions were disrupted, whereas others could be released without dissolving the bilayer. If proteins formed a single uniform coating, such different modes of association would be difficult to explain.

Freeze-fracture experiments revealed internal fracture faces containing particles. A frozen bilayer tends to split along its internal plane, and intramembrane particles are consistent with embedded proteins or protein complexes. The observation did not establish the number of peptide crossings or the identity of a particular particle. A complex may contain several chains, and fracture may preferentially retain components in one leaflet. Nevertheless, the findings supported the idea that proteins penetrate the lipid structure rather than existing exclusively on its surfaces.

Frye and Edidin fused mouse and human cells and followed species-specific surface antigens using fluorescent antibodies in 1970. The initially separated labels became intermixed. This directly supported lateral mobility of a population of surface proteins. It did not demonstrate that every protein was equally mobile or that a labelled external domain could move into the cytosol. Along with biochemical and structural observations, the experiment provided a basis for the fluid mosaic concept, later refined to include restricted diffusion, selective retention and dynamically organized protein assemblies.

Erythrocytes are a classic model for examining membrane proteins because their membrane remnants, or ghosts, contain prominent integral components and a supporting skeleton. Glycophorin and the anion exchanger band 3 remain associated with the bilayer under conditions that can release many peripheral skeletal components. Spectrin, actin and associated proteins form an internal network. Thus the membrane is neither a homogeneous lipid sheet nor a protein shell: it contains chemically distinct types of association that cooperate mechanically and functionally.

Different membranes also devote different proportions of mass to protein. Lipid-rich myelin provides electrical insulation, while energy-transducing membranes contain abundant protein machinery. A mass fraction is not a molecular fraction. For a hypothetical membrane containing 50 mg of protein of average mass 50 kDa and 50 mg lipid of average mass 750 Da, the protein amount is 0.050/50000 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol; lipid amount is 0.050/750 ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻⁵ mol. The ratio is about 67 lipid molecules per protein monomer even though the masses are equal. The average masses and monomer convention are assumptions, and neither the area occupied by proteins nor their oligomer count follows from this ratio alone.

3. Integral, peripheral and lipid-anchored proteins

The first useful question is what kind of interaction holds a protein at the membrane. Integral proteins contain regions that enter the lipid phase. Most familiar examples cross the bilayer completely, but some penetrate one leaflet and never emerge on the other side; these can be termed monotopic integral proteins. A transmembrane protein, in contrast, necessarily has at least one complete crossing. All transmembrane proteins are integral, but not all integral proteins are transmembrane.

A single-pass protein contains one full spanning segment. The segment may principally anchor a large extracellular domain, a large cytosolic domain or domains on both sides. Glycophorin A is a useful example, with an extracellular glycosylated domain and an inner cytosolic region separated by one membrane helix. A multipass protein contains several complete crossings in one polypeptide chain. The helices can organize into a bundle in which different segments contact one another or participate in forming an internal binding pocket. The number of membrane-spanning segments is not the number of subunits: a tetramer may consist of four chains, each containing several crossings.

Peripheral proteins associate noncovalently with the membrane surface. A positively charged domain may bind acidic phospholipid head groups; alternatively, a protein may recognize a particular domain of an integral protein. Many such associations are disrupted by salt, pH changes or loss of a binding partner without dissolution of the bilayer. But peripheral does not mean weakly attached in every circumstance. A protein with several simultaneous binding contacts can form a stable structural network, so resistance to a single salt wash is not proof that the protein is integral.

Lipid-anchored proteins are covalently linked to a lipid or glycolipid which enters one leaflet while the protein domain remains on one side. These proteins may behave like integral components in an extraction experiment even though their polypeptide does not traverse the bilayer. It is therefore helpful to distinguish lipid anchors as a structural category. A transmembrane protein can also contain lipid modifications, and a peripheral protein can use both a membrane-binding domain and a lipid anchor. The categories describe modes of attachment, which may coexist in one molecule.

An amphipathic helix parallel to the membrane provides another mechanism. Its hydrophobic face can penetrate the outer part of a leaflet, while its hydrophilic face stays near water or the lipid head groups. The strength and depth of insertion determine whether the association is reversible or more like a monotopic integral attachment. Mere membrane association never establishes which side a domain faces. We need to trace the chain or test accessibility to determine that property.

4. The alpha helix solves the backbone problem

An isolated peptide bond is polar. Its carbonyl oxygen and amide group can participate in hydrogen bonding with water. Moving such a backbone into the nonpolar core of a membrane without satisfying these interactions carries an energetic cost. In an alpha helix, a carbonyl group at residue i normally hydrogen-bonds with the amide group of residue i+4. Internal hydrogen bonds satisfy much of the backbone's capacity for hydrogen bonding. Meanwhile the side chains project outward, permitting leucine, isoleucine, valine, phenylalanine and other lipid-compatible groups to contact hydrocarbon chains.

This is not simply a rule that a transmembrane sequence contains only nonpolar residues. A charged or polar residue may be accommodated if it hydrogen-bonds to a neighbouring helix, participates in a binding site, lies near an interface, or faces an aqueous internal pore. Aromatic residues such as tryptophan and tyrosine are frequently found near the interface, where they can interact with both the lipid environment and water or head groups. A hydrophobic sequence buried inside a soluble globular protein can also form a helix, so hydrophobicity alone does not prove membrane insertion.

The dimensions of the alpha helix explain the usual size of a membrane span. There are approximately 3.6 residues per turn and an axial rise of about 0.15 nm per residue. A perpendicular helix spanning a 3.0 nm hydrophobic core therefore needs roughly 20 residues. A more precise centre-to-centre estimate uses L = (n − 1) × 0.15 nm, where n is the residue count. Real membrane helices are often around 20–25 residues, depending on thickness, interfacial definitions and tilt. The total bilayer thickness includes the headgroup regions and should not be substituted automatically for hydrophobic-core thickness.

If the helix tilts, the component of its length along the membrane normal is d = L cos θ. The expression comes directly from resolving the helix axis into a perpendicular projection d and a component parallel to the membrane. For a 24-residue segment, L = 23 × 0.15 = 3.45 nm. If d = 3.0 nm, cos θ = 3.0/3.45 = 0.870 and θ ≈ 29.6° measured from the normal. This is a simplified geometric estimate rather than a prediction of actual molecular tilt. The end positions and exact helix geometry vary, and lipids can adapt to the protein.

Hydrophobic mismatch describes a difference between the length of the hydrophobic protein segment and the surrounding hydrophobic layer. Exposing hydrophobic residues to water or polar regions to the hydrocarbon interior is energetically unfavourable. The system may respond by tilting a helix, deforming nearby lipids, shifting the position of a protein or changing the packing of a protein assembly. Thus altering lipid chain length can affect the function of a membrane protein even if its primary sequence stays unchanged. This is a concrete example of structure depending on the surrounding membrane rather than being solely an intrinsic feature of the isolated protein.

Finally, the alpha helix can be an anchor or an active structural element. Several helices can pack so that lipid-facing residues remain nonpolar while internal residues construct a polar pathway or a substrate-binding site. Some membrane proteins exploit relative helix movements to alternate the accessibility of a substrate-binding region. This establishes a structural basis for selective permeability without requiring the cell to make the whole bilayer permeable.

5. Beta barrels: a different solution to the same problem

Beta strands also contain a polar backbone, but the hydrogen bonds that stabilize a beta sheet run between neighbouring strands rather than within an individual alpha helix. A sheet that closes into a barrel can satisfy backbone groups at its joined edges. The result is a stable structure that can present a lipid-compatible exterior and a chemically different interior. In porins, the interior provides a water-filled passage through the membrane.

Side chains project approximately to alternate sides of a beta strand. Thus a sequence with hydrophobic residues at positions 1, 3, 5 and 7 and more polar residues at 2, 4, 6 and 8 can place alternate groups toward lipid and toward the pore. The pattern is useful but not unique to membrane barrels; soluble proteins also contain amphipathic beta sheets. Because the strand extends roughly 0.3–0.35 nm per residue, it can traverse a membrane with fewer residues than an alpha helix, depending on its tilt. A 19-residue hydropathy window can average away the alternating pattern and miss the crossing completely.

Beta-barrel membrane proteins are characteristic of outer membranes of Gram-negative bacteria, mitochondria and chloroplasts. Bacterial porins and mitochondrial VDAC are common examples. Most bacterial inner-membrane and eukaryotic plasma membrane spans are alpha-helical instead. This is a useful generalization, not a claim that all outer-membrane proteins form beta barrels. A protein with an extracellular immunoglobulin-like beta-sheet domain and a single membrane helix is not a transmembrane beta barrel merely because it contains beta sheets.

A barrel can enclose a selectively permeable pore, but neither its shape nor its mere presence tells us the pore's exact selectivity or gating properties. A constriction, charged residues or another domain may restrict passage; some barrels have a plugged or regulated lumen. For examination purposes, identify the fold, then ask what additional evidence is required to determine its biological function. The alpha-helix and beta-barrel solutions share the same physical principle: preserve favourable backbone interactions while presenting a suitable surface to the lipid core.

6. Topology and crossing parity

Membrane topology is most clearly described by identifying two compartments and tracing the mature polypeptide continuously from N to C. Every complete crossing changes which side the next segment occupies. An exposed loop that stays on one side does not count, nor does a re-entrant segment that enters the membrane and returns to the same side. A loop that contains a short hydrophobic region may therefore be mistaken for a full crossing if sequence information is interpreted too literally.

Consider a chain whose N terminus lies outside the cell. One full crossing puts the next segment inside; a second returns it outside; a third puts it inside. The geometric rule follows: odd numbers of full crossings put the termini on opposite sides, while even numbers put them on the same side. If N begins in the cytosol, all the side labels are reversed. The number of crossings refers to the mature chain, not a signal sequence removed during processing. Apply the rule to each polypeptide independently in a multisubunit complex.

For example, a confirmed four-pass protein with extracellular N has its first interhelical loop cytosolic, second loop extracellular, third cytosolic and C extracellular. If an antibody against the third loop binds intact nonpermeabilized cells, the proposed model and evidence conflict. Before rejecting the geometry, check membrane integrity, epitope identity, whether all four predicted segments are complete crossings, and whether more than one protein population is present. A convincing topology must account for internal loops as well as both termini.

Conventional single-pass classifications illustrate how insertion and orientation differ. A type I protein commonly has a cleaved N-terminal signal peptide, an extracellular or lumenal mature N domain and a cytosolic C domain anchored by a retained stop-transfer helix. A type II protein has cytosolic N and noncytosolic C, commonly established by an internal signal-anchor. A type III protein also has noncytosolic N and cytosolic C but uses an uncleaved signal-anchor rather than the typical cleaved signal sequence of type I. Terminology varies among textbook classifications; a precise description of mature N/C positions and signal cleavage is more robust than a Roman numeral.

Multipass proteins may use several internal insertion elements. Re-entrant loops, weakly hydrophobic crossings and membrane-associated hairpins mean the number of hydropathy peaks is not necessarily the number of full spans. Beta barrels obey the same geometric parity principle when the chain is traced continuously, but their strand count cannot be deduced using a detector designed for alpha-helical spans. This distinction links sequence prediction to experimental topology.

7. Establishing orientation and preserving it through vesicles

Large polar protein domains cannot simply pass through an intact hydrocarbon core. Many membrane proteins of the eukaryotic secretory pathway acquire their topology while being synthesized at the endoplasmic reticulum. A hydrophobic targeting segment is recognized, and the growing chain is delivered to a protein-conducting channel. Polar portions can enter the ER lumen through this pathway, and hydrophobic spans can partition laterally into the bilayer. This coordinated insertion avoids forcing an already folded hydrophilic domain through lipid.

A cleavable signal peptide may initiate translocation and then be removed. A downstream stop-transfer sequence halts translocation and remains as a spanning anchor. The mature N region lies lumenally, whereas the downstream C region remains cytosolic. A signal-anchor is different because the targeting sequence itself remains in the mature chain. The orientation of an internal anchor depends in part on its surrounding charges. More positively charged flanking residues often remain on the cytosolic side, a tendency called the positive-inside rule. It is especially useful for bacterial inner-membrane proteins and many ER proteins, but it is not absolute: the sequence context and neighbouring spans can alter the result. A charge mutation may change orientation, but a prediction requires verification.

When a transport vesicle buds from the ER into the cytosol, its lumen is continuous with the ER lumen before scission. The cytosolic surface remains exposed to the cytosol. Fusion of the vesicle with the plasma membrane makes its lumen continuous with the extracellular medium. Thus an ER-lumenal glycosylated domain appears outside the cell, while a cytosolic tail remains inside. Endocytosis preserves the same orientation: the previously extracellular domain becomes exposed to the endosomal lumen. This explains why many extracellular domains are modified by enzymes found within the ER and Golgi, not by enzymes in cytosol.

Use the names of the compartments carefully. In Gram-negative bacteria, the noncytoplasmic side of the inner membrane faces the periplasm, not directly the external environment. The mitochondrial inner membrane separates matrix from intermembrane space. Its insertion machinery differs from that of the ER. The general topological principle is the same, but the terms extracellular and lumenal must refer to the actual compartments. An experiment on a vesicle cannot be interpreted solely from the statement that a reagent was added outside.

The importance of orientation becomes obvious when considering substrates. An ATP-binding domain that faces cytosol can directly encounter cytosolic ATP, whereas an extracellular recognition domain can bind a ligand without that ligand entering the cytosol. A mutation which changes protein insertion can separate an otherwise functional catalytic domain from its substrate. Correct folding, correct localization and correct topology are three distinguishable requirements for membrane protein function.

8. Lipid anchors and regulated recruitment

A soluble protein may need to work close to a membrane without carrying a polypeptide span. Covalent lipid attachment solves this problem: the hydrocarbon group partitions into a leaflet, while the rest of the protein remains aqueous. The attachment strength depends on the anchor chemistry, number of anchors, the presence of complementary charged patches and additional protein-binding interactions.

N-myristoylation usually adds a 14-carbon myristoyl group to the amino-terminal glycine through an amide bond. The glycine is often exposed after removal of the initiating methionine. A single myristoyl group may provide insufficient binding for stable membrane residence, so a basic patch that interacts with acidic lipid head groups or a second lipid anchor can strengthen recruitment. Some proteins can bury their myristoyl group within a hydrophobic pocket and expose it during a conformational change. Thus a stable covalent modification can participate in reversible membrane association without cleavage of the amide bond.

S-palmitoylation commonly attaches a 16-carbon fatty acid to a cysteine side chain through a thioester. The term S-acylation includes other fatty acids as well. Because this linkage can be removed and reattached enzymatically, it can change the time a protein spends at a membrane and its distribution between compartments. Classical N-myristoylation uses an amide rather than a thioester. Prenylation commonly attaches a farnesyl (15-carbon) or geranylgeranyl (20-carbon) isoprenoid to a cysteine near the C terminus through a thioether. Numerous small GTP-binding proteins use this modification, along with other targeting information, to interact with cytosolic membrane surfaces. Lipidation alone is not sufficient to establish the exact compartment or function.

Glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchoring provides an attachment on the noncytosolic side. In the ER, a C-terminal signal region is removed and a preassembled glycolipid anchor is attached to the new terminus. The anchor's lipid portion resides in the lumenal leaflet, and the protein lies within the lumen. At the plasma membrane it is exposed to the extracellular environment. A GPI-anchored protein has no obligatory cytosolic polypeptide tail. If it participates in communication with the cytosol, transmembrane partners or changes in membrane organization can supply the connection.

Phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase C can release many accessible GPI-anchored proteins from intact cells. Release supports the presence of a susceptible GPI tether if the cell membrane remains intact and the cleavage is specific. Failure to release does not rule out GPI anchoring because modifications of the anchor or poor accessibility can make the anchor resistant. A mutation of a candidate lipidation site can provide another line of evidence, but protein expression and folding should be checked to avoid confusing loss of membrane association with degradation.

Noncovalent lipid recognition is a separate mechanism. A protein domain can recognize a particular phosphoinositide head group on the cytosolic leaflet, allowing a change in lipid chemistry to recruit a previously soluble protein. Annexins offer a further example: many associate with acidic membrane phospholipids in a calcium-dependent manner. These cases show that the membrane is a regulated platform rather than a permanent resting place for every associated protein. The molecular distinction is whether the lipid is covalently attached to the protein or recognized through noncovalent interactions.

9. Membrane carbohydrates and surface recognition

The plasma membrane is chemically asymmetric. In animal cells, carbohydrate chains attached to exposed glycoproteins and glycolipids form part of a hydrated surface coat called the glycocalyx. Its composition varies among cells and can include proteoglycans and associated surface molecules. It is neither a rigid cell wall nor another lipid bilayer. The glycocalyx helps protect the surface, affects molecular access and contributes to recognition between cells and their environment.

In glycoproteins, carbohydrates are covalently attached to amino acids; in glycolipids they are part of a lipid. Conventional secretory-pathway N-linked glycosylation occurs at suitable Asn-X-Ser/Thr sequences, where X is normally not proline. Not every potential site is used; it must be accessible to the processing machinery in an appropriate structural and compartmental context. Many O-linked glycans are attached to serine or threonine, without the same universal short sequence requirement. The ER and Golgi process domains exposed to their lumens, so glycan-bearing domains are typically exposed extracellularly when the protein arrives at the plasma membrane. Cytosolic O-GlcNAc is a separate modification and is not evidence of ER lumenal exposure.

Carbohydrates provide structural diversity because the sugar identity, linkage, branching and terminal modifications can vary. ABO blood group antigens illustrate this principle. A and B glycosyltransferases produce different terminal sugar structures on glycolipids and glycoproteins derived from the H antigen. In the common O phenotype, neither A- nor B-specific sugar is added by the corresponding functional transferase. Thus different cell-surface antigenic identities can arise from postsynthetic carbohydrate processing without changing the amino acid sequence of a carrier protein. The distinction between a protein epitope and a carbohydrate epitope is important when interpreting binding experiments.

Lectins recognize particular carbohydrate arrangements. Selectins are membrane-associated adhesion proteins whose carbohydrate recognition helps mediate transient leukocyte contacts with vascular surfaces. Several interactions can collectively stabilize contact even if each individual binding event is reversible. In addition, a dense carbohydrate coat can hinder access of an extracellular protease to a particular protein site. Apparent protection from proteolysis may therefore reflect steric masking rather than location on the cytosolic side.

Glycosylation can itself help map membrane topology. Suppose a multipass protein has two candidate N-linked sites in different loops. If a glycan-dependent molecular mass shift is detected for loop one but not loop two, the modified site supports lumenal exposure during insertion. The unmodified site is weaker evidence: a suitable sequence can face the lumen and remain unoccupied. A deglycosylation treatment can establish whether a mass change really depends on carbohydrate; an independent accessibility experiment establishes which side a domain occupies now. Combining both is stronger than treating glycosylation as an infallible binary label.

The glycocalyx thus connects orientation with function. Protein domains facing the noncytosolic compartment can acquire modifications that contribute to protection and recognition. Their chemical identities and position together determine what other molecules can encounter them. Not every surface protein is glycosylated, and not every glycan functions as a signalling ligand. These qualifications help avoid turning a broad principle into an unjustified universal rule.

10. Lipid–protein interactions and clustering

The membrane environment is an active influence on protein structure. Its thickness, fluidity, curvature, surface charge and packing alter the energetic cost of protein insertion and conformational changes. Hydrophobic mismatch is one example. A transporter may show different activity in two membranes of different lipid chain composition because the conformational transitions require different distortions of the surrounding lipids. Thus protein sequence can remain constant while function changes.

Some effects are more specific than bulk properties. Lipids immediately contacting an integral protein form a local annular shell, and individual lipid molecules can associate with more tightly defined sites. A sterol or phospholipid can stabilize a particular interface; a charged lipid head group can recruit a basic domain; and a lipid can influence the relative stability of alternate protein conformations. Removing the membrane during purification may therefore remove a physical environment as well as particular important lipid contacts. Reconstituting the purified protein in a membrane of defined composition provides a way to test whether activity can be recovered.

Cardiolipin in the mitochondrial inner membrane contributes to the structural environment of respiratory complexes and can stabilize particular assemblies. Cholesterol in animal plasma membranes affects packing and can interact directly with some proteins. A decrease in activity after cholesterol depletion does not distinguish a specific cholesterol-binding requirement from an indirect effect on packing, thickness or the organization of partner proteins. These explanations lead to different experimental predictions. A specific binding mechanism needs more direct evidence than an activity change following extraction.

Proteins interact with one another as well. Multiple subunits can form a channel, a receptor can recruit adaptors, and several integral proteins can associate with one cytoskeletal network. Clustering raises local partner concentration and may change both mobility and biological activity. Some membrane regions are transiently enriched in certain lipids and proteins, often discussed in terms of microdomains or lipid rafts. Such organization is dynamic and dependent on context. Detergent resistance under a particular isolation condition is not, by itself, proof that a stable raft with the same composition existed in the living membrane. Extraction can create or alter lipid assemblies.

To interpret a reconstitution experiment, consider two vesicle preparations containing the same total amount of carrier but different lipid compositions. If one shows twice the transport rate, the lipid environment could have altered the carrier's intrinsic activity. Yet a difference in the proportion of correctly oriented molecules could explain the same result, as could a change in vesicle leakiness or protein stability. Measuring insertion, accessibility and vesicle integrity is needed before assigning a specific causal mechanism. This habit of comparing alternative explanations is central to Part C reasoning.

The influence is reciprocal: protein clusters can crowd adjacent molecules, asymmetric insertion can bend a leaflet, and attachments to skeleton or matrix can impose mechanical constraints. A living membrane is organized by interactions among lipids, proteins and neighbouring structures. Fluidity does not exclude organization, and organization does not imply that all components are fixed.

11. How structural placement enables membrane functions

Structural classes tell us how a protein is attached; functional classes tell us what work it performs. A single-pass protein may be a receptor, an enzyme or an adhesion molecule. A multipass protein may act as a transporter or a receptor. The number of crossings alone cannot establish whether a protein consumes ATP, recognizes a ligand or permits ion passage. We must ask how accessible domains are arranged and how conformational changes or binding events accomplish their tasks.

A channel offers a pathway through which a suitable solute can pass when the channel is open. Its structure shields the pathway from the hydrophobic bilayer, and pore chemistry and geometry contribute to selectivity. Net flow through an open passive channel follows the electrochemical gradient. A channel may require ATP binding or phosphorylation as part of its gating regulation, but ATP-dependent regulation is not the same as direct energy coupling to uphill solute movement. Channels may also be mechanically or voltage regulated. The detailed physical basis of selectivity and gating belongs to a later chapter.

A carrier binds a substrate and changes conformation, allowing the binding region to be accessed alternately from opposite sides. Unlike a continuously open pore, the carrier cycles through states, and finite binding and turnover explain saturation. Some carriers facilitate downhill movement, while others couple downhill movement of one solute to uphill movement of another. Primary active pumps directly couple transport to an energy source, such as ATP hydrolysis in the Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase. Some use light or redox energy instead. The structural requirement is an arrangement that couples energy conversion with directed movement. Distinguishing passive and active mechanisms requires information about gradients and energy use, not simply counting helices.

Receptors recognize information rather than necessarily transporting the ligand. A single-pass receptor can use an extracellular recognition domain and a cytosolic regulatory domain to transmit a signal. A seven-transmembrane receptor can couple ligand binding to conformational changes in its helix bundle and interactions with intracellular partners. A ligand need not enter the cytosol for its presence to influence cytosolic reactions. Conversely, a ligand-gated ion channel performs both recognition and regulated transport. Functional categories can overlap because the same molecular architecture can support multiple biological tasks.

Membrane enzymes are positioned to encounter particular substrates or cofactors. A catalytic domain facing cytosol has direct access to cytosolic ATP or other soluble substrates; one facing extracellular space can modify external molecules. The organized placement of respiratory proteins in membranes allows energy-converting reactions to be coupled to directional processes. Peripheral enzymes may be recruited only when a membrane lipid or partner becomes available, so changing their localization can change activity even before their catalytic chemistry changes.

Adhesion molecules and structural anchors establish physical connections across or along the membrane. Integrins are heterodimeric transmembrane receptors whose extracellular domains interact with extracellular matrix, while their cytosolic regions link through adaptor proteins to the cytoskeleton. Cadherins participate in cell-cell adhesion and have intracellular connections. These arrangements allow force and information to pass through a membrane without making the boundary indiscriminately permeable. The complete signalling pathways and tissue-specific mechanisms belong in later units.

Erythrocytes integrate several tasks in one membrane. Band 3 exchanges anions and interacts with the skeleton through ankyrin-associated connections. Spectrin and actin form a supportive network on the cytosolic surface; glycophorin contributes to external surface chemistry. Weakening the membrane–skeleton attachment can alter cell shape and mechanical stability even when lipid composition is unchanged. Thus membrane organization supports physical integrity as well as selective chemical exchange.

12. Hydropathy plots: sequence evidence and calculation

A protein sequence provides clues about membrane insertion before a structure is determined. Kyte and Doolittle assigned numerical hydropathy scores to amino acids. In their scale, isoleucine scores +4.5, valine +4.2, leucine +3.8, alanine +1.8, glycine −0.4, serine −0.8, aspartate −3.5, lysine −3.9 and arginine −4.5. Positive values indicate greater hydrophobic character on this scale. They are dimensionless sequence-analysis scores, not measured energies in kJ/mol; other scales may have different signs or values.

Plotting each individual residue produces a rapidly fluctuating graph. A typical membrane helix requires a sustained stretch of compatible residues, so a sliding-window average is more informative. Let the odd window width be w, its half-width m=(w−1)/2, and the residue score hⱼ. The mean plotted at central residue i is Hᵢ=(Σ hⱼ)/w, summed from j=i−m to i+m. The window contains exactly 2m+1=w scores. Dividing by w makes averages comparable rather than simply allowing a longer segment to accumulate a larger numerical total. A 19-residue window is commonly used to search for alpha-helical membrane spans; shorter windows highlight local features.

For a small illustrative calculation, take L I V K A. Its mean is (3.8+4.5+4.2−3.9+1.8)/5=10.4/5=2.08. If the next residue is D, shifting the window one position removes L and adds D. The next mean is (10.4−3.8−3.5)/5=3.1/5=0.62. This shows how a local sequence change influences the plot and why neighbouring windows are correlated. A five-residue window does not itself represent a membrane-spanning helix; it is used here solely to make the calculation transparent.

Now consider a 19-residue window composed of eight leucines, six valines, three alanines and two lysines. The sum is 8(3.8)+6(4.2)+3(1.8)+2(−3.9)=30.4+25.2+5.4−7.8=53.2, giving mean 2.80. Replacing two leucines with two aspartates decreases the sum by 2[3.8−(−3.5)]=14.6. The new mean is 38.6/19≈2.03. Both values remain above the often-used heuristic of about +1.6 for a 19-residue window. The substitutions could weaken membrane insertion or disrupt a functional contact, but the precise biological result cannot be determined from the mean alone. The threshold is not a universal thermodynamic boundary.

A broad positive peak is more informative than a narrow spike. If a 300-residue sequence has sustained candidate peaks near residues 30–51, 105–127 and 213–235, it may encode a three-pass protein. If N is independently found extracellular and all three peaks are full mature crossings, C should be cytosolic. If the first peak represents a cleaved signal peptide, the count and the mature N orientation must be re-evaluated. Hydropathy generates a structural hypothesis rather than directly reporting topology.

False positives arise from buried hydrophobic cores in soluble proteins, cleaved signal peptides and membrane-associated hairpins. False negatives arise from polar pore-facing residues and beta-barrel strands with alternating hydrophobicity. Window width can change peak shape and apparent boundaries; overlapping peaks may obscure separate spans. The graph does not reveal which side the chain faces, how many subunits are assembled, or what the protein transports. These limitations make independent sidedness and attachment experiments essential.

13. Detergent extraction and biochemical fractionation

The distinction between integral and peripheral association becomes an experimental question when isolated membranes are treated under different conditions. Equal membrane aliquots can be incubated in buffer, high salt, alkaline carbonate or a suitable detergent, then separated into pellet and supernatant. The distribution of a target protein can be detected with an antibody. Each treatment probes a different part of the molecular association, so the strongest interpretation comes from comparing all of them rather than using one fraction as a label.

High ionic strength screens electrostatic attractions between proteins and charged lipid head groups or between two proteins. Alkaline carbonate can disrupt several peripheral associations and release soluble contents of membrane vesicles. A protein found in the supernatant after such treatment may be peripheral or may have been soluble within a vesicle lumen. Resistance to these treatments does not uniquely prove a full membrane crossing: lipid anchors, monotopic insertion, stable peripheral complexes and insoluble aggregates may all remain pelleted. Include known peripheral and integral markers and distinguish soluble enclosed proteins from genuine membrane-associated proteins.

Detergents are amphipathic molecules that can interact with lipids and hydrophobic protein surfaces. At an appropriate detergent-to-lipid ratio they disrupt the bilayer and form mixed lipid–detergent or protein–detergent assemblies. This allows many embedded proteins to remain dispersed in water without exposing large hydrophobic surfaces. However, solubilization and preservation of the native fold are distinct. Mild nonionic detergents can retain some active proteins and complexes; ionic detergents such as SDS often disrupt folding and associations. Removing important lipids can also alter activity without unfolding every part of the protein. The detergent identity and experimental conditions therefore matter.

In the illustrative data below, protein A is released efficiently by salt and carbonate, whereas protein B remains associated until detergent is added. A is compatible with a peripheral association. B is compatible with an integral or another hydrophobic attachment, but the number of membrane crossings is not determined. A GPI-linked protein might show a pattern similar to B despite having no transmembrane polypeptide. A strongly bound peripheral protein might also resist salt. Use anchor cleavage, sequence or sidedness data to distinguish these possibilities.

Quantitative extraction requires accounting for recovered material. If the pellet contains 72 signal units and the supernatant 28 after high salt, the released fraction is S/(P+S)=28/100=28%. If detergent gives P=8 and S=92, release is 92%. These fractions are valid estimates only if signal is measured in its linear range and corrected for loading and volume. Similar total recovery supports the comparison; large signal loss could indicate proteolysis, aggregation or incomplete recovery. A supernatant value alone cannot establish extraction efficiency.

Finally, a detergent-solubilized channel may still be folded, but it cannot maintain an ion gradient without a sealed bilayer separating two compartments. Reconstitution can restore a functional environment and allow the protein to be examined independently of many other cellular components. The investigator must still check insertion, orientation and integrity. Fractionation establishes chemical association under defined conditions, not complete topology or biological activity.

14. Protease protection and independent topology mapping

Proteases hydrolyse accessible peptide bonds. A large water-soluble protease generally cannot penetrate an intact lipid bilayer, allowing the membrane to protect domains that face the enclosed compartment. This is the basis of a protease protection assay. A protected site is not necessarily enclosed, however: it may be buried in a folded domain, lack an appropriate cleavage site, be shielded by a binding partner or be masked by carbohydrate. The central experimental task is distinguishing membrane-based protection from these alternative causes.

Suppose a target protein has recognizable N-terminal and C-terminal tags. In sealed right-side-out plasma membrane vesicles, extracellular domains face the external buffer while cytosolic domains face the enclosed lumen. Added protease removes N but leaves C; adding detergent before protease results in loss of both tags. With suitable controls, the pattern supports extracellular N and cytosolic C. It does not establish one full crossing: three or five crossings could produce the same terminal positions. Internal loop accessibility and sequence evidence are needed for a complete model.

ER-derived microsomes commonly reseal with cytosolic faces exposed to the surrounding buffer and lumenal domains enclosed. Therefore an accessible cytosolic domain may be removed by added protease while a lumenal domain is protected. This is the reverse of the usual right-side-out plasma vesicle with respect to what compartment the buffer contacts. The physical orientation of the actual preparation must be established. Mixed populations of right-side-out and inside-out vesicles can yield partial protection, which could be misread as a population of partially degraded protein.

The untreated sample establishes the original protein and epitopes. Protease treatment with the membrane intact tests exposed sites. Protease plus detergent removes the barrier, testing whether the protected region can be digested at all. A known enclosed soluble marker should remain protected without detergent; if it disappears, the vesicles may be leaky or incorrectly oriented. A known exposed marker tests protease activity. If the candidate tag survives even with detergent, the site may be resistant or the enzyme inactive. Controls on total recovery and membrane integrity prevent degradation or leakage from being mistaken for a topological result.

A protected fragment may be detected after the exposed part of a chain is removed. Consider a single-pass protein with a 30 kDa N domain, 3 kDa anchor and 10 kDa C domain. After protease treatment outside intact right-side-out vesicles, the N epitope disappears and a C-tagged band near 13 kDa remains. Both tags disappear with detergent. This fits an externally exposed N domain and a protected C domain, assuming the mass estimates neglect glycans, linkers and nonideal gel migration. Disappearance of an antibody signal reports loss of that epitope, not necessarily destruction of the whole protein. Antibodies recognizing different domains make the inference stronger.

An independent accessibility method uses membrane-impermeant antibodies or chemical labels on intact cells. Binding of a nonpermeant antibody to the N tag supports external exposure. Binding of the C antibody only after controlled permeabilization supports a cytosolic C tag. But a negative antibody result could reflect epitope masking, and a positive result could reflect membrane damage. Conventional N-linked glycosylation adds another independent clue about exposure to lumenal biosynthetic machinery. The strongest topology is supported by concordant predictions, cleavage patterns, localization and accessibility. A contradiction invites a new experiment rather than an arbitrary choice of which observation to ignore.

15. Lateral diffusion, anchors and polarized membranes

Proteins that can move within the plane of a membrane do not necessarily explore its entire surface freely. An integral protein may encounter barriers formed by other proteins, interact transiently with lipids or partners, or become tethered to the cytoskeleton. A membrane may consequently contain fast-moving, slowly moving and effectively immobile populations. Lateral movement does not require transferring an aqueous domain through lipid, so a protein can diffuse without changing its N/C orientation. Flipping a large membrane-spanning protein is a different and generally highly unfavourable process.

For an ideal molecule undergoing unbiased random diffusion in an isotropic two-dimensional membrane, the mean squared displacement in each independent coordinate is 2Dt. Adding the x and y contributions gives ⟨r²⟩=4Dt. D is the lateral diffusion coefficient, in units such as µm²/s, and t is elapsed time. This relation describes a statistical average over trajectories, not a fixed speed or the total length of a winding path. If D=0.05 µm²/s and t=20 s, ⟨r²⟩=4×0.05×20=4 µm², so the root-mean-square displacement is 2 µm. Doubling the time increases RMS displacement by √2, not by two, under the stated assumptions.

The cytoskeleton can restrict diffusion. In erythrocytes, spectrin forms a network linked to membrane proteins through components such as ankyrin. A protein directly attached to that network may move little, while another may move within a region bounded by the network. Transient barriers can produce relatively free local motion but restricted motion across larger distances. If a mutation weakens a tether, membrane-protein mobility may increase without any change in the membrane's overall lipid viscosity.

Epithelial cell polarity reveals why restricting motion is useful. Apical and basolateral membrane domains contain different transporters and receptors and face different environments. Tight junctions contribute to restricting mixing of many membrane components, while selective delivery and retention also maintain domain identity. Even a fully functional transporter can become physiologically ineffective if distributed on the wrong side of an epithelial cell. Directional tissue transport depends on both the molecular activity of proteins and their location.

Clustering can likewise change mobility. Ligand binding can promote receptor association or recruitment of a cytoskeletal partner. Extracellular matrix attachment may restrict movement. Multivalent fluorescent antibodies used to detect proteins can themselves crosslink targets, making the measured behaviour different from that of unlabelled proteins. A reduced diffusion coefficient could reflect a larger complex, stronger binding, changed lipid packing or barriers; the value alone cannot reveal the precise cause. It is particularly important to distinguish how quickly a mobile protein moves from how much of the population is mobile, which the next technique allows us to do.

16. FRAP: quantitative interpretation of protein mobility

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) follows redistribution of fluorescently labelled molecules. A region is illuminated strongly, making its fluorophores lose fluorescence. Under the usual interpretation bleaching does not physically remove the proteins. Unbleached molecules from the surrounding membrane can diffuse into the region while bleached molecules leave, leading to local signal recovery. The rate of recovery and its final extent report different aspects of mobility. The technique measures a population over a defined time and area, not the path of each individual molecule.

Let Fᵢ denote corrected prebleach fluorescence, F₀ the intensity immediately after bleaching and F∞ the stable plateau. The fluorescence loss due to bleaching is Fᵢ−F₀. The amount recovered is F∞−F₀. Their ratio estimates the mobile fraction M=(F∞−F₀)/(Fᵢ−F₀); 1−M is the apparent nonrecovering fraction. The derivation simply compares what was restored to what was lost. Intensities should be corrected for background and imaging-related photobleaching. The standard interpretation assumes appropriate labelling, negligible synthesis or turnover over the observation time, an adequate accessible fluorescent reservoir and a meaningful plateau.

For Fᵢ=100, F₀=20 and F∞=76, the loss is 80 and recovery is 56. M=56/80=0.70, and the nonrecovering fraction is 0.30. The half-recovery point is not half the initial fluorescence: it is F₀+(F∞−F₀)/2=20+28=48. The half-time t½ is the time to reach that intensity. If the trace reaches 48 after 5 s, its recovery half-time is 5 s. A plateau below the original intensity may indicate retained molecules, but limited reservoir, photophysical effects or incomplete observation must also be considered.

For a large flat isotropic membrane with an instantaneous uniform circular bleach and diffusion-limited recovery, a standard approximate relation is D≈0.224r²/t½, where r is bleach radius and t½ is measured in seconds. The factor 0.224 is geometry-specific, obtained from the circular diffusion model, not from the simpler mean squared displacement formula. The underlying scaling follows from diffusion: if the characteristic distance doubles, time scales approximately with the square of distance. Thus r²/t has the units of D, but a geometry-specific coefficient is still required. Finite bleach duration, nonuniform beam profiles, binding kinetics and membrane boundaries can require different models.

If r=2 µm and t½=5 s, D≈0.224×4/5=0.1792 µm²/s, or 1.792×10⁻¹³ m²/s. At the same radius with a 10 s half-time, the apparent coefficient is 0.0896 µm²/s. This describes the recovering population under the assumptions; the plateau rather than the half-time determines the apparent mobile fraction. It is incorrect to infer that a low recovery plateau automatically means the mobile molecules move slowly.

Compare two illustrative proteins measured with the same bleach radius and initial intensities of 100 and 20. Protein A reaches 92 with t½=10 s. Protein B reaches 60 with t½=3 s. A has M=(92−20)/80=0.90; B has M=(60−20)/80=0.50. B has a faster recovering component if the recovery is diffusion-limited, yet a larger nonrecovering population. The difference may arise from a strongly attached fraction of B coexisting with rapidly mobile B molecules. Neither curve alone gives the number of subunits or proves a specific molecular anchor.

Changing bleach radius provides a useful diagnostic. If the radius doubles and t½ increases approximately fourfold under comparable conditions, simple diffusion is supported. If t½ changes little, exchange or binding kinetics may dominate recovery. Mixed mechanisms can show intermediate behaviour. Extending the observation period may reveal that an apparently immobile population actually exchanges slowly. Thus immobility in FRAP is operational and time-dependent rather than an assertion of permanent anchoring.

Connect this analysis to topology and attachment. A mutation that disrupts a cytoskeletal binding region can raise the plateau while leaving the half-time of the mobile component similar. A change in the surrounding lipid may instead alter the half-time without changing the plateau. These patterns support different kinds of mechanism but are not unique proof of either cause. The strongest studies combine FRAP with localization, expression, membrane integrity and independent interaction measurements.

17. An unknown protein: combining independent evidence

Imagine a 280-residue plasma membrane protein X. Its hydropathy plot contains broad peaks at positions 48–69, 135–157 and 211–233. None is known to be cleaved. A plausible initial model has three transmembrane alpha helices, but a prediction alone is not proof. An N-terminal antibody binds nonpermeabilized cells, whereas a C-terminal antibody binds only after permeabilization. The terminal orientation agrees with odd crossing parity; it does not yet establish three rather than another odd number.

Loop mapping strengthens the model. The region between the first and second peaks is accessible on the cytosolic side in properly oriented preparations. An occupied conventional N-linked glycosylation site lies between the second and third peaks. This pattern fits the three-crossing sequence: N extracellular, first loop cytosolic, second loop extracellular, C cytosolic. Salt and carbonate treatments leave X membrane-associated, whereas a suitable detergent solubilizes it. The biochemical attachment evidence supports hydrophobic insertion but would not independently prove the topology. Together, the results make the model more persuasive than any one result.

Now suppose a mutant replaces several hydrophobic residues in the central peak with charged residues. Its membrane association decreases and both terminal antibodies bind intact cells. A loss of one full crossing would produce even parity and could account for both tags facing outward. But membrane damage could also let the antibodies enter, and misfolding or altered processing could change the protein population. Check membrane integrity, expression, mature sequence and intracellular localization before concluding that the middle crossing was lost. A coherent model must explain why each observation changed.

A different mutation removes a cytosolic tail interaction sequence. It leaves the hydropathy peaks, glycosylation, terminal accessibility and total surface protein abundance essentially unchanged. FRAP mobile fraction rises from 50% to 85%, while the recovery half-time barely changes. This suggests a change in retention of a subpopulation, perhaps through loss of a cytoskeletal or other interaction. It does not identify the binding partner. Restoration of the original sequence or independent evidence of partner binding would test the proposed explanation more directly. The comparison shows that topology and lateral mobility are distinct properties.

Finally ask what X actually does. If purified X supports net transport down a solute gradient in reconstituted vesicles, passive transport is supported, but the result does not distinguish a channel from an alternating-access carrier. If uphill transport is observed only when an appropriate ATP supply is present and other gradients are controlled, direct energy coupling becomes a candidate mechanism. A three-pass topology cannot establish whether the protein is a transporter at all. Functional classification requires measuring the relevant process. This connects the chapter's structural logic to the study of permeability and transport in the next node.

18. Consolidation and examination reasoning

Membrane-spanning structures solve a physical chemistry problem. The protein backbone forms hydrogen bonds within an alpha helix or between beta strands; side chains compatible with lipid face the bilayer, while other residues can create aqueous or interfacial regions. Monotopic insertion, noncovalent peripheral binding and covalent lipid anchors allow association without a full crossing. Every complete crossing switches the side occupied by the next segment of a mature chain. Biosynthetic insertion establishes topology, which ordinary vesicular traffic preserves.

When analysing an unfamiliar problem, identify exactly what was measured. Hydropathy reports sequence compatibility, extraction reports association under defined conditions, protease protection reports accessibility, glycosylation reports access to particular processing machinery, and FRAP reports lateral redistribution and apparent mobile fraction. Each has alternative interpretations and required controls. The most reliable model accounts for several independent observations. In particular, a negative result is not always proof of absence: a protected site may be inaccessible for reasons other than the membrane, and a potential glycosylation site may be unoccupied even when lumenal.

Three distinctions prevent many common errors: integral is not synonymous with transmembrane; lateral diffusion does not equal transbilayer inversion; and rapid recovery does not mean complete recovery. A receptor, channel and pump may all contain helices, but their functions differ in the molecular events that they couple to the two sides of the membrane. This is the foundation for explaining how a protein can facilitate movement of a solute while the rest of the membrane retains its selective barrier.

19. Part B: conceptual and assertion–reason questions

Question 1. Integral versus transmembrane

Which must cross the whole bilayer? A. Spectrin B. GPI protein C. A protein with a full spanning helix D. Monotopic integral protein

Answer and reasoning

C. The full span defines transmembrane placement. Spectrin is peripheral, GPI anchors one leaflet, and monotopic insertion does not require a complete crossing.

Question 2. Backbone chemistry

What favours an alpha helix in the core? A. Peptide bonds become nonpolar B. Internal hydrogen bonding and lipid-compatible exterior C. All residues must be leucine D. Covalent linkage to every lipid

Answer and reasoning

B. The backbone remains polar, but hydrogen bonds satisfy many groups while appropriate side chains face lipid. A, C and D are not requirements.

Question 3. Beta barrel

What sequence pattern is compatible with a pore-facing beta strand? A. Alternating nonpolar/polar B. Only lysine C. No backbone hydrogen bonds D. Every side chain facing lipid

Answer and reasoning

A. Alternating side-chain directions permit hydrophobic exterior and polar interior. B–D ignore the need to stabilize the backbone and accommodate distinct environments.

Question 4. Parity

Four complete crossings and a cytosolic N terminus imply: A. Cytosolic C B. Extracellular C C. Necessarily ER lumen D. No prediction possible

Answer and reasoning

A. Four side switches return the chain to its original side. B requires odd parity; C confuses compartments; D ignores the confirmed crossings.

Question 5. Signal peptide

A cleaved N-terminal signal peptide and one retained stop-transfer span usually establish: A. Both termini cytosolic B. N lumenal, C cytosolic C. Both lumenal D. Two mature crossings

Answer and reasoning

B. The signal initiates translocation, and the retained stop-transfer helix leaves C cytosolic. The removed peptide is not a mature span, rejecting D. A and C conflict with one crossing.

Question 6. Anchor chemistry

Which usual modification contains a potentially reversible thioester? A. N-myristoylation B. S-palmitoylation C. Prenylation D. N-glycosylation

Answer and reasoning

B. Cysteine S-acylation uses a thioester. The others involve an amide, thioether or carbohydrate linkage respectively.

Question 7. GPI topology

A GPI protein at the cell surface typically has: A. A cytosolic tail by definition B. Extracellular protein domain C. Seven helices D. A covalent bond to actin

Answer and reasoning

B. GPI is added lumenally and remains noncytosolic. It provides no obligatory transmembrane polypeptide or cytosolic tail.

Question 8. Hydropathy

A broad N-terminal hydrophobic peak is: A. Definitive proof of a retained span B. Compatible with a signal peptide or span C. Proof of a beta barrel D. Proof of C-terminal orientation

Answer and reasoning

B. The plot does not measure cleavage, fold or sidedness, rejecting A, C and D.

Question 9. Extraction

Which treatment substitutes an amphipathic environment for hydrophobic surfaces? A. Salt B. DNase C. Detergent D. Antibody

Answer and reasoning

C. Detergent associates with lipid and hydrophobic protein regions. Salt screens charge, DNase cuts DNA, and antibody recognizes epitopes.

Question 10. Assertion–reason

Assertion: a protein can diffuse laterally without inversion. Reason: lateral motion need not transfer an aqueous domain across lipid. A. Both true and R explains A B. Both true but unrelated C. A true, R false D. A false

Answer and reasoning

A. The two distinct geometries explain why mobility need not change sidedness.

Question 11. Assertion–reason

Assertion: failure of PI-PLC release excludes GPI anchoring. Reason: some GPI structures resist PI-PLC. A. Both true B. A false, R true C. Both false D. A true, R false

Answer and reasoning

B. Resistant anchor chemistry or inaccessible sites make nonrelease insufficient to exclude GPI anchoring.

Question 12. Energy coupling

An ATP-regulated K⁺ channel conducts K⁺ downhill. Does ATP necessarily power each crossing? A. Yes B. No; gating and driving force differ C. Yes for multipass channels D. No because lipid is freely ion-permeable

Answer and reasoning

B. Passive channel movement follows the electrochemical driving force. Regulation can use ATP without directly pumping ions. A and C confuse these events; D is false.

20. Part C: quantitative and experimental analysis

Question 1. Molecular ratio

60 mg protein averages 60 kDa and 40 mg lipid averages 800 Da. Calculate lipid:protein molecular ratio.

Answer and reasoning

Protein=0.060/60000=1.0×10⁻⁶ mol. Lipid=0.040/800=5.0×10⁻⁵ mol. Ratio=50:1, using protein monomers and average masses. Equal molecular area is not assumed.

Question 2. Helix tilt

A 25-residue helix spans a 3.12 nm core. Use 0.15 nm per residue centre interval. Find tilt from the normal.

Answer and reasoning

L=24×0.15=3.60 nm. cos θ=3.12/3.60=0.8667, giving θ≈29.9°. The value is relative to the normal; real systems may deform lipids.

Question 3. Hydropathy

V L I A K S D have scores +4.2,+3.8,+4.5,+1.8,−3.9,−0.8,−3.5. Find mean and new mean if D becomes L.

Answer and reasoning

Sum=6.1, mean≈0.87. Replacement adds 7.3, giving 13.4/7≈1.91. This short illustrative window does not identify a full transmembrane span.

Question 4. Sliding window

A 19-residue mean is 2.0. Remove V (+4.2) and add R (−4.5). Find new mean.

Answer and reasoning

Original sum=38.0. New=38.0−4.2−4.5=29.3; mean≈1.54. A heuristic threshold is not definitive biological evidence.

Question 5. Re-entrant loop

A cytosolic N protein has five hydrophobic regions; four are full crossings and one is re-entrant. Where is C?

Answer and reasoning

C is cytosolic. Four complete crossings return to the starting side. The re-entrant segment does not switch sides.

Question 6. Glycosylation

A three-pass protein has extracellular N. Which interhelical loop could be conventionally N-glycosylated? What if the sequon is unused?

Answer and reasoning

Loop 1 cytosolic, loop 2 lumenal/extracellular. Loop 2 can be glycosylated if accessible. Nonoccupancy is not proof of cytosolic orientation.

Question 7. Failed protection control

C tag survives protease on intact right-side-out vesicles but an enclosed soluble marker is digested. Is C necessarily cytosolic?

Answer and reasoning

No. Membrane integrity or orientation failed. The surviving tag may be resistant or masked. Repeat with verified membrane protection and cleavage controls.

Question 8. Extraction recovery

Starting signal 120. Salt P=60, S=20; detergent P=6, S=110. Calculate released fractions and assess.

Answer and reasoning

Salt 20/80=25%; detergent 110/116≈94.8%. Total recovery 66.7% versus 96.7%. Signal loss may bias estimates; check recovery before assigning quantitative differences.

Question 9. FRAP plateau

Fᵢ=120, F₀=30, F∞=93. Find mobile fraction, nonrecovering fraction and half-recovery intensity.

Answer and reasoning

Loss=90, regained=63. M=0.70, nonrecovering fraction=0.30. Half intensity=30+63/2=61.5.

Question 10. FRAP diffusion coefficient

Circular bleach radius 1.5 µm, half-times 3 s and 6 s. Assume uniform instantaneous bleach and diffusion limitation.

Answer and reasoning

0.224r²=0.504 µm². D₁=0.168 µm²/s and D₂=0.084 µm²/s. Valid under the stated model.

Question 11. Radius test

Half-times 4 s at r=1 µm and 5 s at r=2 µm. Does simple unrestricted diffusion explain this?

Answer and reasoning

Not well under comparable geometry. Pure diffusion predicts about 16 s at doubled radius. Binding/exchange or geometry may dominate; the data cannot identify a specific partner.

Question 12. Random motion

D=0.04 µm²/s, t=25 s. Find RMS displacement for ideal two-dimensional diffusion.

Answer and reasoning

⟨r²⟩=4Dt=4 µm²; RMS=2 µm. It is not the total path length.

Question 13. Mutation and mobility

A mutant has unchanged topology and surface expression. FRAP M rises from 0.40 to 0.85 while t½ stays 5 s. A. Inversion B. Reduced retention C. No diffusion D. Proven ATP hydrolysis change

Answer and reasoning

B is supported. More protein becomes recoverable with similar recovery timescale. A conflicts with topology; C with recovery; D is not measured.

Question 14. Competing model

Three hydrophobic peaks, both mature termini extracellular. Which is impossible? A. All three full spans B. Two spans and re-entry C. Four spans including missed weak peak D. Two spans after processing

Answer and reasoning

A. Odd full crossing parity cannot leave both termini on the same side. The other models require independent confirmation.

Question 15. Reconstitution

Flux in lipid A is twice B, but 80% of carriers have correct orientation in A and 40% in B. Total protein is equal. Does intrinsic catalytic activity double?

Answer and reasoning

Not established. Correctly oriented protein already differs twofold. Normalize to active correctly oriented protein and check vesicle leakiness.

Question 16. Endocytosis

An impermeant antibody binds an extracellular receptor domain and is endocytosed. Where is it? A. Cytosol B. Endosomal lumen C. Bilayer core D. Nucleus

Answer and reasoning

B. Vesicle budding encloses extracellular medium, preserving the receptor's noncytosolic orientation. The other locations require additional processes.

Question 17. GPI evidence

Salt-resistant protein has no sustained helix peak, is solubilized by detergent and released by PI-PLC on intact cells. What class is supported and what remains uncertain?

Answer and reasoning

GPI anchoring is parsimonious. Detergent alone fits other hydrophobic associations, and PI-PLC could indirectly release a partner. Confirm covalent anchoring and membrane integrity.
