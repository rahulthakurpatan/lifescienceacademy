Metabolic Flexibility and Maintenance of Metabolic Health

How tissues adjust fuel use to supply and demand, and how physiological experiments reveal the capacity to maintain metabolic health.

1. Why metabolic flexibility matters

A resting skeletal muscle, an exercising skeletal muscle and a muscle recovering from a meal belong to the same animal, but they do not face the same metabolic problem. At rest after an overnight fast, fatty acids released from adipose stores can supply much of the oxidative fuel. Soon after a carbohydrate-containing meal, circulating glucose becomes more abundant and insulin encourages its removal from the circulation. During a sprint, the speed at which ATP must be regenerated increases dramatically. During recovery, the cell must replenish fuel stores and repair cellular structures. A useful metabolic system therefore has to alter the rate and direction of fuel movement rather than commit permanently to one substrate.

Metabolic flexibility describes this ability to adjust substrate use to changing supply and demand. The term is often applied to the shift from fatty acid oxidation in the postabsorptive state towards increased glucose oxidation after insulin stimulation, but its broader meaning includes responses to fasting, lipid availability, physical activity and other physiological challenges. Flexibility is a property of a response under stated conditions. It is not the numerical value of a resting respiratory quotient, nor is it synonymous with the amount of fat oxidised per minute. A person who oxidises substantial fat at rest can still respond poorly to a glucose load; a person who oxidises carbohydrate during vigorous exercise may be responding appropriately.

The distinction between a substrate and a signal is useful. Glucose is a substrate because its carbon can be oxidised or stored. Its concentration also influences pancreatic beta-cell secretion and thus indirectly signals its availability. Fatty acids can supply energy while also participating in regulatory processes. Insulin, by contrast, is primarily a signal at physiological concentrations. Its secretion changes the priorities of different tissues: skeletal muscle increases glucose transport and glycogen synthesis, adipose tissue decreases net release of stored fatty acids, and the liver adjusts glucose production and storage. These events occur together, so a change in one tissue alters the substrates reaching another.

Energy homeostasis is not the constancy of every metabolic variable. ATP production, glucose uptake, plasma insulin, fatty acid turnover and oxygen consumption all vary through the day. Homeostasis means that coordinated responses keep important internal variables within workable ranges despite perturbations. After a meal, a temporary increase in blood glucose is expected. A regulated response brings the concentration back toward its baseline without exhausting the organism's reserve capacity. The temporary deviations and their recovery contain valuable information about how the system operates.

A concentration is a quantity per volume; a flux is the amount entering or leaving a compartment per unit time. Imagine a circulating glucose pool with appearance from food and endogenous glucose production, and disappearance through uptake and urinary loss if present. A pool can maintain a nearly constant concentration while glucose appearance and disappearance both increase. This is similar to a reservoir whose level remains stable while inflow and outflow increase equally, although unlike a rigid reservoir the body can change distribution volume and partition solute among tissues. An unchanged glucose concentration does not demonstrate unchanged metabolism.

The history of fuel selection illustrates why it is necessary to measure dynamic responses. Early studies established that carbohydrate and lipid oxidation can influence one another. Kelley and Mandarino's 2000 synthesis of human muscle investigations emphasized the importance of comparing the fasting and insulin-stimulated conditions rather than extrapolating entirely from resting fuel selection. Subsequent work highlighted the contribution of glucose delivery and uptake to the measured change in substrate oxidation. A modern interpretation distinguishes the capability of the oxidation machinery from the physiological pathways that deliver the substrate to that machinery.

2. Fuel selection, supply and cellular demand

A fuel molecule cannot contribute to ATP production merely by being present in the bloodstream. It must reach a tissue, cross an appropriate membrane, undergo initial reactions and, for complete aerobic oxidation, deliver reducing equivalents to the mitochondrial respiratory system. Glucose enters muscle mainly through glucose transporters, is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate and can enter glycogen synthesis or glycolysis. Fatty acids are taken up through processes involving transport proteins and membrane partitioning, activated to fatty acyl-CoA and either esterified into lipid or oxidised. Long-chain fatty acyl groups generally require the carnitine shuttle to reach the mitochondrial matrix.

The first major branch point for glucose is therefore between immediate breakdown and storage. Glucose transport and hexokinase action do not determine that all incoming glucose will be oxidised. Glycolytic carbon may leave muscle as lactate, especially when glycolytic flux exceeds mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation. Lactate is not simply a waste product; it can be oxidised by other cells or serve as a gluconeogenic precursor. The liver may therefore encounter carbohydrate that has passed through muscle without being completely oxidised there. In an examination, distinguish glucose disappearance from complete oxidation.

Fatty acid availability depends partly on adipose release, dietary lipid delivery and local lipoprotein processing. The rate at which long-chain acyl groups enter mitochondria is affected by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1). Malonyl-CoA, an intermediate formed through acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), inhibits CPT1. The extent and physiological significance of this inhibition vary with tissue and isoform. In a cell attempting to synthesise fatty acids, limiting their simultaneous mitochondrial entry helps avoid opposing fluxes, whereas in an energy-demanding cell reduced ACC activity may favour oxidation.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) links glycolysis with mitochondrial acetyl-CoA production. Its activity is regulated partly by reversible phosphorylation: PDH kinases inhibit the complex, whereas PDH phosphatases remove inhibitory phosphate. The redox and acetyl-CoA status of the mitochondrial matrix influence control of this branch. When fatty acid oxidation is abundant, increased acetyl-CoA and NADH can favour reduced PDH activity. Under suitable conditions this contributes to reduced carbohydrate oxidation, helping coordinate the utilisation of substrates rather than making every available fuel enter the citric acid cycle at its maximum rate.

The glucose–fatty acid cycle, often called the Randle cycle, describes reciprocal effects of substrate oxidation. It is a valuable framework but not a complete theory of insulin resistance. Changes in membrane transport, hormone signalling, lipid intermediates, blood flow and fuel delivery can precede or accompany metabolic competition. In an isolated muscle supplied with abundant fatty acids, inhibition of carbohydrate oxidation may occur even if insulin-dependent transport is intact. In a different preparation, impaired glucose transport can limit carbohydrate oxidation before pyruvate reaches PDH. Identical downstream observations therefore need not have identical upstream causes.

The desired response depends on time scale. Allosteric regulation and substrate delivery can alter metabolic flux within seconds to minutes. Reversible phosphorylation can adjust enzyme activities rapidly. Altered gene expression and mitochondrial protein abundance generally develop over longer intervals. A single meal does not instantly increase the number of mitochondria in muscle, and a few seconds of exercise cannot explain its increased oxygen use through newly synthesised oxidative enzymes. A student should first identify the timing of a response, then determine which regulatory processes can plausibly account for it.

3. Insulin sensitivity and tissue-specific responses

Insulin sensitivity refers to how strongly a biological response changes at a given insulin concentration or across an insulin dose-response relationship. A highly insulin-sensitive muscle increases glucose disposal at relatively modest hormone concentrations. A less sensitive muscle may require more insulin for an equivalent response. Sensitivity and maximal responsiveness are related but distinct: a rightward shift in a dose-response curve increases the dose needed for a specified effect; a reduced plateau indicates a smaller maximal effect under the tested conditions. Both abnormalities can coexist.

The insulin receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase. Binding of insulin activates its kinase activity and promotes phosphorylation of intracellular receptor substrates. Recruitment of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) leads to the production of PIP3 in the plasma membrane. This lipid signal facilitates activation of Akt through upstream kinases. In skeletal muscle and adipose cells, Akt influences the Rab GTPase regulator TBC1D4, facilitating movement of GLUT4-containing vesicles to the cell surface. Increased transporter abundance permits more glucose to enter when an appropriate concentration gradient exists. Insulin is not consumed in transporting each glucose molecule and GLUT4 is not an ATP-driven pump.

In muscle, Akt also influences glycogen synthesis through several targets, including inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3. The resulting regulatory environment promotes glycogen storage when sufficient glucose is available. Consequently, an experiment can observe greater glucose disposal without a proportional rise in carbon dioxide production. Tracing labelled glucose into glycogen would distinguish this storage response from a change in complete oxidation. Exercise-induced glucose transport can involve partially distinct signalling mechanisms, a distinction developed later in this chapter.

The liver has a different role because it buffers systemic glucose supply. Hepatocytes express glucose transport systems, including GLUT2, whose activity is not controlled by acute GLUT4 vesicle translocation. Insulin regulates hepatic glucose handling through enzyme activity and gene expression, promotes glycogen storage, and decreases endogenous glucose output. Akt-mediated regulation of FoxO1 influences expression of gluconeogenic genes. Yet transcriptional regulation does not explain every acute change in hepatic glucose production. Suppression of glycogen breakdown and changes in gluconeogenic substrate delivery are additional mechanisms.

Adipose tissue responds to insulin by reducing lipolysis and favouring lipid storage under suitable nutrient conditions. In part, insulin signalling increases phosphodiesterase activity, lowering cyclic AMP and restraining protein kinase A-dependent stimulation of lipolytic machinery. The net release of fatty acids and glycerol falls. The consequences extend beyond adipose tissue: the liver receives fewer gluconeogenic substrates and a changed fatty acid supply, and skeletal muscle experiences different extracellular fuel competition. Thus an insulin signal confined to one organ can produce secondary metabolic changes in other tissues.

Failure to suppress endogenous glucose production and failure to increase glucose uptake are separate phenotypes. Consider two animals that require equal external glucose infusion during an insulin challenge. One animal might have lower muscle glucose uptake but also lower endogenous glucose production than the other. Equal external infusion cannot identify which tissue responds abnormally without additional measurements. This becomes particularly important in a clamp experiment.

Insulin secretion and clearance also influence measured plasma hormone concentrations. An individual with reduced insulin sensitivity may maintain near-baseline glucose by secreting more insulin. Conversely, lower insulin during fasting could represent improved sensitivity or inadequate secretion, depending on the glucose response. Neither interpretation follows from insulin concentration alone. Evaluation of metabolic health must therefore compare complementary measures rather than impose a universal meaning on one circulating concentration.

4. Mitochondrial capacity and metabolic flexibility

Mitochondria convert chemical energy into a proton-motive force that can drive ATP synthesis. NADH and FAD-linked electron-transfer pathways provide electrons to the respiratory chain, oxygen serves as the terminal electron acceptor, and the inner membrane restricts uncontrolled proton movement. ATP synthase harnesses the electrochemical gradient. This machinery allows carbohydrate-derived and lipid-derived reducing equivalents to converge on common downstream processes even though their initial routes differ. Mitochondrial flexibility includes the ability to accommodate different substrate inputs, ADP demand and changing redox conditions.

Oxidative capacity means the rate that oxidative processes could support under specified conditions, whereas actual oxidation flux is the rate occurring at the moment of measurement. A trained muscle at rest may possess substantial oxidative capacity but consume relatively little oxygen because ATP demand is low. Increasing the rate of contraction raises demand for ATP hydrolysis and can recruit existing respiratory reserve. Therefore a low resting oxygen consumption is not equivalent to defective mitochondria. Conversely a high oxygen consumption may reflect high ATP demand, inefficient coupling, or a combination of causes.

Respiratory measurements can be expressed per tissue mass, per cell or relative to an indicator of mitochondrial abundance. If two muscle samples differ in mitochondrial content, a lower rate per gram of tissue does not prove each mitochondrion is defective. Comparing rates after normalisation to an appropriate mitochondrial marker can help separate quantity from intrinsic performance, although markers have their own limitations. Citrate synthase activity is often used as a proxy for mitochondrial content, not as a direct count of every mitochondrion.

A useful conceptual experiment compares oxygen consumption under controlled substrate conditions before and after adding ADP. The increase after ADP addition reports the ability to accelerate oxidative phosphorylation when phosphorylation demand is available. An uncoupler can dissipate the proton gradient and, within experimental limits, reveal high electron-transfer capacity without requiring ATP synthesis to match the oxygen flux. ATP output and oxygen uptake are thus not interchangeable. Measuring one without the other does not completely establish energy-conversion efficiency.

Mitochondrial impairment may limit substrate oxidation or promote accumulation of metabolic intermediates in some settings. Yet mitochondrial alterations and insulin resistance show complex relationships: differences in activity, training history, tissue composition and substrate oversupply can affect both. The presence of a correlation does not determine whether mitochondrial dysfunction caused impaired insulin signalling, arose downstream of reduced activity or reflects a shared antecedent. Demonstrating a causal role requires a perturbation with informative controls, such as altering a specific mitochondrial process and measuring the predicted effect on fuel handling.

Lipid droplets inside muscle are not necessarily evidence of poor metabolic health. Endurance-trained muscle can contain substantial intramyocellular triglyceride while retaining high oxidative capacity and insulin sensitivity. This apparent athlete's paradox emphasizes that triglyceride storage, turnover, subcellular organisation and signalling-active lipid intermediates are different variables. Total lipid content may reveal the size of a storage pool but not the rate of entry and exit or the effect on insulin signalling. A storage pool can buffer substrate availability when turnover and oxidative demand are appropriately coordinated.

5. AMPK: detecting an energy challenge

Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) responds to cellular energy status and other regulatory inputs. It consists of a catalytic alpha subunit and regulatory beta and gamma subunits. The gamma subunit binds adenine nucleotides. Changes in AMP and ADP relative to ATP influence the phosphorylation state and activity of the kinase. The crucial activating phosphorylation on the catalytic subunit is commonly described at Thr172. Upstream kinases include LKB1 and, in some contexts, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase 2. The latter provides a route through which calcium signals can influence AMPK.

The adenylate kinase reaction, 2 ADP reversibly yielding ATP plus AMP, explains why AMP may rise disproportionately when ATP utilisation increases. Suppose the cell begins with a large ATP pool and small AMP pool. Conversion of a modest amount of ATP into ADP can promote formation of AMP, representing a large proportional change in the previously small AMP pool. At fixed equilibrium conditions, the AMP-to-ATP ratio scales approximately with the square of the ADP-to-ATP ratio. This relationship gives cells a sensitive indicator of changing energy balance without requiring ATP to fall catastrophically.

Activated AMPK generally promotes processes that help restore ATP supply and limits selected ATP-consuming biosynthetic activities. Phosphorylation of ACC can decrease malonyl-CoA production, potentially relieving CPT1 inhibition and favouring fatty acid oxidation. AMPK can also participate in glucose uptake responses during muscle contraction, although multiple signalling routes contribute. This is a regulatory response rather than a guarantee that fat oxidation increases in every experimental condition: substrate availability, oxygen delivery and downstream enzyme capacity remain relevant.

AMPK also influences cellular maintenance, including autophagy and longer-term mitochondrial adaptations. Under energy limitation it can promote aspects of autophagy by regulating initiation proteins and suppressing growth-promoting pathways. Autophagy recycles cellular components and can contribute to quality control. The net metabolic effect depends on whether the tissue has sufficient lysosomal function and whether the response is acute or prolonged. A marker of autophagosome abundance alone cannot distinguish increased formation from impaired degradation; flux measurements are needed to assess completion of the pathway.

6. mTORC1 and the coordination of biosynthesis

The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a kinase found in two major complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2. They have different components and regulatory roles. mTORC1 integrates growth-factor, amino acid and energy information to regulate protein synthesis and cellular growth. mTORC2 has roles including regulation of Akt and aspects of cytoskeletal organisation. Using the unqualified word mTOR in an examination can obscure which complex a question concerns.

Amino acid sufficiency influences Rag GTPases and recruitment of mTORC1 to the lysosomal surface. Growth-factor signals can regulate the small GTPase Rheb through the TSC complex. When conditions favour the active GTP-bound form of Rheb and mTORC1 is positioned appropriately, mTORC1 activity increases. This arrangement provides complementary information: availability of building material and hormonal signals that favour growth. Neither signal is a complete substitute for every other regulatory input.

mTORC1 promotes translation through substrates including S6 kinase and 4E-BP1, which influence the translational machinery. It also restrains aspects of autophagy in nutrient-rich states. AMPK can antagonise mTORC1 through regulation of TSC2 and Raptor, limiting energy-expensive growth programmes during energy stress. These opposing tendencies make physiological sense: a contracting cell with a transient ATP supply challenge has different priorities from a recovering cell with sufficient nutrients.

AMPK and mTORC1 are not simply binary switches whose activities can never overlap. Different cellular locations, upstream inputs and measurement times can reveal partial coexistence of their signals. During exercise and recovery, metabolic stress, mechanical loading, amino acids and hormonal cues change over time. It would be incorrect to infer that activation of AMPK makes all protein synthesis impossible or that every increase in mTORC1 activity is harmful. Both pathways perform essential physiological functions.

7. Circadian organisation of metabolic responses

A circadian rhythm is an endogenous oscillation with a period close to twenty-four hours. Light–dark cycles provide powerful environmental timing cues, and feeding, activity and hormonal rhythms can affect peripheral tissue clocks. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus receives information about environmental light and coordinates daily physiological rhythms through neural, endocrine and behavioural routes. Liver, muscle and adipose cells also contain molecular clocks.

The core molecular oscillator involves transcriptional and translational feedback. CLOCK and BMAL1 promote expression of genes including Period and Cryptochrome. PER and CRY proteins subsequently participate in suppression of their own transcriptional activation. Changes in protein abundance, stability and cellular location introduce a delay, allowing oscillation rather than immediate cancellation. Additional feedback loops help regulate oscillator robustness. This molecular framework connects metabolic timing to the gene regulation principles taught earlier.

Circadian control permits tissues to anticipate recurring changes in feeding and activity. The liver can vary expression of metabolic enzymes across the day; pancreatic hormone responses and skeletal muscle fuel handling can also vary with biological phase. A rhythm measured under ordinary light–dark conditions includes both endogenous timing and direct effects of behaviour. To distinguish these contributions, experiments may control feeding, activity, sleep and external cues, or measure persistent rhythms under constant conditions.

Damiola and colleagues investigated feeding-time effects on mammalian clocks using mice. Restricting food availability to a different phase of the light–dark cycle shifted the timing of rhythmic expression in peripheral tissues while the central SCN oscillator remained much less affected. This supports the idea that feeding is an important timing signal for peripheral clocks. It does not establish that all metabolic responses to a shifted meal are clock-mediated, nor does a result in nocturnal mice establish an optimal meal time for humans.

8. Exercise and adaptive reserve

During muscle contraction, ATP is consumed by myosin ATPase, calcium handling and ion transport. Phosphocreatine buffers early ATP demand, glycolysis can accelerate quickly, and oxidative phosphorylation increases as oxygen delivery and mitochondrial metabolism adjust. The relative contribution of carbohydrate and fatty acid oxidation depends on intensity, duration, feeding state and training. During intense work, carbohydrate can supply ATP at a rapid rate even when substantial fat stores remain.

Contraction stimulates glucose uptake through mechanisms that overlap only partly with insulin signalling. Mechanical, calcium-dependent and energy-sensitive signals influence GLUT4 traffic. AMPK and other regulators can participate, with relative importance depending on the model and contraction pattern. Thus insulin-resistant muscle may still increase glucose uptake in response to contraction. This is not proof that all insulin signalling has become normal; it demonstrates an alternative route to a related transport outcome.

After exercise, glycogen depletion and changes in signalling can promote glucose disposal and glycogen restoration. With repeated training, adaptations can include altered mitochondrial enzyme abundance, capillary supply, transport capacity and substrate handling. These longer-term changes differ from the acute response to a single bout. A comparison performed immediately after exercise and one performed after a recovery interval need not give the same estimate of insulin sensitivity. Experimental reports should identify this timing.

9. Measuring fuel selection: RQ and RER

The respiratory quotient (RQ) is the ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed in a defined oxidative process. Complete oxidation of one mole of glucose consumes six moles of oxygen and produces six moles of carbon dioxide: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O. Its RQ is therefore one. Complete oxidation of palmitic acid consumes twenty-three moles of oxygen and produces sixteen moles of carbon dioxide: C16H32O2 + 23 O2 → 16 CO2 + 16 H2O. The resulting RQ is 16/23, approximately 0.696. Fat is more reduced than carbohydrate and requires more oxygen relative to the carbon dioxide produced.

At the whole-body level, the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) is calculated from carbon dioxide output and oxygen uptake measured in respiratory gases. Under stable conditions these gas exchanges can approximate metabolic gas production and consumption. If oxygen uptake is 0.30 litres per minute and carbon dioxide output is 0.24 litres per minute, the RER is 0.24/0.30 = 0.80. Under simplified assumptions this suggests a mixture of carbohydrate and fat oxidation. It does not identify which tissues consumed the fuels.

If the body uses only carbohydrate and fat, with their quotients approximated by 1.00 and 0.70, the fraction of oxygen consumption associated with carbohydrate oxidation is (RER − 0.70)/0.30. An RER of 0.85 gives 0.50, or half the oxygen consumption assigned to carbohydrate in this simplified two-fuel model. This is not automatically half the mass or half the energy derived from carbohydrate because oxygen requirements per gram and energy equivalents differ. Protein oxidation and nonoxidative processes can complicate the calculation.

During rapid changes in ventilation, body carbon dioxide stores can change. Intense exercise can produce acid–base disturbances accompanied by bicarbonate buffering and additional carbon dioxide elimination. RER can therefore rise above one without requiring an impossible cellular glucose oxidation quotient. Lipogenesis and other metabolic transformations can also complicate gas-exchange interpretation. It is essential to identify steady-state assumptions before using a respiratory ratio as an estimate of substrate oxidation.

A metabolic flexibility index often uses stimulated RER minus fasting RER. In an illustrative example, one subject changes from 0.76 to 0.94, giving a difference of 0.18, whereas another changes from 0.83 to 0.89, giving 0.06. These differences are descriptive rather than diagnostic. The initial RER, achieved insulin concentration, glucose disposal, fatty acid suppression and measurement conditions all affect the interpretation.

10. Glucose tolerance and surrogate indicators

A glucose tolerance test observes the response to a standardised glucose challenge. The blood glucose trajectory reflects absorption, endogenous production, insulin secretion, insulin clearance, tissue glucose uptake and other processes. Oral administration also engages intestinal hormones and gastric emptying, so the response is not equivalent to intravenous administration. A high glucose excursion might indicate slow clearance, excessive endogenous appearance, delayed insulin release or a combination. The curve alone cannot identify a molecular lesion.

For a curve with values of 5, 8, 7 and 5 mmol/L at 0, 30, 60 and 120 minutes, approximate total area under the curve using the trapezoid rule. The first interval contributes (5+8)/2 × 30 = 195 mmol·min/L. The second contributes 225 and the third contributes (7+5)/2 × 60 = 360. The total is 780 mmol·min/L. The baseline rectangle at 5 mmol/L over 120 minutes contributes 600, leaving an incremental area of 180 mmol·min/L in this example. Different sampling schedules and baseline conventions affect the comparison.

Fasting glucose and insulin can be combined into the original approximate HOMA-IR expression, fasting glucose in mmol/L multiplied by fasting insulin in micro-units/mL and divided by 22.5. If glucose is 5 mmol/L and insulin is 9 micro-units/mL, the index is 2.0. This is an estimate based on fasting feedback relationships, not a direct measure of a specific tissue's insulin response. The updated computer model HOMA2 differs from the simple original formula. Insulin assay variation, secretion capacity and population context matter; universal cutoffs should not be inferred from a single numerical example.

Circulating triglycerides and lipoprotein-associated cholesterol provide complementary information about lipid handling. Concentrations reflect production, transport and clearance. A higher triglyceride concentration is not direct evidence that tissue fatty acid oxidation is low: impaired lipoprotein clearance or increased secretion could also contribute. Detailed lipid transport pathology belongs to 7.L.08; here these markers illustrate the need to assess multiple physiological dimensions.

11. Hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp and flux interpretation

The glucose clamp introduced by DeFronzo, Tobin and Andres provides an experimental way to investigate insulin action while controlling glucose concentration. During a hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp, insulin is infused to elevate circulating insulin and glucose is supplied at a variable rate to maintain a selected near-normal glucose level. A feedback loop uses repeated glucose measurements to adjust the infusion. Under comparable steady-state conditions, the external glucose requirement provides information about the organism's response to insulin.

Glucose conservation is the central mathematical principle. In a stable glucose pool with negligible urinary loss, exogenous glucose infusion plus endogenous glucose appearance equals glucose disappearance. In symbols, GIR + EGP = Rd. If the infusion rate is 5 mg/kg/min and endogenous production is 1 mg/kg/min, disappearance is 6 mg/kg/min. A second subject with GIR 5 and EGP 0 has disappearance 5 mg/kg/min. Equal GIR does not imply equal disappearance unless endogenous production is equally suppressed.

A labelled glucose tracer can help estimate rates of appearance and disappearance. Its dilution by unlabelled glucose contains information about entry into the accessible glucose pool. The method requires attention to tracer enrichment, infusion changes, mixing and whether the system is at steady state. Endogenous glucose production is often attributed mainly to liver under relevant conditions, but kidney glucose production can contribute. A systemic tracer does not identify organ-specific production without additional design or assumptions.

At sufficiently high insulin exposure, endogenous production may be strongly suppressed in insulin-sensitive individuals, so glucose infusion can approximate whole-body disposal. The approximation is less reliable when endogenous production persists. Insulin doses, achieved insulin concentrations, body-mass normalisation and duration should be comparable. Expressing a rate relative to total body mass versus lean mass can change the apparent group difference, particularly when body composition varies.

12. Assessing metabolic health without a single universal score

Metabolic health is a multidimensional physiological concept encompassing appropriate energy handling, glucose regulation, lipid transport, tissue insulin responses and the capacity to adapt to challenges. There is no single universally accepted definition that captures every aspect. A stable fasting measurement may coexist with an abnormal challenge response; good glucose control may rely on compensatory insulin secretion; a high mitochondrial capacity may coexist with restricted glucose delivery.

When interpreting evidence, specify the stimulus, measured variable, baseline, response time and comparison group. If a subject exhibits a reduced RER change during insulin stimulation, first ask whether glucose uptake was comparable, whether fatty acid suppression differed and whether gas exchange was measured at steady state. Galgani and colleagues reported that the apparent group difference in glucose-stimulated fuel switching could be accounted for by glucose disposal in their analysis. This illustrates why an observed defect in a whole-body proxy does not automatically establish an intrinsic defect in mitochondrial oxidation.

The study of metabolic flexibility leads naturally to the next chapter on insulin resistance and diabetes. The transition is from successful coordination and methods of assessment to mechanisms that disrupt this coordination. Maintaining this distinction prevents disease associations from being mistaken for universal features of normal physiology.
