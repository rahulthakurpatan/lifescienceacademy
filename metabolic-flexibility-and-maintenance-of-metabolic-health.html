1. Why metabolic flexibility matters

A resting skeletal muscle, an exercising skeletal muscle and a muscle recovering from a meal belong to the same animal, but they do not face the same metabolic problem. At rest after an overnight fast, fatty acids released from adipose stores can supply much of the oxidative fuel. Soon after a carbohydrate-containing meal, circulating glucose becomes more abundant and insulin encourages its removal from the circulation. During a sprint, the speed at which ATP must be regenerated increases dramatically. During recovery, the cell must replenish fuel stores and repair cellular structures. A useful metabolic system therefore has to alter the rate and direction of fuel movement rather than commit permanently to one substrate.

Metabolic flexibility describes this ability to adjust substrate use to changing supply and demand. The term is often applied to the shift from fatty acid oxidation in the postabsorptive state towards increased glucose oxidation after insulin stimulation, but its broader meaning includes responses to fasting, lipid availability, physical activity and other physiological challenges. Flexibility is a property of a response under stated conditions. It is not the numerical value of a resting respiratory quotient, nor is it synonymous with the amount of fat oxidised per minute. A person who oxidises substantial fat at rest can still respond poorly to a glucose load; a person who oxidises carbohydrate during vigorous exercise may be responding appropriately.

The distinction between a substrate and a signal is useful. Glucose is a substrate because its carbon can be oxidised or stored. Its concentration also influences pancreatic beta-cell secretion and thus indirectly signals its availability. Fatty acids can supply energy while also participating in regulatory processes. Insulin, by contrast, is primarily a signal at physiological concentrations. Its secretion changes the priorities of different tissues: skeletal muscle increases glucose transport and glycogen synthesis, adipose tissue decreases net release of stored fatty acids, and the liver adjusts glucose production and storage. These events occur together, so a change in one tissue alters the substrates reaching another.

Energy homeostasis is not the constancy of every metabolic variable. ATP production, glucose uptake, plasma insulin, fatty acid turnover and oxygen consumption all vary through the day. Homeostasis means that coordinated responses keep important internal variables within workable ranges despite perturbations. After a meal, a temporary increase in blood glucose is expected. A regulated response brings the concentration back toward its baseline without exhausting the organism's reserve capacity. The temporary deviations and their recovery contain valuable information about how the system operates.

A concentration is a quantity per volume; a flux is the amount entering or leaving a compartment per unit time. Imagine a circulating glucose pool with appearance from food and endogenous glucose production, and disappearance through uptake and urinary loss if present. A pool can maintain a nearly constant concentration while glucose appearance and disappearance both increase. This is similar to a reservoir whose level remains stable while inflow and outflow increase equally, although unlike a rigid reservoir the body can change distribution volume and partition solute among tissues. An unchanged glucose concentration does not demonstrate unchanged metabolism.

The history of fuel selection illustrates why it is necessary to measure dynamic responses. Early studies established that carbohydrate and lipid oxidation can influence one another. Kelley and Mandarino's 2000 synthesis of human muscle investigations emphasized the importance of comparing the fasting and insulin-stimulated conditions rather than extrapolating entirely from resting fuel selection. Subsequent work highlighted the contribution of glucose delivery and uptake to the measured change in substrate oxidation. A modern interpretation distinguishes the capability of the oxidation machinery from the physiological pathways that deliver the substrate to that machinery.

A useful way to identify flexibility is to separate three questions. First, is the organism able to detect a change in supply or demand? Second, can its organs redistribute substrates so that they reach an appropriate destination? Third, can the recipient cells alter oxidation, storage or release? These stages can fail separately. For example, if beta-cell insulin secretion is insufficient, a muscle with an intact insulin receptor may not experience the signal needed to increase glucose uptake after a meal. If hormone release is normal but muscle glucose transport responds poorly, the signal is present but its cellular effect is limited. If glucose transport increases normally but mitochondrial oxidation remains restricted, the defect lies farther downstream. Measurements must be chosen to distinguish these levels.

The fuel flexibility of an organism is constrained by the characteristics of its constituent tissues. Mature mammalian erythrocytes lack mitochondria and rely on glycolytic ATP production. They cannot switch to mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation when glucose becomes scarce. The adult brain generally uses substantial glucose under ordinary nutritional conditions and can use circulating ketone bodies during sustained fasting, but most long-chain fatty acids do not become its principal oxidative fuel. The heart, by contrast, can oxidise a variety of substrates, including fatty acids, carbohydrate-derived substrates and ketones. The flexibility of the whole animal therefore depends on specialisation and cooperation rather than requiring identical capabilities in all cells.

This specialisation resolves a seeming conflict. A physiological response that decreases glucose use in one organ can help preserve glucose for another organ. In fasting, liver glucose output and adipose fatty acid release support continued energy production despite little intestinal nutrient input. Some muscle and other peripheral tissues increase their reliance on lipid-derived energy, leaving more glucose available for tissues with stronger glucose requirements. The physiological goal is not to maximise the glucose uptake or fat oxidation of each organ independently, but to coordinate the whole system. When analysing a question, consider what the changed response means for both the receiving tissue and competing tissues.

A challenge can be characterised by its magnitude, duration and rate of onset. A small carbohydrate meal increases nutrient supply without immediately imposing a large mechanical workload. A sudden intense contraction increases ATP use almost instantly, before endocrine and circulatory responses have reached new steady levels. A prolonged fast progressively changes the availability of glycogen-derived glucose, fatty acids and ketone bodies. These challenges cannot be assessed by the same single time point or by demanding the same direction of change in RER. A flexible response to one perturbation need not predict the magnitude of a response to an entirely different perturbation.

The distinction between flexibility and metabolic stability becomes particularly clear when a perturbation ends. After a meal, continued maximal glucose disposal in the absence of further glucose entry would threaten glucose availability; after exercise, sustaining the full contraction-related metabolic rate would waste stored energy. A regulated system needs to terminate or modify its response as well as initiate it. Return towards the appropriate baseline is sometimes described as recovery or resilience. It is related to flexibility but is not identical to the capacity to select a fuel. A test that measures only peak substrate oxidation provides little evidence about the speed of recovery.

A comparison of metabolic flexibility between two individuals is interpretable only if the challenge is defined. Consider a study comparing overnight fasting with a controlled carbohydrate meal. The amount and composition of the meal, prior diet, duration of fasting, recent activity and timing of measurements influence the result. If one participant has recently exhausted muscle glycogen and another has not, the same meal may lead to different partitioning of glucose between oxidation and storage. A smaller rise in carbohydrate oxidation could coexist with greater glycogen synthesis. The result cannot be reduced to a simple judgement that a larger change is always better.

Flexibility can also refer to adjusting substrate use when fat availability increases rather than when insulin rises. For example, during a period of lower carbohydrate availability, an increase in lipid-derived fuel use can conserve glucose. Adaptation may require both rapid changes in existing enzyme activity and longer-term adjustments in enzyme abundance. A test performed after only a few minutes of altered substrate supply asks whether rapid adjustment is possible; one performed after several days also includes adaptation to the preceding diet. Neither response is an invariant property independent of the measurement history.

The direction of a metabolic response should be judged relative to the physiological goal. During a carbohydrate challenge, appropriate uptake and storage can limit the rise in circulating glucose. During fasting, restraining unnecessary glucose consumption in some peripheral tissues supports organs with obligatory or important glucose needs. During sustained exercise, the body must increase absolute energy production, even if its relative mixture of fuels changes little. During recovery, replenishment of glycogen can be a major priority. These goals explain why an index derived from one stimulus cannot universally characterise every aspect of flexibility.

2. Fuel selection, supply and cellular demand

A fuel molecule cannot contribute to ATP production merely by being present in the bloodstream. It must reach a tissue, cross an appropriate membrane, undergo initial reactions and, for complete aerobic oxidation, deliver reducing equivalents to the mitochondrial respiratory system. Glucose enters muscle mainly through glucose transporters, is phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate and can enter glycogen synthesis or glycolysis. Fatty acids are taken up through processes involving transport proteins and membrane partitioning, activated to fatty acyl-CoA and either esterified into lipid or oxidised. Long-chain fatty acyl groups generally require the carnitine shuttle to reach the mitochondrial matrix.

The first major branch point for glucose is therefore between immediate breakdown and storage. Glucose transport and hexokinase action do not determine that all incoming glucose will be oxidised. Glycolytic carbon may leave muscle as lactate, especially when glycolytic flux exceeds mitochondrial pyruvate oxidation. Lactate is not simply a waste product; it can be oxidised by other cells or serve as a gluconeogenic precursor. The liver may therefore encounter carbohydrate that has passed through muscle without being completely oxidised there. In an examination, distinguish glucose disappearance from complete oxidation.

Fatty acid availability depends partly on adipose release, dietary lipid delivery and local lipoprotein processing. The rate at which long-chain acyl groups enter mitochondria is affected by carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1). Malonyl-CoA, an intermediate formed through acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), inhibits CPT1. The extent and physiological significance of this inhibition vary with tissue and isoform. In a cell attempting to synthesise fatty acids, limiting their simultaneous mitochondrial entry helps avoid opposing fluxes, whereas in an energy-demanding cell reduced ACC activity may favour oxidation.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) links glycolysis with mitochondrial acetyl-CoA production. Its activity is regulated partly by reversible phosphorylation: PDH kinases inhibit the complex, whereas PDH phosphatases remove inhibitory phosphate. The redox and acetyl-CoA status of the mitochondrial matrix influence control of this branch. When fatty acid oxidation is abundant, increased acetyl-CoA and NADH can favour reduced PDH activity. Under suitable conditions this contributes to reduced carbohydrate oxidation, helping coordinate the utilisation of substrates rather than making every available fuel enter the citric acid cycle at its maximum rate.

The glucose–fatty acid cycle, often called the Randle cycle, describes reciprocal effects of substrate oxidation. It is a valuable framework but not a complete theory of insulin resistance. Changes in membrane transport, hormone signalling, lipid intermediates, blood flow and fuel delivery can precede or accompany metabolic competition. In an isolated muscle supplied with abundant fatty acids, inhibition of carbohydrate oxidation may occur even if insulin-dependent transport is intact. In a different preparation, impaired glucose transport can limit carbohydrate oxidation before pyruvate reaches PDH. Identical downstream observations therefore need not have identical upstream causes.

The desired response depends on time scale. Allosteric regulation and substrate delivery can alter metabolic flux within seconds to minutes. Reversible phosphorylation can adjust enzyme activities rapidly. Altered gene expression and mitochondrial protein abundance generally develop over longer intervals. A single meal does not instantly increase the number of mitochondria in muscle, and a few seconds of exercise cannot explain its increased oxygen use through newly synthesised oxidative enzymes. A student should first identify the timing of a response, then determine which regulatory processes can plausibly account for it.

The concentration of a circulating substrate is not the only determinant of how much can enter a tissue. For a simple tissue supplied by arterial blood, net uptake can be estimated from blood flow multiplied by the arterial minus venous concentration difference, provided the sampled vessels adequately represent the tissue and concentration and flow use compatible blood or plasma units. This is an application of conservation of mass. Suppose a muscle bed receives 0.5 L blood per minute and arterial glucose exceeds venous glucose by 0.4 mmol/L. Its net glucose uptake is 0.20 mmol/min. If blood flow doubles while the difference halves, calculated uptake remains the same. A concentration difference cannot be used as a tissue flux without information about flow.

The calculation also illustrates why exercise changes fuel delivery in more than one way. Increased perfusion can expose more tissue to circulating glucose and oxygen, but transporter activity, intracellular phosphorylation and downstream metabolic reactions determine how much of the delivered glucose is extracted. The arterial concentration multiplied by flow gives the delivery rate; the net arteriovenous difference multiplied by flow gives net exchange. A large delivery rate does not imply complete extraction. During exercise, changes in perfusion and transporter recruitment can occur together, making it misleading to interpret an increased uptake rate solely as an increase in transporter expression.

Once glucose enters muscle, phosphorylation by hexokinase lowers the concentration of free intracellular glucose available to leave through facilitative transporters. This helps maintain the conditions supporting uptake while establishing glucose-6-phosphate as a branch point. During recovery, glycogen formation can divert carbon into a polymer rather than through the entire glycolytic pathway. If glycogen stores are substantially filled, different regulatory constraints may become important. Identifying transport, phosphorylation and storage as separate processes allows an investigator to ask which is limiting under a given condition instead of assigning every change in glucose uptake to the receptor.

The regulation of PDH is a particularly good illustration of metabolic control at a branch point. Conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA is effectively irreversible in mammalian metabolism under ordinary physiological conditions. Activating PDH therefore favours entry of glycolytic carbon into oxidative metabolism rather than its retention as pyruvate or conversion to lactate. During contraction, calcium-dependent signals can promote PDH dephosphorylation in muscle. In prolonged fasting, altered expression and activity of PDH kinases can favour preservation of carbohydrate-derived intermediates. The balance of these controls, rather than the presence of a single enzyme, determines effective pathway activity.

The amount of a metabolic enzyme sets one constraint on flux, but activity at a particular moment depends on substrates, products, covalent modification, cellular environment and competition for downstream pathways. A liver with abundant enzymes for lipid oxidation does not necessarily run all of them at their highest rates after a carbohydrate meal. Likewise, the presence of an active glycolytic pathway does not imply that all pyruvate is oxidised. The metabolic flexibility problem is therefore one of regulating rates through a connected network. For examination purposes, when a question reports enzyme abundance alone, avoid treating it as a direct measurement of pathway flux.

In the classic glucose–fatty acid competition model, abundant fatty acid oxidation can raise mitochondrial acetyl-CoA and NADH, affecting PDH regulation. Changes in citrate can also affect phosphofructokinase activity and influence glycolytic intermediates under suitable experimental conditions. These relationships were important in establishing that fuel selection involves biochemical communication between pathways. However, later human studies showed that reduced insulin-stimulated glucose uptake can itself account for much of a smaller rise in carbohydrate oxidation. The useful interpretation is not that the original cycle is wrong everywhere, but that it describes one set of regulatory possibilities whose contribution must be measured in each context.

A distinction between obligatory and facultative metabolic pathways helps explain tissue differences. An obligatory requirement cannot be fully replaced by another substrate under the stated conditions; facultative use can change substantially according to substrate availability and demand. Erythrocytes need glycolysis for ATP because they lack mitochondria, but oxidative skeletal muscle can vary its mixture of carbohydrate-derived and fatty acid-derived acetyl-CoA. This does not imply that muscle can dispense with every carbohydrate-dependent process. Some glucose-derived intermediates contribute to biosynthesis, and the demands of rapid contractions differ from those of sustained low-intensity work.

In addition to the rate of energy generation, the energetic cost of supplying a particular fuel can influence interorgan coordination. Conversion of lactate back to glucose in the liver consumes energy even though subsequent use of that glucose by muscle can regenerate ATP. Oxidation of fatty acids in the liver can support the energetic cost of gluconeogenesis during fasting. This example connects energy expenditure in one tissue with substrate availability in another. The same carbon atoms can circulate between organs repeatedly, so adding measured rates of uptake across tissues without accounting for recycling may overstate the amount of newly supplied carbon.

The partition between fatty acid storage and oxidation is another important control point. A fatty acid taken up by muscle may be esterified into triglyceride rather than immediately oxidised. During later exercise, release from local lipid stores can contribute to oxidative substrate availability. If isotope-labelled fatty acid is administered and later detected in tissue triglyceride, the experiment establishes incorporation into that pool but not its eventual fate. A time course following the label into other products or respiratory carbon dioxide, with suitable correction for isotope recovery, is necessary to investigate turnover. The distinction between the label's location and the rate of metabolic conversion is fundamental.

An integrated interpretation of substrate competition must also account for simultaneous oxidation of multiple fuels. A model drawing glucose oxidation and fatty acid oxidation as mutually exclusive pathways is too restrictive. Both can supply acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents at the same time, and their relative contributions can vary continuously. Even if increasing fatty acid availability decreases glucose oxidation at a particular workload, glucose oxidation may remain substantial. A change in proportion can arise from an increase in one rate, a decrease in the other, or both. Therefore a question reporting only a ratio must not be answered as if it reported the absolute rates.

3. Insulin sensitivity and tissue-specific responses

Insulin sensitivity refers to how strongly a biological response changes at a given insulin concentration or across an insulin dose-response relationship. A highly insulin-sensitive muscle increases glucose disposal at relatively modest hormone concentrations. A less sensitive muscle may require more insulin for an equivalent response. Sensitivity and maximal responsiveness are related but distinct: a rightward shift in a dose-response curve increases the dose needed for a specified effect; a reduced plateau indicates a smaller maximal effect under the tested conditions. Both abnormalities can coexist.

The insulin receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase. Binding of insulin activates its kinase activity and promotes phosphorylation of intracellular receptor substrates. Recruitment of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) leads to the production of PIP3 in the plasma membrane. This lipid signal facilitates activation of Akt through upstream kinases. In skeletal muscle and adipose cells, Akt influences the Rab GTPase regulator TBC1D4, facilitating movement of GLUT4-containing vesicles to the cell surface. Increased transporter abundance permits more glucose to enter when an appropriate concentration gradient exists. Insulin is not consumed in transporting each glucose molecule and GLUT4 is not an ATP-driven pump.

In muscle, Akt also influences glycogen synthesis through several targets, including inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3. The resulting regulatory environment promotes glycogen storage when sufficient glucose is available. Consequently, an experiment can observe greater glucose disposal without a proportional rise in carbon dioxide production. Tracing labelled glucose into glycogen would distinguish this storage response from a change in complete oxidation. Exercise-induced glucose transport can involve partially distinct signalling mechanisms, a distinction developed later in this chapter.

The liver has a different role because it buffers systemic glucose supply. Hepatocytes express glucose transport systems, including GLUT2, whose activity is not controlled by acute GLUT4 vesicle translocation. Insulin regulates hepatic glucose handling through enzyme activity and gene expression, promotes glycogen storage, and decreases endogenous glucose output. Akt-mediated regulation of FoxO1 influences expression of gluconeogenic genes. Yet transcriptional regulation does not explain every acute change in hepatic glucose production. Suppression of glycogen breakdown and changes in gluconeogenic substrate delivery are additional mechanisms.

Adipose tissue responds to insulin by reducing lipolysis and favouring lipid storage under suitable nutrient conditions. In part, insulin signalling increases phosphodiesterase activity, lowering cyclic AMP and restraining protein kinase A-dependent stimulation of lipolytic machinery. The net release of fatty acids and glycerol falls. The consequences extend beyond adipose tissue: the liver receives fewer gluconeogenic substrates and a changed fatty acid supply, and skeletal muscle experiences different extracellular fuel competition. Thus an insulin signal confined to one organ can produce secondary metabolic changes in other tissues.

Failure to suppress endogenous glucose production and failure to increase glucose uptake are separate phenotypes. Consider two animals that require equal external glucose infusion during an insulin challenge. One animal might have lower muscle glucose uptake but also lower endogenous glucose production than the other. Equal external infusion cannot identify which tissue responds abnormally without additional measurements. This becomes particularly important in a clamp experiment.

Insulin secretion and clearance also influence measured plasma hormone concentrations. An individual with reduced insulin sensitivity may maintain near-baseline glucose by secreting more insulin. Conversely, lower insulin during fasting could represent improved sensitivity or inadequate secretion, depending on the glucose response. Neither interpretation follows from insulin concentration alone. Evaluation of metabolic health must therefore compare complementary measures rather than impose a universal meaning on one circulating concentration.

The concept of sensitivity is often confused with a single measured response at a single insulin dose. Consider a hypothetical dose-response experiment measuring muscle glucose uptake at four increasing concentrations of insulin. Tissue A reaches half of its maximal incremental response at 20 arbitrary concentration units, while tissue B requires 80 units; their maximal responses are equal. Tissue B is less sensitive but can achieve the same response at sufficiently high insulin. In a third tissue the half-maximal concentration is similar to A but the plateau is half as high. The third tissue shows reduced maximal responsiveness. An experimental dose-response curve reveals distinctions that a single fasting insulin measurement cannot.

A receptor's abundance is only one factor in such a curve. The insulin receptor may bind hormone and autophosphorylate normally, yet a downstream signalling defect could reduce Akt activation or GLUT4 trafficking. Alternatively, muscle perfusion might limit delivery of glucose despite intact intracellular signalling. Insulin can influence microvascular recruitment, so changes in delivery and intracellular events need not be independent. To locate the limiting step, compare measurements of receptor activation, downstream phosphorylation, surface GLUT4 abundance, glucose uptake and substrate fate rather than interpreting them as equivalent markers.

The use of glucose transporters illustrates the importance of tissue identity. In a skeletal muscle preparation, inhibition of insulin-induced GLUT4 translocation is likely to reduce the stimulated increment in glucose uptake. A similar experiment in hepatocytes cannot be interpreted by assuming that hepatic GLUT2 is replaced by an insulin-dependent GLUT4 system. The hepatocyte normally adjusts net glucose uptake and output substantially through metabolic reactions such as phosphorylation and dephosphorylation of glucose and through regulation of glycogen metabolism. Thus a signalling mutation with an identical effect on Akt activity may produce qualitatively different output phenotypes in muscle and liver.

Hepatic insulin effects include both direct actions on hepatocytes and indirect actions mediated by other tissues. Consider insulin-mediated inhibition of adipose lipolysis. Glycerol released during triacylglycerol breakdown can provide carbon for gluconeogenesis, while fatty acid oxidation can support hepatic energy requirements and increase mitochondrial acetyl-CoA, an allosteric activator of pyruvate carboxylase. Suppressing adipose substrate release can therefore reduce the support for hepatic gluconeogenesis even when insulin's direct hepatic signalling has not changed. To distinguish these pathways experimentally, one can compare hepatic glucose production when fatty acid and glycerol availability are controlled separately from insulin concentration.

The distinction between direct and indirect insulin action is especially important when reasoning from organ-specific genetic manipulations. If a receptor is selectively removed from adipose tissue and hepatic glucose output increases, this does not establish that the receptor was also removed from liver. The liver may be responding to changed circulating substrate and hormone signals. Tissue specificity of the manipulation must be verified; secondary changes in body composition and chronic adaptations must also be considered. A tissue-selective experiment is not necessarily an isolated tissue experiment because intact circulation preserves interorgan communication.

A decrease in circulating fatty acid concentration during insulin stimulation is often interpreted as suppression of adipose lipolysis. This inference can be reasonable but requires caution. Concentration depends on the balance of release, uptake, re-esterification and oxidation. A lower concentration might reflect faster removal as well as lower release. Tracer measurements of fatty acid or glycerol turnover can provide stronger evidence about release rates. Furthermore, glycerol and fatty acids released by lipolysis need not appear in plasma in a fixed three-to-one molar ratio because some fatty acids may be re-esterified within the tissue.

Insulin sensitivity is not fixed over an animal's life or even throughout a day. Recent exercise, prolonged fasting, circadian phase, hormonal state and the nature of the preceding meals influence the response. Some reductions in insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in a fasting context are adaptive and help spare glucose; chronic dysregulation during nutrient abundance has a different physiological meaning. A comparison between groups must match the metabolic state and stimulus. It is not valid to classify one measurement as harmful without considering the context and the regulated variable.

Insulin acts within a wider network of counterregulatory hormones and neural signals. During fasting, lower insulin permits greater adipose lipolysis while glucagon helps sustain hepatic glucose output. During exercise, catecholamine signals and changes in insulin concentrations can contribute to mobilisation of fuels. When glucose availability falls, regulatory systems need to increase supply and restrain unnecessary consumption. A clamp with elevated insulin and maintained glucose deliberately removes much of this ordinary variation to isolate a particular regulatory response. That makes it powerful but also means its results cannot simply be equated with every everyday situation.

One important distinction is insulin-dependent versus insulin-independent glucose use. Basal glucose uptake persists in many tissues without a meal-induced rise in insulin. GLUT1 supports glucose uptake in various cells, and glucose use by the brain relies substantially on transport mechanisms that are not dependent on acute insulin-stimulated GLUT4 recruitment. Skeletal muscle also has basal uptake and contraction-mediated responses. Therefore the statement that insulin is required for glucose to enter every cell is incorrect. The magnitude and mechanism of insulin's effect depend on the tissue, transporter repertoire and physiological state.

The regulation of insulin secretion makes interpretation of circulating glucose more complicated. Pancreatic beta cells respond to nutrient availability and other signals, and the resulting insulin concentration is influenced by secretion and removal. In a simplified feedback model, reduced tissue responsiveness increases the insulin concentration needed to achieve a given glucose disposal rate. As long as secretion can compensate, glucose may remain within a narrow range. A glucose-only measurement can then underestimate the degree of altered hormone demand. Conversely, if secretion fails to increase adequately, glucose rises even when the residual insulin action in target tissues is relatively preserved. This distinction motivates combined measurements of secretion and sensitivity.

A hypothetical tissue-response experiment can separate defects in glucose delivery and signalling. Two groups receive the same insulin infusion and show similar circulating insulin concentrations. In group B, muscle uptake is lower, but an isolated muscle assay performed with matched glucose and insulin concentrations shows no difference from group A. Reduced blood flow, altered microvascular recruitment or some other in vivo factor becomes plausible. If the isolated preparation still shows impaired uptake with reduced surface GLUT4, an intrinsic cellular mechanism receives greater support. Neither result alone identifies every contributing process, but the contrast narrows the plausible explanations.

4. Mitochondrial capacity and metabolic flexibility

Mitochondria convert chemical energy into a proton-motive force that can drive ATP synthesis. NADH and FAD-linked electron-transfer pathways provide electrons to the respiratory chain, oxygen serves as the terminal electron acceptor, and the inner membrane restricts uncontrolled proton movement. ATP synthase harnesses the electrochemical gradient. This machinery allows carbohydrate-derived and lipid-derived reducing equivalents to converge on common downstream processes even though their initial routes differ. Mitochondrial flexibility includes the ability to accommodate different substrate inputs, ADP demand and changing redox conditions.

Oxidative capacity means the rate that oxidative processes could support under specified conditions, whereas actual oxidation flux is the rate occurring at the moment of measurement. A trained muscle at rest may possess substantial oxidative capacity but consume relatively little oxygen because ATP demand is low. Increasing the rate of contraction raises demand for ATP hydrolysis and can recruit existing respiratory reserve. Therefore a low resting oxygen consumption is not equivalent to defective mitochondria. Conversely a high oxygen consumption may reflect high ATP demand, inefficient coupling, or a combination of causes.

Respiratory measurements can be expressed per tissue mass, per cell or relative to an indicator of mitochondrial abundance. If two muscle samples differ in mitochondrial content, a lower rate per gram of tissue does not prove each mitochondrion is defective. Comparing rates after normalisation to an appropriate mitochondrial marker can help separate quantity from intrinsic performance, although markers have their own limitations. Citrate synthase activity is often used as a proxy for mitochondrial content, not as a direct count of every mitochondrion.

A useful conceptual experiment compares oxygen consumption under controlled substrate conditions before and after adding ADP. The increase after ADP addition reports the ability to accelerate oxidative phosphorylation when phosphorylation demand is available. An uncoupler can dissipate the proton gradient and, within experimental limits, reveal high electron-transfer capacity without requiring ATP synthesis to match the oxygen flux. ATP output and oxygen uptake are thus not interchangeable. Measuring one without the other does not completely establish energy-conversion efficiency.

Mitochondrial impairment may limit substrate oxidation or promote accumulation of metabolic intermediates in some settings. Yet mitochondrial alterations and insulin resistance show complex relationships: differences in activity, training history, tissue composition and substrate oversupply can affect both. The presence of a correlation does not determine whether mitochondrial dysfunction caused impaired insulin signalling, arose downstream of reduced activity or reflects a shared antecedent. Demonstrating a causal role requires a perturbation with informative controls, such as altering a specific mitochondrial process and measuring the predicted effect on fuel handling.

Lipid droplets inside muscle are not necessarily evidence of poor metabolic health. Endurance-trained muscle can contain substantial intramyocellular triglyceride while retaining high oxidative capacity and insulin sensitivity. This apparent athlete's paradox emphasizes that triglyceride storage, turnover, subcellular organisation and signalling-active lipid intermediates are different variables. Total lipid content may reveal the size of a storage pool but not the rate of entry and exit or the effect on insulin signalling. A storage pool can buffer substrate availability when turnover and oxidative demand are appropriately coordinated.

Mitochondrial oxidation depends on at least three separable supply systems: fuel-derived reducing equivalents, oxygen delivery and the availability of ADP and inorganic phosphate. A restriction at any of these points may reduce oxygen consumption. In a resting cell with adequate fuel and oxygen, ADP availability can restrain oxidative phosphorylation because ATP utilisation is low. When contraction increases ATP hydrolysis, ADP availability increases and promotes ATP synthesis; the resulting use of the proton gradient can accelerate electron transfer. This feedback between ATP demand and respiratory flux provides short-term control without requiring a change in mitochondrial number.

The inner mitochondrial membrane is essential because it separates the electrical and chemical components of the proton-motive force. Proton movement through ATP synthase couples the return of protons towards the matrix with ATP formation. Proton leak provides an alternative pathway that dissipates some of the gradient without producing ATP. Such leakage is not always a sign of pathology; it contributes to normal energy expenditure, and specialised uncoupling mechanisms are physiologically important in thermogenic tissues. An assay must identify the purpose of the measured respiration before classifying increased oxygen use as improved ATP-producing function.

A hypothetical respirometry experiment can illustrate the difference. Two samples receive identical mitochondrial substrates. Sample A consumes 40 units of oxygen per time under a low-ADP condition and 160 after ADP addition. Sample B consumes 80 and 160 under the same respective conditions. Both reach the same stimulated oxygen consumption, but B exhibits a greater basal rate and smaller fold stimulation. This could be consistent with increased proton leak or other background respiration in B, yet the data do not establish a specific mechanism. Direct ATP measurements, assessment of oxygen consumption after inhibition of ATP synthase, and appropriate background correction would help distinguish possibilities.

An experiment performed with permeabilised fibres differs from an intact-muscle experiment. Permeabilisation allows investigators to control the extracellular access of substrates and ADP to the mitochondrial system while preserving much of the cellular architecture. It reduces some physiological barriers but also removes the normal control of substrate delivery by membrane transport and blood flow. A normal maximal respiratory rate in permeabilised fibres does not prove normal fuel switching in vivo. Conversely, a reduced in vivo oxidation rate does not prove reduced maximal respiratory capacity in an isolated preparation. Comparing both types of experiment helps localise the restriction.

Mitochondrial biogenesis requires coordinated expression of nuclear and mitochondrial genes, synthesis and import of many proteins, assembly of respiratory complexes and maintenance of mitochondrial DNA. Exercise-related transcriptional regulators such as PGC-1alpha can participate in this programme. Increased abundance of a regulator in an experiment is evidence of a signalling response, not direct proof of completed biogenesis. Measurements of mitochondrial content, enzyme activity and functional respiration taken over an appropriate time course provide complementary evidence. This distinction is relevant in questions that compare acute exercise with long-term training.

Cells also continuously remodel and remove damaged components. Mitochondrial dynamics and selective autophagy contribute to quality control, but the direction of an isolated marker change is not universally diagnostic of improved or impaired health. For example, increased abundance of a protein associated with mitochondrial fission does not by itself establish that the organelle population has become dysfunctional. Cellular maintenance involves turnover, and an increased rate of removal can be adaptive when damaged components are replaced. For this chapter, the important principle is that functional capacity reflects both the quantity and condition of the mitochondrial population.

Oxidative metabolism is sometimes evaluated using a ratio of ATP produced to oxygen consumed. Such a ratio depends on the substrates supplying electrons, coupling efficiency and how oxygen consumption is defined. Electrons entering through different parts of the respiratory chain do not support identical proton translocation. A change in respiratory substrate can therefore alter the relationship between ATP output and oxygen consumption even if the mitochondria are structurally intact. Comparing coupling between samples requires matched substrates and assay conditions. A student should not assign one fixed ATP yield to every molecule of oxygen regardless of the pathway.

The electron transport system generates reactive oxygen species as well as supporting oxidative phosphorylation. Reactive oxygen species can participate in signalling and, at excessive levels or when defences are inadequate, can damage cellular components. Their abundance reflects production and removal rather than production alone. Increased oxygen consumption does not necessarily imply proportionately increased oxidative damage, and measuring a marker of oxidation does not identify the exact source. Metabolic health requires appropriate regulation of redox state, antioxidant defences and repair as well as ATP generation.

Different experimental methods address mitochondrial function at different levels. Whole-body oxygen consumption includes contributions from many tissues and varies with body size, workload and thermogenesis. Oxygen consumption measured in intact cultured cells reflects cell-level energy demand and substrate delivery under the culture conditions. Permeabilised fibres allow more direct manipulation of mitochondrial substrates and phosphorylation demand. Isolated organelles can reveal properties under even more controlled conditions but lack much of the cellular environment. A finding at one level should be connected carefully to the next; the experimental reduction of complexity aids mechanistic interpretation but also limits immediate physiological generalisation.

The relationship between lipid supply and mitochondrial capacity is particularly informative in nutrient oversupply. A cell might receive fatty acids more rapidly than it needs to oxidise them for current ATP demand. Increasing oxidative capacity alone does not guarantee that the extra substrate will be oxidised, because oxidative flux remains coupled to demand. Storage and export processes must handle excess carbon, and the resulting distribution among lipid species can influence signalling. In contrast, if the same cell increases energy demand through contraction, an existing oxidative reserve may become useful. The effect of a given mitochondrial phenotype therefore depends partly on substrate delivery and energetic context.

5. AMPK: detecting an energy challenge

Adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) responds to cellular energy status and other regulatory inputs. It consists of a catalytic alpha subunit and regulatory beta and gamma subunits. The gamma subunit binds adenine nucleotides. Changes in AMP and ADP relative to ATP influence the phosphorylation state and activity of the kinase. The crucial activating phosphorylation on the catalytic subunit is commonly described at Thr172. Upstream kinases include LKB1 and, in some contexts, calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase 2. The latter provides a route through which calcium signals can influence AMPK.

The adenylate kinase reaction, 2 ADP reversibly yielding ATP plus AMP, explains why AMP may rise disproportionately when ATP utilisation increases. Suppose the cell begins with a large ATP pool and small AMP pool. Conversion of a modest amount of ATP into ADP can promote formation of AMP, representing a large proportional change in the previously small AMP pool. At fixed equilibrium conditions, the AMP-to-ATP ratio scales approximately with the square of the ADP-to-ATP ratio. This relationship gives cells a sensitive indicator of changing energy balance without requiring ATP to fall catastrophically.

Activated AMPK generally promotes processes that help restore ATP supply and limits selected ATP-consuming biosynthetic activities. Phosphorylation of ACC can decrease malonyl-CoA production, potentially relieving CPT1 inhibition and favouring fatty acid oxidation. AMPK can also participate in glucose uptake responses during muscle contraction, although multiple signalling routes contribute. This is a regulatory response rather than a guarantee that fat oxidation increases in every experimental condition: substrate availability, oxygen delivery and downstream enzyme capacity remain relevant.

AMPK also influences cellular maintenance, including autophagy and longer-term mitochondrial adaptations. Under energy limitation it can promote aspects of autophagy by regulating initiation proteins and suppressing growth-promoting pathways. Autophagy recycles cellular components and can contribute to quality control. The net metabolic effect depends on whether the tissue has sufficient lysosomal function and whether the response is acute or prolonged. A marker of autophagosome abundance alone cannot distinguish increased formation from impaired degradation; flux measurements are needed to assess completion of the pathway.

AMPK activation is often taught using the ATP-to-AMP relationship, but the kinase does not calculate a ratio in the mathematical sense. Binding of nucleotides to regulatory sites on the gamma subunit changes the enzyme's regulatory state. AMP can promote allosteric activation and protect the activating phosphorylation from dephosphorylation; ADP can also contribute to protection, while ATP can compete with the lower-energy nucleotides. The relative importance of these effects depends on the AMPK complex and experimental conditions. A change in phosphorylation therefore provides evidence of regulation but does not report every aspect of catalytic activity.

The adenylate kinase equilibrium relationship can be derived from the equilibrium expression K = [ATP][AMP]/[ADP]², using concentrations as approximate activities in a simplified model. Rearranging gives [AMP]/[ATP] = K([ADP]/[ATP])². For an illustrative calculation take K = 1 and an initial ADP-to-ATP ratio of 0.10. The predicted AMP-to-ATP ratio is 0.01. If the ADP-to-ATP ratio doubles to 0.20, the predicted ratio becomes 0.04, a fourfold increase. This calculation assumes near equilibrium and comparable activity coefficients; actual intracellular concentrations are influenced by magnesium binding and compartmentation. The value of the example is the nonlinear sensitivity, not a universal numerical estimate of nucleotide abundance.

AMPK phosphorylation of ACC provides a mechanistic link between an energy challenge and substrate entry into mitochondria. ACC catalyses formation of malonyl-CoA from acetyl-CoA and bicarbonate using ATP. Inhibition reduces demand for ATP in this biosynthetic reaction and can reduce the local malonyl-CoA signal that limits CPT1. Under suitable conditions, mitochondrial fatty acid entry may increase. But if a tissue has negligible fatty acid availability or inadequate oxygen delivery, the reduction in malonyl-CoA cannot generate a high rate of oxidation by itself. A signalling event enables a response within a larger physiological network.

An informative experimental strategy uses an ACC variant in which a regulatory phosphorylation site cannot be modified. If activation of AMPK still produces other expected cellular responses but fails to suppress ACC activity, this would support a role for that site in the connection between AMPK and lipid metabolism. Appropriate comparisons include the wild-type protein, comparable expression of the variant, equivalent upstream AMPK activation and confirmation that the substitution has not independently disrupted ACC function. The experiment examines necessity of a regulatory event; it does not imply that this site is the only control of fatty acid oxidation.

Energy charge provides a related but distinct measure of adenine nucleotide distribution. A simplified expression is ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP])/([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]). The index weights ATP fully, ADP halfway and AMP zero within the measured adenine nucleotide pool. In a hypothetical sample containing 8 units ATP, 1.5 ADP and 0.5 AMP, energy charge equals (8 + 0.75)/10 = 0.875. In another sample with 6 ATP, 3 ADP and 1 AMP, it equals 7.5/10 = 0.75. AMPK is not a sensor of this computed index directly; it responds to its own nucleotide-binding and phosphorylation environment. The example distinguishes a useful descriptive statistic from a molecular mechanism.

Local conditions matter when applying the energy sensor model to intact tissues. A whole-body fast does not necessarily mean every cell has inadequate ATP. Liver and muscle can maintain ATP through alternative fuel use while circulating glucose and hormone levels change. Similarly, contraction can activate energy-sensitive pathways in muscle even if blood glucose remains stable. The relevant AMPK signal is local cellular energy stress and associated upstream regulation, not a direct measurement of total body energy stores. Hypothalamic AMPK can participate in regulation of feeding, but its role should not be inferred by simply transferring every observation from skeletal muscle.

Suppose a research group measures phosphorylation of AMPK and ACC in muscle before and after an exercise bout. Increased phosphorylation of both is compatible with the proposed upstream-downstream relationship. To test whether AMPK is necessary for the observed ACC phosphorylation, one could compare otherwise matched preparations with reduced AMPK function. To test sufficiency, one could selectively activate AMPK without reproducing the full contraction stimulus and measure the relevant response. Because other enzymes and signalling pathways may affect both readouts, the simple correlation after exercise is not definitive proof of causal order. The logic of necessity and sufficiency is more important here than detailed laboratory protocols.

6. mTORC1 and the coordination of biosynthesis

The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a kinase found in two major complexes, mTORC1 and mTORC2. They have different components and regulatory roles. mTORC1 integrates growth-factor, amino acid and energy information to regulate protein synthesis and cellular growth. mTORC2 has roles including regulation of Akt and aspects of cytoskeletal organisation. Using the unqualified word mTOR in an examination can obscure which complex a question concerns.

Amino acid sufficiency influences Rag GTPases and recruitment of mTORC1 to the lysosomal surface. Growth-factor signals can regulate the small GTPase Rheb through the TSC complex. When conditions favour the active GTP-bound form of Rheb and mTORC1 is positioned appropriately, mTORC1 activity increases. This arrangement provides complementary information: availability of building material and hormonal signals that favour growth. Neither signal is a complete substitute for every other regulatory input.

mTORC1 promotes translation through substrates including S6 kinase and 4E-BP1, which influence the translational machinery. It also restrains aspects of autophagy in nutrient-rich states. AMPK can antagonise mTORC1 through regulation of TSC2 and Raptor, limiting energy-expensive growth programmes during energy stress. These opposing tendencies make physiological sense: a contracting cell with a transient ATP supply challenge has different priorities from a recovering cell with sufficient nutrients.

AMPK and mTORC1 are not simply binary switches whose activities can never overlap. Different cellular locations, upstream inputs and measurement times can reveal partial coexistence of their signals. During exercise and recovery, metabolic stress, mechanical loading, amino acids and hormonal cues change over time. It would be incorrect to infer that activation of AMPK makes all protein synthesis impossible or that every increase in mTORC1 activity is harmful. Both pathways perform essential physiological functions.

Growth and maintenance require amino acids, ATP and other building blocks. The cell therefore benefits from regulating expensive biosynthetic reactions in relation to environmental conditions. mTORC1 activation after feeding can favour protein synthesis and help support tissue repair. During substantial energy deficiency, sustained activation of the same pathways could create additional ATP demand when the cell is unable to maintain its existing functions. AMPK-mediated inhibition of mTORC1 is one route by which energy sensing can restrain growth signals. The interaction is a homeostatic relationship, not an absolute moral distinction between a beneficial kinase and a harmful one.

Amino acid sensing and growth-factor signalling can be separated experimentally. Imagine a cell preparation divided into four groups: neither amino acids nor growth-factor stimulus, amino acids alone, growth factor alone, and both. If the combined treatment produces stronger mTORC1 downstream phosphorylation than either treatment alone, the result is consistent with convergence of inputs. However, measuring a single downstream phosphorylation site does not directly quantify translation rate. Assessing protein synthesis independently and verifying nutrient and growth-factor conditions would strengthen the inference. The four-condition comparison is useful because it tests interaction rather than considering each stimulus in isolation.

The TSC complex acts as a regulator of Rheb's nucleotide state. When its GTPase-activating action is effective, Rheb is shifted away from the GTP-bound state that supports mTORC1 activation. Growth-factor signalling through Akt can restrain TSC activity in appropriate contexts, favouring Rheb-dependent activation. AMPK can instead promote inhibitory regulation through TSC2 and Raptor when energy supply is challenged. The direction of these signals can be predicted from the physiological task: available nutrients and growth factors favour anabolic investment, whereas energy limitation restrains expenditure. Students should trace the sign of each regulatory interaction rather than memorising only the final output.

Long-term strong activation of mTORC1-dependent pathways can contribute to feedback regulation of insulin signalling in some models, including feedback associated with S6 kinase and insulin receptor substrates. This is distinct from the normal transient activation of mTORC1 after feeding. An observed association between chronic pathway activation and insulin resistance does not establish that a physiological postprandial increase is pathological. Similarly, inhibition of one pathway may alter many other cellular processes. A pharmacological result is most informative when accompanied by evidence that the intended target was altered and that the measured effect is not attributable to unrelated toxicity or broader disruption.

Protein synthesis is not controlled only by amino acid delivery or only by mTORC1. Translation also depends on ribosomes, messenger RNA, initiation factors, energy supply and the availability of aminoacylated transfer RNAs. Activation of an mTORC1 substrate can indicate a favourable growth-regulatory signal without proving that the complete protein synthesis machinery has increased output. Conversely, an acute reduction in one phosphorylation marker cannot establish total cessation of translation. A well-designed experiment measures both signalling and the output it is proposed to control.

Autophagy has an analogous measurement problem. Nutrient sufficiency through mTORC1 can inhibit initiation of autophagy, and energy stress through AMPK can promote aspects of its initiation. However, the number of structures observed at one time is the result of both formation and clearance. A cell with impaired lysosomal degradation may accumulate autophagic structures even if successful recycling is reduced. In a simple rate model, the number increases when formation exceeds removal. Measuring changes over time under controlled conditions, rather than interpreting one structural count as increased recycling, reflects the general flux-versus-pool principle.

The time course of mTORC1 signalling is important in metabolic studies. During recovery from resistance exercise, mechanical and nutrient signals may promote protein synthesis while the previous exercise-induced energy challenge is resolving. Repeated episodes of this sequence can contribute to tissue adaptation. This illustrates a physiological reason why growth-promoting and energy-conserving signals are complementary over time. An experiment examining only a single time point may observe high activity of one pathway and miss the earlier or later activity of the other. Therefore a time-course graph should be read as a series of observations rather than assuming the first measured point explains the entire response.

7. Circadian organisation of metabolic responses

A circadian rhythm is an endogenous oscillation with a period close to twenty-four hours. Light–dark cycles provide powerful environmental timing cues, and feeding, activity and hormonal rhythms can affect peripheral tissue clocks. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus receives information about environmental light and coordinates daily physiological rhythms through neural, endocrine and behavioural routes. Liver, muscle and adipose cells also contain molecular clocks.

The core molecular oscillator involves transcriptional and translational feedback. CLOCK and BMAL1 promote expression of genes including Period and Cryptochrome. PER and CRY proteins subsequently participate in suppression of their own transcriptional activation. Changes in protein abundance, stability and cellular location introduce a delay, allowing oscillation rather than immediate cancellation. Additional feedback loops help regulate oscillator robustness. This molecular framework connects metabolic timing to the gene regulation principles taught earlier.

Circadian control permits tissues to anticipate recurring changes in feeding and activity. The liver can vary expression of metabolic enzymes across the day; pancreatic hormone responses and skeletal muscle fuel handling can also vary with biological phase. A rhythm measured under ordinary light–dark conditions includes both endogenous timing and direct effects of behaviour. To distinguish these contributions, experiments may control feeding, activity, sleep and external cues, or measure persistent rhythms under constant conditions.

Damiola and colleagues investigated feeding-time effects on mammalian clocks using mice. Restricting food availability to a different phase of the light–dark cycle shifted the timing of rhythmic expression in peripheral tissues while the central SCN oscillator remained much less affected. This supports the idea that feeding is an important timing signal for peripheral clocks. It does not establish that all metabolic responses to a shifted meal are clock-mediated, nor does a result in nocturnal mice establish an optimal meal time for humans.

Daily changes in substrate use reflect both anticipatory regulation and immediate responses to behaviour. If an animal eats primarily at a particular phase, the liver may adjust the timing of metabolic gene expression in relation to that recurring event. Eating a meal also changes substrate concentrations and hormone secretion independently of the clock. A rhythmic measurement made during repeated feeding and fasting cannot identify how much of the rhythm arises from the endogenous oscillator and how much arises directly from the periodic meal. This distinction between a rhythm and its cause is fundamental in chronobiology.

Phase refers to the timing of a characteristic point in an oscillation, such as the peak of an expression profile. Amplitude refers to the magnitude of oscillation around its central tendency; period describes the duration of one cycle. Two tissues can have similar periods yet different phases. In a hypothetical experiment, liver expression of a metabolic gene peaks at hour 6 before a change in feeding schedule and at hour 12 afterward. The peak has shifted by six hours, but no conclusion about amplitude or period follows unless those properties were also measured. Timing measurements require samples across a sufficient span of the cycle.

A controlled feeding-time experiment needs to consider total nutrient intake, composition, activity and light exposure. If one group receives less food than another because access is restricted, a change in metabolism may result from altered energy balance instead of timing alone. Pair-feeding or other controls can help separate total intake from feeding phase, though matching intake does not ensure identical activity, sleep or stress. For human investigations, individual circadian phase can differ even at the same clock time. The timing relative to a person's biological rhythms is more informative than an assumption based solely on clock hours.

The core molecular clock provides a causal mechanism for persistence of rhythmic behaviour, but timing signals reach metabolism through several routes. SCN output can influence autonomic function and endocrine rhythms, while feeding directly changes the nutrients and hormones encountered by peripheral cells. Peripheral clocks in turn influence patterns of gene expression and cellular responsiveness. This creates a network in which environmental light, behaviour and metabolism interact. A shifted feeding schedule may alter peripheral phase even when the light schedule remains constant, and the resulting coordination can depend on the relationship between multiple rhythms.

The distinction between an entraining cue and a clock output can be tested conceptually. If a repeating meal shifts the phase of an oscillation, the meal may act as an entraining cue for the oscillator. If a gene's expression rises after each meal but stops oscillating when the repeated meal stimulus is removed, the observed rhythm may instead be dominated by direct responses to feeding. Persistent oscillation after removal of the cue provides stronger evidence for an endogenous component, provided other periodic signals have also been controlled. Circadian studies often combine time-course observations with perturbations for this reason.

A time-of-day comparison of glucose tolerance is vulnerable to differences in prior fasting and activity. Suppose group A undergoes a morning test after eight hours without food, while group B undergoes an evening test after four hours without food. Even if glucose excursions differ, the difference cannot be attributed solely to circadian phase because prior substrate availability and hormone state also differ. A more informative design would match fasting duration and previous meals, control recent exercise and examine biological timing. In some designs the same individuals can be compared at more than one phase to reduce between-person variation.

8. Exercise and adaptive reserve

During muscle contraction, ATP is consumed by myosin ATPase, calcium handling and ion transport. Phosphocreatine buffers early ATP demand, glycolysis can accelerate quickly, and oxidative phosphorylation increases as oxygen delivery and mitochondrial metabolism adjust. The relative contribution of carbohydrate and fatty acid oxidation depends on intensity, duration, feeding state and training. During intense work, carbohydrate can supply ATP at a rapid rate even when substantial fat stores remain.

Contraction stimulates glucose uptake through mechanisms that overlap only partly with insulin signalling. Mechanical, calcium-dependent and energy-sensitive signals influence GLUT4 traffic. AMPK and other regulators can participate, with relative importance depending on the model and contraction pattern. Thus insulin-resistant muscle may still increase glucose uptake in response to contraction. This is not proof that all insulin signalling has become normal; it demonstrates an alternative route to a related transport outcome.

After exercise, glycogen depletion and changes in signalling can promote glucose disposal and glycogen restoration. With repeated training, adaptations can include altered mitochondrial enzyme abundance, capillary supply, transport capacity and substrate handling. These longer-term changes differ from the acute response to a single bout. A comparison performed immediately after exercise and one performed after a recovery interval need not give the same estimate of insulin sensitivity. Experimental reports should identify this timing.

Different types of muscle fibres differ in metabolic machinery and contractile properties. Fibres with substantial mitochondrial capacity and oxidative enzyme abundance are suited to sustained ATP supply, whereas fibres recruited for rapid force production can rely more heavily on rapid glycolytic provision of ATP under relevant conditions. Recruitment patterns change with intensity and task. Whole-muscle measurements therefore represent a mixture of fibre responses. A training-induced change in mean oxidative activity can reflect altered enzyme abundance within fibres, altered fibre characteristics or changes in recruitment under the tested workload.

When comparing exercise tests, distinguish absolute workload from relative intensity. A trained participant and an untrained participant may produce the same mechanical power, but that power can represent a smaller fraction of the trained participant's maximal capacity. Their relative carbohydrate and fat use may differ partly because of the relative demand. Matching exercise at the same percentage of maximal oxygen uptake addresses a different question from matching absolute work. Neither design is inherently wrong; each should be selected according to the hypothesis and interpreted in its own terms.

During recovery, increased glucose disposal may support restoration of glycogen. An increase in uptake therefore does not need to be accompanied by a matching increase in carbohydrate oxidation. To examine the fate of glucose after exercise, combine measures of uptake with estimates of oxidation and glycogen storage. The distinction is important when relating exercise to measured metabolic flexibility: a rise in insulin-stimulated glucose disposal may be physiologically favourable even if the increase in RER is modest because a substantial fraction of glucose is being stored.

The acute substrate response to exercise can be analysed through conservation of energy rather than relying only on percentage contributions. If metabolic energy expenditure rises from 5 kJ/min to 30 kJ/min and the fraction derived from fat falls from 60% to 30%, absolute fat-derived energy increases from 3 to 9 kJ/min, assuming the stated fractions are accurate. The relative contribution has decreased while the absolute contribution has increased. This calculation is particularly helpful when interpreting graphs of relative fuel use across increasing exercise intensity. An increase in carbohydrate dominance does not by itself demonstrate a decrease in the absolute oxidation of fat.

A training study should distinguish adaptations from the effect of recent activity. If trained and untrained participants are tested immediately after identical exercise sessions, acute responses can interact with pre-existing training adaptations. Testing after a standard recovery interval provides a different view, although the appropriate interval depends on the variable being studied. Repeated exercise can influence glycogen content, insulin action and mitochondrial capacity on different time scales. A conclusion that training changed a property should be supported by a design that accounts for the acute exercise history and baseline differences.

A muscle-specific experiment can investigate whether insulin and contraction act through identical pathways. Four groups of muscle preparations are exposed to neither stimulus, insulin alone, contraction alone or both. If both stimuli together produce a larger glucose uptake response than either alone, this supports the existence of at least some nonidentical regulatory influences or unsaturated shared downstream machinery. Additivity is not proof of complete independence, since different upstream signals can converge on the same transport process. Conversely, lack of additivity can arise when a shared downstream step is saturated even if the upstream pathways are distinct.

9. Measuring fuel selection: RQ and RER

The respiratory quotient (RQ) is the ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed in a defined oxidative process. Complete oxidation of one mole of glucose consumes six moles of oxygen and produces six moles of carbon dioxide: C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O. Its RQ is therefore one. Complete oxidation of palmitic acid consumes twenty-three moles of oxygen and produces sixteen moles of carbon dioxide: C16H32O2 + 23 O2 → 16 CO2 + 16 H2O. The resulting RQ is 16/23, approximately 0.696. Fat is more reduced than carbohydrate and requires more oxygen relative to the carbon dioxide produced.

At the whole-body level, the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) is calculated from carbon dioxide output and oxygen uptake measured in respiratory gases. Under stable conditions these gas exchanges can approximate metabolic gas production and consumption. If oxygen uptake is 0.30 litres per minute and carbon dioxide output is 0.24 litres per minute, the RER is 0.24/0.30 = 0.80. Under simplified assumptions this suggests a mixture of carbohydrate and fat oxidation. It does not identify which tissues consumed the fuels.

If the body uses only carbohydrate and fat, with their quotients approximated by 1.00 and 0.70, the fraction of oxygen consumption associated with carbohydrate oxidation is (RER − 0.70)/0.30. An RER of 0.85 gives 0.50, or half the oxygen consumption assigned to carbohydrate in this simplified two-fuel model. This is not automatically half the mass or half the energy derived from carbohydrate because oxygen requirements per gram and energy equivalents differ. Protein oxidation and nonoxidative processes can complicate the calculation.

During rapid changes in ventilation, body carbon dioxide stores can change. Intense exercise can produce acid–base disturbances accompanied by bicarbonate buffering and additional carbon dioxide elimination. RER can therefore rise above one without requiring an impossible cellular glucose oxidation quotient. Lipogenesis and other metabolic transformations can also complicate gas-exchange interpretation. It is essential to identify steady-state assumptions before using a respiratory ratio as an estimate of substrate oxidation.

A metabolic flexibility index often uses stimulated RER minus fasting RER. In an illustrative example, one subject changes from 0.76 to 0.94, giving a difference of 0.18, whereas another changes from 0.83 to 0.89, giving 0.06. These differences are descriptive rather than diagnostic. The initial RER, achieved insulin concentration, glucose disposal, fatty acid suppression and measurement conditions all affect the interpretation.

The gas exchange ratio of a mixture can be derived by summing oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production separately for the contributing fuels. Suppose carbohydrate oxidation consumes oxygen at rate Oc and fat oxidation consumes oxygen at rate Of. Under the two-fuel steady-state model, carbon dioxide production is Oc + 0.70 Of. Total oxygen consumption is Oc + Of. Dividing the first sum by the second yields RER = (Oc + 0.70 Of)/(Oc + Of). Define fc = Oc/(Oc + Of), which is the oxygen-weighted contribution of carbohydrate. Substitution gives RER = fc + 0.70(1 − fc), or 0.70 + 0.30fc. The expression introduced earlier follows directly.

The assumption of oxygen weighting matters when interpreting identical ratios at different metabolic rates. Consider a person resting with oxygen uptake 0.25 L/min and RER 0.80, and later exercising with oxygen uptake 1.50 L/min and RER 0.80 under otherwise ideal steady-state conditions. The estimated fractional contribution of carbohydrate to oxygen consumption is one-third in both conditions. The absolute oxygen consumption attributable to carbohydrate is approximately 0.083 and 0.50 L/min respectively. Absolute oxidation of both fuels can increase without changing their relative proportions. A respiratory ratio is not a direct measure of energy expenditure.

Indirect calorimetry estimates energy expenditure and substrate oxidation from respiratory gas exchange under stated assumptions. Gas volumes should be expressed under compatible reference conditions. In research, protein oxidation can be estimated with nitrogen measurements and appropriate corrections; ignoring it is a simplification whose consequences depend on the situation. The energy released per litre of oxygen varies with substrate because carbohydrate and fatty acid oxidation differ in stoichiometry and energetic properties. A student should not convert oxygen consumption directly into a universal mass of fat oxidised without specifying an appropriate equation and assumptions.

The respiratory ratio can also be expressed as a weighted combination when protein oxidation contributes, but a third unknown cannot be determined from a single ratio without additional information. Two measured gas rates provide limited constraints on several possible substrate oxidation rates. Nitrogen excretion can help estimate protein oxidation under suitable assumptions, but temporal delays between protein breakdown and nitrogen excretion complicate short-term measurements. This is why substrate oxidation equations often specify a nonprotein respiratory quotient or use nitrogen correction rather than assuming all carbon dioxide arises from carbohydrate and lipid oxidation alone.

Interpretation also requires attention to carbon dioxide storage. In a simplified whole-body carbon dioxide balance, expired carbon dioxide equals metabolically produced carbon dioxide minus the rate of increase in the body's carbon dioxide stores. If stores decrease during acute hyperventilation, expired carbon dioxide can exceed concurrent metabolic production. For example, if metabolic production is 0.24 L/min but stored carbon dioxide decreases at an equivalent rate of 0.06 L/min, expired output becomes 0.30 L/min. With oxygen uptake 0.25 L/min, the measured RER is 1.20. The result is consistent with transient changes in gas stores and does not establish complete oxidation of a substrate with cellular RQ 1.20.

When evaluating change in RER from fasting to insulin stimulation, the baseline value imposes practical constraints on the possible difference. A participant whose baseline is already near 0.90 cannot increase by 0.25 within the simplified two-fuel model without exceeding the carbohydrate oxidation endpoint. This does not necessarily show a failure to respond. Absolute baseline and stimulated values, the size of the change and the physiological conditions must be considered together. In addition, stimulated glucose storage may be considerable even if carbohydrate oxidation changes modestly. A measure of fuel switching is only one component of metabolic responsiveness.

10. Glucose tolerance and surrogate indicators

A glucose tolerance test observes the response to a standardised glucose challenge. The blood glucose trajectory reflects absorption, endogenous production, insulin secretion, insulin clearance, tissue glucose uptake and other processes. Oral administration also engages intestinal hormones and gastric emptying, so the response is not equivalent to intravenous administration. A high glucose excursion might indicate slow clearance, excessive endogenous appearance, delayed insulin release or a combination. The curve alone cannot identify a molecular lesion.

For a curve with values of 5, 8, 7 and 5 mmol/L at 0, 30, 60 and 120 minutes, approximate total area under the curve using the trapezoid rule. The first interval contributes (5+8)/2 × 30 = 195 mmol·min/L. The second contributes 225 and the third contributes (7+5)/2 × 60 = 360. The total is 780 mmol·min/L. The baseline rectangle at 5 mmol/L over 120 minutes contributes 600, leaving an incremental area of 180 mmol·min/L in this example. Different sampling schedules and baseline conventions affect the comparison.

Fasting glucose and insulin can be combined into the original approximate HOMA-IR expression, fasting glucose in mmol/L multiplied by fasting insulin in micro-units/mL and divided by 22.5. If glucose is 5 mmol/L and insulin is 9 micro-units/mL, the index is 2.0. This is an estimate based on fasting feedback relationships, not a direct measure of a specific tissue's insulin response. The updated computer model HOMA2 differs from the simple original formula. Insulin assay variation, secretion capacity and population context matter; universal cutoffs should not be inferred from a single numerical example.

Circulating triglycerides and lipoprotein-associated cholesterol provide complementary information about lipid handling. Concentrations reflect production, transport and clearance. A higher triglyceride concentration is not direct evidence that tissue fatty acid oxidation is low: impaired lipoprotein clearance or increased secretion could also contribute. Detailed lipid transport pathology belongs to 7.L.08; here these markers illustrate the need to assess multiple physiological dimensions.

The area under a glucose tolerance curve integrates a concentration over time, not an amount of glucose removed from the body. It is useful for comparing excursions under comparable conditions, but the same area could result from a high early peak followed by rapid recovery or a moderate elevation sustained for longer. Peak height, time to peak and late recovery therefore provide information that the area alone loses. In addition, baseline-subtracted area differs from total area; the choice of including negative excursions below baseline or counting only positive areas should be stated when applicable.

Insulin and glucose curves should be interpreted jointly. If two groups have a similar glucose excursion but one has a much larger insulin response, the higher response may be compensating for reduced tissue sensitivity. Yet a difference in insulin clearance can also change circulating insulin, and secretion cannot be inferred without considering clearance. C-peptide is released with endogenous insulin from proinsulin processing and has different clearance characteristics, making it useful in appropriately designed assessments of secretion. It is not immune to confounding by kidney function or differences in sampling time.

Experimental glucose tolerance curves are influenced by the route of administration. Oral glucose reaches the circulation after gastric emptying and intestinal absorption and stimulates gut-derived signals that can enhance insulin secretion. Intravenous glucose bypasses the gut and permits different control of circulating glucose. A comparison designed to assess the incretin effect can match the glucose profile across the two routes and compare insulin secretion-related responses. If oral and intravenous curves have different glucose concentrations, the hormone responses cannot be attributed to the route alone because beta cells respond to the glucose stimulus itself.

Fasting triglycerides, non-esterified fatty acids and cholesterol carried by different lipoprotein classes report different aspects of lipid transport. Triglycerides in circulation are transported largely within lipoprotein particles, whereas non-esterified fatty acids circulate associated with albumin. A measurement of one pool is not a measurement of the other. The response of non-esterified fatty acids to insulin can help investigate adipose regulation; circulating triglyceride concentrations also reflect secretion and clearance of lipoproteins. A broad metabolic-health assessment therefore benefits from identifying the particular pool under investigation instead of referring vaguely to blood fat.

A fasting surrogate may be useful for comparisons in large studies because it requires less experimental effort than a clamp. However, a model-based estimate can be biased when its assumptions do not describe the tested population well. In particular, insulin secretion and clearance, glucose assay conditions and hepatic versus peripheral responses affect interpretation. Repeat measurements may reduce random sampling variability but cannot eliminate systematic limitations of the model. The choice of an index should follow the scientific question: a convenient group-level estimate is not a substitute for direct measurements when the aim is to identify a tissue-specific mechanism.

11. Hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp and flux interpretation

The glucose clamp introduced by DeFronzo, Tobin and Andres provides an experimental way to investigate insulin action while controlling glucose concentration. During a hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp, insulin is infused to elevate circulating insulin and glucose is supplied at a variable rate to maintain a selected near-normal glucose level. A feedback loop uses repeated glucose measurements to adjust the infusion. Under comparable steady-state conditions, the external glucose requirement provides information about the organism's response to insulin.

Glucose conservation is the central mathematical principle. In a stable glucose pool with negligible urinary loss, exogenous glucose infusion plus endogenous glucose appearance equals glucose disappearance. In symbols, GIR + EGP = Rd. If the infusion rate is 5 mg/kg/min and endogenous production is 1 mg/kg/min, disappearance is 6 mg/kg/min. A second subject with GIR 5 and EGP 0 has disappearance 5 mg/kg/min. Equal GIR does not imply equal disappearance unless endogenous production is equally suppressed.

A labelled glucose tracer can help estimate rates of appearance and disappearance. Its dilution by unlabelled glucose contains information about entry into the accessible glucose pool. The method requires attention to tracer enrichment, infusion changes, mixing and whether the system is at steady state. Endogenous glucose production is often attributed mainly to liver under relevant conditions, but kidney glucose production can contribute. A systemic tracer does not identify organ-specific production without additional design or assumptions.

At sufficiently high insulin exposure, endogenous production may be strongly suppressed in insulin-sensitive individuals, so glucose infusion can approximate whole-body disposal. The approximation is less reliable when endogenous production persists. Insulin doses, achieved insulin concentrations, body-mass normalisation and duration should be comparable. Expressing a rate relative to total body mass versus lean mass can change the apparent group difference, particularly when body composition varies.

A clamp differs from a glucose tolerance test in the direction of experimental control. During a tolerance test, a defined glucose challenge is delivered and the glucose concentration is allowed to vary. During a euglycaemic insulin clamp, glucose concentration is the controlled variable and the variable external infusion is an experimental output. This arrangement reduces the complication that differing glucose concentrations can themselves change glucose uptake. It also makes glucose appearance from food irrelevant if the experimental design excludes oral nutrient intake during the measurement.

A feedback-controlled infusion is not necessarily at steady state throughout the experiment. Early in the clamp, insulin concentrations and glucose distribution may be changing; measured glucose infusion can partly compensate for changes in the circulating glucose pool rather than represent balanced disappearance. At a late stable interval, the approximation is more defensible. If total accessible glucose increases at 0.5 mg/kg/min while GIR and EGP are 5 and 1 respectively, conservation requires Rd = 5 + 1 − 0.5 = 5.5 mg/kg/min, assuming no unaccounted losses. The steady-state formula would overestimate disappearance by 0.5.

Clamp-based glucose disappearance includes oxidation and nonoxidative pathways. If measured disappearance is 7 mg/kg/min and an appropriate estimate of glucose oxidation is 3 mg/kg/min, nonoxidative disposal is estimated as 4 mg/kg/min. Glycogen formation can contribute substantially to this remainder in muscle, but the difference is not a direct assay of muscle glycogen synthesis. Other fates and methodological differences must be considered. Direct measurements of labelled glycogen or tissue glucose uptake can help test more specific claims.

A multi-stage insulin clamp can investigate responses at different insulin exposures. At a lower insulin stimulus, suppression of endogenous glucose production and adipose lipolysis may be prominent; at higher exposure, stimulation of peripheral glucose disposal becomes especially informative. The ranges overlap, and the precise contribution depends on the participants and experimental conditions. Combining multiple stages with tracer estimates of endogenous production can provide a richer dose-response description than a single high-dose measurement. The comparison should use achieved insulin concentrations, not assume that identical infusion rates necessarily produce identical hormone levels.

The relationship between glucose infusion and insulin concentration creates an additional normalization problem. An index expressing disposal relative to achieved insulin exposure may be useful when circulating hormone concentrations differ. Yet the insulin dose-response relationship is not necessarily linear, especially near a plateau. Dividing a glucose disposal rate by insulin concentration does not automatically convert results from any two doses into equivalent sensitivity estimates. A carefully designed study either matches exposure or interprets the response in the context of the relevant curve. Numerical convenience does not remove physiological assumptions.

A tracer experiment can also address adipose lipolysis during insulin stimulation. If the measured glycerol appearance rate decreases substantially, this supports suppression of net glycerol release into the circulation. However, glycerol turnover reflects contributions from multiple tissues and metabolic processes, so tissue-level attribution requires assumptions or additional measurements. A systemic fatty acid tracer can complement glycerol data, and local blood flow and arteriovenous sampling can help examine a specific tissue. The combined approach is more informative than interpreting changes in concentration alone.

A common analytical mistake is to treat the clamp glucose infusion rate as a direct measurement of insulin receptor activity. GIR is the outcome of feedback control applied to an intact organism. It depends on tissue glucose disappearance, endogenous production and, outside steady state, changes in the accessible glucose pool. Receptor phosphorylation is much closer to the molecular signal but does not directly report systemic glucose balance. If both measurements change in parallel, the result is compatible with a connection between signalling and physiology. Demonstrating the specific steps between them requires additional evidence.

12. Assessing metabolic health without a single universal score

Metabolic health is a multidimensional physiological concept encompassing appropriate energy handling, glucose regulation, lipid transport, tissue insulin responses and the capacity to adapt to challenges. There is no single universally accepted definition that captures every aspect. A stable fasting measurement may coexist with an abnormal challenge response; good glucose control may rely on compensatory insulin secretion; a high mitochondrial capacity may coexist with restricted glucose delivery.

When interpreting evidence, specify the stimulus, measured variable, baseline, response time and comparison group. If a subject exhibits a reduced RER change during insulin stimulation, first ask whether glucose uptake was comparable, whether fatty acid suppression differed and whether gas exchange was measured at steady state. Galgani and colleagues reported that the apparent group difference in glucose-stimulated fuel switching could be accounted for by glucose disposal in their analysis. This illustrates why an observed defect in a whole-body proxy does not automatically establish an intrinsic defect in mitochondrial oxidation.

The study of metabolic flexibility leads naturally to the next chapter on insulin resistance and diabetes. The transition is from successful coordination and methods of assessment to mechanisms that disrupt this coordination. Maintaining this distinction prevents disease associations from being mistaken for universal features of normal physiology.

A comprehensive assessment should keep measurement scales separate. Whole-body energy expenditure describes total energetic demand; respiratory ratio describes gas exchange relative to oxygen uptake; a clamp reports response under an imposed hormonal condition; tissue assays investigate local machinery. The measurements address different questions, and combining them can be more informative than ranking them on a single scale. It is possible for an experimental group to show similar total energy expenditure but a different response to a carbohydrate challenge, or similar fasting glucose but different insulin requirements.

The logic of causal inference becomes clearer by constructing alternative explanations before interpreting a result. If a treatment increases the measured change in RER after insulin infusion, it may have increased glucose uptake, reduced circulating fatty acid availability, altered mitochondrial substrate selection, or changed respiratory gas dynamics. Each explanation predicts a different pattern of complementary measurements. If glucose uptake increases while oxidative capacity measured under matched conditions is unchanged, an improved supply of glucose to the oxidative pathways is plausible. If glucose delivery and uptake are unchanged but oxidation changes under controlled conditions, a downstream partitioning mechanism deserves consideration.

Maintaining metabolic health therefore involves coordinated responses across levels of organisation. Cells sense local energy conditions, organs exchange substrates and signals, and the whole animal adjusts behaviour, circulation and endocrine activity to changes in its environment. A useful mechanistic explanation identifies both the regulated process and the conditions under which it operates. This is the foundation for analysing the disorders introduced in the following nodes without equating a single laboratory measurement with the entire physiological state.

A student can integrate the different measures by constructing a prediction table before examining an experimental dataset. For example, if an intervention specifically improves muscle glucose transport, predicted findings may include increased insulin-stimulated muscle glucose uptake and increased whole-body disposal, but the change in RER depends on whether additional glucose is oxidised or stored. If the intervention specifically improves suppression of hepatic glucose output, the external infusion required during a clamp may increase even without a change in muscle uptake. If it improves adipose suppression of lipolysis, circulating substrate availability to both liver and muscle changes. These distinct predictions identify which measurements would be most useful.

Metabolic health also includes the ability to maintain performance during environmental and physiological challenges. A system that maintains fasting glucose at rest but cannot increase ATP production adequately when demand rises has a different limitation from one with preserved oxidative reserve but impaired postprandial glucose regulation. Equally, a normal exercise response does not guarantee that all endocrine feedback loops operate normally. Integrating measurements across challenges provides a more complete description than relying on a single static marker.

Associations between metabolic markers require attention to confounding and direction of causality. Training, age, diet, body composition and sleep can influence several measures simultaneously. If physically active individuals have greater oxidative capacity and greater insulin sensitivity, the correlation could reflect common effects of activity rather than a direct causal relationship between the measured variables. A longitudinal intervention, appropriate controls and measurements of intermediate mechanisms can strengthen causal reasoning. Even then, an intervention can affect multiple pathways, so attributing every benefit to a single molecular target requires evidence beyond simultaneous changes.

Finally, an examination problem may ask for the most defensible inference rather than the most comprehensive possible mechanism. When the data consist only of a change in RER, the correct inference is about gas exchange under the stated assumptions. When a glucose tracer and clamp are combined, the inference can extend to rates of appearance and disappearance. When tissue-specific perturbation and functional assays are added, more precise mechanistic conclusions may become possible. The principle of matching the strength of a conclusion to the strength of the evidence is central to Part C reasoning.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Flexibility means burning fat continuously

Correction: A response is appropriate when it matches fuel availability and demand. Why it is confusing: The popularity of fasting fat oxidation obscures the need for glucose uptake after meals. Examination reasoning: A high basal fat oxidation rate does not demonstrate a large or appropriate response to feeding.

2. A stable glucose concentration means no glucose turnover

Correction: Appearance and disappearance can be large and balanced. Why it is confusing: Concentration is easier to observe than flux. Examination reasoning: Use mass conservation; measure tracer turnover if the rates matter.

3. Insulin is needed for every cell to take up glucose

Correction: Tissue transport mechanisms differ; basal and contraction-stimulated uptake can occur. Why it is confusing: GLUT4 is overgeneralised from muscle. Examination reasoning: Identify tissue and transporter before predicting the effect.

4. All glucose taken up is oxidised

Correction: Glucose can enter glycogen, lactate and other pathways. Why it is confusing: Disposal and oxidation are sometimes used loosely. Examination reasoning: Compare tracer uptake with gas exchange or storage data.

5. Liver uses acute insulin-dependent GLUT4 recruitment as its main glucose control

Correction: Hepatic glucose handling is regulated strongly by enzymatic pathways and endogenous output. Why it is confusing: Muscle signalling diagrams are transferred to liver. Examination reasoning: Distinguish transport from regulation of net production.

6. Mitochondrial oxygen consumption equals ATP output

Correction: Coupling, leak and substrate identity affect ATP yield. Why it is confusing: Oxygen use is a convenient proxy. Examination reasoning: Consider the proton gradient and measure ATP production when coupling is the question.

7. Low resting respiration establishes low capacity

Correction: Current demand can be small while maximal capacity remains large. Why it is confusing: A single observation is treated as a maximum. Examination reasoning: Compare respiration under controlled stimulation.

8. Muscle triglyceride accumulation always demonstrates insulin resistance

Correction: Storage, turnover and signalling intermediates differ. Why it is confusing: The total lipid pool is treated as a toxic species. Examination reasoning: Account for the trained-muscle phenotype and substrate turnover.

9. AMPK measures systemic fat stores directly

Correction: Its regulation reflects local cellular nucleotide and signalling conditions. Why it is confusing: Energy balance is confused with cellular energy status. Examination reasoning: Specify the cell and the physiological challenge.

10. AMPK activation guarantees fat oxidation increases

Correction: Availability, oxygen, downstream capacity and demand can limit flux. Why it is confusing: A permissive regulatory event is treated as sufficient. Examination reasoning: Trace the entire pathway from substrate delivery to oxidation.

11. mTORC1 activation is always harmful

Correction: Normal nutrient and recovery signalling supports biosynthesis. Why it is confusing: Chronic dysregulation is confused with physiological activation. Examination reasoning: Distinguish duration, context and complex identity.

12. Peripheral clocks follow only light

Correction: Feeding and other signals can shift peripheral clocks. Why it is confusing: Central entrainment is confused with all tissue entrainment. Examination reasoning: Separate SCN from peripheral timing and control behaviour.

13. All rhythmic outputs prove endogenous oscillation

Correction: Periodic external inputs can generate rhythmic responses. Why it is confusing: A daily pattern is equated with a clock. Examination reasoning: Test persistence under controlled conditions.

14. RER is identical to cellular RQ in all circumstances

Correction: Gas stores and nonsteady ventilation can alter pulmonary exchange. Why it is confusing: The same algebraic ratio is used at different scales. Examination reasoning: Check steady state and acid–base context.

15. Higher RER means higher absolute carbohydrate oxidation

Correction: The total oxygen uptake matters as well as the ratio. Why it is confusing: Relative fuel choice is confused with absolute rate. Examination reasoning: Calculate oxygen-weighted contributions and state assumptions.

16. GIR directly equals muscle glucose uptake

Correction: It reflects systemic disappearance minus endogenous appearance at steady state. Why it is confusing: The clamp is described as an insulin sensitivity test without explaining conservation. Examination reasoning: Use tracer estimates and tissue measurements.

17. A normal fasting glucose proves normal insulin sensitivity

Correction: Compensatory secretion can maintain glucose. Why it is confusing: Feedback compensation is overlooked. Examination reasoning: Examine insulin, response to challenge and secretion/clearance.

18. HOMA gives a universally valid tissue-specific diagnosis

Correction: It is a fasting model estimate with population and assay limitations. Why it is confusing: A convenient numerical index looks definitive. Examination reasoning: Use it for the question it can answer and corroborate with other measures.

Revision and mastery

Metabolic flexibility describes adjustment to a specified physiological challenge. Insulin coordinates tissue-specific glucose disposal, production and lipolysis; mitochondrial capacity and demand constrain oxidative flux. AMPK and mTORC1 integrate energy and growth signals, while peripheral and central clocks organise metabolic timing. Gas exchange, glucose tolerance, fasting indices and clamps provide complementary evidence and have distinct assumptions.

Key equations

  • RER = VCO₂ / VO₂.
  • Two-fuel oxygen-weighted carbohydrate fraction = (RER − 0.70)/0.30.
  • Steady-state Rd = GIR + EGP (negligible losses).
  • HOMA-IR approximation = fasting glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (µU/mL) / 22.5.
  • Net tissue uptake = flow × arteriovenous concentration difference.
  • Energy charge = (ATP + 0.5 ADP)/(ATP + ADP + AMP).
