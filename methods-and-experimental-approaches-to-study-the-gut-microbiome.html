1. From physiology to measurable evidence

A change in intestinal physiology can be accompanied by a change in the community of microorganisms that inhabits the intestine. The two changes need not have the same cause. For example, an animal that consumes a different diet may experience a change in the supply of fermentable carbohydrates to the colon and simultaneously a change in intestinal transit. Both processes can change the microbial community, and transit can independently affect the concentration of fermentation products in faeces. Before proposing that a particular bacterium caused a physiological response, an investigator must establish which organisms, genes, chemical compounds and host responses were actually measured. The central lesson of microbiome methodology is that different measurements provide different kinds of evidence.

The term microbiota refers to the organisms associated with a defined environment, while microbiome is often used for the community together with its genes and ecological context. The exact usage varies between authors. In a sequence-based investigation, the observable object may be DNA rather than organisms. A gene sequence in a faecal sample is evidence that corresponding genetic material reached the analysed specimen. It is not by itself proof that a living cell carrying that gene was present at the time of sampling, that the gene was expressed, or that the encoded protein performed a particular reaction in the host. A sample can contain living cells, damaged cells, free DNA, bacterial viruses, host cells and dietary residues. Each contributes differently to the measurement.

A useful way to organise evidence is to distinguish identity, abundance, potential, expression, chemical state and function. Identification asks what kinds of organisms are present. Abundance asks how many cells or marker copies belong to each kind. Functional potential asks what biochemical processes the genes present could support. Expression asks which genes are being transcribed or translated under the observed conditions. Chemical measurements ask which substrates and products are present. Physiological measurements ask how the host or microbial ecosystem behaves. None of these levels is identical to the others. A conclusion becomes stronger when evidence at several levels agrees and when appropriate controls eliminate plausible alternative explanations.

The historical development of microbial ecology illustrates why these distinctions matter. Growth on laboratory media originally offered an effective way to identify and characterise individual microorganisms. However, investigators could see microbes in environmental material that did not grow under the conditions used. Molecular comparisons of small-subunit ribosomal RNA genes later made it possible to investigate community members without first isolating them. High-throughput DNA sequencing extended this idea from individual marker genes to the genetic content of whole communities. RNA and metabolite measurements provide further layers of interpretation. The newer methods complement rather than abolish the older ones.

The Human Microbiome Project provided a large-scale example of using both marker-gene and community DNA sequencing in human-associated habitats. Its 2012 report showed that taxonomic composition can differ substantially between people even where the distributions of broad metabolic pathways are more similar. This is consistent with functional redundancy: distinct organisms may possess genes for related biochemical tasks. Such overlap does not establish that the rates of all reactions are identical or that a particular species is interchangeable with every other species. The result also illustrates how a study's conclusions depend on the level at which its measurements are compared.

Consider a physiological question concerning butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid that can supply energy to colonocytes. Counting organisms previously described as butyrate producers is a useful starting point, but a change in those counts does not measure butyrate production. A gene encoding a pathway enzyme can be measured in community DNA; its RNA abundance can be examined; the product can be measured chemically; and effects on epithelial physiology can be assessed in a suitable experimental model. The interpretation of each step must remain specific to the measurement. A careful study is built around a testable question rather than around whichever sequencing platform is available.

A study of an ecological community can use an organism as its unit of description or a process as its unit of description. If a population of microorganisms has expanded, a taxonomic observation may be sufficient to describe that expansion, provided that abundance is measured correctly. If a physiological process has changed, taxonomic descriptions need to be connected with biochemical measurements. The same chemical reaction may be carried out by multiple groups, and the same organism may change its metabolism without any detectable change in its population size. This is why methods designed to investigate community membership and methods designed to investigate metabolic function are often used together.

A measurement is said to be operationally defined when its meaning is specified in terms of the observation used to obtain it. For example, detectable sequence richness depends on the chosen marker and analytical rules; viable colony counts depend on the conditions under which cells are able to form colonies. Operational definitions do not weaken science. They make a statement precise enough to reproduce and test. The expression detectable under these conditions is often more informative than the unqualified statement present or absent. The operational definition also tells the investigator which assumptions are needed to compare two studies.

Method comparison requires separating sensitivity, specificity and reproducibility. Sensitivity describes the ability to detect a target when it is present at a relevant level. Specificity describes the ability to distinguish that target from other material. Reproducibility concerns the consistency of results across repeated analyses or investigations. A method may be highly reproducible while detecting the wrong biological quantity: consistently recovering DNA more efficiently from one group of bacteria is an example. This distinction is useful in examination problems in which repeated observations have small variability but differ systematically from a known reference.

The fact that a microbial community is complex does not imply that every investigation needs to measure every possible molecular layer. A study designed to determine whether the relative representation of broad bacterial lineages changes after a dietary intervention may be adequately served by a well-controlled marker assay. A study designed to investigate expression of a specific pathway needs additional information. A study that claims a change in host physiology is mediated through a microbial reaction needs evidence linking the reaction to the outcome. In each case the simplest method sufficient to answer the stated question is preferable to an elaborate analysis that never measures the relevant variable.

2. Sampling the gastrointestinal ecosystem

The gastrointestinal tract contains a sequence of distinct habitats. The stomach, small intestine and colon differ in acidity, available nutrients, passage time, digestive secretions and oxygen availability. Even within the colon, organisms in the lumen are exposed to conditions that differ from those near the mucus and epithelial surface. These gradients can influence which cells grow, which substrates they use and which products reach the host. A sample taken from one compartment is not a random collection of all microorganisms living throughout the digestive tract.

Stool is a practical specimen for investigations involving repeated sampling from the same person. Collection does not require removal of tissue, and specimens contain abundant microbial material from the distal intestinal lumen. A faecal sample also reflects processes such as transit, water absorption and the shedding of microorganisms from other locations. Its composition therefore represents the output of a changing intestinal system, not an exact map of the community attached to every region of the gut. When a question concerns the interaction of a microorganism with the intestinal epithelium, a mucosal specimen may be more informative.

Mucosal biopsies sample a defined tissue-associated environment. The bacterial DNA in a biopsy may be mixed with a large quantity of host DNA, and the tissue represents a small geographical area. Clinical procedures used to obtain the sample can alter the local environment. Mucosal communities cannot be assumed to be represented by faeces, nor can a single biopsy represent an entire colon. Comparisons between biopsy and stool require attention to the different proportions of host and microbial material, the location of sampling and the circumstances of collection.

A representative sampling plan begins by defining the population or compartment about which the investigator wants to make a statement. Repeated faecal samples are appropriate for an investigation of the time course of community change in healthy volunteers. A comparison of organisms in inflamed and non-inflamed mucosa may need anatomically matched tissue observations. A study of metabolite delivery to the host may require blood or other host measurements in addition to faecal chemistry. Selecting an easier sample at the cost of changing the biological question can result in a precise but irrelevant answer.

A sample is also a snapshot in time. Meals, changes in intestinal transit and medication exposures may alter the measurements. A specimen obtained before a dietary change and a second obtained after the change can reflect both the intervention and ordinary day-to-day fluctuation. Repeated baseline measurements give a better picture of each individual's variability. Collection time, recent diet, medication history and stool consistency are examples of useful metadata, meaning recorded information about the biological and technical circumstances of a sample. Metadata permit an analysis to distinguish competing explanations, provided the corresponding factors vary in a way that can be evaluated.

The units of abundance deserve particular attention in faecal investigations. A count expressed per gram of wet stool depends on water content as well as the number of cells. Two specimens containing the same number of cells per gram of dry material may differ substantially when expressed per gram of wet material. Neither denominator is inherently wrong; the appropriate denominator depends on the hypothesis. A report that compares bacterial density with intestinal transit should state which denominator it used and discuss how differences in water content could influence the result.

Spatial sampling can be understood by considering the movement of a soluble fermentation product. The substrate enters the colon in the luminal contents, where it can be metabolised by microorganisms. Some of the product diffuses toward the epithelium and is absorbed; some is used by other microbes; the remaining material may leave in faeces. The location where the product is recovered therefore need not be the location where it was generated. An investigation of luminal fermentation may combine stool chemistry with other measures, whereas an investigation of epithelial contact with the producing cells requires evidence of their spatial relationship. The desired physiological conclusion determines the relevant compartment.

Sampling within one specimen can introduce another type of variation. Faecal material is physically heterogeneous, containing particles of undigested food, mucus and water in addition to microbial cells. If the question concerns the average microbial profile of the specimen, a sampling strategy that represents that material is necessary. If the question concerns microbes associated with a particular food particle or mucus fragment, averaging would remove the distinction of interest. Homogenisation is consequently an analytical decision, not a universal requirement that is biologically neutral. It depends on whether spatial variation is a feature to be measured or a source of unwanted sampling variation.

Anatomically paired samples are especially informative when two compartments are compared. A mucosal sample and stool from the same individual share many characteristics of the host, including genetic background and some aspects of recent exposure. Differences between them may therefore be more informative about compartment-specific patterns than a comparison in which all biopsies come from one population and all stool specimens from another. Pairing does not remove differences in the way the specimens are obtained, processed or interpreted. An appropriate analysis considers both the relationship between the samples and differences in their measurement characteristics.

A distinction should also be made between a specimen that is unavailable and a specimen in which a feature was not detected. In a longitudinal study, some participants may not provide material at every scheduled time point. If missing specimens occur more often among participants experiencing a gastrointestinal response, the remaining observations may describe a selected subset of the participants. Replacing unavailable observations with zero abundance would confuse missing information with a biological claim. A scientifically transparent analysis states how many participants contributed observations at each point and distinguishes missing samples from measured non-detection.

3. Sample handling, extraction and contamination

The material entering the analytical instrument is no longer identical to the original biological specimen. During collection and storage, cellular metabolism may continue, some cells may lose viability and biomolecules may degrade. A preservation method that keeps DNA suitable for sequencing does not necessarily preserve RNA, chemical metabolites or living cells. Consequently the intended measurement must determine the way samples are handled. Investigators comparing groups should minimise systematic differences in collection delays and preservation and should record unavoidable differences.

Extraction transforms cells and other material into an analyzable mixture of nucleic acids. Cell envelopes differ between microorganisms. Some bacteria release their DNA readily, whereas cells with more resistant envelopes can be underrepresented when the method does not disrupt them effectively. Even when the true community contains equal numbers of two species, the extracted DNA may contain different amounts of their genomes. Increasing the number of sequencing reads does not correct an earlier failure to recover one of the organisms. This is a distinction between measurement precision and measurement accuracy.

A batch is a group of specimens processed together under a particular set of technical conditions. Reagent composition, instruments or operators can differ between batches. If all control samples are processed in one batch and all disease samples in another, disease status becomes inseparable from the processing difference. Even a dramatic separation between groups in a statistical analysis would be ambiguous. Distributing specimens from different biological groups across batches reduces this risk, while recording batch identifiers permits investigators to examine technical contributions to the observed variation.

Contamination is particularly consequential when the amount of target microbial material is small. Suppose an extraction introduces 100 contaminant marker copies into a sample with 10,000 genuine copies. The contaminant contribution is approximately one percent. If another sample contains only 100 genuine copies, the same contamination accounts for half of the combined signal. The contamination did not become more extensive in absolute terms; it became a larger fraction of the detected material. Salter and colleagues demonstrated the importance of reagent-derived DNA in low-biomass sequence-based microbiome work. Their findings explain why negative controls are especially valuable in such samples.

An extraction blank passes through the extraction process without a biological specimen. It can reveal background material introduced by reagents or handling. A no-template amplification control tests for contamination associated with the amplification reaction. These controls answer overlapping but not identical questions. A positive control with known material tests whether the assay recovers an expected signal. A mock community containing known organisms or DNA permits comparison between expected and measured composition. None of these controls alone proves that every specimen has been measured without bias.

A mock community may differ from actual intestinal material in cell structure, sample matrix and abundance range. A method that recovers a clean mixture well can still perform poorly in complex stool. Conversely, an organism detected in a negative control is not automatically absent from all real samples: some genuine community members can also occur as contaminants. The interpretation depends on the amounts observed, the distribution of signals across samples and controls and independent biological evidence. A scientifically defensible study reports how these issues were evaluated rather than silently treating all detected sequences as members of the gut community.

Bias may arise before an instrument measures a single molecule. A useful conceptual model is that the number of recovered templates for a taxon depends on the number originally present, the efficiency of cell disruption and recovery, and the number of relevant genes carried per genome. Later amplification and sequence processing can impose further selection. In a simplified proportional model the observed signal is the true abundance multiplied by several taxon-specific factors. If those factors differ between taxa, the ratio of observed signals differs from the ratio of biological abundances. The factors are not usually known exactly, so interpretation must acknowledge uncertainty.

For an illustrative example, suppose that the original specimen contains 500 cells of each of two organisms carrying one marker copy per cell. A particular extraction recovers 80 percent of templates from the first organism and 20 percent from the second. The resulting extract contains approximately 400 and 100 templates, and the first organism represents 80 percent of the recovered DNA markers. Increasing sequencing depth may give a very precise estimate of 80 percent, but the true cell fraction was 50 percent. The example shows why a precision improvement cannot remove a systematic recovery bias.

Storage effects differ between biological targets for a mechanistic reason. Living cells can respond to the environment, whereas the investigator often wants to characterise the state before the specimen left the host. A delay that allows cells to continue consuming substrates can change metabolite concentrations. RNA turnover may change transcript profiles during the same period. DNA can persist longer than RNA or viable growth capacity, but DNA recovery is not unaffected by degradation or changes in the cellular material. Different endpoints therefore need appropriately validated preservation strategies, and identical storage does not guarantee equal preservation of every feature.

It is important not to confuse technical variation with biological variation. Variation between aliquots of a homogenised sample provides some information about the consistency of analytical recovery. Variation between two samples taken from the same individual at different times also includes biological temporal changes. Variation between different individuals includes additional differences in diet, physiology and life history. These layers of variation can be studied separately by designing nested comparisons rather than treating all measurements as exchangeable. The resulting interpretation is more informative than a single statement that the assay has a given amount of variability.

The effect of contamination can sometimes be evaluated by examining its relationship to sample biomass. A signal that is detected in blanks and occupies a progressively larger fraction of samples as genuine microbial material declines is consistent with contamination. However, genuine taxa can also have ecological relationships with low-biomass environments, so a statistical pattern alone is not decisive. A good interpretation combines information about control specimens, the amount of starting material, biological plausibility and independent detection where appropriate. It also reports the criteria used to decide which signals to exclude.

Batch effects can arise from changes in the chemical composition of reagents even when the nominal method is unchanged. They can also arise when different operators process samples, different versions of a reference database classify reads or specimens remain in storage for different periods. These technical factors can cause shifts in profiles that are comparable to biological differences. Balancing groups across batches is valuable because it makes the biological question estimable independently of those effects. Random allocation to processing order reduces the chance that a hidden time trend becomes associated with the group being compared.

4. Culture and culturomics

A culture is a population of microorganisms maintained under conditions that support growth. Classical microbial isolation allows a scientist to separate a community into organisms that can be studied individually. An isolate can be characterised with respect to morphology, biochemical properties, genetic identity and its ability to use substrates. If a cultured isolate produces a compound from a specified substrate under defined conditions, the observation directly supports its ability to carry out that conversion under those conditions. It does not establish the same rate of conversion within a living host.

Growth requires an appropriate environment. Gut microbes differ in their requirements for oxygen, nutrients and interactions with other organisms. Some depend on products released by community partners, and an environment suitable for one species may not be suitable for another. The failure of an organism to grow in a particular culture is therefore not sufficient evidence that it was absent from the original sample. Some cells may be viable but not recoverable by the selected method. The set of recovered isolates reflects both biological abundance and the selection imposed by culture conditions.

A colony-forming unit, abbreviated CFU, describes a unit capable of forming a visible colony under the specified assay conditions. A colony may arise from one cell or a group of associated cells. As a result, the measured number of CFUs cannot always be interpreted as the number of individual viable cells. A microbiologist also distinguishes cells that are alive from cells that are capable of division in the selected laboratory environment. Culture measurements are valuable precisely because they provide evidence of growth capacity, but their operational definition must not be lost when they are compared with DNA measurements.

Culturomics extends classical culture by systematically considering a wider range of growth conditions and identification methods. It is motivated by the possibility that many organisms overlooked by routine culture can be recovered when biological requirements are better represented. Identification of recovered isolates can draw on mass-spectral protein patterns or genetic information. A 2018 review by Lagier and colleagues describes the renewed role of culture in investigating the human microbiota. The concept does not imply that all microorganisms become cultivable or that culture-based recovery becomes an unbiased estimate of community composition.

Culture and sequencing offer complementary answers to experimental questions. If community DNA contains a gene associated with a metabolic pathway, recovery of a living organism carrying that gene makes it possible to evaluate its phenotype. If an organism is isolated but rarely detected in a sequence profile, the discrepancy may reflect selective recovery, poor marker amplification or a low initial abundance. Comparing results helps identify the assumptions behind both measurements. The appropriate response to disagreement is not to decide that one whole class of methods is always superior.

An isolate also lacks the ecological interactions of the original community. Cross-feeding occurs when one organism uses a compound produced by another. An isolate capable of using lactate may produce little product in an environment with no lactate supply, even though its activity is substantial in a mixed community. Conversely an isolate may perform a reaction in an artificial environment that is uncommon in the intestine because the relevant substrate is scarce. Experiments comparing isolates and defined communities can help separate direct metabolic capabilities from community-dependent behaviour.

Classical microbiology distinguishes enrichment from quantitative enumeration. Enrichment allows organisms possessing a particular growth capability to become more represented under a selected environment. It is useful when investigators want to recover organisms with a particular phenotype, even when they are initially rare. Enumeration seeks to estimate how many growth-capable units were present in the original specimen under specified conditions. A population after enrichment does not retain the original proportions because growth rates and resource use differ. The investigator must know which type of experiment produced the data before interpreting colony frequency as community abundance.

A conceptual colony-count example helps establish the meaning of CFU. Suppose a calibrated assay estimates 2 × 10^6 colony-forming units per gram of a sample under one set of conditions. A microscopic method estimates 10^8 cells per gram in that sample. It would be unjustified to calculate that exactly 98 percent of the cells are dead. The microscopy count and colony count refer to different operational entities, and the difference can include cells not capable of forming colonies in the test environment, cell aggregates, counting errors and cells that are no longer viable. The correct conclusion is that the growth-based count is much lower than the total cell estimate.

Comparing a cultured organism's behaviour with the function of an intact community requires a control that isolates the relevant difference. In a conceptual experiment investigating substrate conversion, a comparison between substrate-containing material with an isolate and otherwise comparable material without the isolate addresses whether the observed conversion depends on that biological addition. A second comparison lacking the substrate can reveal whether the product is already present or generated from other material. The conclusion remains confined to the experimental environment. Transferring the claim to the intestine requires considering whether the substrate is available and whether community partners or host processes alter the reaction.

A culture-based experiment can also distinguish between organisms that perform sequential steps in a reaction chain. Suppose one organism converts a dietary compound to an intermediate but cannot convert the intermediate further, while another organism converts the intermediate to the final product. A mixed community can exhibit production even when neither isolate produces the final compound directly from the original substrate. Separate observations of the two organisms provide mechanistic information, while the mixed observation demonstrates a community-level function. This is a useful example of why the activities inferred from a list of species are not necessarily additive.

Culture has a distinct role in the evaluation of taxonomy inferred from sequence information. A genetic profile may reveal a lineage that appears associated with a phenotype, but a recovered isolate supplies material for independent confirmation of its identity and evaluation of biological traits. Recovery does not guarantee that the isolate represents every strain assigned to the same taxonomic category. Closely related isolates may differ in metabolic capabilities, and a phenotype observed in one cannot automatically be assigned to the entire species. A scientifically sound conclusion distinguishes the observed isolate phenotype from the broader taxonomic association.

5. 16S rRNA gene profiling

Ribosomes synthesise proteins by translating messenger RNA. The small ribosomal subunit of bacteria and archaea includes 16S ribosomal RNA, a molecule with an essential structural and functional role. Its gene contains regions that are relatively conserved across many organisms as well as regions that vary between lineages. Conserved regions permit broad amplification of related genes, while variable regions provide information that can distinguish many microbial groups. This combination makes the 16S rRNA gene a useful molecular marker.

In marker-gene profiling, community DNA is examined for selected regions of the 16S rRNA gene. The amplification step preferentially increases the quantity of targeted DNA molecules, and sequencing determines the nucleotide order in the resulting fragments. Similarities and differences between those sequences can be compared with reference databases. The output is a description of the detected marker sequences and their inferred taxonomic identities. It is usually presented as a table describing the abundance of different sequence features across specimens.

The words 16S rRNA gene sequencing distinguish the usual DNA-based assay from a measurement of RNA. The DNA marker is not a direct measurement of the number of ribosomes, current protein synthesis or microbial growth rate. It may remain detectable after cell death. Furthermore, bacteria can possess more than one copy of the gene in their genomes. An organism with six copies can contribute six times as many starting templates as an organism with one copy if equal numbers of cells are recovered and all other conditions are identical. A marker-copy abundance is therefore not automatically a cell abundance.

Primers recognise particular sequence regions. A mismatch between a primer and a target sequence can reduce amplification efficiency. A bacterial lineage that amplifies poorly may appear less abundant than it truly is, while another lineage with more compatible primer-binding sites becomes overrepresented. Selection of different variable regions changes which organisms are discriminated effectively. Consequently profiles generated by distinct marker regions or assays are not always directly comparable, even when both are described as 16S sequencing.

Taxonomic resolution depends on the information present in the marker and the quality of the reference database. Two distinct species may share an identical sequence over a chosen short region. A longer or more informative marker may distinguish some such organisms, but closely related strains can remain indistinguishable even when a larger portion of the gene is examined. Reporting the species name with high confidence merely because software has supplied it can exaggerate the actual resolving power of the measurement. Assignment at genus or higher levels may be more defensible when evidence is insufficient.

Operational taxonomic units group sequences according to an analytical similarity rule. Amplicon sequence variants distinguish inferred exact biological marker sequences after modelling or removing errors. Neither concept is equivalent in all cases to a biological species. One species can contribute multiple variants, and distinct species can share a variant. The measured number of sequence features is therefore an operational measure of richness, influenced by the assay and analysis, rather than an exact species census.

Appropriately chosen small-subunit markers can profile bacterial and archaeal communities, but many common 16S primer sets are biased towards bacteria. Fungi and other microbial eukaryotes require suitable eukaryotic markers, such as fungal internal transcribed spacer regions, when marker profiling is used. Viruses do not possess a universal cellular 16S rRNA gene. A standard bacterial marker assay cannot directly describe the complete gut virome. The breadth of the biological conclusion must match the target selected by the assay.

A marker profile may suggest functional differences because relatives often share some genetic features. But closely related organisms may possess different accessory genes, and distantly related organisms may perform similar reactions. Inferring functions from taxonomy depends on reference genomes and assumptions about gene conservation. For a hypothesis that specifically concerns genes encoding a biochemical pathway, direct community DNA analysis is generally more informative than assigning pathway capacity solely from a short marker sequence.

The conserved and variable regions of the 16S gene reflect its biological function and evolutionary history. Because the RNA participates in a fundamental cellular process, many positions are constrained by the structure and operation of the ribosome. Other positions tolerate substitutions more readily and therefore retain useful differences between lineages. Similarity of a short marker sequence provides evidence of relatedness but is not a measurement of every difference in the genome. Two organisms can share a small-subunit marker sequence and still possess different genes for substrate utilisation or host interaction.

Amplification can distort relative representation through unequal efficiency. Consider two DNA targets with the same initial number of copies, one of which amplifies less efficiently than the other. Even a modest difference in the average multiplication per cycle can accumulate across many cycles. The target with greater amplification efficiency becomes more represented in the final product, despite equal starting abundance. This is a conceptual reason why amplification bias must be distinguished from biological change. It also explains why comparing profiles made with different primer sets requires care.

An amplification-derived sequence can occasionally combine portions of different templates. Such a chimera may resemble a sequence from a previously unobserved organism unless analytical checks recognise it. Errors in reading bases can likewise produce variants that do not exist in the original specimen. Analytical processing attempts to distinguish meaningful biological sequence variation from measurement artefacts. It does not transform an assay into an error-free census. Apparent richness can increase when artefactual features are retained, especially when many low-frequency features are included.

Reference-based classification is an inference from similarity and other evidence. When a reference database contains multiple indistinguishable entries over the sequenced region, the appropriate classification may stop at their shared taxonomic group. Conversely, when the true organism is missing from the database, a close known relative may appear to be the best match. The confidence of assignment and the taxonomic rank used are therefore part of the result. A taxonomic table containing names does not demonstrate that every name has been resolved with equal accuracy.

Library depth and marker resolution are independent aspects of measurement. If the only sequenced region is identical in two species, sequencing it many more times will not distinguish them. More depth improves the chance of sampling rare sequences and reduces some forms of random sampling uncertainty, but it does not add nucleotide positions that were never examined. A method with greater resolving power must measure informative features, such as additional genomic regions. This is a common experimental reasoning problem: collecting more of the same information cannot solve a problem caused by lack of discriminatory information.

A marker library can also contain sequences from cellular structures or organisms that are not the intended targets, depending on the primers and specimens. For example, organellar ribosomal genes have evolutionary relationships with bacterial genes. In samples containing dietary plant material or host-derived material, off-target amplification can reduce the representation of useful target reads. The specific outcome depends on the assay. The general lesson is that assay specificity must be examined in the context of the actual sample, not assumed solely from the name of the marker.

6. Shotgun metagenomics

A metagenome is the collective genetic material recovered from a community. Shotgun metagenomics samples fragments of community DNA rather than restricting sequencing to one preselected taxonomic marker. These fragments can originate from bacterial and archaeal chromosomes, mobile genetic elements, viruses and microbial eukaryotes, as well as the host or dietary material. The breadth of the method is an important advantage, but the relative amount of each kind of DNA affects the amount of usable information obtained.

A sequence read is the nucleotide sequence determined from a DNA fragment. Reads can be compared with reference sequences to infer organismal identity or possible gene functions. Overlapping reads may also be assembled into longer contiguous sequences, allowing some genes or genomic regions to be reconstructed. Assembly is a useful conceptual extension of reading a marker: instead of examining only the label on a biological entity, the investigator attempts to recover parts of its genetic information. Nevertheless, repeated sequences and mixtures of closely related strains can make reconstruction ambiguous.

Annotation assigns possible biological meaning to a sequence using evidence such as similarity to known genes, conserved domains and genomic context. A sequence resembling a known enzyme may be classified as a possible member of that functional family. The strength of the assignment depends on the quality of the match and the diversity of functions within the family. The presence of a predicted gene for a pathway step does not necessarily show that an intact pathway exists in one organism. Several organisms may encode different steps, and some of the predicted enzymes may act on other substrates.

The shotgun approach can sometimes achieve species or strain resolution beyond that of a short 16S marker. The improvement arises because more independent genomic features are available for comparison. It still requires sufficient informative sequence coverage and suitable references. Poorly represented organisms can remain difficult to identify, and closely related genomes may share most reads. The absence of a taxonomic assignment therefore differs from biological absence. Unclassified reads can represent organisms absent from reference collections, as well as other sources of uncertainty.

Shotgun sequencing provides evidence about functional potential. If genes involved in carbohydrate breakdown become more abundant in community DNA, the community may possess an increased representation of the corresponding genetic capacity. However, the DNA profile cannot by itself show whether the genes were transcribed, whether their protein products were active or whether the environment supplied appropriate substrates. An intact genetic pathway can be inactive during a particular physiological state. A change in genetic potential is one possible explanation for a change in function, not a direct measurement of that function.

Host DNA is an especially important consideration in tissue-associated samples. Imagine two sequencing libraries containing the same total number of reads. In the first, most reads originate from microorganisms, while in the second most originate from host cells. The second provides less microbial information even though the sequencing instrument generated the same number of reads. This example explains why total sequencing output and usable microbial sequencing depth are different quantities. More reads cannot undo other problems such as contamination or systematic differences in extraction efficiency.

A useful way to understand shotgun sequencing is to imagine a mixed collection of books from which short pieces of text have been recovered. A passage unique to a particular book can identify its origin, while a passage repeated across many books is ambiguous. Overlapping passages may be assembled into longer text. The analogy is limited because genomes can contain rearrangements, repeated sequences and genes exchanged between organisms; they are not fixed independent books. Nevertheless it illustrates why both the amount of sequence and its uniqueness matter when attributing DNA fragments to particular organisms.

Sequencing coverage describes how extensively a reference sequence has been represented. Breadth describes the proportion of positions with evidence, while depth describes how many observations cover positions on average or at a specific site. A set of reads concentrated in one conserved gene provides narrow coverage even when many reads are available. Broad coverage over multiple informative regions can give stronger evidence for the presence of a genome. In a mixed community, rare genomes are likely to have lower coverage than common genomes, limiting what can be reconstructed from them.

Functional annotation can be uncertain for several reasons. Two enzymes may share a conserved domain but catalyse distinct reactions. A sequence may contain only part of a gene, or a gene may be present in a form that does not produce a functional protein. A pathway prediction may rely on several annotated steps that occur in different organisms. In a cross-feeding community, the presence of genes across several organisms can be biologically meaningful, but the distribution of those genes must be established before claiming that an individual species possesses the complete pathway.

The number of reads assigned to a gene is influenced by gene length as well as abundance. Longer genes offer more possible fragments than shorter genes, other factors being equal. When investigators compare representation across genes or samples, they use appropriate measures of gene abundance and consider the denominator employed. A gene's fraction of all assigned reads cannot be interpreted as its copy number per microbial cell without further information. This is conceptually similar to the distinction between marker read fraction and cell fraction, although the relevant technical biases differ.

Suppose a study observes an increased abundance of genes involved in a pathway after a change in diet. There are at least three classes of biological explanation. Organisms possessing those genes may have become more represented; the average genomic representation of the genes may have changed within a taxonomic group, for example through shifts between strains; or the total community composition may have changed in a way that alters the denominator. A taxonomic analysis, absolute measurement and a gene-level comparison provide complementary evidence. The metagenomic observation alone does not choose uniquely among those explanations.

Shotgun sequencing is broader than targeted marker sequencing, but it does not guarantee equal detection of all components of the microbiome. A DNA-based library does not directly describe RNA viruses unless an appropriate RNA-derived measurement is included. Some genomes may be poorly represented because of extraction or reference limitations. Many reads in a tissue sample may originate from the host. Therefore claims about comprehensiveness need to be conditioned on the sample preparation, the molecules targeted and the depth of interpretable sequence information.

7. Metatranscriptomics and metabolomics

Transcription produces RNA molecules using DNA as a template. A metatranscriptome describes the RNA recovered from an ecological community. By examining microbial transcripts, a scientist can investigate which genes were expressed in the sampled population. RNA abundance depends on the number of cells carrying the gene, the number of gene copies in each cell and the production and degradation of its transcripts. A high abundance of a transcript can therefore arise from many cells expressing a gene modestly, or fewer cells expressing the same gene strongly.

A comparison of RNA and DNA measurements helps to distinguish these possibilities. Suppose the abundance of a gene in community DNA increases twofold and its RNA abundance also increases twofold. Under simplified assumptions and compatible normalisation, the ratio of RNA to DNA remains unchanged. There is no evidence from that ratio alone that each copy of the gene produced more transcript. If the DNA abundance remains constant while RNA abundance increases, the observation is more consistent with altered transcription or RNA stability, although compositional effects and technical biases must still be considered.

The community contains ribosomal RNA as well as messenger RNA and other RNA classes. Ribosomal RNA can account for a large portion of extracted RNA, so its representation influences the information available about less abundant transcripts. RNA is also susceptible to changes during collection and degradation. An apparently reduced transcript signal may reflect biological regulation or the loss of particular RNA molecules before they could be measured. Metatranscriptomics studies must therefore distinguish technical recovery from the biological pattern of gene expression.

Franzosa and colleagues compared metagenomic and metatranscriptomic profiles of the human gut in a 2014 study. Their work illustrates why measurements of genetic capacity and gene expression are complementary. The expression of microbial functions can differ from what would be expected from the abundance of the corresponding genes. The underlying biological interpretation is that microorganisms respond to their local environment, while DNA abundance also reflects changes in which organisms and genes occur in the community.

Metabolomics examines small molecules in a biological sample. Chromatography can help separate compounds and mass spectrometry measures ions according to their mass-to-charge relationships. Nuclear magnetic resonance can provide information about molecular structure and abundance through interactions of nuclei with magnetic fields. Targeted metabolomics measures a defined set of compounds, often using standards for identification and quantification. Untargeted approaches detect a broader range of chemical features; some features cannot immediately be assigned a confident molecular identity.

Metabolite concentration is determined by production, consumption, absorption, transport and dilution. Consider a chemical pool in the intestinal lumen. Its amount can increase when microbial synthesis increases, but it can also increase when host absorption or microbial consumption decreases. A faecal concentration is an observation of the remaining material after many processes have acted. It cannot generally be converted into a production rate without additional evidence. This distinction is especially important when a physiological hypothesis concerns the amount of a microbial metabolite that reaches host tissues.

The source of a detected metabolite may be microbial, host-derived or dietary. Several sources may contribute to the same molecular pool. Stable-isotope tracing provides one conceptual approach to following atoms from a labelled substrate into a product. Detection of label in a product supports conversion of the substrate or a derived intermediate, but does not necessarily identify the organism responsible. Combining tracing with controlled microbial comparisons and physiological measurements can strengthen the attribution. Specialist laboratory details are not needed to understand this inference.

A combination of molecular layers provides a more useful interpretation than a list of independent observations. Suppose a dietary intervention increases the representation of genes involved in fermentation, raises selected transcripts and changes the chemical profile. These observations are compatible with a change in microbial metabolic activity. However, even their agreement does not establish that microbial products caused the measured host response. The study still needs a comparison or intervention capable of separating the effects of diet itself from those mediated through microorganisms.

The biological meaning of a transcript abundance can be considered at three scales. At the community scale it describes the amount of RNA assigned to a gene or functional class in the recovered material. At the population scale the investigator may ask whether cells of a particular taxon contribute more or fewer transcripts. At the cellular scale the question is whether individual cells have altered the expression of the gene. A community-wide increase can arise without a change at the cellular scale when the producing population becomes larger. Conversely, cells can strongly induce a gene while their population declines, leaving the total transcript amount unchanged. The scale of the conclusion must therefore be explicit.

The relationship between transcription and metabolism includes translation, protein stability and enzyme regulation. RNA is an intermediate in the flow of information from many genes to proteins, and a change in its abundance may precede a change in the amount of enzyme. The enzyme may remain active after its transcript has declined. Furthermore, a protein can be present without catalysing its reaction at a high rate if its substrate is scarce or an inhibitory product accumulates. For these reasons, even an accurate estimate of messenger RNA per gene copy is not a measurement of enzyme flux. It is evidence about a regulatory layer of the biological system.

Metaproteomics, the examination of proteins recovered from a community, can provide an intermediate layer of evidence between transcripts and chemical measurements. A detected protein corresponding to an enzyme is closer to the biochemical machinery of a reaction than the gene encoding it. However, proteins can share sequence fragments, and their measured abundance is influenced by recovery and detection. The presence of an enzyme does not reveal the concentrations of substrates, cofactors and inhibitors. Thus a protein measurement complements RNA and DNA rather than converting every inferred pathway into an established physiological process. Detailed protein identification methods belong to the Academy's methods units; the relevant principle here is the hierarchy of inference.

A metabolite pool can be described quantitatively using a balance equation. Let M represent the amount of a chemical in a defined intestinal compartment, measured for example in micromoles. Let P be the combined rate of its appearance from production and input, and L the combined rate of disappearance by consumption, absorption and outflow, with both rates expressed in micromoles per unit time. The change in the amount per unit time is P − L. If the amount is approximately constant over an observation interval, appearance and disappearance rates are approximately balanced. A large amount can coexist with a low rate of turnover, and a small amount can coexist with rapid turnover.

As an illustrative example, imagine two intestinal compartments each containing 20 micromoles of a compound. In the first, 5 micromoles per hour appear and 5 disappear; in the second, 50 micromoles per hour appear and 50 disappear. The chemical pools remain constant and equal, but the rates of turnover differ tenfold. A measurement of pool amount alone cannot distinguish these two states. If the second compartment absorbs more of the compound into the host, the physiological exposure may also differ, even though a snapshot of the luminal amount looks identical. The numerical example makes clear why steady concentration should not be interpreted as absence of metabolic activity.

Concentration introduces an additional consideration because it is the amount divided by volume or mass. If water is removed from a compartment while its chemical amount stays constant, concentration increases. Conversely, a larger amount of water can lower concentration without reducing the amount of chemical present. Comparisons of chemical concentration between faecal samples must consider this issue along with differences in production and absorption. A report using micromoles per gram wet stool is measuring a different quantity from a report using micromoles excreted per day. These measurements may be related under suitable assumptions, but one is not a substitute for the other.

A targeted assay may be useful when a hypothesis specifies particular molecules, because calibration provides a clearer basis for comparing their amounts. In an untargeted study, a feature is often initially described by analytical properties such as its mass-to-charge value and the position at which it appears in a separation. A feature is not necessarily a uniquely identified molecule: several compounds can share a mass, and one compound can generate multiple signals. Consequently a difference between groups in the intensity of a feature is first a difference in an analytical observation. A biological claim about a named compound requires sufficiently supported identification.

Stable-isotope tracing helps overcome some of the limitations of static concentration measurements. The stable isotopes of an element have different masses but participate in broadly similar chemical reactions. When a substrate containing a distinguishable isotope is supplied in a controlled investigation, detection of that isotope in downstream molecules helps reveal the movement of atoms through biochemical transformations. The pattern of labelled products depends not only on the reactions but also on dilution by unlabelled pools and alternative pathways. A label in a host molecule may have passed through several microbial and host reactions. The route is investigated by comparing plausible intermediates and suitable controls rather than assuming that the first detectable label establishes the complete mechanism.

A well-designed multi-layer study can distinguish several hypotheses that otherwise fit the same observation. If a host response changes while microbial community DNA remains similar, the relevant explanation may involve changes in expression or substrate availability rather than replacement of the microbial community. If DNA and RNA profiles change but the measured product remains constant, production and loss may have changed together. If a product changes without a detectable shift in either microbial DNA or RNA, altered host handling, a dietary input or an unmeasured pathway may explain the result. This reasoning is more informative than ranking molecular methods as if one of them always provides the final answer.

8. Alpha diversity and quantitative interpretation

Diversity measures describe the structure of a community. Alpha diversity refers to diversity within a defined sample or community. Richness is the number of distinct categories represented. Evenness describes how uniformly abundance is distributed among these categories. Two communities can have the same richness but differ in evenness, and a diversity index can combine both aspects. The categories used might be species, genera or sequence features; the identity of these categories must be stated because the resulting measures need not be interchangeable.

Let n_i be the observed count for category i and N be the sum of all counts. The observed fraction p_i is n_i divided by N. Shannon diversity is H = −Σ p_i ln(p_i), where ln denotes the natural logarithm. The contribution of a very rare category is small because its fraction is small, although its logarithm has a large negative magnitude. A category with zero observed count is assigned a contribution of zero, using the limiting value of p ln(p) as p approaches zero. The maximum possible index increases with richness. For a fixed richness, increasing evenness raises Shannon diversity; simply adding categories does not guarantee a higher index if the distribution also becomes more uneven.

For an illustrative community with counts 50, 50, the proportions are 0.5 and 0.5. Each term contributes −0.5 ln(0.5), approximately 0.3466. Adding the two gives H ≈ 0.6931. A second illustrative community has counts 90 and 10. Its two terms are −0.9 ln(0.9) and −0.1 ln(0.1), approximately 0.0948 and 0.2303. Its total H ≈ 0.3251. The richness is two in both cases. The lower Shannon index in the second community results from the dominance of one category, not the disappearance of the other.

For S equally abundant categories, p_i = 1/S. Substituting this into the expression for H gives H = −S(1/S)ln(1/S) = ln(S). This is the maximum Shannon value for fixed richness S. Exponentiating H gives an effective number of equally abundant categories: exp(H) equals S for a perfectly even community. For the preceding examples, the effective numbers are approximately 2 and 1.38 respectively. The second value expresses the diversity of an uneven two-category community in a form that is easier to compare with an even community.

Sequencing depth influences the detection of uncommon features. A community sampled with very few reads can appear to have low richness simply because rare categories have not been observed. Increasing depth often reveals additional features until the observed richness begins to approach a plateau. A rarefaction curve plots the expected richness against the number of reads sampled. Comparing samples at different levels of coverage without accounting for this issue can confuse measurement effort with biological diversity. Even Shannon diversity, although generally less sensitive than raw richness to rare features, can be affected by sampling.

A diversity index is not a diagnostic measure of health. A rise in Shannon diversity can reflect a redistribution of abundances rather than an improvement in host physiology. An ecosystem may acquire additional unwanted organisms while its diversity increases. Likewise a community adapted to a particular habitat may naturally have lower richness than another community. The interpretation of diversity requires a defined biological comparison, and a physiological claim requires independent evidence about function or host outcomes.

The categories used to calculate alpha diversity must be comparable between samples. Richness measured at genus level is normally less than or equal to richness measured at finer taxonomic resolution for the same fully classified set of organisms. If one sample is analysed as species and another as genera, their richness values cannot be interpreted as if the categories were identical. Likewise the number of amplicon sequence variants is a measure of marker diversity, which can be influenced by more than one variant in a genome. An investigator should define the feature type, quality filters and taxonomic resolution before interpreting differences in diversity.

The effect of adding a rare category illustrates why different diversity indices can respond differently to the same event. Imagine a sample with two categories in equal proportions. If a third category becomes detectable at a very small abundance, richness increases from two to three. Shannon diversity increases much less dramatically because the newly observed category accounts for only a small fraction of the total. The increase in richness may be important for a question about rare organisms, while the modest change in Shannon diversity may be informative for a question about the overall distribution. A single index cannot capture every aspect of community structure.

The Simpson concentration D = Σ p_i² describes the probability that two independent draws from an idealised community belong to the same category, when the proportions represent that community. For proportions 0.5 and 0.5, D = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50. For 0.9 and 0.1, D = 0.81 + 0.01 = 0.82. The larger value represents greater concentration in one category and thus less diversity in this example. Some authors report 1 − D, the Gini–Simpson index, or 1/D, the inverse Simpson index. Their numerical direction is different from D. An exam question must identify the convention before interpreting whether an increase indicates greater diversity.

Evenness can be expressed using Pielou's index J = H/ln(S) for observed richness S greater than one. It compares observed Shannon diversity with the maximum for that richness. In the two-category profiles of equal and unequal abundance, H takes the values 0.693 and 0.325. Dividing each by ln(2) gives approximately 1.00 and 0.469. Thus the two samples have equal observed richness but different evenness. For S = 1 the formula has a zero denominator, and an implementation must use a stated convention or regard the value as undefined. Mathematics here clarifies why richness alone cannot describe a distribution.

Under a simplified random sampling model, if a feature has a true fraction p among detectable molecules, the probability that a single independently sampled read does not represent it is 1 − p. The probability of missing it in n independent draws is (1 − p)^n. The probability of observing it at least once is one minus that quantity. A category present at fraction 0.001 has a probability of detection of approximately 9.5 percent in 100 independent draws, but about 63.2 percent in 1,000 draws. Real sequencing reads are not always independent and biological representation may be biased, so this model is illustrative, not a prediction of exact assay performance. It explains why non-detection is sensitive to sampling effort.

Rarefaction can be understood as examining how observed richness changes when fewer reads are sampled from a larger library. If many rare categories occur in the library, repeatedly drawing a smaller set of reads will often miss some of them. A curve that is still rising steeply suggests that more sampling could reveal additional detectable features. A curve approaching a plateau indicates that additional reads may add fewer observed categories under the existing assay. Neither situation proves that all species in the biological habitat have been identified, because the curve only describes the material represented by that analytical process.

It is possible for an intervention to affect diversity without a uniform effect on every participant. One individual's community may become more even while another's becomes dominated by one taxon. Reporting only the average change can obscure the distribution of individual responses. A paired design can show whether the direction of change is consistent across participants and how the magnitude compares with baseline variability. A useful figure displays both individual values and a group summary where appropriate. This reasoning applies to many physiological measurements, not only microbial diversity, because population averages do not describe every individual's response.

9. Beta diversity and distance plots

Beta diversity compares communities rather than describing one community in isolation. A dissimilarity measure converts two profiles into a number reflecting how differently their categories are represented. Measures differ in their treatment of presence, abundance and evolutionary relatedness. A presence-based measure emphasises whether categories are shared; an abundance-based measure also considers their representation; and phylogenetic measures can incorporate the relationships between organisms. A meaningful conclusion identifies the chosen metric.

For presence or absence data, Jaccard dissimilarity can be written as one minus the number of categories shared divided by the number present in either sample. If sample A contains categories a, b and c while sample B contains b, c and d, two categories are shared and four occur in the union. Jaccard similarity is therefore 2/4 = 0.5, and dissimilarity is 0.5. The measure ignores differences in abundances of the shared categories. Two communities with exactly the same set of observed categories have zero Jaccard dissimilarity regardless of their relative proportions.

Bray–Curtis dissimilarity for non-negative abundance profiles x and y is Σ|x_i − y_i| / Σ(x_i + y_i), provided the denominator is positive. For illustrative profiles (50, 30, 20) and (20, 30, 50), the numerator is 30 + 0 + 30 = 60 and the denominator is 200, giving 0.30. Both profiles contain all three categories, so their Jaccard dissimilarity is zero. Bray–Curtis identifies a redistribution of abundance that the presence-based measure ignores. These results are not contradictory; the metrics answer different questions.

A distance matrix contains the dissimilarity for each pair of samples. Ordination provides a low-dimensional representation of relationships in such data. In a principal coordinates plot, points typically represent individual samples. Points positioned near one another have similar profiles according to the selected distance to the degree that the plotted axes preserve the distance structure. Axes are mathematical summaries rather than direct measurements of individual microbes. The fraction of variation represented and the limitations of displaying only two dimensions should be considered.

A visible separation between two groups does not identify the biological cause of the separation. It may reflect differences in age, diet, medication, sampling site or processing batch. Group dispersion also matters: a group with highly variable profiles differs from a group tightly concentrated around a typical profile. Some statistical comparisons of multivariate distances can respond to differences in dispersion as well as location. Interpretation of a distance plot requires an examination of the design and the spread of points, not merely their colours or apparent separation.

Within-person and between-person comparisons illustrate different uses of beta diversity. A person may have a community that differs substantially from another person's community while showing relatively small differences from their own repeated measurements. A dietary intervention might cause a modest shift that is easier to detect against each person's baseline than against the full variation between people. Pairing measurements can improve sensitivity to change, but the analysis must account for repeated observations from the same individual.

A distance matrix has important mathematical properties that help students interpret community comparisons. A sample has zero distance from itself under the usual dissimilarity definitions, and symmetric measures assign the same distance from A to B as from B to A. Different metrics can disagree about which pair is most different because they emphasise different features. A comparison should use a metric appropriate to the hypothesis. If the question concerns the appearance of new lineages, a presence-based measure may be useful. If the question concerns changes in dominant populations, an abundance-weighted measure may be more informative.

Consider three illustrative profiles A = (70, 20, 10), B = (70, 30, 0) and C = (10, 20, 70). A and B share two of three observed categories, giving Jaccard dissimilarity 1/3. A and C share all three, giving zero. Their Bray–Curtis dissimilarities are quite different: A versus B is (0 + 10 + 10)/200 = 0.10, whereas A versus C is (60 + 0 + 60)/200 = 0.60. The presence metric regards A and C as identical at the level of detected membership, but the abundance metric recognises their very different distributions. Neither measure is erroneous; the choice depends on the biological distinction of interest.

Phylogenetic dissimilarity incorporates the evolutionary relationships represented by a phylogenetic tree. Consider two communities that each possess a different species from the same genus and a third community possessing distantly related lineages. A simple presence-based comparison at species level can regard the three sets as equally distinct if there is no overlap. A phylogenetic comparison can distinguish the case in which different but closely related species occupy the communities from the case in which their evolutionary histories are very different. Measures such as UniFrac use shared and unshared branch lengths in a tree; weighted versions also consider representation. The result depends on the quality of the inferred phylogeny and feature measurements.

An ordination is particularly helpful when there are many samples and a distance table is too large to interpret visually. The positions of plotted points attempt to preserve relevant relationships in a limited number of dimensions. A group that overlaps another in the first two displayed coordinates may still differ along other coordinates. Similarly a visually striking separation can represent only one component of the overall variation. Axis direction or sign can often be changed without altering the underlying relationships, so a claim that moving to the right means increased health is not meaningful unless the biological association with that coordinate has been established separately.

The interpretation of group dispersion deserves an explicit example. Suppose measurements from one treatment group cluster tightly around a central profile and measurements from a second group scatter broadly around approximately the same centre. The groups differ in variability even though their central tendencies are similar. A test based on distances may detect differences related to this spread. A meaningful biological interpretation could be that the exposure produces heterogeneous responses, rather than that it shifts everyone toward a particular composition. Examination questions may ask students to distinguish a change in location from a change in dispersion by inspecting the distribution of points.

The unit of observation in an ordination matters. A plot showing forty points from four individuals sampled ten times each contains forty specimens, not forty independent individuals. If each individual has a characteristic profile, points from the same person may cluster irrespective of the intervention. Statistical testing should respect those dependencies. Pairing and appropriate restrictions on comparisons can help distinguish within-person changes from between-person differences. An apparently large sample count on a plot is not a substitute for identifying the actual independent biological units.

10. Relative abundance, absolute load and qPCR

Most sequencing profiles describe the relative representation of features within a library. When fractions are expressed as percentages they must sum to 100 percent. This constraint is called compositional closure. An increase in the fraction of one category requires a decrease in the combined fractions of others even if the absolute abundance of every other category is unchanged. A relative increase should therefore be interpreted as a change in representation within the measured community, not automatically as a numerical expansion of cells.

Consider an illustrative specimen containing 200 cells of taxon A and 800 cells of taxon B per chosen unit. A accounts for 20 percent of the community. In a second specimen, A still contains 200 cells but B has fallen to 200 cells. A now accounts for 50 percent. The relative proportion of A has increased 2.5-fold even though its absolute abundance has not changed. A conclusion that A proliferated would be incorrect. The observed pattern instead reflects a decrease in the total microbial load caused by the decline in B.

The opposite situation is also possible. If all categories double in absolute abundance, the relative profile remains unchanged. A sequencing comparison based on proportions alone cannot detect this uniform change in load. When a biological hypothesis concerns the delivery of microbial products to a host, the total amount of microbial material may be relevant. Vandeputte and colleagues demonstrated the importance of incorporating microbial load into quantitative gut microbiome profiling. Their work provides an empirical reason to distinguish composition from microbial density.

Quantitative PCR, or qPCR, measures amplification through a detectable signal that increases with the amount of product formed. A sample that starts with more target copies generally reaches a defined detection threshold in fewer amplification cycles, provided efficiency and measurement conditions are comparable. The cycle at which this threshold is crossed is often denoted Cq. With ideal doubling per cycle, a difference of one cycle corresponds to a twofold difference in starting copies. A standard curve relating known target amounts to threshold cycles can be used to estimate the number of copies in an unknown sample.

A difference of three cycles at ideal efficiency corresponds to a factor of 2 cubed, or eight. If specimen X reaches a threshold at cycle 20 and specimen Y at cycle 23, X had approximately eight times the starting target copies in the measured material under the stated ideal assumptions. This is not automatically an eightfold difference in the number of cells per gram: extraction recovery, original specimen mass, dilution, gene copy number and inhibition must be considered. The relation also changes when amplification efficiency differs from ideal doubling.

Independent estimates of total microbial load can come from suitable cell-counting methods or calibrated molecular measurements. Multiplying a relative taxon fraction by an estimated total cell density can approximate taxon density when the fractions are sufficiently representative of cells. If the fraction is a DNA marker-copy fraction and organisms differ in copy number, multiplication without correction can be misleading. Likewise a cell count may include damaged cells or non-target particles depending on the method. Agreement between independent methods strengthens an interpretation, while disagreement can reveal limitations.

Microbiome datasets have compositional properties that affect relationships between features. A negative correlation between two relative proportions does not by itself demonstrate competition. In the simplest two-category community, the fractions are forced to sum to one, so they are perfectly negatively related whenever they vary. Log ratios between positive components can express changes in their relative representation without interpreting the total sequencing count as biological abundance. The ratio still does not establish which component changed in absolute terms. Absolute load measurements and appropriate designs are required for that conclusion.

Compositional reasoning becomes especially important when total microbial load changes due to altered passage time or another physiological factor. Suppose a community contains taxa A, B and C at densities of 10, 20 and 70 million cells per gram. Their fractions are 10, 20 and 70 percent. After an exposure, C declines to 20 million while A and B stay constant. Total load becomes 50 million cells per gram, and the fractions become 20, 40 and 40 percent. Both A and B now appear to have doubled in relative representation although neither changed numerically. A conclusion that the exposure stimulates growth of both taxa would be unsupported.

Even a constant fraction can hide substantial changes. If a taxon contributes 25 percent of a community containing 4 × 10^8 cells per gram, its approximate density is 10^8 cells per gram under the assumption that the fraction represents cells. If total density subsequently rises to 8 × 10^8 cells per gram and the fraction remains 25 percent, the estimated density of the taxon rises to 2 × 10^8 cells per gram. The relative result looks unchanged, while its absolute density has doubled. When a mechanism depends on the number of cells capable of producing a compound, this distinction can change the interpretation of the experiment.

Log ratios are useful because compositions represent information about relative relationships. If fractions p_A and p_B are calculated using the same total, their ratio p_A/p_B equals the ratio of the corresponding underlying measured amounts before division by that total. Taking a logarithm converts multiplicative differences into additive differences. For example, an A:B ratio of 2 has natural log approximately 0.693, while a ratio of 1/2 has natural log approximately −0.693. A change in the log ratio from −0.693 to 0.693 indicates a fourfold increase in the A:B ratio. It does not tell whether A grew, B declined or both changed. The ratio concerns the quantities actually measured, not necessarily unbiased cell numbers.

A zero observed count creates a problem for ordinary log ratios because the logarithm of zero is undefined. Non-detection can result from absence, low abundance or measurement limitations. Replacing zeros with small positive values allows some mathematical calculations but introduces assumptions that can influence results, especially for rare features. The exact statistical treatment belongs to more specialised analysis, but the fundamental point is relevant to experimental reasoning: an analytical convenience must not be mistaken for direct evidence of the biological amount. The interpretation should acknowledge whether the result depends on features near the detection limit.

The ideal qPCR relation follows from a simple model of amplification. Let N_0 be the number of target copies at the start of a reaction and N_c their amount after c cycles. At ideal efficiency N_c = N_0 × 2^c. Suppose samples X and Y are measured with the same threshold T and reach it at Cq_X and Cq_Y. Then N_X × 2^Cq_X = N_Y × 2^Cq_Y = T. Rearrangement gives N_X/N_Y = 2^(Cq_Y − Cq_X). This relation assumes comparable thresholds and amplification efficiencies. At a per-cycle multiplication factor of 1.9 rather than 2, a three-cycle difference corresponds to 1.9 cubed, approximately 6.86 rather than 8.

Absolute quantification needs a connection between the measured aliquot and the original specimen. If a calibrated molecular assay gives a concentration of copies in the analysed extract, the estimate for the original specimen depends on the total extract amount, any dilution and the mass or volume of specimen represented. Recovery efficiency can further alter the estimate. The outcome should be expressed in clear units such as target gene copies per gram. To express it as cells per gram requires an additional assumption or calibration about the number of target copies per cell. A conversion that ignores these distinctions may report many decimal places without achieving biological accuracy.

Flow cytometry can provide another type of microbial load estimate by examining individual particles passing through a detection region. With suitable identification and calibration, counted events can be related to a known volume and then to the original sample. Cell aggregates can register differently from separated cells, and particles that do not represent the target cells can complicate classification. Microscopy can provide a complementary observation of morphology and spatial arrangement. Neither technique independently supplies the detailed taxonomy of all cells counted. Combining a load measurement with a community composition measurement may therefore be more informative than either alone.

A known added reference, often described conceptually as a spike-in, can provide information about recovery or relative scaling. Its usefulness depends on the stage at which it is added. A DNA reference added after extraction cannot reveal how efficiently intact native cells released their DNA earlier. A reference introduced before processing can report more of the recovery chain but may still differ from the organisms of interest in cell properties and analytical behaviour. The purpose is to provide a measurement anchor, not to eliminate every possible bias. Understanding the timing of a control addition is essential to deciding which sources of variation it can address.

11. Experimental design, controls and causal inference

An observational study compares biological conditions without assigning the exposure of interest. A cross-sectional study samples participants at one time or over a short defined period. It can reveal associations but often cannot establish temporal order. A longitudinal study follows individuals over time, providing information about variation, sequence and persistence. Temporal precedence is useful for causal reasoning, but it is not sufficient: an earlier event may be associated with a later response because both are influenced by an unmeasured third factor.

A confounder is a factor that can create or distort an association between an exposure and an outcome. For example, participants with a chronic intestinal condition may differ from controls in both dietary habits and medication use. A microbial feature associated with the condition might instead be associated with the treatment or with changes in food intake. Recording these variables and selecting a design that permits meaningful adjustment can help distinguish the possibilities. If every affected participant receives the same treatment and no controls do, a simple statistical adjustment may not separate condition and treatment reliably because the two factors have not varied independently.

A randomised intervention allocates an exposure using a random mechanism. It can help balance pre-existing differences between groups and provide stronger evidence about the consequences of the intervention. Nevertheless, if the intervention is a dietary change that affects both host physiology and microbes, an observed physiological effect does not establish microbial mediation. To investigate mediation, additional comparisons are necessary: measurement of relevant microbial functions, controlled manipulation of microbial contributions or carefully justified mechanistic evidence.

The experimental unit is the smallest independently assigned unit of an experiment. In an animal study, animals sharing a cage can share environmental exposures and microorganisms. When treatment is assigned to cages, the cage rather than each individual animal may be the independent unit of assignment. Sampling several animals per cage does not necessarily provide the same information as independently treated cages. The distinction prevents pseudoreplication, in which dependent observations are analysed as if they represented independent experimental units.

Biological replicates represent independent biological units appropriate to the question. Technical replicates repeat part or all of a measurement on the same biological material. Technical replication can estimate measurement variability and reveal technical failure, but does not provide new independent individuals. Ten sequencing libraries from one stool specimen cannot establish variation among ten people. Conversely one measurement from each of ten independent participants supplies biological replication but does not directly estimate repeated-measurement precision.

Negative controls, positive controls and biological comparison groups have distinct roles. A blank evaluates background contamination. A known positive material checks measurement capability. A matched untreated group helps evaluate what might have happened without an intervention. An assay can perform perfectly in the laboratory yet the study can remain biologically confounded. Equally, a well-randomised biological study can be undermined by a biased or contaminated measurement. A strong study requires both valid measurement and valid design.

When a study measures thousands of microbial features, some associations can arise by chance even if none truly differ. Testing many hypotheses without accounting for multiplicity increases the chance of false discoveries. An independent validation cohort can help determine whether a result generalises beyond the initial dataset. Effect sizes and uncertainty are also important: a small but statistically detectable difference may be physiologically unimportant, while an uncertain estimate in a small sample may be compatible with a meaningful effect. Statistical significance does not independently establish mechanism.

The next chapter examines germ-free and gnotobiotic animals, which can support controlled tests of microbial effects. These models are valuable because they allow questions about microbial exposure to be investigated more directly than ordinary observations. They also introduce their own limitations, such as differences in developmental history and the challenges of transferring human microbial communities into another host. Understanding the evidence supplied by culture, sequencing and chemical measurements prepares the reader to evaluate those experiments without confusing detection, association and causation.

The selection of biological controls begins with a counterfactual question: what would have happened to the same kind of biological unit in the absence of the exposure? It is impossible to observe one individual simultaneously in both its exposed and unexposed state under otherwise identical circumstances. A comparison group is used to approximate the missing counterfactual. Random allocation is useful because, before treatment, the groups are expected to be similar with respect to both measured and unmeasured factors on average. In finite studies, imbalance may still occur, and loss of participants or deviations from the assigned intervention may complicate the comparison.

A crossover study is a design in which a participant experiences more than one condition in a defined sequence. The participant can serve as their own comparison, reducing the influence of stable individual differences. However, the microbiome can retain changes caused by earlier exposures, and host physiology can change over time. An insufficient separation between conditions may therefore create a carryover effect. Randomisation of condition order and attention to the time required for recovery are important design concepts. A crossover design is not appropriate for every question, particularly when an exposure can produce persistent changes.

The distinction between a confounder and a mediator helps clarify causal hypotheses. A confounder is a prior factor that influences both the exposure and outcome or otherwise produces a misleading association. A mediator lies on the proposed causal pathway from exposure to outcome. In a hypothesis where diet changes microbial activity, which in turn changes host physiology, microbial activity is the proposed mediator. Adjusting away the mediator may remove part of the effect that the investigator wishes to understand. By contrast, an unrelated medication that influences microbial activity and host physiology may require control as a confounding influence. The biological ordering of variables should guide analysis rather than including every measured variable without a causal rationale.

Reverse causation is especially relevant to microbiome observations because the host creates the habitat occupied by microorganisms. Intestinal inflammation can modify secretions, epithelial function and nutrient availability. Such environmental changes may favour some microbial populations and disadvantage others. Therefore an association between a microbial feature and inflammation can arise because the host condition changed the community, because microbial activity contributed to the host condition, or because the two processes reinforce one another. Longitudinal data can constrain the timing of possible explanations, and controlled perturbations can test particular mechanisms, but a single cross-sectional profile cannot establish direction.

An example of experimental design integrates the measurements introduced in this chapter. Suppose investigators hypothesise that a dietary carbohydrate increases microbial butyrate production and that this change improves an epithelial barrier property. The exposure is the dietary change, while the proposed microbial intermediate is production of butyrate. Relevant observations include the representation of genes involved in fermentation, expression of those genes, chemical products and the host barrier endpoint. Repeated sampling characterises temporal patterns, and a comparable control diet helps distinguish the intervention effect from ordinary change. Independent biological units, balanced processing and predefined endpoints help protect the study from misleading technical and statistical effects.

The resulting findings still need to be interpreted according to their limits. If the treated group has higher faecal butyrate and improved barrier measurements, the data support an association within the intervention response. They do not establish that butyrate produced by a particular species caused the barrier change. Diet may act directly on the host, and faecal butyrate may increase for reasons other than greater synthesis. A controlled comparison of microbial contributions, supported by appropriate product and host measurements, can address part of the causal question. This illustrates why an experimentally assigned diet and an experimentally manipulated microbial factor answer different questions even when they occur in the same biological system.

Independent validation is valuable because the discovery process itself selects promising patterns. If a study examines a large number of microbial features and reports the most extreme differences, those selected differences may be larger than their true population effects. A separate group of participants or an independent experiment tests whether the association persists. Reusing the same dataset to select the feature and evaluate its reproducibility does not provide independent validation. Predefining an outcome where possible and reporting uncertainty help readers distinguish a test of a hypothesis from an exploratory discovery that requires further study.

An informative examination answer often states the measurement, the inference it supports and the strongest unresolved alternative explanation in that order. For example, a sequence profile shows a relative increase in a taxon; this supports increased representation among recovered marker molecules; a decrease in other taxa or altered assay recovery remains possible. A chemically measured product increases; this supports an increased measured pool; altered production, consumption, absorption or dilution remains possible. This form of reasoning is not excessive scepticism. It is the discipline that permits evidence from several methods to be combined into a coherent biological explanation rather than allowing one appealing observation to carry a conclusion it cannot establish.

Misconceptions and examination traps

1. Stool is the whole gut

Correct concept: Stool samples distal luminal output, not every compartment. Why it occurs: Convenient access encourages overgeneralisation. Exam reasoning: A biopsy-specific claim based only on stool requires spatial evidence.

2. A read is a cell

Correct concept: Reads depend on extraction, gene copies and amplification. Why it occurs: Count-like output resembles cell census data. Exam reasoning: Distinguish read proportions from cell counts.

3. DNA proves viability

Correct concept: DNA can persist after loss of viability. Why it occurs: Genetic presence is mistaken for a living organism. Exam reasoning: Use growth or independent viability evidence when relevant.

4. 16S identifies every species

Correct concept: Marker sequence resolution varies. Why it occurs: Taxonomy software may report precise names. Exam reasoning: Check whether informative sequences separate the taxa.

5. 16S measures every virus

Correct concept: Viruses lack a universal cellular 16S marker. Why it occurs: Community sequencing is mistaken for comprehensive profiling. Exam reasoning: Choose an assay appropriate to the target.

6. Culture failure proves absence

Correct concept: Growth depends on recoverability in the tested environment. Why it occurs: A negative plate looks like an empty habitat. Exam reasoning: Interpret non-recovery conditionally.

7. Shotgun DNA equals function

Correct concept: Genes represent potential. Why it occurs: Pathway annotations look like measured metabolic activity. Exam reasoning: Seek expression and functional evidence.

8. More RNA always means greater expression per cell

Correct concept: Population size, gene copies and RNA stability matter. Why it occurs: Transcript abundance is confused with per-cell regulation. Exam reasoning: Compare appropriately calibrated DNA and RNA.

9. A higher metabolite concentration means faster production

Correct concept: Absorption, consumption, transport and dilution influence the pool. Why it occurs: A concentration is treated like a rate. Exam reasoning: Consider mass balance and additional measurements.

10. Relative increase proves growth

Correct concept: A decrease in other taxa can raise the fraction. Why it occurs: Percentages are read as absolute counts. Exam reasoning: Estimate total load and correct assay bias.

11. Higher richness always means better health

Correct concept: Diversity measures ecological structure, not clinical benefit. Why it occurs: The word diversity carries a positive association. Exam reasoning: Require host physiological outcomes.

12. Same alpha diversity means same community

Correct concept: Different taxonomic identities can share a diversity index. Why it occurs: A single summary statistic hides identity. Exam reasoning: Compare composition and beta diversity.

13. Separated clusters prove causality

Correct concept: Separation depends on metric, confounding and dispersion. Why it occurs: Plots visually imply a mechanism. Exam reasoning: Check design, controls and competing explanations.

14. Technical repeats are independent subjects

Correct concept: Repeated aliquots share biological origin. Why it occurs: A large measurement count looks like a large sample size. Exam reasoning: Identify independent assignment or sampling units.

15. A clean negative control eliminates all bias

Correct concept: Blanks address certain contamination sources, not all measurement bias. Why it occurs: One passing check is mistaken for full validity. Exam reasoning: Evaluate positive controls, batch, extraction and sampling.

16. Temporal precedence is sufficient for causality

Correct concept: A prior event may share a cause with the outcome. Why it occurs: Before-and-after change resembles intervention evidence. Exam reasoning: Consider confounding, comparison groups and causal tests.

Original examination practice

Part B

1. What does conventional 16S rRNA gene profiling directly examine?

  1. All microbial metabolites
  2. All viral genomes
  3. Selected DNA regions of the small-subunit rRNA gene
  4. Current translation rate
Answer and explanation

C. The marker assay targets DNA; it does not quantify translation, chemical products or the complete virome. A 16S DNA read is not a measurement of active ribosomes. Metabolite assays and viral-targeted methods address different molecular quantities.

2. Which specimen is usually most practical for repeated noninvasive intestinal sampling?

  1. Portal blood
  2. Stool
  3. Resected intestinal wall
  4. Mucosal biopsy
Answer and explanation

B. Stool can be collected repeatedly without an invasive procedure; biopsy is more spatially specific. Mucosal biopsy is appropriate for some location-specific hypotheses but is invasive. A blood specimen measures a different compartment.

3. Which property of the 16S gene makes it useful for taxonomic profiling?

  1. It contains conserved and variable regions
  2. It occurs only in fungi
  3. It is translated into an enzyme
  4. It is identical in all bacteria
Answer and explanation

A. Conserved regions enable broad targeting while variable regions differentiate many lineages. Conservation enables broad primer design, whereas variable bases support comparison among taxa. Neither feature guarantees species-level resolution for every organism.

4. Which measurement most directly provides evidence of growth under defined conditions?

  1. Read proportion
  2. Gene annotation
  3. Shannon index
  4. Colony formation
Answer and explanation

D. Colony formation is evidence of reproductive capacity under the tested conditions, unlike the other measurements. DNA, RNA and chemical measurements can support other inferences but alone do not show that a cell multiplied in the tested medium.

5. What is a metagenome?

  1. Only secreted proteins
  2. Collective genetic material from a community
  3. A list of host hormones
  4. Only expressed mRNA
Answer and explanation

B. Community DNA comprises genetic material from multiple organisms, not only expression products. A metagenome may include genetic material from many taxa; a metatranscriptome instead describes recovered RNA.

6. Which approach is most appropriate for measuring community messenger RNA?

  1. Metatranscriptomics
  2. Culture alone
  3. Jaccard dissimilarity
  4. 16S DNA profiling
Answer and explanation

A. RNA profiling examines transcripts, whereas DNA profiling measures genetic material. An ordinary DNA library reports genetic potential. Culturing isolates provides different phenotypic evidence, not a community-wide transcript profile.

7. Which term describes the number of observed categories within a sample?

  1. Beta diversity
  2. Dispersion
  3. Richness
  4. Functional flux
Answer and explanation

C. Richness counts observed categories; it does not measure their evenness or activity. Evenness and Shannon diversity concern the distribution of categories; beta diversity compares samples.

8. Which index explicitly compares community dissimilarity using presence and absence?

  1. Cq
  2. CFU
  3. Shannon H
  4. Jaccard dissimilarity
Answer and explanation

D. Jaccard uses shared and total observed categories. Shannon is a within-community measure. An abundance-sensitive Bray–Curtis measure can distinguish relative profiles even when the sets of detected taxa are identical.

9. Which is an extraction negative control?

  1. A tube without specimen processed through extraction
  2. A sample with known target DNA
  3. A second sample from an unrelated person
  4. A tube containing a known mock community
Answer and explanation

A. A blank detects introduced background; known DNA and mock communities are positive controls. A positive reference tests recovery; the extraction blank instead tests for background introduced in the absence of the specimen.

10. What does a mock community of known composition help assess?

  1. The patient's clinical diagnosis
  2. Assay recovery and representation bias
  3. The direction of microbial causation
  4. The host's total energy expenditure
Answer and explanation

B. Expected composition can be compared with recovered signals to evaluate measurement performance. A mock community may expose taxon-specific recovery bias but cannot perfectly reproduce the complexity of stool.

11. Which statement about conventional bacterial marker profiles is accurate?

  1. They distinguish every strain
  2. They measure all viruses
  3. They provide absolute cell counts directly
  4. Their proportions can depend on primer and gene-copy bias
Answer and explanation

D. Primer compatibility, gene-copy number and marker resolution constrain inference. Equal cell counts can yield unequal marker counts, and a short identical marker region may be shared by multiple species.

12. Which method can measure a specified chemical product using calibration standards?

  1. 16S amplicon sequencing
  2. Taxonomic annotation
  3. Targeted metabolomics
  4. Beta diversity
Answer and explanation

C. Targeted metabolomics can identify and quantify selected chemicals; the other approaches are not direct chemical assays. A calibrated targeted assay is useful when identity and amount of a specific product matter. A diversity index is not a chemical concentration.

13. What is a biological replicate in a human population study?

  1. A measurement from another independently sampled participant
  2. Repeated reads from one library
  3. A second scan of one metabolite spectrum
  4. Two PCR reactions from one extract
Answer and explanation

A. Independent participants contribute information about biological variability. Repeated technical measurements do not. Aliquots and repeated library preparations estimate technical variation; they do not increase the number of independent humans.

14. Why is a standard bacterial 16S marker inappropriate for comprehensive viral profiling?

  1. Viruses have no nucleic acids
  2. Viruses cannot occur in the gut
  3. Viruses lack a universal cellular 16S rRNA gene
  4. Viruses always contain fungal ITS
Answer and explanation

C. Viruses can contain DNA or RNA but do not possess universal cellular ribosomal markers. Viruses are not cellular organisms with ribosomes; viral DNA and RNA require suitable targets.

15. In ideal qPCR, a lower Cq generally indicates what when assays are comparable?

  1. Higher richness necessarily
  2. More initial target copies
  3. Fewer initial copies
  4. Lower sequencing depth
Answer and explanation

B. Greater initial template reaches the threshold in fewer cycles, subject to efficiency and comparability. The result depends on comparable reaction efficiency, threshold and calibration. Cq alone is not an absolute cell count.

16. What does functional redundancy mean in community ecology?

  1. Diversity must decrease
  2. All genes are always expressed
  3. Every species has an identical genome
  4. Different organisms can support overlapping functional capacities
Answer and explanation

D. Overlapping capabilities need not imply identical regulation, ecological roles or reaction rates. Genes in distinct taxa can encode overlapping functions, but expression, substrate access and the actual host effect may differ.

17. What is the maximum Shannon index for S equally abundant categories using natural logs?

  1. S²
  2. 0 for every S
  3. 1/S
  4. ln(S)
Answer and explanation

D. Each category contributes −(1/S)ln(1/S), and summing S terms gives ln(S). Uneven distributions yield a smaller Shannon index at the same richness. The maximum occurs at equal proportions.

18. What is a cross-sectional study?

  1. A randomised microbial transfer experiment
  2. A comparison at one time or defined short interval
  3. Repeated measurements over many years by definition
  4. A method of DNA extraction
Answer and explanation

B. Cross-sectional designs describe contemporaneous associations rather than tracking change. A longitudinal design follows change through time. Neither design alone guarantees that an observed association is causal.

19. Which issue arises when all cases are processed in one batch and all controls in another?

  1. Confounding of biological group with batch
  2. Improved absolute quantification
  3. Guaranteed removal of contaminants
  4. Perfect biological replication
Answer and explanation

A. Group and technical conditions co-vary completely, making their effects difficult to separate. A perfectly aligned batch and biological group cannot be distinguished statistically from those data alone without additional assumptions.

20. What does a principal coordinates plot generally represent?

  1. Exact intestinal anatomy
  2. Direct rates of bacterial metabolism
  3. A reduced-dimensional representation of sample dissimilarities
  4. Absolute cell density along each axis
Answer and explanation

C. Ordination summarises distance relationships, not literal biological coordinates. Axis coordinates are calculated summaries and should not be interpreted as measured concentration, anatomy or causation.

Part C

21. Sample X has 100 cells A and 400 B; sample Y has 100 A and 100 B. What changed for A?

  1. Its fraction rose from 20% to 50% without growth
  2. Its fraction fell
  3. Its absolute abundance fell
  4. Its cell number quadrupled
Answer and explanation

A. 100/500 = 0.2 and 100/200 = 0.5. Absolute A is unchanged; B declined. A change in denominator alone explains the proportion increase, so concluding that A grew would be incorrect.

22. Two samples have proportions (0.5,0.5,0) and (0,0.5,0.5) over taxa A, B and C. Which combination of alpha and beta diversity is correct?

  1. Both have H≈0.693; both dissimilarities are zero.
  2. The second has higher Shannon diversity; Jaccard dissimilarity is 1/3.
  3. Both have H≈1.099; Bray–Curtis is 1.
  4. Both have Shannon H≈0.693; Jaccard dissimilarity is 2/3 and Bray–Curtis is 0.5.
Answer and explanation

D. Each sample has two equally abundant taxa, so H=ln(2). They share one of three taxa, so Jaccard dissimilarity=1−1/3=2/3. Bray–Curtis=(0.5+0+0.5)/(1+1)=0.5. Equal alpha diversity does not imply identical composition.

23. An equal-depth sample contains four equally represented taxa; after treatment the same four taxa have proportions (0.7,0.1,0.1,0.1). Which result best describes the change?

  1. Richness falls to one and H becomes zero.
  2. Richness stays at four and H rises to 1.609.
  3. Richness stays at four, but Shannon H falls from about 1.386 to 0.940.
  4. Richness rises to five because the dominant taxon is more abundant.
Answer and explanation

C. Initially H=−4(0.25 ln 0.25)=1.386. After treatment H=−0.7 ln 0.7−3(0.1 ln 0.1)≈0.940. All four taxa remain, so observed richness is unchanged; the loss of evenness explains the lower H.

24. Samples A={a,b,c}, B={b,c,d,e}. Find Jaccard dissimilarity.

  1. 0.2
  2. 0.6
  3. 0.4
  4. 0.8
Answer and explanation

B. Intersection has 2 and union has 5; dissimilarity = 1−2/5=0.6. Using the intersection divided by union gives similarity rather than dissimilarity.

25. For abundance profiles (40,60) and (70,30), Bray–Curtis dissimilarity is:

  1. 0.60
  2. 0.30
  3. 1
  4. 0
Answer and explanation

B. Absolute differences sum to 60; total counts across profiles sum to 200; 60/200=0.3. Using only one sample's total as the denominator would incorrectly double the dissimilarity for these equal-depth profiles.

26. A taxon comprises 10% of 10^9 cells/g in one sample and 20% of 10^8 cells/g in another. Assuming unbiased cell fractions, what happens?

  1. It falls to zero
  2. It doubles
  3. It is unchanged
  4. It falls from 10^8 to 2×10^7 cells/g
Answer and explanation

D. Multiply each fraction by load; the relative increase masks a fivefold absolute decrease. The fraction doubles, but total density decreases tenfold; the combined result is a fivefold fall.

27. A community contains 100 cells of taxon A with one 16S gene copy per cell and 100 cells of taxon B with four copies. Extraction recovers all A templates but only half of B templates. Assuming equal amplification thereafter, what is the expected marker fraction for B?

  1. Two-thirds, although B represents half the cells.
  2. Four-fifths, because extraction does not affect marker fractions.
  3. One-half, because both taxa have equal cell numbers.
  4. One-third, because B has lower recovery.
Answer and explanation

A. A contributes 100×1×1=100 recovered copies; B contributes 100×4×0.5=200. B therefore contributes 200/300=2/3. Both copy-number differences and unequal recovery can separate marker proportions from cell proportions.

28. Two qPCR samples cross a common threshold at cycles 18 and 22. Each cycle multiplies product by 1.9 rather than 2. What is the estimated starting-copy ratio X:Y?

  1. 16:1 irrespective of efficiency.
  2. 4:1 because there are four cycles between thresholds.
  3. About 13.0:1 under the common-efficiency model.
  4. 1:13 because the earlier threshold indicates fewer copies.
Answer and explanation

C. With common multiplication factor 1.9, X:Y=1.9^(22−18)=13.0321. The ideal-doubling estimate of 16 is inappropriate here. The earlier threshold indicates more starting copies if other conditions are comparable.

29. Two specimens have 100 contaminant copies each; their authentic copies are 900 and 100. Which interpretation is best?

  1. Both have 50% contamination
  2. The specimen with more authentic copies is more contaminated proportionally
  3. The fractions are 10% and 50%
  4. Both have 10% contamination
Answer and explanation

C. 100/(900+100)=10%; 100/(100+100)=50%. Equal background affects low-biomass samples more. Absolute contaminant counts are equal, but the denominator differs, so the relative effect is much larger in the low-biomass specimen.

30. A calibrated community DNA assay finds twofold more copies of a gene after an intervention, while a matched RNA assay finds fourfold more transcripts. Which statement is best?

  1. The transcript-to-gene-copy ratio quadruples, proving fourfold reaction flux.
  2. The transcript-to-gene-copy ratio halves because DNA copies increase.
  3. The gene is necessarily expressed fourfold more per microbial cell.
  4. The transcript-to-gene-copy ratio doubles, but enzyme activity and reaction flux remain unmeasured.
Answer and explanation

D. For comparable absolute measurements, the new RNA/DNA ratio is 4/2=2 times baseline. This is evidence of increased transcript abundance per gene copy, not necessarily per cell if copy number or population composition differs. Protein abundance, activity and flux are distinct.

31. Faecal butyrate rises while microbial pathway DNA and transcripts are unchanged. Which explanation is compatible?

  1. Reduced host absorption could elevate the residual pool
  2. Butyrate production must have doubled
  3. All butyrate producers disappeared
  4. RNA assays directly prove production rate
Answer and explanation

A. A pool reflects several inputs and losses, including absorption. Production cannot be inferred uniquely. Unchanged DNA and RNA do not demonstrate constant flux. A static chemical pool also reflects loss, transport and dilution.

32. Cases and controls separate in an ordination, but each group used a different extraction kit. What is the strongest interpretation?

  1. The kits cannot matter because sequencing was deep
  2. Biological and batch explanations cannot be separated from these data alone
  3. Disease has been proven to cause dysbiosis
  4. The plot identifies the causal taxon
Answer and explanation

B. Perfect confounding prevents a clean attribution. Balanced processing or additional independent evidence is needed. The visual separation cannot establish disease causation. A balanced design or an independently replicated result is needed.

33. A diet trial randomises treatment to four cages, each with five mice. What are the treatment assignment units?

  1. Five cages
  2. Four cages
  3. One sequencing read per animal
  4. 20 independent mice
Answer and explanation

B. Treatment was allocated to cages; shared exposure and microbial exchange make animals within cages dependent. Individual mice may be measured separately, but cage-level assignment determines replication of the treatment.

34. Five aliquots of each of two people are sequenced before and after a diet change. A test treats the 20 libraries as 20 independent subjects. What is the principal problem?

  1. The test inflates biological replication; there are two independent people with paired observations and nested technical replicates.
  2. There are ten independent people because there are ten baseline libraries.
  3. The 20 libraries are independent because each was sequenced separately.
  4. Only one independent person is represented because all libraries use the same method.
Answer and explanation

A. The independent human participants number two, each measured repeatedly. Aliquots add technical information, not independent participants. A paired analysis at participant level respects the design, but two participants still provide very limited population-level inference.

35. Taxon T is detected in 8/10 high-depth libraries and 2/10 low-depth libraries, and all high-depth libraries belong to cases. Which conclusion is most defensible?

  1. The taxon is fourfold more abundant in every case.
  2. The taxon is absent from all eight negative controls.
  3. Detection frequency is confounded with library depth; compare at adequate comparable coverage or validate with an independent targeted assay.
  4. Depth cannot alter detection frequency when a taxon is biologically present.
Answer and explanation

C. A low-abundance taxon may be missed at limited sampling depth. Since depth is associated with group, detection prevalence is ambiguous. Presence frequency is also not a quantitative abundance ratio.

36. Two shotgun libraries each contain 10 million reads, of which 90% and 10% are microbial. A microbial gene accounts for 1% of microbial reads in both. Which inference is justified?

  1. Each contains 100,000 gene reads because total library sizes are equal.
  2. The second specimen necessarily contains ninefold fewer microbial cells per gram.
  3. The gene's measured fraction within microbial reads is ninefold lower in the second.
  4. The libraries contain about 90,000 and 10,000 reads for the gene, but the equal within-microbial fractions alone do not establish equal absolute gene copies per gram.
Answer and explanation

D. The usable microbial reads are 9 million and 1 million; 1% gives 90,000 and 10,000. Unequal host DNA representation affects read counts. Neither total reads nor within-microbial fractions supply an independently calibrated cell or gene density.

37. A marker region is identical in two species. Which follow-up is best to distinguish them?

  1. Treat the species as proven identical
  2. Calculate Shannon diversity again
  3. Use informative genomic features via suitable shotgun analysis
  4. Increase reads of exactly the same indistinguishable marker
Answer and explanation

C. Additional reads of the same sequence cannot resolve indistinguishable targets; informative genomic variation may. Sequencing more identical marker fragments improves sampling precision but adds no discriminatory sites.

38. A taxon's proportion and another taxon's proportion are negatively correlated in a two-taxon system. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. Competition is proven
  2. The first must increase in absolute abundance
  3. One inhibits the other
  4. The association can arise from compositional closure alone
Answer and explanation

D. Their fractions sum to one, forcing an inverse relation without evidence of biological interaction. Absolute counts could both increase, both decline or move in different directions while fractions remain negatively related.

39. A labelled dietary substrate yields labelled product in stool. What does this establish most directly?

  1. Atoms from the substrate or its intermediates entered the product
  2. Host absorption is absent
  3. One specific bacterium is responsible
  4. The product cannot have a host contribution
Answer and explanation

A. Tracing supports a conversion route, but additional comparisons are needed to attribute the reaction to an organism. The label establishes an atom-transfer relationship; an unlabeled pool, host metabolism and cross-feeding can complicate source attribution.

40. An intervention changes both microbial composition and host glucose. What additional evidence best tests microbial mediation?

  1. A larger title on an ordination plot
  2. A controlled microbial comparison or manipulation with measured host outcomes
  3. Only a cross-sectional correlation
  4. More technical replicates of the same library alone
Answer and explanation

B. Randomisation establishes effects of the assigned intervention more strongly than microbial mediation; a specific causal comparison helps. Microbial measurements should be connected to a controlled comparison and a relevant physiological endpoint. Correlation after an intervention alone cannot establish mediation.

SET and application

41. Assertion–reason: Stool is useful for longitudinal sampling because repeated collection is noninvasive. Evaluate both.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Assertion true; reason false
  3. Both false
  4. Both true; reason unrelated
Answer and explanation

A. Noninvasive sampling permits repeated collection without biopsy. This is a practical advantage for repeated sampling, not evidence that stool represents every intestinal compartment.

42. Assertion–reason: Every species has exactly one 16S gene because rRNA participates in translation. Evaluate.

  1. Assertion true; reason false
  2. Both false
  3. Assertion false; reason true
  4. Both true
Answer and explanation

C. Copy number varies; rRNA is a ribosomal component involved in translation. The importance of ribosomal RNA does not constrain each organism to a single genomic copy of its gene.

43. Assertion–reason: DNA pathway detection proves pathway flux because DNA can be transcribed. Evaluate.

  1. Assertion true; reason false
  2. Both true
  3. Both false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Answer and explanation

D. The possibility of transcription does not demonstrate expression, active enzymes or flux. Transcription is only one link between DNA and flux; substrate availability, enzyme function and competing reactions also matter.

44. What would a presence-based metric report if two samples share every detected taxon but have different proportions?

  1. Maximum dissimilarity
  2. Zero Jaccard dissimilarity
  3. A Shannon value of zero
  4. A direct absolute-load difference
Answer and explanation

B. Presence-based Jaccard ignores the redistribution of abundances. Abundance-sensitive metrics may nevertheless detect a large difference because the shared taxa can have very different proportions.

45. A student measures target gene copies per gram wet stool. Which extra information helps interpret differing water content?

  1. Only the sequencing instrument name
  2. Only a taxonomic tree
  3. Dry mass or moisture information
  4. The colour of the plot
Answer and explanation

C. The denominator changes with hydration, so wet and dry mass comparisons need context. Changes in water can alter the denominator even when the amount of genetic material associated with dry material remains unchanged.

46. Which finding is the most direct evidence that an isolate performs a reaction in the tested culture environment?

  1. An unrelated person's diversity score
  2. Measured substrate conversion into a product by the isolate with appropriate controls
  3. Predicted pathway potential alone
  4. A short marker taxonomic assignment
Answer and explanation

B. Measured conversion supports phenotype under tested conditions; genetic and ecological information alone do not. Gene detection or taxonomic identification supplies weaker evidence of conversion than a controlled measurement of substrate disappearance and product appearance.

47. Which pairing correctly links question and method?

  1. Genetic potential — shotgun metagenomics
  2. RNA expression — stool wet weight
  3. Viable growth — Shannon index
  4. Absolute production flux — 16S read percentages
Answer and explanation

A. Shotgun DNA supports genetic capacity; other pairings confuse measured quantities. The other molecular layers need their own measurements. Genetic potential does not automatically establish transcription or activity.

48. What is an important limitation of untargeted metabolomics?

  1. It never detects chemicals
  2. It cannot provide any useful comparisons
  3. It measures every compound with equal sensitivity
  4. Some features lack confident molecular identification
Answer and explanation

D. Detected spectral features may not be confidently assigned identities without further evidence. Accurate mass alone can be shared by different molecules; additional identification evidence and standards may be needed.

49. Which design distinguishes within-person change from baseline variation most directly?

  1. Repeated measurements before and after the intervention with suitable controls
  2. One specimen per unrelated person only
  3. Technical replicates of a single sample only
  4. A single negative control only
Answer and explanation

A. Repeated baseline and follow-up observations establish temporal patterns and support within-person comparisons. Repeated observations from the same person account for individual baseline variation, whereas unrelated cross-sectional participants cannot provide that comparison.

50. Two independent analyses agree that a gene is present, expressed, and a matching product is found. Which statement is strongest?

  1. Host causality is proven
  2. Convergent evidence supports the proposed function, but a causal host effect needs further tests
  3. No controls are required
  4. The chemical necessarily comes from one organism
Answer and explanation

B. Multiple measurement layers strengthen the hypothesis without uniquely establishing source or host mediation. The observations support the proposed biochemical link but not uniquely its organismal source, reaction rate or causal effect on the host.

Revision and mastery

Choose the method by the quantity that must be measured: culture demonstrates recoverable growth, marker DNA profiles selected lineages, community DNA supports genetic potential, RNA supports expression and metabolomics measures chemical features. None alone establishes a causal physiological effect.

Examination revision strategy

Practise distinguishing the measured quantity from the inferred quantity. Recalculate Shannon and Bray–Curtis by hand, compare relative and absolute abundance, then examine the experimental unit, control groups and plausible alternative explanations before interpreting a data figure.

Mastery checklist

  • I can interpret composition, load, diversity and experimental controls quantitatively.
