1. The biological problem: seeing the contents of another cell

A virus that has entered a living cell can escape recognition by an antibody even while it is making hundreds of copies of its own proteins. The cell membrane separates these proteins from the extracellular fluid, and intact antibodies ordinarily cannot diffuse through the lipid bilayer. The immune system therefore needs a means of investigating what is being synthesized and degraded inside living cells without opening them indiscriminately. A second problem arises when a macrophage engulfs a microorganism. The macrophage must communicate information about the organism to lymphocytes that have never physically encountered it. In both cases the information can be communicated in the form of molecular fragments displayed at the plasma membrane. The molecular apparatus that makes this display possible is the major histocompatibility complex, or MHC.

The word antigen refers to a substance recognized by an antigen receptor. Recognition by an antibody or B-cell receptor frequently involves a surface formed by the folded three-dimensional antigen. Recognition by conventional alpha-beta T cells is different. For most such cells the ligand is a complex made from a short peptide and a host-derived MHC molecule. The peptide is derived by cutting a larger protein into fragments. The MHC molecule holds one such fragment in a groove and places it at a membrane surface where another cell can inspect it. The T-cell receptor contacts the exposed peptide and the surrounding MHC platform. Thus antigen recognition by these T cells depends on both the sequence of the fragment and the molecular identity of the presenting platform.

Antigen processing is the collection of events that makes suitable fragments available for loading. Antigen presentation is the display of peptide-bound molecules where a T-cell receptor can encounter them. These are related but not identical functions. A cell can produce a peptide without presenting it if the peptide cannot reach a suitable MHC molecule. A cell can also display many MHC molecules but fail to present a particular peptide if it is never generated from the source protein. This distinction allows us to interpret a common experiment: a cell that fails to stimulate a T-cell clone with intact protein may recover its stimulatory ability when a suitable short peptide is supplied. The rescue identifies a difference between the two conditions, but does not automatically identify the precise processing enzyme at fault.

There are two major classes of classical peptide-presenting MHC molecules. Class I normally provides a sample of the proteins associated with the intracellular environment and is surveyed by CD8 T cells. Class II is particularly important for reporting material handled in endocytic compartments and is surveyed by CD4 T cells. This division is useful because the two major kinds of lymphocyte coordinate different responses. An infected epithelial cell that displays a viral peptide in class I may become a target for an already activated cytotoxic lymphocyte. A macrophage presenting microbial peptides in class II may receive assistance from a helper lymphocyte that enhances its antimicrobial functions. These are typical outcomes, not automatic consequences of a particular peptide appearing on a cell.

The presentation system operates continuously even in the absence of infection. Normal turnover of self proteins supplies peptides that occupy class I molecules. Class II molecules on antigen-presenting cells likewise carry many self-derived peptides. The immune system does not use the mere presence of an MHC molecule as evidence of danger. It combines the specificity of lymphocyte receptors, the selection of lymphocytes during development, the activation state of presenting cells and other contextual signals. This continuous display also means that a cell lacking stable surface class I is unusual: innate natural killer cells may detect the loss of inhibitory self signals. The surveillance of class I expression and the inspection of particular peptide–MHC complexes are complementary but mechanistically distinct processes.

For the rest of this chapter, a useful way to organize the subject is to follow the antigen, the MHC molecule and the responding lymphocyte separately. The antigen has an origin and a route through cell compartments. The MHC molecule is synthesized, assembled, loaded and transported. The responding cell must bear a receptor compatible with the displayed complex and be in a state that allows a response. Only after the three histories intersect at the cell surface can antigen-specific cellular recognition occur. This framework connects the molecular biology of protein turnover and membrane traffic with the cell biology of immunity.

2. From graft rejection to the molecular explanation of restriction

The name major histocompatibility complex reflects an earlier biological question rather than the function that immunology now emphasizes. Histocompatibility describes the compatibility of tissues between individuals. Transplanted tissue can be rejected when donor and recipient differ in inherited molecules that the recipient's immune system recognizes. Experiments in genetically defined mice established that a major inherited region profoundly influenced graft survival. Mouse geneticists called this region H-2. The corresponding human region was defined through human leukocyte antigens, abbreviated HLA. These findings showed that important cell-surface immunological differences could be followed through families and inherited strains rather than being generated independently in each lymphocyte.

An important change in interpretation came from investigations of antiviral cellular immunity by Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty in the 1970s. In studies using lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus in mice, the investigators examined the ability of immune lymphocytes to kill infected target cells. Infection alone was insufficient to make a target susceptible to a particular antiviral response: compatibility of the relevant H-2 molecules between responding and target cells also mattered. Conversely, an uninfected target with the matching H-2 type was not killed merely because it shared that inherited type. These observations revealed two requirements for antigen-specific cytotoxic recognition and led to the concept of MHC restriction.

Consider the logic of a simplified version of this classical experiment. A T-cell population comes from an infected mouse expressing MHC type X. Target cells are prepared in four categories: type X infected, type X uninfected, type Y infected, and type Y uninfected. Specific lysis of the first category, but not the remaining three, supports the proposition that the response needs the viral antigen in the appropriate genetic context. The matching uninfected control excludes simple recognition of type X alone. The mismatched infected control excludes a response that depends only on infection. The mismatched uninfected control establishes a background response. This four-way comparison is an instructive model of the historical finding, not a reproduction of numerical data from the original experiments.

A further question was how a cell could display the relevant combination. In one possible model, T cells would use separate receptors for the foreign antigen and for MHC. In another, a single antigen receptor would sense a composite structure. Molecular work on T-cell receptors and MHC proteins supported the latter explanation for conventional peptide-specific T cells. The sequence and structure of the receptor and the physical arrangement of its ligand revealed that the receptor could contact a peptide together with its presenting molecule. The immunological specificity observed in whole animals consequently became understandable in terms of interactions between macromolecular surfaces.

In 1987, the crystallographic studies of Pamela Bjorkman and colleagues supplied an important structural explanation using HLA-A2, a human class I molecule. The extracellular platform possessed a groove with a beta-sheet floor and flanking alpha helices. Polymorphic residues were concentrated in regions relevant to peptide binding and recognition. The structural finding helped explain why a protein originally identified as a transplantation antigen could act as a platform for short antigenic peptides. A small change in the amino acid sequence of the platform could change the chemical properties of a binding pocket or the region contacted by a T-cell receptor. The resulting differences had consequences for both which peptides were displayed and how they were recognized.

The historical sequence teaches an important lesson about evidence. A graft experiment identifies inherited determinants of a tissue response, but cannot establish the atomic shape of a binding groove. An infected-target assay establishes the dependence of cellular recognition on particular variables, but cannot by itself show the identities of all molecular contacts. A crystal structure shows the arrangement of atoms in a selected molecular state, but cannot alone demonstrate the activation of living lymphocytes. The modern model gains strength because genetic, cellular, biochemical and structural evidence supports the same connected explanation. When solving an experimental question, identify the type of evidence and the precise inference it justifies before choosing an answer.

3. Class I architecture: a platform assembled from three components

A conventional class I peptide–MHC complex contains a polymorphic heavy chain, the smaller protein beta-2-microglobulin, and a bound peptide. The heavy chain is a membrane glycoprotein with an extracellular part, a membrane-spanning segment and a cytoplasmic tail. Its extracellular part is organized into three domains, named alpha 1, alpha 2 and alpha 3. These names describe regions of the same heavy-chain polypeptide, not three independent subunits. The alpha 1 and alpha 2 domains together form the peptide-binding platform. The alpha 3 domain lies closer to the membrane and has an immunoglobulin-like fold. Beta-2-microglobulin associates noncovalently beneath the platform and does not possess its own membrane-spanning segment.

The relation between the three components can be understood from the stability of the assembled molecule. The alpha 1 and alpha 2 domains form a specialized upper surface rather than a generic protein-binding pocket. Beta-2-microglobulin helps stabilize the folded heavy chain, and a compatible peptide stabilizes the upper platform. The membrane anchorage comes from the heavy chain. Removing beta-2-microglobulin generally impairs the assembly and stable surface expression of classical class I molecules, even though the heavy-chain gene itself may be transcribed normally. This is a useful example of a phenotype caused by loss of a molecular partner rather than by loss of the gene encoding the visibly affected protein.

The binding groove resembles an extended channel between two raised sides. The two alpha helices form much of the sides, whereas beta strands contribute the floor. Both ends of the conventional class I groove are relatively closed. This arrangement favors peptides that can fit between the terminal binding regions, often around eight to ten amino acids long. Nine residues are common for many alleles, but no single peptide length is an absolute rule for every class I molecule. Longer peptides may bulge in the middle while maintaining suitable terminal contacts; some alleles can accommodate unusual lengths. A student should distinguish a typical length preference derived from the structure from a universal prohibition against any longer fragment.

The binding surface is not chemically uniform. Pockets formed by particular heavy-chain residues can accommodate side chains from selected positions of a peptide. These positions are referred to as anchor residues because they make substantial contributions to compatibility with a particular MHC allele. A peptide may share its anchor residues with several unrelated peptides, yet differ at positions exposed to a T-cell receptor. A single MHC type can consequently display many different sequences without being equivalent to a single clonally specific antibody. The underlying selectivity is sufficient to favor some fragments over others, but broad enough to sample diverse proteins.

It is possible for a mutation in a peptide to have very different effects depending on the position affected. An altered anchor can reduce the availability of the peptide–MHC complex by weakening loading or stability. A substitution at a TCR-facing position can preserve the stability of the displayed complex while abolishing recognition by one clone. Not every residue belongs exclusively to one category, because a substitution can change peptide conformation and influence contacts indirectly. An experiment comparing a peptide mutant with the parent peptide therefore benefits from two separate measurements: binding or surface display by the relevant MHC molecule, and recognition by a defined T-cell receptor.

The membrane-proximal alpha 3 domain contributes to interactions with the CD8 coreceptor. CD8 is on the responding T lymphocyte rather than being part of the antigen-presenting class I molecule. Its contact supports class I-associated recognition while the antigen receptor independently engages the peptide-binding platform. The coreceptor does not determine which peptide fits the groove. If a mutation selectively weakens the CD8 interaction without altering folding or peptide binding, a T cell may respond less efficiently despite continued presentation. This is a distinct defect from one that removes the ligand from the surface entirely. Actual mutations may have additional structural effects, so an experimental conclusion requires verification of the assumed selectivity.

4. Class II architecture: a groove shared between two membrane chains

Class II MHC molecules solve the same broad problem of peptide display using a different subunit arrangement. Their principal extracellular platform is formed from two distinct membrane glycoproteins, called the alpha and beta chains. Each chain contains two extracellular domains and its own membrane-spanning segment. The alpha 1 and beta 1 domains contribute to the peptide-binding groove. The alpha 2 and beta 2 domains are positioned below the platform. In contrast to class I, class II does not need beta-2-microglobulin as a structural subunit. The names beta chain and beta-2-microglobulin are not interchangeable: one is a transmembrane class II component and the other is a separate nontransmembrane class I partner.

The geometry of the class II groove is comparatively open at its ends. A peptide can extend beyond the binding platform on either side, which is why class II commonly presents fragments of roughly thirteen to twenty-five residues or sometimes longer. A central portion of about nine residues commonly forms the binding core. Important pockets interact with selected side chains in this core, while flanking residues remain outside the central platform and can contribute to recognition or binding properties. Comparing total peptide length to groove length without recognizing this distinction would lead to the mistaken conclusion that class II cannot hold a peptide longer than its central binding surface.

A longer peptide may contain several overlapping sequences capable of occupying the binding core in different positions. Moving the peptide by one residue changes the set of side chains that face the pockets and the set exposed to the receptor. Such alternative alignments are called binding registers. A single substitution may favor one register over another rather than simply converting a binder into a nonbinder. The concept explains why the identity of the entire protein or even the entire peptide does not fully specify the molecular surface recognized by a T cell. To compare two antigenic fragments fairly, their presented register and relevant MHC type must be considered.

The CD4 coreceptor interacts with the membrane-proximal region of class II, particularly the beta 2 domain. It supports recognition by conventional CD4 T cells, many of which perform helper or regulatory functions. The peptide-binding surface itself is recognized by the antigen receptor. As with class I and CD8, a coreceptor association establishes a typical relationship between receptor class and presenting platform without eliminating the need for antigen specificity. CD4 cannot turn every class II complex into a stimulatory ligand for an arbitrary T cell.

The distinct chain arrangements also affect how inherited variation can contribute to the peptide-binding surface. For HLA-DP and HLA-DQ, alpha and beta genes can both contribute polymorphic residues. HLA-DR has a comparatively conserved alpha chain while much of its relevant variation is associated with the beta chains. This contrast is useful because students sometimes assume that every human class II molecule has the same pattern of genetic variation. The precise diversity of class II heterodimers depends on the expressed loci and compatible chain combinations, not simply on multiplying all possible alpha and beta alleles indiscriminately.

Both class I and class II must maintain their folded extracellular structures while moving between intracellular compartments and the plasma membrane. Their domains contain noncovalent contacts and covalent structural features typical of membrane glycoproteins. Glycosylation and molecular chaperones can contribute to successful assembly, while peptide occupancy stabilizes the mature complexes. The assembly routes differ because the two classes encounter their usual peptide cargo in different places. In the next mechanistic sections, the structural properties of the grooves will be connected to the transport routes that supply appropriate fragments.

5. HLA, H-2, polygeny, polymorphism and codominant inheritance

The major histocompatibility complex is a genomic region containing several genes involved in immunity. In humans the HLA region is located on chromosome 6. The principal classical class I heavy-chain genes are HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C. The principal classical class II systems are HLA-DP, HLA-DQ and HLA-DR. Beta-2-microglobulin is encoded outside the HLA region, on human chromosome 15. These facts show why the phrase MHC molecule should not be treated as a synonym for all products of genes inside the MHC region, nor should every structural partner of a presenting molecule be assumed to originate from that genomic cluster.

In a mouse, H-2 is the name of the corresponding major histocompatibility complex. Representative conventional class I molecules are H-2K and H-2D, with H-2L expressed in some haplotypes. Mouse class II molecules include I-A and, in strains that express it, I-E. The names differ between species but the two major peptide-presenting classes are homologous. Examination questions frequently use the human and mouse naming systems in the same problem. Rather than memorizing names as unrelated lists, first classify each molecule by the type of peptide platform it forms and the conventional T-cell coreceptor associated with it.

Polygeny means that the genome contains several related genes whose products contribute to the same broad function. HLA-A and HLA-B are different genes, not simply two alleles of one gene. Polymorphism means that a given gene has multiple allelic variants in the population. A person may inherit one HLA-A allele from each parent, while other individuals carry additional alternatives at that same locus. A system can be polygenic without being highly polymorphic, or polymorphic without having several loci. The HLA region combines both features, increasing the diversity of presenting molecules within individuals and among individuals.

Codominant expression means that inherited alleles from both parents can contribute expressed products. If a person is heterozygous at each of HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C, the six allelic heavy-chain products can be expressed, assuming ordinary expression and no exceptional loss. A person homozygous at HLA-A but heterozygous at the other two loci has five distinct conventional heavy-chain types under the same assumptions. This count concerns heavy-chain allotypes, not the number of different peptides bound and not the total number of class I molecules present on one cell. Each of those five allotypes can be present in many copies and carry a wide range of peptides.

A haplotype is a set of alleles inherited together along one chromosome. Because HLA loci are physically linked, parental combinations can often be followed as units in a simple family pedigree. Consider two parents with haplotypes A/B and C/D, with all four distinct. Ignoring recombination, a child can receive A or B from the first parent and C or D from the second. The four equally probable haplotype pairs are AC, AD, BC and BD. Conditional on the first child's haplotype pair, a second child has a probability of one quarter of matching both parental haplotypes and one half of matching exactly one. These are inheritance probabilities under a deliberately simplified model, not universal predictions of clinical transplant compatibility.

Variation in the peptide-binding groove changes which peptide sequences can be displayed efficiently. In a population exposed to diverse infectious challenges, individuals carrying different presenting alleles may differ in their ability to display fragments of a pathogen. A peptide poorly presented by one allele may bind another well. This broadens antigen coverage at population level and provides one rationale for the maintenance of extensive diversity. No single mechanism explains all evolutionary patterns at HLA loci: heterozygote advantage, pathogen-mediated selection and other processes can contribute. Furthermore, possessing more presenting allotypes is not equivalent to a guarantee of protection, because the available T-cell repertoire, pathogen biology and other components of immunity influence actual responses.

The MHC region also contains genes with immune functions unrelated to forming conventional peptide-binding platforms. The term class III region refers historically to a genomic portion containing, among other genes, some complement components. These products do not present peptides through a third conventional MHC groove. Similarly, nonclassical class I-related molecules have specialized functions and generally differ in polymorphism, expression or cargo. Keeping the genomic classification separate from the molecular classification prevents an error that would incorrectly assign a CD4 or CD8 partner to a fictional class III antigen-presenting molecule.

6. Who displays which molecules, and why expression matters

Classical MHC class I is expressed by most nucleated cells. This broad distribution is biologically appropriate because viruses and other intracellular disturbances can occur in many tissues, not only within white blood cells. A cell capable of presenting peptides from its internal protein pool provides a potential target for CD8 T-cell surveillance. The amount of surface expression varies across tissues, cell types and inflammatory conditions. Mature mammalian erythrocytes generally lack classical class I expression, while anucleate platelets can carry class I; thus the shorthand about nucleated cells is not a logically reversible rule. Expression depends on lineage and cellular biology rather than on the mere physical presence of a nucleus.

Constitutive class II expression is particularly characteristic of professional antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells, B lymphocytes and many macrophages, although expression in macrophages varies with their differentiation and activation state. These cells possess mechanisms for handling material acquired from outside the cell. Thymic epithelial cells also express class II, helping shape developing T cells rather than acting simply as peripheral microbial scavengers. Some additional cell types can express class II after exposure to inflammatory signals, notably interferon gamma. It is therefore inaccurate to state that class II exists only on the three commonly listed professional APC types.

Professional antigen presentation is not defined solely by the ability to carry a peptide-bound MHC molecule. Dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells can express accessory molecules and support T-cell interactions, but their physiological roles are not identical. Dendritic cells are particularly effective at priming naive T lymphocytes. Macrophages frequently interact with previously activated helper cells in infected tissues. B cells use their antigen receptors to capture particular antigens efficiently and present peptides from that material to helper cells. A tissue cell expressing induced class II may present a peptide without providing the costimulatory context needed to prime naive T cells.

Interferons can alter antigen presentation at more than one stage. Interferon gamma promotes expression of many class I pathway components and can increase class II transcription in responsive cells through the class II transactivator CIITA. Type I interferons also promote class I-associated antiviral surveillance. Because several molecular components may change together, an increase in surface MHC after cytokine exposure should not automatically be attributed to an increase in one transporter or one protease. A useful experiment measures transcription, total protein and surface complex abundance separately. If the first two increase but the third does not, assembly or trafficking may impose a downstream limitation.

A quantitative measurement of MHC expression also requires distinguishing total class I or class II molecules from a specific peptide–MHC population. An antibody directed against a shared folded structure may report many complexes irrespective of the displayed peptide. A peptide–MHC-specific reagent or a cognate T-cell reporter gives more selective information, but the latter also reflects accessory signals and receptor sensitivity. Thus a result showing stable total MHC abundance alongside diminished activation of one T-cell clone can be consistent with a selective change in peptide supply. It need not mean that the total presentation machinery is unaffected in every respect.
