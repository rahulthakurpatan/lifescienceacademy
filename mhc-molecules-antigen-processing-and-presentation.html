1. The biological problem: seeing the contents of another cell

A virus that has entered a living cell can escape recognition by an antibody even while it is making hundreds of copies of its own proteins. The cell membrane separates these proteins from the extracellular fluid, and intact antibodies ordinarily cannot diffuse through the lipid bilayer. The immune system therefore needs a means of investigating what is being synthesized and degraded inside living cells without opening them indiscriminately. A second problem arises when a macrophage engulfs a microorganism. The macrophage must communicate information about the organism to lymphocytes that have never physically encountered it. In both cases the information can be communicated in the form of molecular fragments displayed at the plasma membrane. The molecular apparatus that makes this display possible is the major histocompatibility complex, or MHC.

The word antigen refers to a substance recognized by an antigen receptor. Recognition by an antibody or B-cell receptor frequently involves a surface formed by the folded three-dimensional antigen. Recognition by conventional alpha-beta T cells is different. For most such cells the ligand is a complex made from a short peptide and a host-derived MHC molecule. The peptide is derived by cutting a larger protein into fragments. The MHC molecule holds one such fragment in a groove and places it at a membrane surface where another cell can inspect it. The T-cell receptor contacts the exposed peptide and the surrounding MHC platform. Thus antigen recognition by these T cells depends on both the sequence of the fragment and the molecular identity of the presenting platform.

Antigen processing is the collection of events that makes suitable fragments available for loading. Antigen presentation is the display of peptide-bound molecules where a T-cell receptor can encounter them. These are related but not identical functions. A cell can produce a peptide without presenting it if the peptide cannot reach a suitable MHC molecule. A cell can also display many MHC molecules but fail to present a particular peptide if it is never generated from the source protein. This distinction allows us to interpret a common experiment: a cell that fails to stimulate a T-cell clone with intact protein may recover its stimulatory ability when a suitable short peptide is supplied. The rescue identifies a difference between the two conditions, but does not automatically identify the precise processing enzyme at fault.

There are two major classes of classical peptide-presenting MHC molecules. Class I normally provides a sample of the proteins associated with the intracellular environment and is surveyed by CD8 T cells. Class II is particularly important for reporting material handled in endocytic compartments and is surveyed by CD4 T cells. This division is useful because the two major kinds of lymphocyte coordinate different responses. An infected epithelial cell that displays a viral peptide in class I may become a target for an already activated cytotoxic lymphocyte. A macrophage presenting microbial peptides in class II may receive assistance from a helper lymphocyte that enhances its antimicrobial functions. These are typical outcomes, not automatic consequences of a particular peptide appearing on a cell.

The presentation system operates continuously even in the absence of infection. Normal turnover of self proteins supplies peptides that occupy class I molecules. Class II molecules on antigen-presenting cells likewise carry many self-derived peptides. The immune system does not use the mere presence of an MHC molecule as evidence of danger. It combines the specificity of lymphocyte receptors, the selection of lymphocytes during development, the activation state of presenting cells and other contextual signals. This continuous display also means that a cell lacking stable surface class I is unusual: innate natural killer cells may detect the loss of inhibitory self signals. The surveillance of class I expression and the inspection of particular peptide–MHC complexes are complementary but mechanistically distinct processes.

For the rest of this chapter, a useful way to organize the subject is to follow the antigen, the MHC molecule and the responding lymphocyte separately. The antigen has an origin and a route through cell compartments. The MHC molecule is synthesized, assembled, loaded and transported. The responding cell must bear a receptor compatible with the displayed complex and be in a state that allows a response. Only after the three histories intersect at the cell surface can antigen-specific cellular recognition occur. This framework connects the molecular biology of protein turnover and membrane traffic with the cell biology of immunity.

2. From graft rejection to the molecular explanation of restriction

The name major histocompatibility complex reflects an earlier biological question rather than the function that immunology now emphasizes. Histocompatibility describes the compatibility of tissues between individuals. Transplanted tissue can be rejected when donor and recipient differ in inherited molecules that the recipient's immune system recognizes. Experiments in genetically defined mice established that a major inherited region profoundly influenced graft survival. Mouse geneticists called this region H-2. The corresponding human region was defined through human leukocyte antigens, abbreviated HLA. These findings showed that important cell-surface immunological differences could be followed through families and inherited strains rather than being generated independently in each lymphocyte.

An important change in interpretation came from investigations of antiviral cellular immunity by Rolf Zinkernagel and Peter Doherty in the 1970s. In studies using lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus in mice, the investigators examined the ability of immune lymphocytes to kill infected target cells. Infection alone was insufficient to make a target susceptible to a particular antiviral response: compatibility of the relevant H-2 molecules between responding and target cells also mattered. Conversely, an uninfected target with the matching H-2 type was not killed merely because it shared that inherited type. These observations revealed two requirements for antigen-specific cytotoxic recognition and led to the concept of MHC restriction.

Consider the logic of a simplified version of this classical experiment. A T-cell population comes from an infected mouse expressing MHC type X. Target cells are prepared in four categories: type X infected, type X uninfected, type Y infected, and type Y uninfected. Specific lysis of the first category, but not the remaining three, supports the proposition that the response needs the viral antigen in the appropriate genetic context. The matching uninfected control excludes simple recognition of type X alone. The mismatched infected control excludes a response that depends only on infection. The mismatched uninfected control establishes a background response. This four-way comparison is an instructive model of the historical finding, not a reproduction of numerical data from the original experiments.

A further question was how a cell could display the relevant combination. In one possible model, T cells would use separate receptors for the foreign antigen and for MHC. In another, a single antigen receptor would sense a composite structure. Molecular work on T-cell receptors and MHC proteins supported the latter explanation for conventional peptide-specific T cells. The sequence and structure of the receptor and the physical arrangement of its ligand revealed that the receptor could contact a peptide together with its presenting molecule. The immunological specificity observed in whole animals consequently became understandable in terms of interactions between macromolecular surfaces.

In 1987, the crystallographic studies of Pamela Bjorkman and colleagues supplied an important structural explanation using HLA-A2, a human class I molecule. The extracellular platform possessed a groove with a beta-sheet floor and flanking alpha helices. Polymorphic residues were concentrated in regions relevant to peptide binding and recognition. The structural finding helped explain why a protein originally identified as a transplantation antigen could act as a platform for short antigenic peptides. A small change in the amino acid sequence of the platform could change the chemical properties of a binding pocket or the region contacted by a T-cell receptor. The resulting differences had consequences for both which peptides were displayed and how they were recognized.

The historical sequence teaches an important lesson about evidence. A graft experiment identifies inherited determinants of a tissue response, but cannot establish the atomic shape of a binding groove. An infected-target assay establishes the dependence of cellular recognition on particular variables, but cannot by itself show the identities of all molecular contacts. A crystal structure shows the arrangement of atoms in a selected molecular state, but cannot alone demonstrate the activation of living lymphocytes. The modern model gains strength because genetic, cellular, biochemical and structural evidence supports the same connected explanation. When solving an experimental question, identify the type of evidence and the precise inference it justifies before choosing an answer.

3. Class I architecture: a platform assembled from three components

A conventional class I peptide–MHC complex contains a polymorphic heavy chain, the smaller protein beta-2-microglobulin, and a bound peptide. The heavy chain is a membrane glycoprotein with an extracellular part, a membrane-spanning segment and a cytoplasmic tail. Its extracellular part is organized into three domains, named alpha 1, alpha 2 and alpha 3. These names describe regions of the same heavy-chain polypeptide, not three independent subunits. The alpha 1 and alpha 2 domains together form the peptide-binding platform. The alpha 3 domain lies closer to the membrane and has an immunoglobulin-like fold. Beta-2-microglobulin associates noncovalently beneath the platform and does not possess its own membrane-spanning segment.

The relation between the three components can be understood from the stability of the assembled molecule. The alpha 1 and alpha 2 domains form a specialized upper surface rather than a generic protein-binding pocket. Beta-2-microglobulin helps stabilize the folded heavy chain, and a compatible peptide stabilizes the upper platform. The membrane anchorage comes from the heavy chain. Removing beta-2-microglobulin generally impairs the assembly and stable surface expression of classical class I molecules, even though the heavy-chain gene itself may be transcribed normally. This is a useful example of a phenotype caused by loss of a molecular partner rather than by loss of the gene encoding the visibly affected protein.

The binding groove resembles an extended channel between two raised sides. The two alpha helices form much of the sides, whereas beta strands contribute the floor. Both ends of the conventional class I groove are relatively closed. This arrangement favors peptides that can fit between the terminal binding regions, often around eight to ten amino acids long. Nine residues are common for many alleles, but no single peptide length is an absolute rule for every class I molecule. Longer peptides may bulge in the middle while maintaining suitable terminal contacts; some alleles can accommodate unusual lengths. A student should distinguish a typical length preference derived from the structure from a universal prohibition against any longer fragment.

The binding surface is not chemically uniform. Pockets formed by particular heavy-chain residues can accommodate side chains from selected positions of a peptide. These positions are referred to as anchor residues because they make substantial contributions to compatibility with a particular MHC allele. A peptide may share its anchor residues with several unrelated peptides, yet differ at positions exposed to a T-cell receptor. A single MHC type can consequently display many different sequences without being equivalent to a single clonally specific antibody. The underlying selectivity is sufficient to favor some fragments over others, but broad enough to sample diverse proteins.

It is possible for a mutation in a peptide to have very different effects depending on the position affected. An altered anchor can reduce the availability of the peptide–MHC complex by weakening loading or stability. A substitution at a TCR-facing position can preserve the stability of the displayed complex while abolishing recognition by one clone. Not every residue belongs exclusively to one category, because a substitution can change peptide conformation and influence contacts indirectly. An experiment comparing a peptide mutant with the parent peptide therefore benefits from two separate measurements: binding or surface display by the relevant MHC molecule, and recognition by a defined T-cell receptor.

The membrane-proximal alpha 3 domain contributes to interactions with the CD8 coreceptor. CD8 is on the responding T lymphocyte rather than being part of the antigen-presenting class I molecule. Its contact supports class I-associated recognition while the antigen receptor independently engages the peptide-binding platform. The coreceptor does not determine which peptide fits the groove. If a mutation selectively weakens the CD8 interaction without altering folding or peptide binding, a T cell may respond less efficiently despite continued presentation. This is a distinct defect from one that removes the ligand from the surface entirely. Actual mutations may have additional structural effects, so an experimental conclusion requires verification of the assumed selectivity.

4. Class II architecture: a groove shared between two membrane chains

Class II MHC molecules solve the same broad problem of peptide display using a different subunit arrangement. Their principal extracellular platform is formed from two distinct membrane glycoproteins, called the alpha and beta chains. Each chain contains two extracellular domains and its own membrane-spanning segment. The alpha 1 and beta 1 domains contribute to the peptide-binding groove. The alpha 2 and beta 2 domains are positioned below the platform. In contrast to class I, class II does not need beta-2-microglobulin as a structural subunit. The names beta chain and beta-2-microglobulin are not interchangeable: one is a transmembrane class II component and the other is a separate nontransmembrane class I partner.

The geometry of the class II groove is comparatively open at its ends. A peptide can extend beyond the binding platform on either side, which is why class II commonly presents fragments of roughly thirteen to twenty-five residues or sometimes longer. A central portion of about nine residues commonly forms the binding core. Important pockets interact with selected side chains in this core, while flanking residues remain outside the central platform and can contribute to recognition or binding properties. Comparing total peptide length to groove length without recognizing this distinction would lead to the mistaken conclusion that class II cannot hold a peptide longer than its central binding surface.

A longer peptide may contain several overlapping sequences capable of occupying the binding core in different positions. Moving the peptide by one residue changes the set of side chains that face the pockets and the set exposed to the receptor. Such alternative alignments are called binding registers. A single substitution may favor one register over another rather than simply converting a binder into a nonbinder. The concept explains why the identity of the entire protein or even the entire peptide does not fully specify the molecular surface recognized by a T cell. To compare two antigenic fragments fairly, their presented register and relevant MHC type must be considered.

The CD4 coreceptor interacts with the membrane-proximal region of class II, particularly the beta 2 domain. It supports recognition by conventional CD4 T cells, many of which perform helper or regulatory functions. The peptide-binding surface itself is recognized by the antigen receptor. As with class I and CD8, a coreceptor association establishes a typical relationship between receptor class and presenting platform without eliminating the need for antigen specificity. CD4 cannot turn every class II complex into a stimulatory ligand for an arbitrary T cell.

The distinct chain arrangements also affect how inherited variation can contribute to the peptide-binding surface. For HLA-DP and HLA-DQ, alpha and beta genes can both contribute polymorphic residues. HLA-DR has a comparatively conserved alpha chain while much of its relevant variation is associated with the beta chains. This contrast is useful because students sometimes assume that every human class II molecule has the same pattern of genetic variation. The precise diversity of class II heterodimers depends on the expressed loci and compatible chain combinations, not simply on multiplying all possible alpha and beta alleles indiscriminately.

Both class I and class II must maintain their folded extracellular structures while moving between intracellular compartments and the plasma membrane. Their domains contain noncovalent contacts and covalent structural features typical of membrane glycoproteins. Glycosylation and molecular chaperones can contribute to successful assembly, while peptide occupancy stabilizes the mature complexes. The assembly routes differ because the two classes encounter their usual peptide cargo in different places. In the next mechanistic sections, the structural properties of the grooves will be connected to the transport routes that supply appropriate fragments.

5. HLA, H-2, polygeny, polymorphism and codominant inheritance

The major histocompatibility complex is a genomic region containing several genes involved in immunity. In humans the HLA region is located on chromosome 6. The principal classical class I heavy-chain genes are HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C. The principal classical class II systems are HLA-DP, HLA-DQ and HLA-DR. Beta-2-microglobulin is encoded outside the HLA region, on human chromosome 15. These facts show why the phrase MHC molecule should not be treated as a synonym for all products of genes inside the MHC region, nor should every structural partner of a presenting molecule be assumed to originate from that genomic cluster.

In a mouse, H-2 is the name of the corresponding major histocompatibility complex. Representative conventional class I molecules are H-2K and H-2D, with H-2L expressed in some haplotypes. Mouse class II molecules include I-A and, in strains that express it, I-E. The names differ between species but the two major peptide-presenting classes are homologous. Examination questions frequently use the human and mouse naming systems in the same problem. Rather than memorizing names as unrelated lists, first classify each molecule by the type of peptide platform it forms and the conventional T-cell coreceptor associated with it.

Polygeny means that the genome contains several related genes whose products contribute to the same broad function. HLA-A and HLA-B are different genes, not simply two alleles of one gene. Polymorphism means that a given gene has multiple allelic variants in the population. A person may inherit one HLA-A allele from each parent, while other individuals carry additional alternatives at that same locus. A system can be polygenic without being highly polymorphic, or polymorphic without having several loci. The HLA region combines both features, increasing the diversity of presenting molecules within individuals and among individuals.

Codominant expression means that inherited alleles from both parents can contribute expressed products. If a person is heterozygous at each of HLA-A, HLA-B and HLA-C, the six allelic heavy-chain products can be expressed, assuming ordinary expression and no exceptional loss. A person homozygous at HLA-A but heterozygous at the other two loci has five distinct conventional heavy-chain types under the same assumptions. This count concerns heavy-chain allotypes, not the number of different peptides bound and not the total number of class I molecules present on one cell. Each of those five allotypes can be present in many copies and carry a wide range of peptides.

A haplotype is a set of alleles inherited together along one chromosome. Because HLA loci are physically linked, parental combinations can often be followed as units in a simple family pedigree. Consider two parents with haplotypes A/B and C/D, with all four distinct. Ignoring recombination, a child can receive A or B from the first parent and C or D from the second. The four equally probable haplotype pairs are AC, AD, BC and BD. Conditional on the first child's haplotype pair, a second child has a probability of one quarter of matching both parental haplotypes and one half of matching exactly one. These are inheritance probabilities under a deliberately simplified model, not universal predictions of clinical transplant compatibility.

Variation in the peptide-binding groove changes which peptide sequences can be displayed efficiently. In a population exposed to diverse infectious challenges, individuals carrying different presenting alleles may differ in their ability to display fragments of a pathogen. A peptide poorly presented by one allele may bind another well. This broadens antigen coverage at population level and provides one rationale for the maintenance of extensive diversity. No single mechanism explains all evolutionary patterns at HLA loci: heterozygote advantage, pathogen-mediated selection and other processes can contribute. Furthermore, possessing more presenting allotypes is not equivalent to a guarantee of protection, because the available T-cell repertoire, pathogen biology and other components of immunity influence actual responses.

The MHC region also contains genes with immune functions unrelated to forming conventional peptide-binding platforms. The term class III region refers historically to a genomic portion containing, among other genes, some complement components. These products do not present peptides through a third conventional MHC groove. Similarly, nonclassical class I-related molecules have specialized functions and generally differ in polymorphism, expression or cargo. Keeping the genomic classification separate from the molecular classification prevents an error that would incorrectly assign a CD4 or CD8 partner to a fictional class III antigen-presenting molecule.

6. Who displays which molecules, and why expression matters

Classical MHC class I is expressed by most nucleated cells. This broad distribution is biologically appropriate because viruses and other intracellular disturbances can occur in many tissues, not only within white blood cells. A cell capable of presenting peptides from its internal protein pool provides a potential target for CD8 T-cell surveillance. The amount of surface expression varies across tissues, cell types and inflammatory conditions. Mature mammalian erythrocytes generally lack classical class I expression, while anucleate platelets can carry class I; thus the shorthand about nucleated cells is not a logically reversible rule. Expression depends on lineage and cellular biology rather than on the mere physical presence of a nucleus.

Constitutive class II expression is particularly characteristic of professional antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells, B lymphocytes and many macrophages, although expression in macrophages varies with their differentiation and activation state. These cells possess mechanisms for handling material acquired from outside the cell. Thymic epithelial cells also express class II, helping shape developing T cells rather than acting simply as peripheral microbial scavengers. Some additional cell types can express class II after exposure to inflammatory signals, notably interferon gamma. It is therefore inaccurate to state that class II exists only on the three commonly listed professional APC types.

Professional antigen presentation is not defined solely by the ability to carry a peptide-bound MHC molecule. Dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells can express accessory molecules and support T-cell interactions, but their physiological roles are not identical. Dendritic cells are particularly effective at priming naive T lymphocytes. Macrophages frequently interact with previously activated helper cells in infected tissues. B cells use their antigen receptors to capture particular antigens efficiently and present peptides from that material to helper cells. A tissue cell expressing induced class II may present a peptide without providing the costimulatory context needed to prime naive T cells.

Interferons can alter antigen presentation at more than one stage. Interferon gamma promotes expression of many class I pathway components and can increase class II transcription in responsive cells through the class II transactivator CIITA. Type I interferons also promote class I-associated antiviral surveillance. Because several molecular components may change together, an increase in surface MHC after cytokine exposure should not automatically be attributed to an increase in one transporter or one protease. A useful experiment measures transcription, total protein and surface complex abundance separately. If the first two increase but the third does not, assembly or trafficking may impose a downstream limitation.

A quantitative measurement of MHC expression also requires distinguishing total class I or class II molecules from a specific peptide–MHC population. An antibody directed against a shared folded structure may report many complexes irrespective of the displayed peptide. A peptide–MHC-specific reagent or a cognate T-cell reporter gives more selective information, but the latter also reflects accessory signals and receptor sensitivity. Thus a result showing stable total MHC abundance alongside diminished activation of one T-cell clone can be consistent with a selective change in peptide supply. It need not mean that the total presentation machinery is unaffected in every respect.

7. MHC restriction: how specificity is shared between peptide and platform

MHC restriction is an observable property of the interaction between a T cell and a presenting cell. A conventional T-cell clone that recognizes peptide P on class I allele X may fail to recognize the same peptide on allele Y. It may also fail to recognize peptide Q on allele X. The relevant ligand is not simply a peptide traveling in the extracellular fluid, but a peptide in a particular molecular context. There are at least three reasons why changing the MHC allele might abolish a response: the new allele may bind the peptide poorly, it may display the peptide in an altered conformation, or the receptor may be unable to contact the altered MHC surface productively even if peptide binding is preserved. A complete explanation of restriction must allow for all three possibilities.

The restriction of a mature T-cell repertoire is related to its developmental history. Developing T cells in the thymus encounter self peptide–MHC complexes on thymic stromal and hematopoietic cells. Positive selection favors cells able to recognize self MHC in an appropriate range of interactions. Negative selection and other tolerance mechanisms reduce the probability that strongly self-reactive cells leave the thymus in a harmful state. This is why a conventional mature repertoire is generally adapted to the MHC environment in which it developed. Detailed stages and signaling thresholds of thymic selection belong to the later T-cell development node; here the important point is that restriction is not acquired by a mature lymphocyte simply because it sees a foreign protein for the first time.

A helpful distinction is between MHC restriction and MHC binding specificity. An MHC molecule can bind many different peptides sharing compatible physical features. A T-cell clone, in contrast, responds preferentially to particular composite surfaces. If ten peptides bind one allele strongly, there is no requirement that the same T-cell clone recognizes all ten. Conversely, a clone may cross-react with several related or even structurally distinct complexes. Biological recognition is neither completely promiscuous nor perfectly one-to-one. These two selectivity filters operate at different levels and must be analyzed separately in questions concerning mutations, immune escape or altered peptide ligands.

The structural orientation of the T-cell receptor allows it to sense the region where peptide and MHC meet. The variable receptor loops interact with a molecular landscape whose chemical and geometric properties depend on both contributors. The relative contributions of particular receptor loops differ between individual complexes, so rigid assignments such as one receptor loop recognizing only peptide and another recognizing only MHC are unsafe. The CD8 or CD4 coreceptor contacts the appropriate membrane-proximal MHC region and helps connect extracellular recognition to intracellular signaling, but is not a second independent receptor for a foreign antigen.

An important complication arises when lymphocytes encounter MHC molecules from genetically different individuals. A T cell may recognize an allogeneic MHC–peptide complex even though the presenting allele was not the one involved in its ordinary self-restricted response. Alloreactivity is possible because the receptor has a finite range of compatible surfaces, and a new MHC–peptide combination can resemble or otherwise interact productively with its usual ligand. Thus the observation of graft rejection does not contradict the general principle of MHC restriction. The same receptor architecture that normally makes specificity dependent on self MHC can permit cross-recognition of some foreign MHC surfaces. The mechanisms of transplant management are outside the present scope.

To distinguish restriction from a global inability of a T-cell population to function, one should test the population against a known compatible positive-control ligand. If a clone fails to respond to mismatched infected targets but responds strongly to matching infected targets, viability and at least some aspects of effector competence are supported. If no target elicits a response, failure may lie in the clone rather than in MHC compatibility. A separate measurement of peptide occupancy on the targets further distinguishes failure to generate a ligand from failure to recognize a ligand that is demonstrably present. This reasoning is the foundation for interpreting the processing defects discussed below.

8. Class I processing begins with proteins in the intracellular pool

The class I pathway relies on the routine destruction of proteins. Living cells continually synthesize proteins and replace those that are damaged, misfolded, no longer needed or deliberately targeted for turnover. When a virus infects a cell, the intracellular protein pool may also contain proteins encoded by the virus. Class I processing samples this pool rather than detecting a foreign amino acid chemistry unique to viruses. Viral proteins are built from the same ordinary amino acids as host proteins, so the machinery that generates peptides does not need to classify the intact source protein as foreign before cutting it. The lymphocyte repertoire provides much of the selectivity for recognizing the resulting antigenic information.

The proteasome is a large proteolytic complex capable of degrading many intracellular proteins. In the well-known ubiquitin-dependent pathway, ubiquitin modifications mark a protein for recognition by the regulatory components of the proteasome. ATP-dependent unfolding and movement of the substrate into the proteolytic core make internal peptide bonds accessible to catalytic sites. The resulting products are fragments rather than necessarily individual amino acids. Some fragments are subsequently degraded completely, whereas a small subset becomes available for class I loading. Ubiquitin-dependent degradation is a major route, but not every class I peptide must originate from a protein bearing a conventional polyubiquitin tag.

A distinction is necessary between a protein's degradation rate and its contribution to antigen presentation. Rapid degradation may make fragments available sooner than would be expected from the abundance of the mature functional protein. Newly made proteins that fail to fold or assemble properly can also supply peptides. On the other hand, a highly abundant stable protein may provide enough fragments through ordinary turnover to be well represented. A protein's abundance, translation rate, susceptibility to proteolysis, cleavage preferences and subsequent peptide transport all influence the final repertoire. A simple statement that abundant proteins always generate abundant surface epitopes would neglect several of these intervening filters.

Proteasomal cleavage is sequence-dependent. A protease does not produce equal amounts of every possible peptide within a source protein because particular residues and local structural features affect cleavage. If cleavage occurs inside a potential epitope, that particular molecule can no longer supply the intact epitope through an ordinary direct fragmentation route. Conversely, cleavage at a suitable boundary can release a fragment already close to the required length. Aminopeptidases may subsequently remove residues from the amino terminus, so an initially longer fragment can be a productive precursor. Generation of the correct carboxyl terminus is often strongly dependent on the initial cleavage history.

Under inflammatory conditions, some cells increase expression of alternative proteasome catalytic subunits, producing immunoproteasomes with altered cleavage preferences. This change can enhance the generation of some class I ligands while reducing others. It is incorrect to infer that immunoproteasomes exist exclusively in infected cells or that their action makes all peptides more immunogenic. Constitutive expression occurs in some immune cell types, and inflammation influences expression elsewhere. For exam purposes, the key concept is that a change in protease composition can alter the peptide repertoire without a corresponding change in the amino acid sequence of the MHC molecule or the source antigen.

The physical location of protein degradation matters because class I heavy chains fold in the lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum, while much proteasomal degradation occurs in the cytosol or nucleus. A peptide produced on the cytosolic side of the ER membrane cannot simply assume that it has access to an empty groove on the other side. Peptides are polar molecules whose spontaneous passage through the hydrophobic core of a lipid membrane is strongly restricted. The classical pathway therefore contains a selective transport stage linking cytosolic degradation to lumenal assembly. This topological distinction explains why defects in membrane transport can reproduce some of the consequences of defects in proteolysis even when the proteasome continues to operate normally.

Consider a cell synthesizing a model antigen that contains a known class I epitope in its interior. When a proteasome inhibitor is applied, presentation of that epitope may decrease while the amount of intact antigen increases. The two observations are not contradictory because the degraded fraction supplies peptide whereas undegraded antigen contributes to the measured total protein. However, inhibitors can also alter general cell stress, protein synthesis and viability. Demonstrating loss of presentation while preserving viable cell number and relevant surface molecules strengthens the proposed processing interpretation. Rescue with a short compatible peptide can help place the defect before stable peptide–MHC recognition, but the design must account for whether the added peptide can actually encounter receptive MHC molecules.

9. TAP and the energetic barrier between cytosol and endoplasmic reticulum

TAP stands for transporter associated with antigen processing. The conventional transporter is composed of TAP1 and TAP2, two associated proteins that form an ATP-binding cassette transporter in the ER membrane. Each contributes membrane-spanning regions and a nucleotide-binding region. The transporter binds peptide substrates from the cytosolic side and uses ATP-dependent conformational transitions to deliver them into the ER lumen. This is a form of active transport and differs from movement through a continuously open aqueous pore. The energy comes from ATP hydrolysis rather than from the antigen's affinity for a distant cell-surface receptor.

The transporter has preferences for the length and sequence of the peptides it translocates. It can accommodate fragments longer than the final ligands of many class I molecules. A transportable fragment does not need to be the final epitope, because subsequent trimming can occur. Conversely, a peptide that binds a particular MHC allele very strongly in a purified binding assay might be supplied inefficiently through the physiological pathway if it is poorly transported or rarely generated. Binding affinity is therefore one determinant of presentation, not a substitute for understanding the pathway upstream of the groove.

TAP's cytosolic substrate-binding and ATP-handling functions must be connected to a change in the membrane-spanning region. Without conformational coupling, ATP might still bind or even be hydrolyzed without productive peptide translocation. This distinction is common to ABC transporters studied in cellular organization and membrane transport. An ATP-binding mutation, a mutation in the peptide-interacting region and a defect in coupling can all reduce peptide movement but have different effects in biochemical assays. The first may prevent the normal nucleotide-dependent transition; the second may reduce recruitment of peptide; the third may waste energy without completing transport. The phenotype alone does not establish which molecular step is defective.

The ER contains class I heavy chains at various assembly stages. If TAP activity is lost, many newly synthesized class I molecules receive insufficient compatible cargo. The resulting shortage of stabilizing ligands can lead to retention, degradation or reduced lifetime of incompletely assembled complexes. Consequently, a TAP-deficient cell often displays less stable classical class I at its surface even though its HLA heavy-chain genes and beta-2-microglobulin gene remain intact. Residual presentation can occur through alternative sources of peptides, including some fragments that are already generated in the secretory pathway. The careful description is reduced conventional TAP-dependent presentation, not mathematically zero display of every possible class I ligand.

An important tool for localizing a defect is to compare antigens with different subcellular routes. Suppose a peptide is normally released from a cytosolic precursor, while an engineered version is directed into the ER lumen through a signal sequence and is known to be liberated correctly there. If TAP-deficient cells fail to present the cytosolic version but present the ER-directed version, the contrast supports a requirement for access to the ER. A signal sequence does not guarantee successful epitope generation, so a meaningful comparison includes controls verifying that the ER-targeted construct is processed as intended and reaches a compatible loading environment. This is a logic of compartment-specific rescue rather than a universal claim about every targeting sequence.

ATP dependence can also be investigated in isolated transport systems by comparing peptide entry under conditions with and without functional nucleotide-dependent transport. Such experiments are especially useful because they can avoid many unrelated consequences of ATP depletion in whole cells. If the ATP concentration of a living cell is reduced globally, translation, vesicle traffic, ionic regulation and multiple other processes may fail together. Reduced presentation in that situation cannot identify TAP as the sole ATP-dependent step. A selective transporter mutation or a sufficiently specific biochemical measurement is more informative about the mechanism than a broad metabolic perturbation.

The outcome of peptide transport depends on competition among many available substrates. The cytosol contains fragments of host proteins along with any microbial fragments. A change in the amount of one substrate can affect the availability of transport capacity or subsequently compete for compatible MHC binding. At low substrate concentrations, transport may increase with substrate abundance; at high concentrations, finite numbers of transporters can impose saturation. The exact kinetic behavior depends on the conditions and should not be inferred from a simple linear model over all concentrations. The examination principle is that membrane transport has finite capacity and can introduce selectivity independently of final antigen recognition.

10. Assembly and quality control of class I in the ER

Class I heavy chains are synthesized on ribosomes associated with the rough ER, where the extracellular portion of the future surface molecule enters the lumen. The orientation is preserved during subsequent membrane traffic, so a domain facing the ER lumen eventually faces the extracellular environment. The newly synthesized heavy chain must acquire an appropriate conformation before it can form a stable complex. Folding involves molecular chaperones and the formation of appropriate structural contacts. The glycoprotein's carbohydrate modifications also participate in the ER quality-control machinery. These general cell biological processes are adapted to the special challenge of assembling a molecule whose final stability depends partly on a peptide supplied by a different route.

Calnexin assists early folding of many newly synthesized class I heavy chains. Association with beta-2-microglobulin helps stabilize a partially assembled structure. The resulting assembly can engage components of the peptide-loading complex, including calreticulin, ERp57, tapasin and TAP. These names refer to proteins with distinct roles rather than interchangeable enzymes. Calreticulin helps in glycoprotein quality control, ERp57 participates in the chaperone-associated assembly network, and tapasin links peptide-receptive class I molecules to the loading apparatus and contributes to peptide selection. TAP itself is the peptide transporter. The molecular organization increases the probability that transported fragments encounter receptive presenting molecules in a suitable state.

Tapasin is especially helpful for understanding quality control as a selective process. Its contribution is not simply to ensure that any peptide occupies a groove. Class I molecules loaded with sufficiently stabilizing cargo are generally better suited for successful transport and persistence at the cell surface. By supporting peptide exchange and the selection of stable complexes, the loading system can favor ligands with appropriate interactions. Different MHC alleles vary in their dependence on tapasin. A tapasin defect can therefore change both total surface expression and the composition of the displayed peptide population, with a degree of impact that depends on the allele and cellular context.

Fragments arriving in the ER may require shortening before they fit the class I groove. ER aminopeptidases, commonly represented by ERAP1 and in humans also ERAP2 where expressed, can remove residues from peptide amino termini. Trimming can generate a compatible length and expose an appropriate terminal residue. It can also destroy an epitope if the protease removes a residue essential for binding or recognition. Consequently, an increase in aminopeptidase activity is not guaranteed to increase every particular peptide's presentation. A loss of trimming can reduce presentation of an epitope supplied as an extended precursor while increasing another peptide that would otherwise be overtrimmed.

Stable peptide loading allows the mature complex to move through the secretory pathway, passing from the ER through the Golgi apparatus and ultimately reaching the plasma membrane. The peptide is not secreted as a free molecule in the usual route; it remains noncovalently associated with the presenting platform. The heavy chain's membrane anchor holds the complex in the lipid bilayer with the groove facing the extracellular space. Surface abundance depends on continued arrival, internalization, turnover and the lifetime of the bound peptide. Complexes that reach the membrane are not permanent records of everything that has ever been synthesized inside the cell.

The phrase peptide loading should not be confused with antigen uptake by the cell. A class I ligand can be loaded without the cell ever having endocytosed the original protein, because the source protein can be synthesized in the cytosol. Likewise, the ER loading complex does not directly transport a whole viral protein into the lumen. The route normally degrades proteins first, moves selected fragments across the membrane and assembles a stable display complex. The pathway transforms the chemical information contained in a protein sequence into a membrane-bound signal readable by a T-cell receptor.

Consider a hypothetical comparison of wild-type and beta-2-microglobulin-deficient cells that express equal amounts of heavy-chain messenger RNA. The deficient cells display far less folded class I as measured with an antibody that recognizes the mature conformation. A protein assay that detects the unassembled heavy chain might nonetheless show significant intracellular material. The transcript measurement rules out one explanation, reduced expression of the heavy-chain gene, while the folded surface measurement indicates a defect in assembly or trafficking. Restoring beta-2-microglobulin and observing recovery would further support causation. This comparison demonstrates why assays that measure different stages of a pathway need not produce the same numerical pattern.

A complementary example is a cell with a functional beta-2-microglobulin gene but impaired TAP transport. Supplying an appropriate peptide that reaches the relevant loading compartment may stabilize some of its class I molecules. The same intervention is less likely to restore normal complexes in a cell that completely lacks the structural beta-2-microglobulin partner. A short peptide can supply missing cargo but cannot replace a missing polypeptide subunit. This contrast is valuable when examining rescue experiments because the two genetic defects may look superficially similar at the surface despite affecting different molecular components.

11. Class II processing begins with acquired proteins and compartmental digestion

The typical class II pathway begins when an antigen-presenting cell acquires proteins from outside itself. There are several biologically distinct routes of acquisition. A macrophage can engulf a large particle or microorganism by phagocytosis, a process that forms an intracellular membrane-bound compartment. A dendritic cell can sample extracellular material by endocytic and macropinocytic processes. A B lymphocyte can bind a particular molecular structure through its membrane immunoglobulin and internalize the receptor-associated antigen. In each case, the material becomes available within the endocytic system. The mechanism of initial capture can influence how much antigen is delivered, where it travels and how efficiently its fragments reach class II molecules.

Once extracellular material is enclosed by an endocytic vesicle, the lumen is topologically continuous with the extracellular side of the plasma membrane, even though the vesicle is physically inside the cell. Proteins in that lumen are separated from the cytosol by a lipid membrane. This relationship is important because class II processing does not ordinarily require the ingested protein to cross into the cytosol before it is fragmented. The cell can use proteases active within the lumen of the endocytic system and subsequently load peptide onto lumen-facing class II molecules. The transport challenge is therefore different from the classical class I pathway, in which many fragments cross from cytosol to ER through TAP.

Endocytic compartments undergo maturation involving changes in membrane identity, fusion events, protein composition and lumenal acidity. Vacuolar ATPases use energy from ATP hydrolysis to pump protons into the lumen, contributing to an acidic environment. The resulting pH favors the operation of several lysosomal and endosomal enzymes, including proteases collectively represented by the cathepsins. Acidification also affects the stability of internalized proteins and their interactions with receptors. It does not itself cut every peptide bond: enzymes catalyze the hydrolytic reactions that convert proteins into smaller fragments.

Proteolysis is again a selective process rather than a guarantee that every possible sequence will be available. The folded antigen can protect internal regions from enzymes until its structure is disrupted. Disulfide bonds, association with other proteins and local chemical properties can affect accessibility. An early cleavage event can expose a region for subsequent digestion or destroy a potential epitope. A useful class II fragment must survive long enough to encounter a peptide-receptive molecule and have a compatible core sequence. If digestion is too extensive, a potentially antigenic region may disappear into fragments too small or chemically unsuitable for binding.

Different presenting cells can handle captured material with different balances between preservation and destruction. A macrophage that specializes in microbial clearance can possess strong degradative capacity, whereas dendritic cells can preserve certain antigens sufficiently to support efficient presentation. This is not an absolute distinction between all macrophages and all dendritic cells. It reflects the diversity of cell subsets, maturation states and intracellular environments. In an experimental comparison, equal uptake of a labeled protein does not establish equal rates of proteolysis or equal presentation of its particular epitope. Uptake, fragmentation and surface display must be measured or inferred with appropriate controls.

B-cell receptor-mediated uptake is especially informative because it links two modes of antigen recognition. Imagine a B-cell receptor that binds a surface region on a folded protein. The B cell internalizes the protein and may generate a class II peptide from a different part of the same molecular antigen. A helper T cell recognizing that peptide need not recognize the original B-cell epitope. The connection is the physical association of the captured material with the protein fragments presented. This provides a molecular foundation for linked recognition in T-dependent antibody responses, developed fully in the next chapter. A B cell and T cell can cooperate while recognizing different molecular surfaces.

Inhibiting endosomal acidification can decrease presentation of particular class II epitopes by impairing proteolytic processing and other pH-dependent events. Such a perturbation may simultaneously interfere with invariant-chain degradation, peptide editing or trafficking. Therefore, reduced response after inhibition cannot establish which single acidic protease was responsible. A short peptide that binds the appropriate class II molecule can sometimes bypass the need to degrade intact protein, while a surface expression measurement can determine whether sufficiently receptive molecules remain. If peptide rescue fails, an additional assembly, loading or viability defect may be involved. The experimental task is to distinguish several linked requirements rather than to equate acidification with one reaction.

12. Invariant chain directs class II molecules away from premature loading

Class II alpha and beta chains, like the class I heavy chain, enter the secretory pathway during their synthesis. They initially fold within the ER. Yet the principal peptide cargo of the class II pathway is normally acquired in endocytic compartments rather than supplied through TAP into the ER. This creates a sorting problem: the presenting molecule must avoid premature occupation of its groove by inappropriate lumenal material and reach the compartments where suitable acquired protein fragments are produced. The invariant chain, also known as CD74, helps coordinate both tasks.

The invariant chain associates with newly assembled class II alpha–beta heterodimers. A portion of invariant chain occupies the peptide-binding groove, preventing ordinary peptide ligands from freely taking its place during early assembly. Other regions contribute to the trafficking of the complex toward the endocytic pathway. The protein thus combines a protective role at the groove with a targeting role at the compartment level. These functions explain why invariant chain deficiency is more complicated than simply removing a peptide competitor: it may change where and in what state class II molecules encounter cargo.

The association with invariant chain is temporary. As the complex reaches progressively maturer endosomal compartments, proteases degrade much of the invariant chain. A remaining fragment, called class II-associated invariant chain peptide or CLIP, stays bound within the groove. CLIP represents a segment of the invariant chain itself, not a peptide extracted from a foreign organism. It occupies a position similar to that of an eventual antigenic ligand and keeps the platform in an occupied state during the transition. The stepwise loss of invariant chain connects trafficking to controlled exposure of the groove rather than permitting indiscriminate loading throughout the early secretory pathway.

The removal of most of invariant chain and the displacement of CLIP are distinct events. Proteolysis of invariant chain produces the groove-associated fragment, but a compatible antigenic peptide must eventually replace that fragment for productive conventional presentation. HLA-DM, a specialized class II-like molecule, facilitates exchange of CLIP and other bound peptides. HLA-DM is not simply the protease that cuts invariant chain to CLIP. Its role in peptide exchange is mechanistically different from hydrolysis of peptide bonds. Keeping this separation clear prevents a common examination error in which acid proteases and HLA-DM are assigned interchangeable activities.

A hypothetical mutant class II molecule that assembles correctly but cannot interact normally with invariant chain can follow an altered trafficking route or experience changes in premature peptide occupancy. The exact phenotype depends on what the mutation affects. A defect that eliminates invariant-chain binding entirely is not equivalent to a defect that preserves groove occupation but removes a cytoplasmic sorting signal. The latter could protect the groove during early assembly yet fail to deliver the complex efficiently to the desired endocytic compartments. In mechanistic questions, identifying whether a protein acts as a binding partner, targeting adaptor or catalyst helps predict the consequences of a selective mutation.

An experiment can compare the intracellular localization of class II with markers of the ER and late endosomes. In a wild-type cell the class II population may be distributed among different stages of the biosynthetic pathway, while a sorting-defective mutant may show an altered pattern. Colocalization supports the conclusion that molecules occupy the same broad compartment but does not by itself demonstrate a direct molecular interaction. A complementary assay of association with invariant chain, together with an antigen presentation assay, would strengthen the interpretation. This illustrates why microscopy, biochemical association and functional readouts answer related but distinct questions.

The invariant chain is not an obstacle that an antigen has to overpower in a random competition at the cell surface. It participates in a regulated itinerary that increases the likelihood of loading within appropriate intracellular compartments. The final class II ligand repertoire reflects the balance of antigen digestion, class II availability, groove chemistry and peptide exchange. This coordination makes the class II pathway more than a simple reaction between purified MHC and peptide in solution.

13. Peptide exchange, editing and the mature class II ligand

The class II pathway must solve a problem that is easy to overlook in a static drawing of an MHC molecule. CLIP binds within the groove, and the arrival of an antigenic fragment does not necessarily cause immediate displacement of this pre-existing ligand. Peptide release and peptide capture both have kinetic barriers. If CLIP remains associated for a long time, even many suitable fragments in the same compartment may not gain access to the binding platform during its intracellular residence. A cell therefore benefits from an exchange factor capable of promoting the release of bound peptides and allowing alternative fragments to occupy the groove.

HLA-DM is such a factor. It is a nonclassical class II-like heterodimer that associates transiently with peptide-receptive or conformationally suitable class II molecules. Its interaction can promote the release of CLIP and other exchange-susceptible peptides, thereby increasing opportunities for loading of compatible ligands. HLA-DM does not attach a peptide covalently to class II, and it is not the membrane transporter that moves peptides from cytosol to endosomal lumen. Rather, it acts within the loading environment to facilitate a change in the peptide-binding state of the presenting molecule. The class II platform is the substrate of this editing process; peptides with favorable interactions can emerge from repeated cycles of release and capture.

The evidence for this activity includes biochemical work published by Lisa Denzin and Peter Cresswell in 1995, demonstrating that HLA-DM facilitated displacement of CLIP and promoted peptide loading. An independent report by Sloan and colleagues in the same year described enhancement of peptide binding through release of invariant-chain-derived peptide. The significance of these experiments lies in identifying a molecular activity rather than merely observing a correlation between DM expression and a class II phenotype. In a reduced biochemical system, peptide exchange can be studied with fewer confounding effects from antigen uptake, transcriptional regulation and movement of whole vesicles.

Peptide editing changes the repertoire, not simply the quantity of class II molecules. Some complexes are relatively resistant to DM-mediated exchange and persist, while others undergo efficient replacement. Resistance to exchange often correlates with useful structural stability, but it is not identical to a single universal dissociation constant. Two peptides with similar equilibrium affinity may respond differently to DM because their binding conformations and kinetic transitions differ. A student should therefore resist describing DM as an instrument that measures affinity numerically and invariably retains the peptide with the lowest equilibrium dissociation constant. The biological process is influenced by conformational properties, peptide concentrations, time and the particular class II allele.

HLA-DO is another class II-related molecule capable of modulating DM activity in selected antigen-presenting cells, including B cells. Its function illustrates that peptide editing can itself be regulated. The effect of DO should not be generalized into a universal on–off switch across all cell types or conditions. At the examination level it is sufficient to distinguish the conventional peptide-display molecules, the editing factor DM and the modulator DO. These three categories cannot be recognized solely from the fact that the corresponding proteins have structurally related extracellular domains.

Loss of DM often leads to increased accumulation of CLIP-bound class II and abnormal representation of antigen-derived ligands. The extent of this phenotype depends partly on the stability of CLIP binding to the particular MHC molecule. Some class II alleles exchange CLIP relatively readily even without normal DM activity. Therefore it is not justified to infer that a DM-deficient cell must have no class II protein on its surface. A normal or near-normal measurement of total class II could conceal a major change in the proportions of CLIP-containing and antigen-containing complexes. A CLIP-specific reagent and an assay of presentation to a defined T-cell clone would provide information that a general class II antibody cannot.

After acquisition of suitable peptide cargo, class II complexes can reach the plasma membrane through vesicular transport. Their subsequent stability and internalization influence how long an antigen remains available for lymphocyte recognition. This allows a presenting cell to carry information about previously acquired material even when the intact extracellular antigen is no longer present. The persistence is finite and differs between complexes and cell states. Dendritic-cell maturation can alter trafficking and turnover, contributing to changes in the abundance and persistence of class II at the surface. Presentation is thus a dynamic cellular state rather than a permanent endpoint at which peptide turnover stops.

14. Cross-presentation and the relationship between antigen origin and T-cell response

The ordinary class I and class II pathways provide a useful first approximation, but antigen origin does not uniquely determine the class of molecule on which every peptide will appear. An important biological exception becomes clear when considering the initiation of a cytotoxic response to a virus that infects tissue cells but does not efficiently infect dendritic cells. The infected tissue cells may display viral peptides on class I, but they may be unable to provide the full signals required to activate naive CD8 T cells. If only directly infected cells could generate viral class I complexes, an uninfected dendritic cell would have difficulty communicating information about the virus to the naive CD8 population.

Cross-presentation solves this problem by enabling acquired extracellular antigen to contribute peptides to class I molecules on a recipient presenting cell. Dendritic cells are especially important in this process, with certain conventional dendritic-cell subsets particularly proficient in many settings. A dendritic cell can acquire material from an infected or damaged cell, process some of its proteins and display resulting fragments through its own class I molecules. The responding CD8 lymphocyte recognizes the peptide on the dendritic cell's MHC platform, not necessarily on the MHC molecule of the original donor cell. When the responding lymphocyte becomes an effector, it can survey infected targets that display the same compatible ligand.

The distinction between cross-presentation and cross-priming is essential. Cross-presentation describes the generation and display of an acquired antigen in class I. Cross-priming describes successful activation of naive CD8 T cells through that mode of presentation in a suitable immunological context. A dendritic cell can present acquired antigen without inducing a strong effector response; in some contexts such presentation contributes to tolerance. Consequently, finding an extracellularly derived peptide on class I is evidence of presentation, but is not sufficient to demonstrate that naive lymphocytes have differentiated into functional cytotoxic effectors. A functional priming experiment must measure appropriate changes in the responding population.

Classical studies by Michael Bevan in 1976 showed that antigen supplied on cells with one MHC background could induce cytotoxic responses restricted by a different appropriate MHC background in the recipient. The results are important because they point to an intermediate presenting cell that acquires antigenic material and represents it within its own restriction context. The original experiments involved minor histocompatibility antigens, not a demonstration that every extracellular protein follows one particular molecular route. Later cellular and molecular studies helped explain how acquired protein reaches class I processing machinery. The distinction between original functional evidence and subsequent mechanistic interpretation is useful when discussing the history of the concept.

One broad model of cross-presentation is the cytosolic route. Acquired protein enters an endocytic compartment, and some of the material gains access to the cytosol. Proteasomal degradation can generate fragments in the cytosol, after which TAP-dependent transport and class I loading may occur in the ER or in suitably equipped endosomal compartments. The exact mechanisms that transfer proteins across endosomal membranes and the relative contributions of loading compartments vary across experimental systems. For the present syllabus, the essential concept is the reuse of cytosolic processing machinery for material that originally entered by extracellular uptake. This explains why a pathway beginning with endocytosis can nevertheless depend on the proteasome and TAP.

A second broad model is the vacuolar route. Peptide generation and loading can occur within vesicular compartments, sometimes without a requirement for cytosolic proteasomal processing or TAP transport. Endosomal proteases and class I molecules present in the relevant compartment provide a possible route for display. The two models are not mutually exclusive across all cells or antigens. The importance of each depends on the acquired material, receptor-mediated uptake, cell subset and cellular state. Therefore a single observation that a particular cross-presented epitope is TAP-dependent does not establish universal TAP dependence of cross-presentation.

The fact that dendritic cells can efficiently acquire extracellular material does not alone explain why cross-presentation is possible. Many cells endocytose material but do not necessarily preserve it, deliver it to the appropriate class I loading system or supply signals capable of priming naive T cells. Excessive digestion can destroy prospective class I fragments before they enter a productive route. Conversely, prolonged preservation with insufficient processing could fail to release useful peptides. Dendritic-cell specialization includes adaptations in antigen handling that influence this balance, but the molecular details need not be identical for every antigen. In an examination experiment, equal uptake should be treated as a controlled prerequisite, not as a direct measure of cross-presentation efficiency.

An additional complication is the transfer of already formed peptide–MHC complexes from one cell to another, sometimes called cross-dressing. This differs conceptually from processing an acquired antigen and loading a newly generated peptide onto the recipient cell's own MHC. Both can make antigen-associated information available on a recipient cell, but the molecular identity of the presenting platform provides a means to distinguish them. If a donor expresses allele X and the recipient expresses allele Y, detection of a donor-derived complex containing X on the recipient does not demonstrate loading of donor antigen onto Y. Demonstrating a peptide associated with Y and controlling for synthesis by the recipient provides stronger evidence for reprocessing of acquired material.

A common experimental design uses a model protein containing a known class I epitope. An antigen-bearing donor lacks the MHC allele recognized by a responder clone. A dendritic cell carrying that allele is allowed to acquire donor material. If the responder recognizes the dendritic cell only after antigen transfer, the result is consistent with cross-presentation. To exclude simpler explanations, investigators must ensure that the recipient did not itself synthesize the antigen through contamination or transfer of genetic material, and that the donor was not the cell directly responsible for the response. A presentation-specific reagent can supplement the functional response. A loss of response following selective impairment of processing in the recipient then supports a mechanistic interpretation.

The classification of a pathway should therefore begin with the MHC class, then identify the antigen's origin and route, and finally identify the outcome for the responding lymphocyte. These are separate properties. An extracellular protein on class I is a cross-presentation case; if naive CD8 activation results, cross-priming has occurred. An extracellular protein presented on class II follows the ordinary class II route unless other evidence shows unusual handling. A cell expressing class I and class II may use both routes simultaneously for different parts of one source antigen.

15. Intracellular delivery to class II and nonclassical recognition

The conventional class II pathway is often described as the presentation of extracellular antigens, yet the peptide cargo of class II is not limited to proteins that originally existed outside the cell. Cytosolic and nuclear proteins can enter degradative compartments through cellular turnover pathways. Autophagy provides one biologically important example. During macroautophagy, intracellular material is enclosed within a membrane-bound structure and delivered to the lysosomal system, where its contents can encounter degradative enzymes. In a class II-expressing cell, some fragments generated from such material may reach class II loading compartments. The final presenting platform remains class II even though the source material was originally intracellular.

This observation is not a contradiction of the topological organization of the classical routes. Rather, it illustrates that membrane traffic can change the compartmental accessibility of the antigen. Once an intracellular protein enters an endolysosomal compartment, lumenal proteases can generate fragments in an environment compatible with class II loading. A mutation affecting delivery into that compartment may selectively reduce representation of certain intracellular proteins without eliminating all uptake of extracellular proteins. Conversely, a defect in a shared class II loading component could affect peptides from both sources. An exam question asking whether all class II ligands are exogenous should therefore be answered with an explanation of route rather than a rigid rule.

Some conventional class I ligands are likewise supplied independently of the usual cytosolic proteasome–TAP sequence. Proteins associated with the secretory pathway can give rise to fragments already on the lumenal side of the ER membrane. Under appropriate conditions these fragments may be available for class I loading without the standard transport step. Other specialized routes contribute additional exceptions. A TAP loss-of-function mutation is still a strong perturbation of the principal class I pathway, but any remaining class I presentation should prompt examination of antigen origin and alternative routes rather than an assumption that the mutation must have failed.

Not all antigen-specific T cells use conventional polymorphic peptide-presenting MHC molecules. CD1 family members present certain lipid antigens to particular T-cell populations. MR1 presents selected small metabolite-derived antigens, notably microbial riboflavin pathway derivatives, to mucosal-associated invariant T cells. These examples establish that the range of structures inspected by T lymphocytes is broader than the classical peptide model. The existence of these systems does not alter the basic relationships of conventional class I with CD8 and class II with CD4, because the nonclassical pathways involve different presenting platforms and often specialized lymphocyte populations. The relevant examination distinction is between a general statement about all T cells and a carefully qualified statement about conventional peptide-specific alpha-beta T cells.

Superantigens provide a different kind of exception. Certain microbial proteins can bind outside the usual peptide-binding groove on class II molecules while interacting with selected variable regions of T-cell receptors. This arrangement can activate many cells sharing susceptible receptor-variable-region features rather than only the small population specific for a conventional processed peptide. A superantigen should not be treated simply as a very high-affinity version of an ordinary peptide ligand. Its binding geometry and the pattern of responding clones are different. Detailed disease manifestations are outside the current chapter; the concept is useful for avoiding the misleading generalization that every class II-associated T-cell response requires the same peptide processing and docking arrangement.

There are also evolutionary limits to the scope of classical MHC terminology. Conventional MHC-based adaptive immunity is a characteristic feature of jawed vertebrates, whereas jawless vertebrates possess a distinct adaptive recognition system based on variable lymphocyte receptors. Plants and bacteria have sophisticated mechanisms for recognizing threats or foreign molecular patterns but do not possess the classical vertebrate MHC–TCR presentation system. Comparing these organisms is useful because antigen presentation is sometimes confused with any process involving recognition of a pathogen. The term here refers to a defined cellular communication mechanism with specific molecular partners, not a general synonym for immune detection.

16. Affinity, peptide occupancy, competition and lifetime

A peptide–MHC complex forms through noncovalent interactions. Hydrogen bonds between the peptide backbone and residues of the groove contribute to recognition of the extended peptide chain. Side-chain packing, hydrophobic contacts, electrostatic interactions and van der Waals contacts contribute to selectivity and stability. Water molecules and changes in molecular conformations also influence the energetic outcome. A favorable binding interaction need not result from a single exceptionally strong contact; it can arise from many individually modest interactions working together in a geometrically compatible arrangement. This is why a mutation that shifts the alignment of a peptide can affect multiple contacts at once.

A simplified binding model is useful for reasoning about molecular occupancy. Suppose a soluble peptide P interacts reversibly with a single kind of receptive MHC molecule M to produce complex MP. If the association rate constant is k_on and the dissociation rate constant is k_off, the forward reaction rate is proportional to the free peptide concentration and free MHC concentration, while the reverse rate is proportional to the complex concentration. At equilibrium the rates are equal. Rearranging that equality gives K_d = [M][P]/[MP] = k_off/k_on. Square brackets indicate molar concentrations, k_on has units of inverse concentration per unit time, k_off has units of inverse time and K_d has units of concentration. A smaller K_d indicates higher equilibrium affinity for this model.

The fractional occupancy of available MHC molecules is the concentration of complex divided by total MHC concentration. Total MHC is the sum of free and bound forms. Using the definition of K_d to replace the free MHC concentration with K_d times complex concentration divided by free peptide concentration yields a simple expression: fraction bound equals [P]/(K_d + [P]). The expression assumes one independent binding site, equilibrium, a specified free peptide concentration and an accessible population of MHC molecules. It is not an equation for the percentage of all T cells that will respond. Binding of peptide to the presenting molecule occurs before and is distinct from recognition of that complex by a T-cell receptor.

Consider two peptides presented in separate idealized equilibrium experiments, each at a free concentration of 100 nanomolar. Peptide A has a dissociation constant of 20 nanomolar and peptide B a dissociation constant of 200 nanomolar. The predicted fractional occupancy for A is 100 divided by 120, or approximately 0.833. For B it is 100 divided by 300, or approximately 0.333. If the total number of equally accessible binding sites is the same, A occupies a larger fraction. This result does not establish which peptide is more immunogenic in a living organism, since peptide supply, receptor specificity, co-stimulation and the composition of the lymphocyte repertoire may differ.

The equilibrium model also explains why high affinity is not the same as permanent occupancy. Even a strongly binding peptide can dissociate, and if free peptide is continually replenished the average fractional occupancy can remain high despite ongoing molecular exchange. The same equilibrium occupancy may be maintained by rapid association and rapid dissociation or by slow association and slow dissociation, provided their ratio is the same. These two cases may behave differently during vesicle transport or after peptide supply ceases. A concentration measurement at one time point cannot by itself determine both kinetic rate constants.

If a preformed complex is placed in conditions where released peptide cannot rebind, and dissociation follows first-order kinetics with a constant rate k_off, the remaining amount is N(t) = N(0) exp(−k_off t). Taking the natural logarithm gives ln[N(t)/N(0)] = −k_off t. The half-life is obtained by setting the fraction remaining to one half, so t_half = ln 2/k_off. If k_off is 0.02 per minute, the half-life is about 34.7 minutes. If k_off is 0.10 per minute, it is about 6.93 minutes. These numerical examples concern dissociation alone, not total cell-surface persistence, because internalization and degradation could add additional routes of disappearance.

A further model considers competing peptides A and B that occupy the same groove and cannot bind simultaneously. At equilibrium the relative abundances of free, A-bound and B-bound states are proportional to 1, [A]/K_A and [B]/K_B. Normalizing these three terms by their sum gives the fractional occupancy for each peptide. For example, let [A] = 40 nM and K_A = 10 nM, while [B] = 200 nM and K_B = 100 nM. The relative weights are 1, 4 and 2. The A-bound fraction is 4/7, the B-bound fraction is 2/7 and the free fraction is 1/7. Although B is five times more abundant, A occupies a larger fraction because its binding is more favorable under these conditions.

Competition makes the concentration of a peptide biologically important even when its affinity is modest. If the concentration of B in the preceding example increases to 1000 nM while the other quantities remain unchanged, its weight becomes 10 and the denominator becomes 15. A now occupies 4/15, whereas B occupies 10/15. A peptide's contribution to the displayed repertoire therefore depends on the abundance of competing ligands. However, intracellular presentation rarely behaves like a completely homogeneous equilibrium solution: fragments are generated and destroyed, MHC molecules move through compartments, and editing factors alter exchange. The model provides a baseline for interpreting controlled binding experiments, not a complete simulation of a cell.

The free energy of binding offers another way of expressing the physical meaning of K_d. For an ideal dilute solution, the standard binding free energy can be related to the dissociation constant through ΔG° = RT ln(K_d/C°), where C° is the standard concentration of one mole per litre, R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature. The ratio inside the logarithm is dimensionless. At 298 K, a tenfold decrease in K_d changes the standard binding free energy by approximately −RT ln 10, or −5.71 kJ per mole. The sign is negative because tighter binding has a more favorable standard free energy. This comparison does not specify whether the improvement comes from enthalpy, entropy or both, and it cannot predict whether a T-cell receptor recognizes the altered complex.

The intracellular pathways use energy to establish conditions for processing and loading. Proteasomal substrate unfolding, TAP transport and vesicular traffic depend on energy-consuming cellular processes, and endosomal acidification is maintained by proton pumps. The act of peptide binding within a groove is fundamentally a molecular association rather than a direct requirement that each bound peptide be attached using ATP. Distinguishing an energy-dependent supply route from the thermodynamics of final binding helps avoid a common conceptual error: a favorable interaction may occur spontaneously once its molecular partners meet, even if cellular work was required to bring the partners into the same compartment.

17. Reading experimental evidence: necessity, sufficiency and rescue

A useful examination problem can be built from the observation that a processing-defective cell displays little stable MHC class I. Several explanations are possible before additional experiments are conducted. The class I heavy chain may be poorly expressed; beta-2-microglobulin may be absent; intracellular peptides may be insufficient; the transport machinery may fail; or surface complexes may be removed rapidly. A general measurement of class I abundance alone cannot decide among these hypotheses. The experimental design must introduce a perturbation or comparison that affects one proposed cause differently from another.

A classical model system used to explore the relationship between peptides and class I stability is the mouse lymphoma cell line RMA-S. Studies by Ljunggren and colleagues in 1990 reported that reduced culture temperature allowed a population of class I complexes to reach the cell surface in a relatively unstable state. Related studies demonstrated that suitable peptides could associate with and stabilize receptive molecules. The historical finding is not that all class I molecules need to be cooled before binding; the mutant system made otherwise difficult-to-observe receptive states accessible to investigation. The work supplied evidence that peptide availability contributes to assembly and stability rather than being merely optional decoration on an already fully stable platform.

Subsequent genetic restoration of antigen presentation in RMA-S through a transporter component helped connect the mutant phenotype to peptide transport. A restoration experiment has a different logical strength from the original correlation. If introduction of the functional gene corrects the antigen presentation defect, the result supports a causal contribution of that gene. Appropriate controls include cells receiving a nonfunctional or empty construct and a measurement of expression of the introduced gene. The combined evidence from genetic restoration, surface measurements and peptide rescue explains why a transport defect can alter the stability of a molecule encoded by different genes.

A simple teaching dataset can contrast wild-type cells, TAP-deficient cells and beta-2-microglobulin-deficient cells. Suppose all three have comparable viable cell numbers and express the same antigen precursor. If the wild type activates a class I-restricted reporter strongly, TAP-deficient cells respond weakly but recover after the addition of a suitable peptide, and beta-2-microglobulin-deficient cells remain weak even after peptide addition, the pattern supports distinct limitations. The first mutant can retain structural potential but lacks efficient peptide supply; the second lacks an essential conventional assembly partner. The inference assumes that the peptide is accessible to receptive molecules and that the reporter does not require additional signals missing selectively in one condition.

An analogous experiment in the class II pathway compares presentation of intact protein with presentation of a defined peptide. An uptake inhibitor might prevent entry of intact protein while leaving surface receptive class II available for peptide loading. A defect in a protease may reduce the generation of a peptide from the whole protein but not affect the response to externally provided fragment under suitable conditions. A selective change in HLA-DM activity could alter peptide exchange and thus have an effect on both conditions depending on the allele, peptide and loading route. The value of the protein-versus-peptide comparison is that it changes the processing requirements without changing the nominal T-cell epitope.

To determine whether a class II mutant is defective specifically in CLIP replacement, measuring total class II is insufficient. A comparison should include the proportion of class II occupied by CLIP, the presence of antigen-derived complexes and response of a clone specific for one processed epitope. If loss of DM increases CLIP and reduces several antigen-specific responses without reducing total class II proportionally, the evidence favors altered peptide exchange rather than simply absence of the platform. Reintroduction of functional DM can provide a rescue. A failure to rescue with a mutant DM protein supports the importance of the affected activity only if that protein is expressed and localized appropriately.

A T-cell response may be used as a sensitive readout of peptide presentation, but it integrates several stages of cell communication. The responding cell must encounter the presenting cell, engage its receptor, activate intracellular signaling and produce the selected measurable response. If a naive T cell is used, costimulation and cytokine environment can be important. A lack of cytokine production cannot automatically establish that the peptide–MHC ligand is missing. Measurements with peptide–MHC-specific antibodies or other binding reagents can provide more direct evidence about the ligand, while assays of T-cell receptor response reveal functional recognition. These measurements are complementary rather than interchangeable.

Flow cytometric measurement of surface MHC is another common type of evidence. A fluorescence value reflects binding of the chosen detection reagent, influenced by the amount of accessible target and the conditions of measurement. When comparing mutants, one should control instrument settings, viable-cell gating, background fluorescence and staining specificity. If an antibody recognizes a conformation-dependent epitope, its signal can fall because of altered folding even when the total quantity of heavy-chain protein does not fall to the same extent. An intracellular protein assay detecting a different feature may help distinguish those possibilities. The chapter on Methods will treat the technology in detail; the present concern is what biological variable is represented by a measured signal.

Pulse-chase reasoning can distinguish production from persistence. Imagine labeling newly synthesized MHC molecules during a short interval and then monitoring that labeled cohort over time while subsequent synthesis contributes unlabeled molecules. Early intracellular measurements indicate the formation and maturation of the cohort, whereas later surface measurements can provide information about transport and loss. A mutation that delays arrival at the surface may produce a different time course from one that allows normal arrival followed by rapid disappearance. The distinction can be invisible in a single steady-state measurement because both mutations might yield similar final surface levels.

Another experimental question concerns which stage of cross-presentation depends on cytosolic processing. A dendritic cell can be exposed to extracellular protein, and antigen-specific class I display can be measured in the presence or absence of a selective TAP perturbation. If peptide display decreases, uptake must be measured independently to ensure the cells acquired comparable antigen. A positive control using a peptide known to reach class I binding sites can test whether the remaining presenting and responding machinery is competent. Comparison with a defined endosomal protease perturbation may provide further evidence about the route. No single inhibitor result establishes a universal mechanism, because different epitopes may use different pathways and drugs can have off-target effects.

The underlying reasoning is the distinction between necessity and sufficiency. A component is necessary for a process under the specified conditions if its selective removal prevents the process. A component is sufficient only if its provision, together with the stated background conditions, produces the outcome without requiring an additional missing factor. TAP may be necessary for a selected conventional class I epitope, but its expression alone is not sufficient for presentation in a cell lacking appropriate MHC. Peptide binding may be sufficient to stabilize a receptive molecule under a defined biochemical condition, but it is not sufficient to activate every naive T cell. Clearly stating the conditions prevents these terms from becoming vague synonyms for importance.

18. Antigen presentation as a physiological communication system

At the tissue level, antigen presentation provides a connection between events occurring at sites of infection and lymphocytes organized in lymphoid tissues. A dendritic cell can encounter microbial material and inflammatory signals in a peripheral tissue. During maturation it can alter its antigen processing, surface peptide–MHC display, accessory molecule expression and migratory behavior. Arrival in a draining lymph node allows the cell to encounter many naive T lymphocytes circulating through that organ. The biological advantage is not simply transport of an intact pathogen to lymphocytes, but coordinated presentation of antigenic information in a cellular context capable of promoting appropriate activation.

A professional antigen-presenting cell can communicate more than the identity of the fragment it displays. The peptide–MHC complex provides specificity through the T-cell receptor. Costimulatory interactions, such as CD80 or CD86 engaging CD28, contribute to activation of naive T cells in many circumstances. Cytokines help shape subsequent differentiation. A peptide–MHC molecule should therefore be interpreted as one element of a larger communication system rather than as a signal that universally causes the same outcome. Display in an inflammatory context and display in a context without appropriate costimulation can have different consequences for the same responding clone. Detailed activation and tolerance mechanisms are reserved for the later chapters.

Macrophages illustrate the relationship between antigen handling and local effector function. A macrophage that has engulfed a microorganism can present peptides associated with class II. A compatible activated helper T cell can deliver signals that alter the macrophage's antimicrobial state. In this case presentation allows the lymphocyte to direct assistance toward a cell associated with the relevant antigen. The process differs from recognition of an infected target by a cytotoxic CD8 T cell through class I, where a major outcome may involve elimination of the target. The two arrangements connect the source and location of antigenic information to the biological response most suited to the problem.

B cells illustrate another use of class II. Binding a folded antigen through the B-cell receptor enriches the internalized material even when its concentration in the extracellular environment is low. Processing can generate several peptide fragments. Some may bind class II molecules and be recognized by helper T cells. Thus the specific B-cell receptor determines which molecular material is captured efficiently, whereas class II binding and T-cell receptor specificity determine which portions support T-cell assistance. The interaction does not require the B-cell receptor and T-cell receptor to bind the same amino acid sequence or the same structural epitope. This relationship is important for understanding antibody responses to proteins and linked recognition of a hapten attached to a carrier protein.

Class I surveillance also intersects with innate immunity. If an abnormal cell reduces its surface class I to diminish recognition by CD8 T cells, it may lose inhibitory interactions that normally restrain natural killer cells. The outcome is not guaranteed NK-mediated killing, because NK cells integrate multiple inhibitory and activating receptor signals. Nevertheless, the two surveillance strategies impose different constraints on cells that alter antigen display. A loss of class I can reduce conventional peptide-specific recognition while increasing susceptibility to some innate responses. The direction of a phenotype therefore depends on the effector population tested. This explains why a mutation can have apparently opposing consequences in two distinct immune assays.

Antigen presentation is valuable in research because it allows investigators to separate questions about antigen supply from questions about receptor recognition. Peptides can be compared for binding to selected alleles, antigen-expressing cells can be compared with peptide-exposed cells, and responder populations can be tested against defined ligands. In vaccine biology, the form of an antigen and its delivery influence the pathways through which it becomes available to B and T cells. A protein acquired by dendritic cells may provide class II ligands and, in suitable contexts, cross-presented class I ligands. A protein synthesized within a cell can enter class I processing directly. The immunological consequence depends on additional signals and the responding repertoire; the presence of a given antigen format is not a guarantee of a particular protective outcome.

The pathway also offers a framework for interpreting immune failure. Loss of a transporter, structural partner or loading factor can reduce the diversity or abundance of useful peptide–MHC complexes. A defect in class II expression can compromise the interactions through which helper T cells coordinate multiple arms of adaptive immunity. Mutations affecting processing may selectively remove some epitopes rather than eliminating every response. These distinctions prepare the student for later chapters on immunodeficiency and immune modulation without requiring clinical classification or treatment details here.

Ultimately, the presentation system is a link between information encoded in protein sequences, the intracellular organization of membrane-bound compartments and the specificity of lymphocyte receptors. It is not an independent mechanism for deciding that a molecule is dangerous; it supplies part of the evidence on which immune responses depend. The next chapter applies this principle to the B cell, which both recognizes native antigen through its own receptor and converts captured protein into information that can be read by a helper T cell.
