1. The biological question: why growth needs a rate

A newly inoculated microbial culture may look almost unchanged for some time, then become visibly cloudy over a relatively short interval. The cells have not suddenly acquired a new mechanism of reproduction at the moment the cloudiness becomes apparent. The increasing population has gradually reached a density that scatters enough light to be noticed. Each of many cells now contributes descendants to the population. This difference between a small population producing a few new cells and a large population producing many new cells is the central reason microbial growth requires a mathematical description that accounts for the population already present.

Growth has more than one meaning in biology. An individual bacterial cell grows when it accumulates membrane, protein, nucleic acid, cell wall and other cellular material. A population grows numerically when the number of cells increases. A filamentous fungus may produce a great deal of biomass without making a corresponding number of independently countable individuals. Even among unicellular bacteria, a division defect can produce long cells whose total mass increases while cell number changes very little. A mathematical model therefore starts by naming what is being measured rather than treating the word growth as a complete definition.

Let N be the number of cells, or the number of cells per unit volume in a culture whose volume remains constant. Let t denote the elapsed time after an explicitly chosen starting point. If a student measures the culture at two moments, N₁ and N₂ are two observations, not rates. The difference N₂ − N₁ tells us the net change in number, and the quotient (N₂ − N₁)/(t₂ − t₁) tells us the average absolute change per unit time. An absolute change is important when estimating the total production of cells, but it is not generally sufficient for comparing their physiological performance.

Consider two hypothetical cultures in the same environment. Culture A contains 100 cells and rises to 200 in one hour. Culture B contains 10,000 and rises to 20,000 in the same hour. A adds 100 cells and B adds 10,000. B therefore has a hundredfold larger absolute increase, but both populations have doubled. If they are homogeneous populations with negligible loss, the capacity for reproduction per unit of existing population is the same. An absolute difference alone would lead to the mistaken conclusion that B has a hundredfold higher intrinsic rate of proliferation. Normalization to the population size eliminates that distinction.

The term kinetics refers to the quantitative description of change with time. In microbial physiology the relevant question is not just whether conditions permit growth but how rapidly a defined biological quantity changes, over what interval, and under what assumptions. A growth curve is the time-dependent observation; a growth equation is a mathematical model for that observation. Two cultures may show similar changes over a short period even if their later curves differ. The model must be tested within the interval to which it is applied.

Cells are able to multiply because they acquire resources and use chemical energy to make cellular structures. In a growing bacterium, replication produces the genetic material necessary for daughter cells, while biosynthesis increases the material available for partitioning. Binary fission normally increases the number of independently living cellular units. However, it does not create cellular mass from nothing. The two daughters together initially contain approximately the mass of their parent immediately before separation, with ongoing synthesis contributing new material before and after the division event. This is why a count trajectory and a biomass trajectory need not have identical detailed shapes at the level of a single cell.

In the earlier growth-curve model, freshly transferred cells may show a period of adaptation, followed by a period of rapid numerical increase, then growth limitation and eventually a decline in recoverable cells. Kinetic calculations in this chapter are based primarily on the exponential portion. They do not automatically describe the entire period since inoculation. If the graph tells us that an exponential phase starts two hours after inoculation, a rate estimated from that phase cannot be extrapolated backwards to the inoculation time without an additional assumption about the lag phase.

There are two complementary ways to formulate the exponential model. The first uses generations and binary multiplication. It is accessible because it follows the descendants of an initial cell. The second uses a continuous rate proportional to the population, which makes the mathematics suitable for an asynchronous culture with many cells dividing at different times. They are not competing explanations of growth. When their assumptions agree, they predict exactly the same population ratios and doubling times. Understanding their connection removes the need to memorize unrelated equations.

A useful distinction should be maintained throughout this chapter between directly measured quantities and quantities inferred using a model. A count at a given moment is an observation with measurement error. A calculated generation time based on two such counts is an estimate. A fitted specific growth rate is a summary of the change during an interval. A prediction of how many cells will exist later is conditional on the continuation of the estimated conditions. These three activities—measurement, estimation and prediction—have different sources of uncertainty.

2. Binary fission as the basis of geometric population increase

Suppose one cell divides into two viable daughter cells and both daughters subsequently divide. Starting from a single founder, the first division gives two cells, the second complete round gives four, and the third gives eight. There are eight cells after three rounds, not six, because every member of the population can contribute to the next round. The same principle applies to a culture containing many founders: if each cell successfully produces two daughters and neither is lost, each completed round multiplies the population by two.

An ideal synchronized population is a convenient starting model. Synchronization means that all cells are at similar stages and divide at approximately the same time. At the initial moment N₀ cells are present. One complete generation later N₁ = 2N₀. Two generations later N₂ = 2N₁ = 4N₀. After three generations N₃ = 2N₂ = 8N₀. Repeating the multiplication n times gives N = N₀ × 2ⁿ. The exponent counts how many factors of two have been applied, whereas the base two represents the number of daughters produced per dividing parent. This is called geometric growth when described in equal discrete rounds.

The net number of additional cells is not N₀ × 2ⁿ; it is N − N₀. For example, four founders undergoing three rounds give 32 cells in total, an increase of 28. Under the special assumption that each division replaces one parent with two daughters and there is no loss, each individual fission event contributes one net cell. Hence the cumulative number of fission events is also 28. It can be obtained by adding four first-round events, eight second-round events and sixteen third-round events. The final number of descendants and the number of division events differ because the original four cells were already present.

A linear growth model instead adds the same number at every interval. If four cells somehow produced four additional cells per generation-equivalent period regardless of their abundance, the sequence would be 4, 8, 12, 16. In the binary growth model the sequence is 4, 8, 16, 32. Both models give the same answer after one period, so a single observation of doubling cannot by itself establish exponential kinetics. The distinction emerges only when several time intervals reveal whether differences or ratios remain approximately constant.

The discrete picture also clarifies why the count of generations is not the same as the count of cell divisions in a culture. A generation corresponds to a doubling of the population under the idealized exponential model. A thousand founders all dividing once give two thousand cells: one population generation has occurred, but one thousand individual division events occurred. Calling the generation number one thousand would confuse the events occurring in parallel with the number of successive rounds experienced by a lineage. This distinction is especially useful when a problem asks separately about descendant number, number of generations and net additions.

A real lineage can show considerable variability. Cells may not receive exactly the same amount of material at division, and physiological state, cell age and environmental fluctuations can influence how soon the daughters divide. A cell may fail to divide or produce a nonviable descendant. The simple geometric expression assumes that these effects are negligible for the chosen interpretation or that their combined effect can be captured by a constant net population doubling rate. It is not a microscopic law that every bacterium must execute at identical intervals.

Even for an ideal synchronized population, the actual numerical trajectory is stepwise. In a complete round, the count remains nearly constant until cells separate, then increases. If the exact same generation time g applies to every cell, the observed count in this special idealization is N₀ × 2 raised to the integer number of completed rounds. A graph of actual discrete counts would contain horizontal segments separated by jumps. The smoothly interpolated expression N₀ × 2^(t/g) agrees with the counts at the full-generation moments but should not be interpreted as the literal number of cells between two perfectly synchronized division events.

The ordinary broth culture differs from this idealization. It typically contains cells distributed across different stages of their own cycles. Some are growing towards division while others have just separated. When the culture is sufficiently large, division events overlap in time and the population trajectory can be treated as smooth. This explains why a mathematical expression containing a fractional generation can describe the behavior of a population even though no individual cell completes a fraction of a division event in the sense of producing a fractional daughter. The fractional generation is an equivalent change in population number on a logarithmic scale.

Worked example 1. A hypothetical culture begins the exponential interval with 7,500 independently countable cells. After four complete population doublings, N = 7,500 × 2⁴ = 7,500 × 16 = 120,000 cells. The net increase is 112,500 cells. Under loss-free binary division, the cumulative event number is also 112,500. If there were deaths, the same net increase could arise from a larger number of fissions accompanied by losses, so the event count could no longer be recovered from the two population sizes alone.

3. Generation time and the meaning of fractional generations

Generation time is conventionally denoted by g or t_d in elementary microbial population kinetics. Here g is the time required for a specified population quantity to double during steady exponential growth. A single cell also has an interdivision time, the interval between its birth and its next division. When every cell has the same interdivision time and there are no losses, that interval coincides with population doubling time. In a heterogeneous population the population doubling time is an aggregate property and need not equal the ordinary arithmetic average of observed individual cycle times.

A culture growing exponentially with a generation time of 30 minutes undergoes two equivalent doublings per hour. With a generation time of two hours, it undergoes only half an equivalent doubling per hour. This inverse relationship is fundamental: shorter generation time means faster proportional population growth. A large generation time is not evidence of greater growth simply because the number is larger. It expresses the time cost of multiplying by a fixed factor.

If a population grows through n equivalent generations in elapsed time t, then n = t/g and g = t/n. The relationship follows from the definition: the elapsed time is the number of equivalent generation intervals multiplied by the duration of each interval. In a constant-rate model one can use fractional values of n. For example, 45 minutes at a generation time of 30 minutes corresponds to n = 1.5. The expected population ratio is 2^1.5 = 2√2 ≈ 2.828, not three and not four.

A second example makes the interpretation of fractions more concrete. A population whose number rises by 50 percent has become 1.5 times its starting size. It has not undergone half a doubling, because halfway in time between N₀ and 2N₀ under exponential growth is √2 N₀, about 1.414N₀. Instead the equivalent generation number is log₂(1.5) ≈ 0.585. In general, a fractional increase expressed as a percentage does not translate linearly into a fraction of a generation.

Sometimes a textbook denotes the number of generations per time by k. We will use k = n/t = 1/g, with units of generations per hour if time is expressed in hours. This symbol must not be confused with the natural-logarithmic specific growth rate μ, which has units of inverse time and differs numerically by a factor of ln 2. Different authors use the letter k for different rate constants. An examination problem should therefore be interpreted according to the equation supplied or the quantity explicitly defined rather than according to the letter alone.

The measured generation time belongs to the particular organism and conditions under which the estimate was made. A species may grow rapidly in a nutrient-replete medium at a favorable temperature and much more slowly in another environment. It is scientifically inappropriate to say that a familiar laboratory bacterium always has a fixed short generation time under all circumstances. The genome makes particular biochemical strategies possible, but the environment and current physiology constrain their performance. Temperature, nutrient availability, pH and oxygen can affect rates of biosynthesis or loss; these effects are developed further in the next chapter.

Worked example 2. A population of 2 × 10⁴ cells per millilitre grows with g = 40 min for 3 h 20 min, with no lag or loss and fixed volume. Convert 3 h 20 min to 200 min before dividing by 40 min. The ratio is five generations and N = 2 × 10⁴ × 32 = 6.4 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre. Expressing time as 3.20 hours would be incorrect: 20 minutes is one third of an hour. The equivalent calculation is (10/3 h)/(2/3 h) = 5. Both approaches give the same dimensionless exponent.

Worked example 3. A population quadruples over 90 min. The factor four is 2², so there are two equivalent generations. The generation time is 45 min, and k = 60/45 = 4/3 generations per hour. The percentage increase is 300 percent of the initial population, not 400 percent, because the final amount includes the original 100 percent. Population ratio, percentage increase and equivalent generations answer three distinct questions.

4. Finding unknown generation numbers using logarithms

Population multiplication is easy to compute when n is known. The inverse problem is more common in an experiment: the investigator measures the starting and ending population and wishes to determine how many equivalent doublings occurred. A logarithm is the inverse of exponentiation. If a positive number R equals 2ⁿ, then n = log₂ R. Taking the ratio of the two observed population values removes the starting scale and leaves the multiplication factor.

Begin with N = N₀ × 2ⁿ. Divide both sides by N₀ to obtain N/N₀ = 2ⁿ. The ratio must be positive. Taking natural logarithms gives ln(N/N₀) = n ln 2, using the logarithmic rule that the logarithm of a power equals the exponent times the logarithm of the base. Rearranging gives n = ln(N/N₀)/ln 2. The same derivation using common logarithms yields n = log₁₀(N/N₀)/log₁₀2. The base of the logarithm is not a biological property of the culture; it is a choice of mathematical representation, and both numerator and denominator must use the same base.

The quotient rule also permits the expression n = [log₁₀ N − log₁₀ N₀]/0.30103 when both numbers use the same population units. With logarithms, scientific notation is useful. For a quantity a × 10ᵇ, log₁₀ of its numerical value is log₁₀ a + b. If the same powers of ten occur in both starting and ending values, they cancel in the ratio. In formal dimensional terms the logarithm should apply to a dimensionless ratio, or to a numerical value expressed relative to a specified reference unit. It is therefore important to express both observations on the same basis before taking logarithms.

Suppose the count increases from 10⁴ to 10⁷ cells per millilitre. The ratio is 10³, or a thousandfold increase. Three is the difference in base-ten logarithms, not the number of binary generations. The latter is 3/0.30103 ≈ 9.966. A common error is to equate an increase of three orders of magnitude with three doublings. Three doublings give only eightfold multiplication. Ten doublings give 1,024-fold multiplication, which explains why a thousandfold change corresponds to slightly fewer than ten generations.

Worked example 4. A constant-volume culture rises from 2.5 × 10⁵ to 1.0 × 10⁷ cells per millilitre during a four-hour exponential interval. The ratio is 40. Because 2⁵ = 32 and 2⁶ = 64, we can predict that n lies between five and six. Numerically n = ln 40/ln 2 = 3.688879/0.693147 ≈ 5.32193. Therefore g = 4/5.32193 h ≈ 0.75161 h, or 45.10 min. A plausibility check is that four hours divided by roughly five and a third gives slightly less than an hour. Inserting the estimated g back into the original expression recovers the observed factor 40 to rounding accuracy.

A logarithm of a ratio smaller than one is negative. If a viable count falls from 800 to 200, log₂(200/800) = −2. This says the population has experienced a net reduction by a factor of four, equivalent to two successive halvings. The negative value is not a negative number of actual divisions. In that interval the growth-only interpretation has failed because net loss exceeds production. Similarly, the value n = 0 corresponds to no net change. Dividing elapsed time by zero to report a doubling time is not a meaningful finite result. These cases motivate a more general treatment in terms of net specific rate.

5. From a generation model to a continuous rate equation

The multiplication model gives the population at a selected time but does not initially describe what happens during a small time interval. To develop a continuous description, imagine a culture containing so many asynchronously dividing cells that its count can be treated as a smooth variable. During a short interval Δt, some cells complete division. If the environment and physiological state remain effectively unchanged, the expected number of additional cells over a sufficiently short time is approximately proportional to the number currently present. Twice as many equivalent cells provide twice as many opportunities for division during that short interval.

The proportionality is not meant to imply that an individual cell divides at a randomly selected instant without a cell cycle. It summarizes the behavior of a population with a stable distribution of physiological states. When cells of different ages are present in stable proportions, doubling the population approximately doubles the number of cells in each of those states. The number about to divide also increases proportionally. This population-level reasoning motivates the relation ΔN ≈ μNΔt for a sufficiently small Δt.

As the interval becomes very short, the difference quotient ΔN/Δt approaches the derivative dN/dt in the continuous idealization. The derivative is the instantaneous absolute rate of net population increase. Thus dN/dt = μN. Rearrangement gives μ = (1/N)(dN/dt). The constant μ is the specific growth rate under a constant exponential regime. The adjective specific indicates that growth has been normalized to the amount of population already present.

The unit follows directly from this definition. Suppose N is expressed as cells per millilitre and t in hours. Then dN/dt has units cells per millilitre per hour. Dividing by N cancels both the cell and volume units and leaves h⁻¹. For a biomass concentration measured in grams per litre, the corresponding rate has units grams per litre per hour, and normalization again gives h⁻¹. The numerical value of μ does depend on the chosen time unit, but not on whether a fixed concentration is expressed per litre or per millilitre.

In the differential equation dN/dt = μN, a fixed positive μ produces a changing absolute rate. At a particular moment a culture with N = 100,000 cells per millilitre and μ = 0.5 h⁻¹ has an instantaneous net rate of 50,000 cells per millilitre per hour. At twice that concentration, the instantaneous rate is 100,000 cells per millilitre per hour. It would be incorrect to add exactly 50,000 cells per millilitre over the next full hour on the assumption that the initial absolute rate remains unchanged. The new cells that appear during that hour also contribute to subsequent growth.

To obtain the exact constant-rate prediction, divide dN/dt = μN by N, giving dN/N = μ dt. Integrate from the initial time t = 0, when the population is N₀, to a later time t with population N. The integral of dN/N from N₀ to N is ln N − ln N₀, equivalently ln(N/N₀). The integral of μ dt over the interval is μt when μ is constant. Consequently ln(N/N₀) = μt. Exponentiation gives N/N₀ = e^(μt), or N = N₀ e^(μt). Here e is the natural exponential base, approximately 2.71828. The exponent μt is dimensionless, which offers a powerful check on whether the time conversion was made correctly.

This derivation shows why μ is the natural-log rate rather than the exact fractional increase over any arbitrary one-hour interval. With μ = 0.5 h⁻¹, the one-hour factor is e^0.5 ≈ 1.64872, so the population grows by approximately 64.87 percent during one hour, not 50 percent. Over a very short interval the percentage increase is approximately 100μΔt, because e^x ≈ 1 + x when x is near zero. Over a longer interval, the difference between instantaneous rate and finite proportional increase becomes appreciable.

Worked example 5. A culture begins a steady interval with 2 × 10⁶ cells per millilitre and μ = 0.4 h⁻¹. After 2.5 h the exponent is 0.4 × 2.5 = 1. The final concentration is 2 × 10⁶ × e ≈ 5.4366 × 10⁶ cells per millilitre. Its initial instantaneous rate is 8 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre per hour; its final instantaneous rate is approximately 2.1746 × 10⁶ cells per millilitre per hour. The average absolute rate across the interval is (5.4366 − 2.0) × 10⁶ / 2.5 ≈ 1.3746 × 10⁶ cells per millilitre per hour. This average lies between the initial and final instantaneous rates, as expected for continuously accelerating exponential growth.

The exponential relation applies equally if the initial time is not the time of inoculation. For a steady interval beginning at t₁, the equation is N(t₂) = N(t₁)e^[μ(t₂ − t₁)]. This expression is invariant to a shift in the time origin. It is particularly convenient when observations begin after the lag phase and no reliable count at inoculation is available. It also prevents a common examination mistake: using the final clock time rather than the actual length of the interval used to estimate the rate.

6. The mathematical bridge between μ, g and k

The generation equation and the continuous exponential equation describe the same population trajectory if they are defined for the same steady exponential phase. Substitute n = t/g into the geometric expression to obtain N = N₀ × 2^(t/g). Since 2^a = e^(a ln 2), this becomes N = N₀ e^[(ln 2/g)t]. Comparing the exponent with μt gives μ = ln 2/g. Alternatively, set N/N₀ = 2 at t = g in the continuous equation, then 2 = e^(μg), and taking logarithms gives ln 2 = μg. The two derivations reach the same result from different starting points.

The corresponding expressions are g = ln 2/μ, μ = k ln 2, and k = μ/ln 2 when k denotes doublings per time. These are not three separately adjustable parameters of the model. Knowing any one determines the other two. If a question gives mutually inconsistent values, the inconsistency may indicate different time units, different measured quantities, different intervals or an invalid constant-rate assumption.

The numerical value ln 2 ≈ 0.693147 appears because a doubling is a factor of two and the exponential equation uses natural logarithms. It is not an empirical correction specific to bacteria. If the question asked for the time required for a tripling, the factor would be ln 3 instead. A tenfold increase requires ln 10/μ. A change from any initial value to a target R times greater requires t = ln R/μ for positive μ. Thus the doubling time is one member of a more general family of fold-change times.

As an illustration, a doubling time of 20 minutes equals one third of an hour. Then μ = 0.693147/(1/3 h) = 2.07944 h⁻¹. In minute units the same rate is 0.0346574 min⁻¹. The generation frequency is 3 generations per hour or 0.05 generations per minute. Neither 3 h⁻¹ nor 0.05 min⁻¹ is the numerical value of the natural-log specific growth rate. In a problem where the definition of k is supplied as dN/dt = kN, that k would instead be 2.07944 h⁻¹. The formula, not the label, establishes the meaning.

Worked example 6. Culture A has μ = 0.8 h⁻¹. Culture B has g = 60 min. Convert B to a natural-log specific rate: μ_B = ln 2/1 h ≈ 0.69315 h⁻¹. A has the larger specific rate and its doubling time is 0.693147/0.8 ≈ 0.86643 h = 51.99 min. If both begin at identical concentrations and grow for 3 h under constant conditions, their ratio N_A/N_B is e^[(0.8 − 0.693147) × 3] ≈ 1.378. The ratio is not simply 0.8/0.693147 because the rate difference acts continuously on populations that themselves change.

Worked example 7. A culture increases sixfold during a five-hour interval. The average log-specific rate is ln 6/5 = 1.791759/5 = 0.35835 h⁻¹. Assuming steady exponential growth, the doubling time is ln 2/0.35835 ≈ 1.9343 h, or 116.06 min. The equivalent number of generations is log₂ 6 ≈ 2.585. As a check, five hours divided by 2.585 gives the same generation time. The distinction between the observed sixfold change and the inferred doubling time matters if growth is nonuniform: the two endpoints establish the interval-average log rate but not necessarily a constant instantaneous rate.

There is an additional difference between time to double a population and time to accumulate a specified absolute number of new cells. When a population of 1,000 cells and a population of 1,000,000 cells have the same μ, both double in the same time. The larger population will nevertheless accumulate an extra thousand cells in a much shorter time. In the exponential model, the time required to add a fixed amount A is ln(1 + A/N₀)/μ. The expression decreases as N₀ increases because the same additional number represents a smaller proportional change. This is why a culture with a larger inoculum may reach an absolute detection threshold earlier without having any higher specific growth rate.

7. Representing exponential growth on arithmetic and logarithmic axes

A graph provides information beyond the final answer of a calculation. On ordinary arithmetic axes, equal vertical distances represent equal absolute changes in the population. Starting with 100 cells, a sequence of doublings produces values 100, 200, 400, 800 and 1600. The successive absolute increases are 100, 200, 400 and 800. A line drawn through the points becomes increasingly steep. The upward curvature in the underlying smooth model follows from the fact that the derivative μN increases as N increases, even though μ itself is constant.

On a logarithmic vertical scale, equal distances represent equal ratios. The distance from 100 to 200 is the same as from 200 to 400 because both changes are twofold. This compresses large values and makes it possible to display several orders of magnitude on one graph without making the early low-density points visually indistinguishable. When time is on an ordinary linear horizontal axis and population is on a logarithmic vertical axis, the result is a semilogarithmic plot.

Take the natural logarithm of the exponential model, giving ln(N/N_ref) = ln(N₀/N_ref) + μt, where N_ref is a fixed reference quantity of the same unit as N. This has the standard straight-line form y = a + mt. The intercept a is the logarithm of the starting quantity relative to the reference, and the slope m is μ. Because the reference is constant, changing it shifts the line vertically but does not change its slope. In many textbook graphs the reference unit is implicit and the axis is simply labeled ln N, with the population unit specified separately.

If the graph uses base-ten logarithms instead, then log₁₀(N/N_ref) = log₁₀(N₀/N_ref) + (μ/ln 10)t. The slope m₁₀ is μ/ln 10. Therefore μ = ln 10 × m₁₀ ≈ 2.302585m₁₀, and g = log₁₀2/m₁₀ ≈ 0.30103/m₁₀. If the graph uses base two, the slope is k = 1/g. Equal numbers on graphs of different log bases do not represent equal biological rates unless the base conversion is made.

A plot with an explicitly logarithmically scaled vertical axis may label the ticks as actual concentrations such as 10⁴, 10⁵ and 10⁶ rather than as 4, 5 and 6. In that case it is important to read the values as counts and take their logarithms before calculating the slope. If the displayed ordinate is already log₁₀ N, the plotted ordinate values should be subtracted directly; taking the logarithm a second time would be an error. The distinction is not always obvious at first glance, but the axis labels tell us which operation is appropriate.

A line that rises steeply on a log plot signifies a larger specific rate only when compared on the same vertical scale and horizontal time unit. Two graphs printed at different sizes or plotted with different axis ranges can make identical rates look different. The visual angle is not an intrinsic growth property. Use numerical coordinates to compare slopes, and convert units before interpreting the result.

Parallel straight lines on the same semilogarithmic axes represent equal specific rates even if their vertical positions differ. A higher line corresponds to a greater population at the same time, not necessarily more rapid reproduction. The ratio between populations is constant when their slopes are equal. If the lines have different slopes, the ratio changes with time. A lower initial population may eventually exceed a higher initial population if its rate is greater and the exponential regime persists for long enough.

Worked example 8. A base-ten plot rises from log₁₀ N = 4.2 at t = 1 h to log₁₀ N = 5.1 at t = 4 h. Its slope is (5.1 − 4.2)/(4 − 1) = 0.30 h⁻¹ in base-ten log units. The natural-log rate is 0.30 × 2.302585 ≈ 0.69078 h⁻¹. The doubling time is 0.30103/0.30 ≈ 1.00343 h, or 60.21 min. The slight deviation from exactly one hour is expected because 0.30 is a rounded value for log₁₀2. This calculation would be wrong by a factor of 2.302585 if the base-ten slope were directly called μ.

Worked example 9. On natural-log axes culture A has slope 0.6 h⁻¹, while culture B has slope 0.2 h⁻¹. At the beginning of the compared interval B is ten times as abundant as A. The two populations will become equal when N_A0 e^(0.6t) = 10N_A0 e^(0.2t), so e^(0.4t) = 10. Taking logarithms gives t = ln 10/0.4 = 5.75646 h. This is a conditional crossing time. If the culture with the larger rate enters stationary phase before the crossing, the real data may never show it overtaking the other population.

8. Estimating a reliable rate from experimental measurements

The equations developed so far establish a relationship between a rate and a population trajectory. An experimental estimate begins with measured quantities that are never perfectly exact. Cell counts may vary because the sample is small, optical measurements may vary because the instrument has limited precision, and even nominally identical cultures may display biological variation. A rate computed from two numbers is meaningful only if the numbers refer to a comparable measurement made over a well-defined interval.

The first analytical task is to identify the portion of the trajectory that is compatible with a constant specific rate. A graph of count against time may be difficult to inspect for this purpose because the exponential region curves upward. A natural-log graph makes the task easier: a section containing several approximately collinear points supports the constant-rate model. Points before that section may indicate lag or a transitional physiological state; points after it may indicate slowing or net loss. There is no requirement that the straight section begin at inoculation or continue until the final observation.

Consider a hypothetical series measured every hour after inoculation. At hours zero and one the count remains near 10,000 cells per millilitre. At hours two, three and four the counts are 20,000, 40,000 and 80,000. At hour five the count is 100,000 and by hour six it is 105,000. A student who uses the first and last observations obtains an average logarithmic change of ln(10.5)/6, approximately 0.392 h⁻¹. This number accurately summarizes the ratio over six hours, but it is not the constant specific growth rate of the exponential phase. Using hours one through four, the population grows eightfold in three hours, giving μ = ln 8/3 = ln 2 h⁻¹. The last two intervals show much smaller proportional increases, and the first shows almost none.

Choosing endpoints only because they are the earliest and latest points on a graph is therefore not a sound general procedure. Equally, choosing two nearby points because their slope is especially large can lead to an overestimate when measurement noise produces a steep local difference. One should first identify a biologically credible interval, then estimate its slope using the available observations. A fitted line through multiple points is usually more robust than a single pair, provided the points belong to the same phase and the measurement conditions are appropriate.

The idea of a least-squares fit can be understood without advanced statistical theory. Each observation has a time tᵢ and a transformed value yᵢ = ln(Nᵢ/N_ref). A proposed straight line predicts yᵢ as a + μtᵢ. The vertical difference between the observed and predicted value is the residual. Squaring the residuals prevents positive and negative errors from cancelling and gives a larger penalty to larger deviations. The least-squares line is the one minimizing the sum of these squared differences. For unweighted observations its slope is the sum of (tᵢ − mean t)(yᵢ − mean y) divided by the sum of (tᵢ − mean t)². The numerator measures how time and log population vary together, while the denominator measures how much the sampling times vary.

A fitted line is not automatically a good model. If residuals are systematically positive at both ends and negative in the middle, the trajectory may be curved. A very high coefficient of determination can occur even for a smooth nonexponential trajectory observed over a short time. A visual examination of the residual pattern, consecutive interval slopes and knowledge of the culture's physiology are all relevant. When examining log-transformed counts, note that the fit treats a proportional discrepancy similarly at low and high population values; this may be useful, but it does not make every measurement equally precise.

Worked example 10. Imagine measurements at 0, 1, 2 and 3 h with natural-log population values relative to a fixed reference of 8.0, 8.6, 9.1 and 9.9. The average time is 1.5 h and the average log value is 8.9. The products of deviations are (−1.5)(−0.9) = 1.35, (−0.5)(−0.3) = 0.15, (0.5)(0.2) = 0.10 and (1.5)(1.0) = 1.50. Their sum is 3.10. The squared time deviations sum to 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 = 5 h². Hence μ = 3.10/5 = 0.62 h⁻¹. The intercept is 8.9 − 0.62 × 1.5 = 7.97. The fitted values are 7.97, 8.59, 9.21 and 9.83. The deviations are 0.03, 0.01, −0.11 and 0.07. The estimated doubling time is 0.693147/0.62 ≈ 1.118 h, or 67.1 minutes. These small residuals do not establish a mechanistic cause for each deviation. The numerical data are illustrative, not reported measurements.

Sampling frequency matters because a long interval hides transitions. Two observations separated by four hours could agree exactly with the constant-rate equation while the true culture grew fast for two hours and slowly for two. Intermediate observations distinguish the two trajectories. Conversely, taking points so close together that the population barely changes can make the estimated slope highly sensitive to measurement error. The practical goal is to collect enough information to identify a stable region without assuming that more time points necessarily cure poor measurement quality.

An experiment comparing two strains or two conditions should use compatible measurement definitions, matched time units, an appropriate reference condition, and comparable physiological starting states. Replicate cultures help distinguish reproducible changes in growth rate from variation in inoculation or sampling. Technical replicate measurements estimate the variability of the measurement itself, whereas independently initiated biological replicates include variation among cultures. These are complementary forms of evidence, not substitutes. A simple calculation of two growth rates is therefore the beginning of interpretation, not the whole interpretation.

9. What exactly does the population variable measure?

The symbol N is convenient in a mathematical expression, but it may represent a direct cell count, a count of recoverable colony-forming units, or an estimated cell concentration inferred from optical density. These observations are related under appropriate conditions but are not inherently identical. Growth kinetics becomes biologically informative when the distinction is preserved.

A colony-forming unit, abbreviated CFU, is an entity that produces a detectable colony under the chosen counting conditions. It may be one cell, a pair or a cluster. If the average cluster size is stable and the same fraction of viable cells forms colonies at each measurement, a CFU trajectory may track numerical proliferation proportionally. A constant multiplier between two measures cancels when a ratio is calculated. For instance, if every CFU represents exactly two cells throughout an interval, doubling the CFU count also doubles the cell count. The logarithmic slopes of both trajectories are the same even though their values differ by a factor of two.

If clustering changes over time, the multiplier is no longer constant. Suppose a population contains 1,000 living cells arranged in groups of four, so that there are 250 CFUs. Later the same 1,000 cells become separated into individual units, yielding 1,000 CFUs. A count-based formula applied to the CFUs would infer a fourfold increase and two equivalent CFU doublings. Yet there was no numerical cell multiplication. The observation is genuine, but its biological meaning is the increase in recoverable units rather than a fourfold increase in individual cells. Microscopic counting or an independent biomass estimate could help distinguish these explanations.

Similarly, optical density (OD) is a measure of attenuation of light passing through a sample. In a suspension of many microbial cells, scattering is often an important contributor. Within a suitable range and with stable cell properties, a background-corrected OD can be approximately proportional to suspended cellular biomass or number. At very high density, multiple scattering and instrumental limitations may disrupt that proportionality. At very low density, the background signal may be comparable to the microbial contribution. An OD value is not a direct count of living cells and should not be automatically converted to CFU by a universal factor.

A simple background example illustrates why preprocessing changes kinetic estimates. Suppose true cell-associated signals at two times are 0.10 and 0.20, and the medium contributes a constant measured signal of 0.05. The instrument readings are 0.15 and 0.25. The raw ratio is 1.667, while the corrected ratio is 2. If the interval is one hour, the uncorrected log rate is ln(5/3) ≈ 0.511 h⁻¹ and the corrected rate is ln 2 ≈ 0.693 h⁻¹. A constant additive offset does not cancel in a ratio. This is distinct from a stable multiplicative conversion factor, which does cancel. The lesson is applicable to any count proxy with a nonzero blank.

The same logic applies to biomass. Let X denote biomass concentration, commonly expressed as grams of dry cellular material per litre, and let m denote the average mass per counted cell in compatible units. Then X = Nm when N is cell concentration. Taking logarithms of the positive quantities relative to consistent references gives ln X = ln N + ln m up to a constant. Differentiating gives μ_X = μ_N + d(ln m)/dt. Here μ_X is the specific rate of biomass increase and μ_N is the specific rate of increase in cell number. The difference between these two rates describes the change in mean biomass per cell.

During steady balanced growth, the average composition and mass of a typical asynchronous population can remain approximately stable. As one subset of cells grows, others divide, and the overall distribution of cell sizes can remain constant. In that case d(ln m)/dt is approximately zero, and μ_X and μ_N agree. This is not a requirement of all microbial populations. During adaptation to new conditions, a change in average cell size or biochemical composition can produce unequal biomass and count rates. During a division defect, mass can increase without comparable multiplication. Conversely, fragmentation of chains can increase counted units without much new mass.

Worked example 11. At the start of a two-hour interval a population has 2 × 10¹¹ cells per litre and biomass 0.08 g per litre. At its end the population has 4 × 10¹¹ cells per litre and biomass 0.24 g per litre. The count ratio is 2, so the interval-average μ_N is ln 2/2 ≈ 0.34657 h⁻¹. The mass ratio is 3, so μ_X = ln 3/2 ≈ 0.54931 h⁻¹. The mean mass per cell rose from 4 × 10⁻¹³ g to 6 × 10⁻¹³ g in the hypothetical measurements, a factor of 1.5. Its average log rate is ln 1.5/2 ≈ 0.20273 h⁻¹. The sum of the count and mean-mass rates is approximately 0.54930 h⁻¹, consistent with the biomass rate. These illustrative values correspond to 0.4 and 0.6 picograms per counted cell; the ratio identity applies independently of the particular absolute masses. The result is evidence that the two endpoints do not have the same average mass per counted cell. It does not establish the molecular reason for the difference.

An additional test of a rate calculation is to ask whether two endpoint measurements were converted to the same original-sample basis. If the first observation is reported as a concentration in an undiluted sample while the second is a count in a diluted aliquot, their raw numerical ratio has no direct interpretation as growth. The earlier chapter on population measurement establishes how a known dilution factor and sampled volume are used to reconstruct the concentration. Those corrections must be made separately for each time point, before computing the logarithm. In contrast, the same fixed sampling volume at both time points introduces a common multiplicative factor and cancels in the ratio.

Additional worked example. Suppose an abstract measurement reports 40 recoverable units in an aliquot corresponding to 0.002 mL of original culture at the first time and 60 units in an aliquot corresponding to 0.0005 mL of original culture at the second time. The original concentrations are 20,000 and 120,000 recoverable units per mL, respectively. The ratio is six rather than 60/40 = 1.5. If the observations are separated by two hours, their logarithmic average rate is ln 6/2 ≈ 0.89588 h⁻¹. Under the steady exponential assumption, the corresponding doubling time is approximately 46.42 minutes. The aliquot volumes and observations are synthetic examples for data interpretation, not a prescribed experimental procedure. A count-based estimate still inherits the biological limits of the recoverable-unit definition.

An investigator may also want to compare a rate before and after a perturbation that changes how well a measurement reflects its target. If an optical signal changes abruptly at the moment of the perturbation but subsequently resumes its original slope, that discontinuity should not be interpreted as a brief episode of infinite biological growth or death. It may reflect dilution, altered optical properties or a change in the detection system. Conversely, a sustained change in slope after a verified comparable measurement is stronger evidence for a changed proportional rate. Establishing the validity of the measurement basis precedes any mechanistic inference. A useful examination strategy is to mark an abrupt discontinuity and estimate slopes within the well-defined intervals on either side, rather than fit a single straight line across the discontinuity.

A further prerequisite is the relationship between population number and concentration. If the total number is T and culture volume is V, then N = T/V. For constant volume, N₂/N₁ equals T₂/T₁, so the same μ is obtained from either basis. If volume changes, μ_N equals μ_T − d(ln V)/dt. Consider a hypothetical transfer in which a population is diluted from 10 mL to 20 mL with no immediate change in total cell number. Its concentration is halved, but no cells need have died. The apparent instantaneous concentration change is caused by a physical dilution, not a change in reproductive physiology. The dilution should be recorded and corrected or treated as a new starting point when interpreting later growth.

This chapter uses constant-volume populations unless a problem explicitly states otherwise. The distinction prepares the student for later study of microbial cultivation engineering, where changes in concentration reflect both biological production and material flow. Here the important lesson is to keep the measurement basis constant or account for the change explicitly.

10. Experimental evidence for exponential and balanced growth

A mathematical law for a biological population earns its usefulness by agreement with observation, not by the elegance of its equation. Early microbial physiologists were able to compare cultures quantitatively because measurements across time revealed reproducible intervals of approximately constant proportional growth. Observing a straight portion on a logarithmic plot established that exponential kinetics could characterize a particular physiological state. It did not imply that all organisms grow at their fastest possible rate at all times.

A classic study by Schaechter, Maaløe and Kjeldgaard in 1958 compared the chemical composition and size of Salmonella typhimurium populations under different growth conditions. The investigators measured cell mass and nucleic acid contents across stable growth conditions, rather than assuming that a single endpoint count represented cell size. At a common temperature, richer growth media supported different rates and characteristic cell sizes and composition. When the temperature was changed within a given medium, the same relationship between rate and composition did not simply carry over. This comparison is a particularly useful control in interpreting the association: an intervention that changes μ is not necessarily equivalent to every other intervention that changes μ. The importance of the work for this chapter is conceptual: researchers treated exponential growth as a reproducible physiological condition and examined how cellular composition and size differed between steady growth states. Their results helped establish that growth rate is closely associated with many features of bacterial physiology. The work should not be interpreted as proving that every species has identical cell size or macromolecular composition at the same specific rate, or that temperature and nutrients influence every component through the same mechanism.

The crucial contrast is between balanced growth within a condition and comparison between conditions. In a balanced exponentially growing population, the amounts of major cellular constituents increase proportionally, such that their fractions of total biomass remain approximately constant. Across two different steady growth conditions, those fractions can differ. A population growing rapidly can allocate different proportions of its biosynthetic machinery from a slower-growing population. Thus equal specific rates for cell number and biomass within one interval do not mean all cells always have the same absolute size or molecular composition in all environments.

This distinction can be examined using a conceptual paired measurement. Suppose total cellular protein and biomass both increase fourfold in a steady interval, while total cellular RNA also increases fourfold. The fractions of protein and RNA relative to biomass remain unchanged between the endpoints. This observation is consistent with balanced growth for the measured components. In a second interval, total RNA increases sixfold while biomass doubles. The RNA fraction rises threefold, showing that the measured components are not increasing in fixed proportions. Whether the cause is altered synthesis, degradation or a change in the population's composition requires further evidence.

In a comparative experiment, a stable reference culture supplies the baseline relationship between growth and the variables being measured. A culture with a genetic or environmental perturbation may show a lower slope, but this observation alone cannot identify the affected molecular process. Slower ribosomal activity, reduced substrate uptake, altered energy metabolism, increased cell loss or a longer transitional phase can each produce slower apparent population increase. A comparison of cell counts, biomass, viable counts and relevant physiological measurements can distinguish among alternatives more effectively than a single OD curve.

In a test of a particular growth model, one useful control is a matched unperturbed culture observed through the same time interval. If both the reference and perturbed population show a similar curvature, a common change in their environment or measurement method may be more plausible than an effect unique to the perturbation. If only the perturbed culture has a persistent slope difference under the same measurement conditions, the evidence for an altered net rate is stronger. The comparison does not by itself specify the biochemical target. A rescue or independent physiological measurement can strengthen that causal inference.

The standard interpretation of exponential growth is thus supported by an interaction between biological observation and mathematical representation. Researchers observe successive changes; a log transformation tests whether the change is proportional; complementary measurements determine whether a rate of count increase also represents biomass increase and whether the composition remains stable. Growth kinetics serves as the quantitative foundation for comparing different physiological states without assuming that the rate alone explains the mechanism behind them.

11. Changing conditions: instantaneous and interval-average growth rates

The constant μ model is useful precisely because it is simple enough to reveal departures from its assumptions. When the environment changes, the relationship dN/dt = μN can still describe the instantaneous net rate if μ is allowed to depend on time. Now write μ(t) = (1/N)(dN/dt), where the parentheses indicate a time-dependent function. At each instant, the rate expresses the current proportional change in the chosen population quantity. The function may be positive, zero or negative, depending on the physiological and environmental circumstances.

The same separation of variables gives dN/N = μ(t)dt. Integrating between times t₁ and t₂ gives ln[N(t₂)/N(t₁)] = ∫ from t₁ to t₂ of μ(t)dt. The integral can be understood as the area under the rate-versus-time curve, with negative contributions subtracting from positive ones. Exponentiating gives the population ratio. This relation is useful even if the exact shape of the time-dependent function is unknown, because the endpoints determine its accumulated logarithmic effect.

For a measured interval of length Δt, define the average specific rate μ̄ = ln(N₂/N₁)/Δt. It equals the time average of the instantaneous net specific rate when the population quantity varies smoothly and remains positive. It is not an arithmetic average of absolute population changes. It is also not generally the arithmetic mean of generation times. Generation time is the reciprocal of generation frequency, and reciprocals do not average linearly.

A simple case is a culture that passes through several intervals with approximately constant but different rates. If the first interval lasts t₁ with rate μ₁ and the second lasts t₂ with rate μ₂, the count after the first interval is N₁ = N₀e^(μ₁t₁). In the second interval, the starting count is N₁, not N₀. Hence N₂ = N₁e^(μ₂t₂) = N₀e^(μ₁t₁ + μ₂t₂). The same argument extends to any finite number of intervals. Logarithmic increments add because population multiplication factors multiply.

The overall average rate is (μ₁t₁ + μ₂t₂)/(t₁ + t₂). In other words, the rate must be weighted by the duration of each regime. If two regimes last equally long, their mean rate is the arithmetic average of their μ values. If they have unequal durations, the longer interval contributes more. This follows directly from the accumulated logarithmic effect, rather than from an arbitrary choice of averaging convention.

Worked example 12. An illustrative population grows with μ = 0.6 h⁻¹ for two hours and then with μ = 0.3 h⁻¹ for four hours. Its total logarithmic increase is 0.6 × 2 + 0.3 × 4 = 2.4. The overall factor is e^2.4 ≈ 11.023. The six-hour average rate is 2.4/6 = 0.4 h⁻¹. Taking the unweighted mean of 0.6 and 0.3 would give 0.45 h⁻¹ and predict e^2.7 ≈ 14.880, which is too high because the slower period lasted longer. The equivalent total generation number is 2.4/ln 2 ≈ 3.462. Dividing six hours by this equivalent generation number gives an effective doubling time of 1.733 h. This value describes the net change across the two intervals; it does not describe the actual doubling time within either interval.

Worked example 13. Consider two equal-duration intervals of one hour each. During the first, g = 20 minutes; during the second, g = 60 minutes. In the first hour there are three equivalent doublings; in the second there is one. The total is four doublings over two hours, giving an overall effective doubling time of 30 minutes. The arithmetic mean of the two stated generation times is 40 minutes and is wrong for this question. Equivalently, the total growth factor is 8 × 2 = 16. The reciprocal relation explains why the effective doubling time is the harmonic mean of the two positive generation times when the intervals have equal duration. More generally, the effective generation frequency is the duration-weighted average of the local frequencies.

A rate-versus-time graph and a population-versus-time graph convey complementary information. On a natural-log population plot the instantaneous slope at each point represents μ(t). On a rate plot the area determines how far the log-population graph moves vertically. A horizontal line at positive μ on the rate plot corresponds to a rising straight line on the natural-log plot. A declining μ that remains positive corresponds to a log-population curve whose slope becomes smaller, while the absolute population continues to increase. A negative μ corresponds to a falling log-population trajectory. This separation of derivative, integral and measured quantity prevents many apparently paradoxical interpretations.

Suppose one observes that the instantaneous specific rate falls from 0.8 to 0.4 h⁻¹. This indicates a reduction in proportional rate but not necessarily a reduction in the number of cells being added per hour. If the population has meanwhile grown to three times its original size, the absolute rate at the second moment is 0.4 × 3N₀ = 1.2N₀ per hour, compared with 0.8N₀ per hour initially. The absolute rate has increased by 50 percent even while the specific rate has halved. The opposite combination is possible when population size falls. Always identify which rate a graph or question is asking about.

12. Lag, physiological shifts and the detection threshold

A culture begins to grow in a new environment only as its existing physiological machinery becomes compatible with the conditions. The interval before sustained multiplication is often called lag. Its duration depends on the previous state of the inoculum and the new environment. Cells may need to change transport capacity, synthesize metabolic enzymes, repair cellular damage or rebuild biosynthetic activity. A near-constant count does not establish that metabolic reactions are absent. Some biomass and composition changes may precede a visible change in cell number.

The simplest mathematical representation treats lag as a period of zero net growth lasting L, followed by constant exponential growth. If t is measured from inoculation, the population for t less than or equal to L is N₀, and for t greater than L it is N₀e^[μ(t − L)]. This is a deliberately simplified piecewise model, useful for calculations when the question explicitly supplies a lag duration and a stable later rate. In a real culture the transition may be gradual, different cells may resume growth at different moments, and the initial population quantity may not remain constant.

The key consequence of this model is that the elapsed time contributing to exponential increase is t − L, not t. Worked example 14. A culture starts with 5 × 10³ viable cells per millilitre, experiences a 90-minute lag with no net count change, and then doubles every 30 minutes. After a total of 4.5 hours, the exponential interval is 3 hours, corresponding to six doublings. The final concentration is 5 × 10³ × 64 = 3.2 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre. Ignoring lag would count nine doublings and produce 2.56 × 10⁶, eight times too high. The error is multiplicative because each mistakenly included generation doubles the prediction.

A log-linear fit to the later exponential phase can be used to estimate an apparent lag in the simple model. Write ln N = ln N₀ + μ(t − L). The fitted line has an intercept a = ln N₀ − μL when time is counted from inoculation. If the original N₀ is reliably known, then L = (ln N₀ − a)/μ. Geometrically, extrapolate the late straight line backwards to the original log count; the corresponding time is the apparent lag. This definition is operational and model-dependent. It does not measure the exact time at which every cell restarted division. A gradual acceleration can yield the same apparent lag as a perfectly flat initial interval of a particular length.

Worked example 15. A hypothetical culture begins with 10⁴ CFU per millilitre. A later natural-log fit, using CFU per millilitre as the reference unit, gives ln N = 7.8240 + 0.69315t with t in hours from inoculation. The initial log count is ln 10⁴ ≈ 9.21034. Thus L = (9.21034 − 7.8240)/0.69315 ≈ 2.00 h. The later slope gives g ≈ 1 h. If two strains have identical later slopes but different fitted intercepts despite equal initial counts, the simplest interpretation is different apparent lag lengths, not different exponential rates. Additional observations of the early interval would test that explanation.

The time at which a culture becomes detectable is a related but distinct quantity. Assume a measurement detects the population reliably only after it reaches a threshold N*. In the idealized lag-plus-exponential model the detection time is L + ln(N*/N₀)/μ, provided the threshold lies above the initial population and is reachable within the exponential regime. A larger inoculum reduces the time required to reach a fixed threshold even if μ and L do not change. A longer lag delays detection even if the later doubling time is unchanged. Therefore one cannot compare growth rates using detection times alone unless starting populations, thresholds and lag behavior are controlled.

Worked example 16. Two cultures have the same μ = ln 2 h⁻¹, a one-hour lag and a detection threshold of 10⁶ cells per millilitre. A begins with 10⁴ and B begins with 2.5 × 10³. A must multiply a hundredfold, requiring log₂ 100 ≈ 6.644 hours of exponential growth. Its detection time is about 7.644 h. B must multiply four hundredfold, requiring log₂ 400 ≈ 8.644 h. Its detection time is about 9.644 h. The difference is exactly two hours because B starts with one quarter as many cells. The observation that A becomes detectable earlier does not imply a faster specific rate.

A second distinction arises during a shift from one steady environment to another. In the classic companion work by Kjeldgaard, Maaløe and Schaechter in 1958, shifts between growth media revealed different temporal responses of cellular processes as bacteria moved between physiological states. Their reported response to a shift to a faster-growth medium showed an early acceleration of RNA synthesis, a subsequent change in optical-density increase and later changes in DNA synthesis and cell division. The order of the observations is more important here than memorizing the study-specific times. If all cellular processes had shifted immediately and proportionally, their normalized rates would have changed together. The temporal separation contradicted that simple model. This provides an important precedent for avoiding the assumption that cell number, DNA, RNA and biomass always adjust immediately at identical rates. A shift in growth conditions may cause a transient unbalanced period before a new steady pattern is established.

A two-stage growth curve with a temporary slowing between stages can arise when microorganisms encounter two usable carbon sources and consume them with a preference or sequential pattern. This is the classical idea of diauxic growth. The later increase may require altered expression of metabolic machinery suited to the remaining substrate. Its detailed regulation belongs to the study of gene expression and metabolism. For kinetic purposes, a graph can contain two approximately straight regions with different slopes and a transition between them. A single straight-line estimate across both regions will not accurately characterize either exponential state.

13. Positive production, death and the meaning of a net rate

Population measurements record changes in the number or amount of a defined quantity. They do not automatically reveal all of the processes producing those changes. A population may be dividing while some cells are dying. In this situation the overall numerical rate is the difference between a production term and a loss term.

For a simple conceptual model, let b be the rate of production of additional viable cells per existing viable cell per unit time and d the rate of loss of viable cells per existing viable cell per unit time. Both are nonnegative and have units of inverse time. For binary division one division adds one net cell to the population before accounting for loss. The instantaneous balance is dN/dt = bN − dN = (b − d)N. Here the letter d in the loss term is a named parameter, distinct from the differential symbol in dN/dt. The observable net specific rate is μ_net = b − d.

If b exceeds d, the viable population increases. If the two are equal, the population can remain numerically constant even while both division and loss occur. If d exceeds b, the viable population decreases. A graph with zero slope does not identify which of these underlying processes is absent. A stationary-phase population could exhibit very low division and loss, or substantial turnover that approximately balances. In addition, actual stationary cultures may have changing viable fractions, maintenance activities and complex transitions. The simple balance is a conceptual interpretation, not a claim that every stationary population has an exact rate equality.

Worked example 17. Two hypothetical cultures both have net μ = 0.2 h⁻¹. In A, b = 0.2 h⁻¹ and d = 0; in B, b = 0.7 h⁻¹ and d = 0.5 h⁻¹. The predicted viable-number trajectories are identical if their initial counts are identical and the rates remain constant. Their physiological behavior is very different: the second culture has substantial simultaneous production and loss. A viable-count time course alone cannot identify b and d separately. Independent observations of division and loss would be required. This is an example of non-identifiability: more than one underlying mechanism produces the same observed result.

A negative specific rate is mathematically meaningful for a declining positive population quantity. When μ_net is negative and constant, a half-life of that quantity can be defined as ln 2/|μ_net|. It is the duration required for the observed number to decline to half of its current value. It is not a positive generation time for reproduction. A loss rate measured from colony-forming units may reflect death, failure to form colonies under the test conditions, or changes in aggregation. Its interpretation requires a clear statement of what was measured.

Worked example 18. A viable count falls from 8 × 10⁶ to 1 × 10⁶ CFU per millilitre over three hours. The ratio is 1/8 = 2⁻³. The average log rate is ln(1/8)/3 = −ln 2 h⁻¹. If the decline was constant and exponential, the recoverable count had a half-life of one hour. The reduction relative to the initial count was 87.5 percent, not 300 percent. The three equivalent halvings describe the cumulative factor, while the percentage reduction is based on the initial amount. A claim that all cells physically lysed would require evidence beyond the CFU measurement.

This interpretation establishes an important boundary of the doubling formula. If the net rate is zero, no finite positive doubling time follows from the measured trajectory. If it is negative, inserting that rate into ln 2/μ produces a negative mathematical time, not a biologically meaningful forward doubling interval. If rates are changing, a culture could later increase, but that prediction needs a model for the later conditions rather than the current rate alone.

14. When a single growth rate hides a mixed population

The treatment of one constant rate assumes that the population behaves as a reproducible whole. This is often a useful approximation when a culture consists of a single strain under stable conditions. It is not always justified when different physiological or genetic groups coexist. Even two groups that independently follow simple exponential growth can produce a combined trajectory that is not a single exponential.

Suppose two distinguishable populations A and B share an environment in which neither appreciably changes the conditions experienced by the other during the chosen interval. They begin at N_A0 and N_B0 and have constant net specific rates μ_A and μ_B. Their individual counts are N_A0e^(μ_At) and N_B0e^(μ_Bt). The total count is the sum of those two expressions. In general a sum of exponentials with unequal exponents cannot be rewritten as one exponential with a constant rate. The logarithm of a sum is not the sum of logarithms.

Differentiating the sum gives the total absolute growth rate μ_AN_A + μ_BN_B. Dividing by the total count gives μ_total = (μ_AN_A + μ_BN_B)/(N_A + N_B). The total specific rate is therefore a weighted mean of the two rates at that moment, with weights given by the current fractions of the two populations. This is a different average from the duration-weighted mean in a piecewise temporal model. Here the two groups grow simultaneously, so the weighting is by their abundances rather than by the time spent in each regime.

If the rates differ, the faster group becomes a larger fraction of the total. The aggregate specific rate shifts toward its rate even though neither group's rate has changed. This provides a conceptual explanation for why a log-population curve may bend upwards in a mixture. A similar change in slope could arise from a homogeneous culture adjusting its physiology. Consequently the curvature alone cannot tell us which biological explanation is correct. Measurements that discriminate groups are needed to identify a changing composition.

Worked example 19. Two hypothetical groups initially each contribute 1,000 cells. Group A doubles every hour, while group B doubles every two hours. After four hours A has 16,000 cells and B has 4,000. The total is 20,000, ten times the initial 2,000, so the interval-average total specific rate is ln 10/4 ≈ 0.57565 h⁻¹. Initially the instantaneous total rate is (ln 2 + ln 2/2)/2 ≈ 0.51986 h⁻¹. After four hours, A constitutes 80 percent of the total and the instantaneous total rate is 0.8 ln 2 + 0.2(ln 2/2) ≈ 0.62383 h⁻¹. The average rate lies between these instantaneous rates. Treating the total as an exact exponential with the initial instantaneous rate would underpredict the final total.

The ratio of the groups has a simpler behavior than the total. Dividing their individual equations gives N_A/N_B = (N_A0/N_B0)e^[(μ_A − μ_B)t]. Thus the natural logarithm of the abundance ratio is linear with slope μ_A − μ_B. The ratio can be inferred if the two groups are measured separately. A total count alone is insufficient because many combinations of component abundances produce the same total. If the two groups have equal rates, their ratio remains unchanged even though both populations grow.

It is important not to apply the independent-growth assumption without checking it. When the groups compete for a limiting nutrient, release inhibitory products or otherwise modify the shared environment, the individual rates may themselves depend on time and on the abundance of both groups. The simple two-rate calculation is useful for learning how growth differences influence relative abundance within a defined interval. It is not a complete model of ecological competition, which requires additional biological assumptions and is addressed in the ecology curriculum.

Population heterogeneity also explains why a population doubling time need not equal the average interdivision time of isolated cells. Lineages that reproduce faster can contribute disproportionately to later population measurements. Even in a genetically uniform culture, different cell ages and physiological histories affect the distribution of interdivision intervals. The time required for the total population to double is determined from that population's trajectory, not simply by averaging a few division times. Direct single-cell observations can complement population measurements, but the two results have different definitions.

For an ideal deterministic binary cycle with equal cell-cycle lengths and asynchronous balanced growth, cells have a stable age distribution that contains more recently born cells than cells close to division. At each division one old cell is replaced by two young cells; as time advances, the number of cells produced per time increases with population size. Therefore younger cohorts contain more individuals than equally narrow cohorts born earlier. This is why a snapshot of an exponentially growing population is not a uniform sampling of all ages across the cycle. It is enough here to understand the qualitative bias: a snapshot fraction is not generally identical to the fraction of a single cell cycle spent in a state. The molecular regulation of the division cycle is the subject of the later binary fission chapter.

15. How measurement error affects kinetic conclusions

The mathematical use of ratios is powerful but does not make data immune to error. If one observation is systematically biased, the ratio changes. Let a true population quantity N at time t be multiplied by a time-dependent measurement factor q(t), yielding observed M(t) = q(t)N(t). Then ln(M₂/M₁) = ln(N₂/N₁) + ln(q₂/q₁). Dividing by the elapsed time gives the observed interval-average rate as the true average rate plus ln(q₂/q₁)/Δt. This identity unifies several earlier examples involving aggregation, incomplete recovery or changing optical response.

If q is constant, the added term is zero. A measurement that consistently underestimates counts by 20 percent may still provide an accurate proportional rate. If q rises with time, the observed rate is higher than the underlying rate, and if q falls, it is lower. The biological reason for a time-dependent q must be established independently. For example, the fraction of cells capable of producing colonies under the measurement conditions might change during stress recovery, making viable-count increase partly reflect restored culturability rather than numerical division.

A simple sensitivity calculation can show how the same endpoint error matters differently for different intervals. Suppose a reported final count is 10 percent higher than its true value, while the initial count is accurate. The logarithmic difference caused by the error is ln 1.1 ≈ 0.09531. If measurements are only 15 minutes apart, the rate is overestimated by about 0.38124 h⁻¹. If they are two hours apart, the overestimate is about 0.04766 h⁻¹. A longer interval reduces the impact of that fixed log-ratio discrepancy, but it can increase the risk that the culture changes its physiological state. This explains why the best interval is neither automatically the shortest nor the longest.

A first-order approximation provides an additional way to reason about small relative errors. If the initial measurement has fractional error ε₁ and the final measurement has fractional error ε₂, their measured ratio differs from the true ratio by (1 + ε₂)/(1 + ε₁). For small errors, ln(1 + ε) is approximately ε. Thus the error in the estimated specific rate is approximately (ε₂ − ε₁)/Δt. Errors of equal relative size and sign tend to cancel; opposite errors reinforce one another. The approximation should not be used for very large errors or measurements near zero because the logarithmic relation becomes increasingly nonlinear.

Counts near the detection limit introduce a further issue. The logarithm of zero is undefined. If an observation reports zero colonies, that does not necessarily prove the source culture contained exactly zero living cells; it establishes that no recoverable colonies were observed in the sampled quantity under the measurement conditions. Replacing zero with an arbitrary tiny number can generate an artificially steep negative slope and a misleading half-life. A valid interpretation distinguishes a nondetection from a quantified positive observation. For basic exam reasoning, one should avoid fitting a log slope through a zero count unless the problem provides a defensible treatment of the measurement limit.

Worked example 20. A hypothetical culture is measured at 1 and 3 h. Reported counts are 1.00 × 10⁵ and 4.00 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre. Assume for a sensitivity analysis that the first count could range from 0.90 to 1.10 × 10⁵ and the second from 3.60 to 4.40 × 10⁵. The central rate estimate is ln 4/2 = 0.69315 h⁻¹. The smallest possible ratio within these stated limits is 3.60/1.10 = 3.27273, giving μ ≈ 0.59281 h⁻¹. The largest ratio is 4.40/0.90 = 4.88889, giving μ ≈ 0.79348 h⁻¹. Under a constant positive-rate assumption, the corresponding doubling times are approximately 70.16 and 52.41 min. This calculation gives a range based on the supplied endpoint bounds, not a statistical confidence interval. The uncertainty in doubling time is asymmetric because doubling time depends on the reciprocal of rate.

Comparative experiments need adequate precision to establish that an apparent rate difference is real. A culture yielding an estimated μ of 0.70 h⁻¹ and another yielding 0.72 h⁻¹ should not automatically be described as physiologically different. Measurement variability, strain variability and the choice of fitting window could account for such a small difference. Reproducibility across independent observations and appropriately interpreted uncertainty support a stronger conclusion. The examination-level point is not to perform advanced statistical testing here but to distinguish a numerical estimate from a justified biological inference.

16. Connecting rate, physiology and growth yield without confusing them

A specific growth rate measures how rapidly a population quantity increases in proportion to itself. Growth yield measures how much biomass is produced from a specified amount of consumed resource. The distinction can be understood from the material demands of reproduction. Cells must acquire building blocks and energy. The speed with which they assemble new biomass and the efficiency with which they convert resources into biomass are not identical properties.

A hypothetical culture with a relatively high μ can consume an available resource rapidly and stop growing at a modest final biomass. Another culture with a smaller μ may convert a larger fraction of the available substrate into biomass and reach a higher final mass over a longer period. There is no mathematical contradiction. The exponential slope describes a local or interval-specific rate, whereas the final mass depends on the resources consumed, conversion efficiency, loss and environmental limitations. The next chapter on growth yield and nutrient limitation develops these relationships quantitatively.

This distinction is useful when interpreting growth curves with different plateaus. If two cultures begin at the same biomass, their initial slopes differ, and their final plateaus are equal, then one may have grown faster without achieving a higher final biomass. If the initial slopes are equal and one plateaus earlier at a smaller biomass, the later difference may concern nutrient availability or other growth limitations rather than the initial physiological rate. If both slopes and plateaus differ, both kinetic and capacity-related factors may contribute. An observed plateau cannot by itself establish which particular nutrient was limiting.

The relationship between growth rate and the metabolic machinery can be approached causally. Cells need proteins to catalyze biochemical reactions, transport systems to acquire substrates and an energy supply to support biosynthesis. A change in the environment can reduce the flux through one or more essential processes, lowering the rate at which the cell produces material for reproduction. Conversely, a favorable environment may permit a greater rate of synthesis, provided other cellular functions are able to keep pace. A successful division also requires duplication and distribution of genetic material and organization of the cell envelope. A perturbation can therefore reduce cell number growth by slowing biosynthesis or by interfering specifically with the physical completion of division.

The bacterial growth curve supplies an integrative phenotype, a measurable consequence of all these processes together. For example, if cell division is impaired but biosynthesis remains active, the number-based rate may be low while the biomass-based rate remains positive for a time. If a metabolic defect reduces both biosynthesis and cell production, both rates may fall. If loss of viability increases while intact cellular material remains, the CFU-based rate may fall while OD changes much more slowly. These patterns suggest hypotheses, but none identifies an individual molecular target without additional experiments.

Growth-rate estimates also help characterize different ecological and physiological conditions. For organisms adapted to nutrient-poor environments, a modest rate of growth may still allow persistence and reproduction under conditions unfavorable to other organisms. A high maximal rate under one laboratory condition does not prove an organism will outperform every other organism in every environment. Temperature, nutrient sources and stress may change the ordering of growth rates. The comparison must state the condition under which the rates were estimated.

The quantity μ_max refers to a maximum specific rate under a defined set of conditions and model assumptions. A measured positive constant rate is not automatically μ_max. A culture may be growing steadily in a medium that supports a lower rate than another medium. Establishing a maximum requires comparison across relevant conditions or an explicitly justified model. This chapter deliberately distinguishes observed rates from maximum rates without introducing a substrate-response equation. The dependence of growth on limiting nutrients and the quantitative modeling of microbial cultivation have dedicated places in the Academy sequence.

17. Integrated quantitative reasoning and limits of prediction

An unfamiliar growth problem becomes easier when it is translated into a sequence of biological and mathematical decisions. First identify the measured quantity, because individual cell number, viable units, biomass and concentration may have different trajectories. Next identify the time interval and whether the conditions justify constant exponential growth. A problem about total time after inoculation may include lag; a problem about an exponential window may not. Then establish the information given: a population ratio, generation time, generation frequency, specific rate or a log-graph slope. Convert the information to a consistent basis before solving for the requested quantity.

An effective way to avoid mistakes is to calculate the dimensionless logarithmic increment A = ln(N₂/N₁). For a constant-rate interval this equals μΔt. It also equals n ln 2. The population factor is e^A. When conditions change, the increments for successive intervals add, while the growth factors multiply. This common quantity links the different formulations. A correct equation should have a dimensionless exponent, a result with the same units as the requested quantity, and a sign consistent with the observed direction of change.

Worked example 21. A hypothetical culture begins at 3 × 10⁴ cells per millilitre. The first hour is an idealized lag with no count change. The next two hours have a generation time of 40 minutes. During the next three hours the measured net specific rate is 0.4 h⁻¹. At the end of the second interval the culture has undergone three equivalent doublings, giving 2.4 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre. In the third interval the multiplication factor is e^1.2 ≈ 3.32012. The final concentration is approximately 7.9683 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre. The total accumulated log change is 3 ln 2 + 1.2 ≈ 3.27944. Dividing by the total six-hour duration gives an overall μ̄ ≈ 0.54657 h⁻¹. Dividing instead by the five hours after lag gives approximately 0.65589 h⁻¹. Both averages are mathematically correct for their stated intervals; they answer different questions. Neither represents a single constant rate throughout the complete record.

Worked example 22. A culture A begins at 1 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre and has a steady base-ten log slope of 0.20 per hour. Culture B begins at 5 × 10⁴ cells per millilitre and has μ_B = 0.70 h⁻¹. Convert A's slope to μ_A = 0.20 ln 10 ≈ 0.46052 h⁻¹. B's rate is higher, so B may catch up. Equality requires e^[(0.70 − 0.46052)t] = 2, yielding t ≈ 2.8944 h. At that time A has approximately 1 × 10⁵ e^(0.46052 × 2.8944) ≈ 3.792 × 10⁵ cells per millilitre. B gives the same value within rounding. The result assumes both cultures remain in their stated exponential regimes until the predicted crossing. If one becomes nutrient-limited earlier, the calculation cannot establish the actual crossing time.

An exponential prediction becomes physically impossible when extrapolated indefinitely in a finite batch culture. Every doubling demands additional cellular material and energy. The medium has finite resources, and changes in the environment can eventually reduce the rate. Suppose a small culture contains 10⁴ cells initially and doubles every half hour. Continuing that assumption for 24 hours gives 48 doublings and a mathematical prediction of about 2.815 × 10¹⁸ cells. This arithmetic follows from the model but does not establish a feasible outcome in an ordinary vessel. An equation can be evaluated outside the range where its biological assumptions are valid; numerical correctness does not guarantee physiological plausibility.

The most useful interpretation of a good growth model is therefore conditional: given the stated quantity, starting population, observed interval and growth conditions, it accurately summarizes proportional change and may predict behavior while those conditions continue. It provides a standard against which deviations can be detected. When a curve departs from the model, the departure is often informative rather than a mathematical inconvenience. It can indicate adaptation, limitation, changes in cell size or viability, population heterogeneity or a measurement artifact. Distinguishing these possibilities requires complementary observations and careful experimental reasoning.

By the end of this chapter the student should be able to derive both growth equations from their assumptions, explain the difference between a generation and a division event, convert between log bases and time units, interpret slopes and intercepts, estimate rates from appropriate windows, handle lag and changing conditions, distinguish net proliferation from production and loss, and explain why growth rate differs from yield. These concepts prepare the ground for quantitative study of resource limitation and growth efficiency, followed by the cellular mechanism of microbial reproduction.

18. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original CSIR-NET-style practice. All numerical data are illustrative. Choose an answer before opening its explanation.

B1. A population doubles every 30 min. Which quantity is 2 h⁻¹ in the notation of this chapter?

  1. Natural-log specific rate μ
  2. Generation frequency k
  3. Absolute rate dN/dt
  4. Generation time g
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Two doublings occur per hour. μ = 2 ln 2 ≈ 1.386 h⁻¹; the absolute rate also depends on N; g is 0.5 h.

B2. Two cultures have equal positive μ and different initial counts. Which is necessarily equal under the model?

  1. Absolute rate of cell production
  2. Final population size
  3. Population doubling time
  4. Time to reach any fixed count
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. g = ln 2/μ. The absolute rate and final count scale with the initial count. The time to a fixed target also depends on the initial count.

B3. What is the slope of a graph of log₁₀ N against time in constant exponential growth?

  1. μ
  2. μ ln 10
  3. μ/ln 10
  4. g/ln 2
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Changing from natural to common logarithms divides by ln 10. A applies to a natural-log axis, B reverses conversion, D has units of time.

B4. After 3 equivalent generations, a population of 100 has become:

  1. 300
  2. 400
  3. 700
  4. 800
Answer and reasoning

Answer: D. 100 × 2³ = 800. The net addition is 700, not the final population. The other values use additive or incorrect exponential reasoning.

B5. A positive μ decreases, but N still increases. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. The population necessarily decreases
  2. The absolute rate necessarily decreases
  3. The proportional rate is smaller
  4. The doubling time becomes smaller
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. A positive μ implies increasing N, and absolute rate μN can rise or fall. A lower steady positive μ corresponds to a longer doubling time.

B6. Which measurement difference cancels exactly in a growth ratio?

  1. A constant additive blank
  2. A fixed multiplicative calibration factor
  3. A changing fraction of culturable cells
  4. A changing mean cell mass when converting biomass to count
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. A common multiplier cancels in a quotient. An additive blank does not; the other two factors can vary with time and therefore change the ratio.

B7. Which observation is consistent with zero net numerical growth but not proof of no division?

  1. A horizontal viable-count curve
  2. A straight positive natural-log slope
  3. A continuously increasing cell count
  4. A measured positive net specific rate
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. A flat viable count shows no net change, potentially because gains and losses balance. B, C and D describe positive net increase.

B8. Assertion: an exponential population can have fractional equivalent generations. Reason: equivalent generations are defined by the log₂ population ratio.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. A log₂ ratio need not be an integer. This is an aggregate measure, not fractional physical daughter cells.

B9. Which assumption is required to treat the slope of log biomass as the slope of log cell number?

  1. Mean biomass per counted cell remains constant
  2. All cells divide simultaneously
  3. All cells are genetically different
  4. The culture is in decline phase
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. X = Nm; equal specific rates follow when mean mass m is constant. Synchrony and genetic difference are unnecessary; decline is not a requirement.

B10. A log count decreases steadily. What does the magnitude of its negative natural-log slope describe?

  1. Number of completed divisions
  2. Specific rate of decline of the measured quantity
  3. Time for one division
  4. Biomass yield
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The slope is a net logarithmic rate. It does not determine actual division events, nor does it directly express time or yield.

B11. Assertion: a larger inoculum may reach a fixed threshold earlier even with unchanged μ. Reason: it needs a smaller fold increase to reach that threshold.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. For a fixed threshold above both starting counts, t = ln(N*/N₀)/μ, assuming equal lag. A larger N₀ reduces the required logarithmic increase.

B12. Which observation supports balanced growth for the measured components?

  1. Cell count doubles but biomass stays fixed
  2. RNA and protein fractions of biomass change rapidly
  3. Cell count, biomass and measured components all increase proportionally
  4. CFU decreases while OD stays fixed
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Proportional increases preserve composition. A and B imply changes in average mass or composition. D does not establish balanced growth.

19. Part C — Quantitative and experimental reasoning

Original CSIR-NET-style practice. All numerical data are illustrative. Choose an answer before opening its explanation.

C1. A culture rises from 3 × 10⁴ to 9.6 × 10⁵ cells/mL in 150 min of exponential growth. Find g.

  1. 15 min
  2. 30 min
  3. 50 min
  4. 75 min
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Ratio = 32 = 2⁵. Five equivalent generations in 150 min give 30 min. The other times would imply 10, 3 or 2 generations, inconsistent with 32.

C2. A natural-log plot increases by 1.8 over 3 h. Find the expected four-hour multiplication factor at the same rate.

  1. 2.4
  2. e^0.6
  3. e^2.4
  4. 2^2.4
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. μ = 1.8/3 = 0.6 h⁻¹. Over four hours the logarithmic increment is 2.4, so the factor is e^2.4 ≈ 11.02. A is the increment, B uses one hour, D uses the wrong base.

C3. A base-ten log slope is 0.15 per hour. What is g?

  1. 0.15 h
  2. 0.346 h
  3. 2.01 h
  4. 6.67 h
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. g = log₁₀2/0.15 = 2.0069 h. 0.15 is the slope; 0.346 is approximately μ in h⁻¹; 6.67 is the time for a tenfold change.

C4. Two populations initially have equal counts. A has g = 30 min and B has g = 60 min. What is N_A/N_B after 3 h?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 8
  4. 64
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. A undergoes six doublings, B three. Ratio = 2^(6−3) = 8. The value 64 is A's growth factor alone; 2 and 3 confuse rate or generation differences with a ratio.

C5. A culture has a two-hour lag, then g = 45 min. After five total hours, the factor relative to its initial count is:

  1. 4
  2. 8
  3. 16
  4. 2^(20/3)
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The exponential interval is three hours, or four generations. The factor is 16. A and B undercount generations; D wrongly includes lag.

C6. Two hours at μ = 0.4 h⁻¹ followed by one hour at μ = 0.7 h⁻¹ give what overall average rate?

  1. 0.5 h⁻¹
  2. 0.55 h⁻¹
  3. 1.1 h⁻¹
  4. 1.5 h⁻¹
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Accumulated log change = 0.8 + 0.7 = 1.5; dividing by three hours gives 0.5 h⁻¹. B is the unweighted mean, C adds rates, D is the dimensionless increment.

C7. A sample's true count doubles but its average CFU cluster size doubles too, with constant recovery. What happens to CFU?

  1. Halves
  2. Unchanged
  3. Doubles
  4. Quadruples
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. CFU is proportional to cells divided by cluster size. Both numerator and denominator double. The remaining options neglect one factor or multiply instead of dividing.

C8. OD readings increase from 0.12 to 0.22 in 1 h, with a blank of 0.02 and valid linearity. What is μ estimated from corrected OD?

  1. ln(11/6)
  2. ln 2
  3. 0.10
  4. 2
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Corrected readings are 0.10 and 0.20; the ratio is two. A uses raw readings; C is the absolute signal increment; D is the ratio, not its logarithm.

C9. A count stays at 10⁶ cells while dry biomass doubles over two hours. Which conclusion follows?

  1. Mean mass per cell halves
  2. μ_X equals μ_N
  3. Mean mass per cell doubles
  4. Exactly half the cells die
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. X/N doubles. The count-based rate is zero while biomass has positive average log rate. No count change alone specifies death or division.

C10. A population's total count is unchanged during a transfer from 5 mL to 20 mL. What is the concentration ratio after/before?

  1. 4
  2. 1
  3. 1/2
  4. 1/4
Answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Concentration is count divided by volume. Fourfold volume increase gives one-quarter concentration without loss. A reverses the ratio, B confuses count and concentration, C is the wrong factor.

C11. A viable count falls sixteenfold in four hours. If the decrease is exponential, what is its half-life?

  1. 15 min
  2. 1 h
  3. 2 h
  4. 4 h
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Sixteenfold decrease = four halvings, giving one hour each. The other times do not reproduce four halvings across four hours.

C12. A has net rate 0.3 h⁻¹, B has b = 0.8 and d = 0.5 h⁻¹. At identical starting viable counts, which is correct?

  1. B must have a steeper net growth curve
  2. B has zero net growth
  3. Both can have the same net trajectory
  4. A must have no cell death
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. B's net rate is 0.3 h⁻¹. Equal net rates give equal trajectories under constant conditions. A's underlying production and loss cannot be determined from its net rate.

C13. An equal mixture of two groups begins with 100 cells each. A doubles every hour and B every two hours. What fraction is A after two hours?

  1. 1/2
  2. 2/3
  3. 3/4
  4. 4/5
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. A has 400 cells, B 200, so A is 400/600 = 2/3. The initial fraction was one-half; C and D overestimate the enrichment.

C14. A and B have the same μ, but A starts at twice B's abundance. Which log₂ plot relationship follows?

  1. A slope is twice B slope
  2. A and B cross after one generation
  3. A is one log₂ unit above B throughout
  4. A and B have equal intercepts
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The ratio remains two, so log₂A − log₂B = 1. Slopes are equal; intercepts differ; no crossing occurs under the stated model.

C15. A rate is estimated using two measurements 30 min apart. The final reading is biased 20% high while the initial reading is accurate. What is the rate bias?

  1. 0.2 h⁻¹
  2. ln 1.2/0.5 ≈ 0.365 h⁻¹
  3. ln 0.8/0.5
  4. No bias because ratios cancel
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. A changing multiplicative factor adds ln(1.2)/0.5. A ignores the logarithmic/time relationship; C gives the wrong direction; D applies only to a constant multiplier.

C16. A mutant and control show the same exponential slope, but the mutant reaches a lower plateau. Which inference is best?

  1. The mutant necessarily has a lower exponential specific rate
  2. The mutant necessarily has a lower growth yield
  3. The measured exponential rates agree; later growth limitation may differ
  4. The mutant cannot divide
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Equal slopes mean equal measured specific rates over the fitted intervals. A plateau depends on resources, yield, inhibition and loss; it cannot establish yield alone. Growth itself rules out D under a valid cell-count measure.
