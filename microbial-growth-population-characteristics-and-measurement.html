1. What grows when a microorganism grows?

A flask containing a few suspended bacterial cells may look exactly like sterile water. After a period of suitable growth, the liquid may become visibly cloudy. The change raises a deceptively simple biological question. Has each original cell become very large, have new cells appeared, or has the material surrounding the cells changed? These alternatives have different implications for cellular physiology. Cloudiness by itself cannot distinguish them. The subject of microbial growth therefore begins with a distinction between the biological processes taking place in a cell and the population-level observations by which those processes are investigated.

Growth at the level of a single cell is a net increase in its cellular material. A cell builds proteins, nucleic acids, membrane lipids, cell-wall constituents and other molecules from imported or internally generated precursors. These reactions require chemical energy, appropriate raw materials and a functioning catalytic system. Merely moving water into the cell can make it swell without producing additional cellular dry material. Similarly, a cell that replaces damaged proteins at the same rate at which they are degraded is maintaining its structure, not necessarily increasing its mass. When synthesis exceeds removal, dry biomass accumulates. This distinction is important because a larger cell need not have reproduced and a metabolically active cell need not have accumulated material.

Consider an ideal bacterium before binary fission. Its cell envelope must enlarge sufficiently to enclose additional cytoplasm. DNA must be copied so that new cellular compartments can inherit genetic information. Ribosomes and enzymes must be available to support the descendants after separation. A division site is formed, the envelope constricts, and two distinct daughter cells can emerge. The number of cells changes at the completion of a division, whereas biosynthesis may have been proceeding for much of the preceding interval. The mechanics of bacterial septation are treated separately in the chapter on binary fission; for the present purpose, the important fact is that growth in mass and the creation of new individual cells are separable events.

A population is a collection of cells defined by some common property, such as membership of the same microbial species in a culture. A mixed community includes multiple populations; unless a method identifies particular organisms, a community-level count does not determine the abundance of any one species. Population growth in its numerical sense means an increase in the number of cells belonging to that population. In a closed hypothetical population with no cell loss and no recruitment, a single completed binary division replaces one cell by two and therefore adds one to the total. Ten such division events add ten cells regardless of whether they occurred simultaneously. This simple bookkeeping does not assume that every cell has the same length, mass or division schedule. Indeed, natural microbial populations usually contain cells in different physiological states.

When thousands of cells are examined together, the pattern of the population can be much smoother than the change in any individual cell. The cell number may increase gradually at the scale of the measurement even though each division is an identifiable event. In contrast, a population of cells that divide in perfect synchrony would show abrupt changes in cell number. The difference between continuous and discrete appearance is a consequence of how the observation is made, not a fundamentally different process of reproduction. Subsequent growth-kinetics calculations approximate population size by a continuous variable when cell numbers are sufficiently large and conditions are appropriate.

The term growth is also applied to organisms for which an individual-cell count is not the most natural measure. A filamentous fungus may develop branching hyphae with compartments that are difficult to separate into a single consistent numerical unit. A bacterial chain may consist of multiple distinguishable cells attached end to end. Fragmentation can convert one long filament into many independent particles without synthesizing much new material. For a fungal culture, the total dry mass or the expansion of a colony may be more informative about some aspects of growth than a tally of fragments. The definition must therefore be linked to the organism and the question.

A microbiologist who studies population growth must decide whether the desired outcome is more living reproductive units, more physically identifiable cells or more biological material. These objectives are related because growing cells often divide, but none is guaranteed to remain proportional to another. Throughout this chapter, a reading on an instrument is treated as evidence about a defined property of the culture, not as an automatic synonym for life or reproduction. That habit is the foundation for interpreting the growth curves and kinetic measurements that follow.

2. Separating cell number, average cell mass and total biomass

A simple mass balance makes the preceding distinction quantitative. Let N be the number of recognizable individual cells in a sample, and let the dry mass of cell i be m_i. The total cellular dry mass M is the sum of the individual masses. If N is positive, the mean dry mass per cell is m̄ = M/N. Multiplying both sides by N gives M = N m̄. This is an accounting identity, not an empirical growth law. It holds for a heterogeneous population as long as M and N refer to the same set of cells and the mean is calculated using those cells. If an assay includes extracellular material in M while N includes only cells, the identity no longer describes the measured quantities without qualification.

In a liquid culture we often compare concentrations rather than entire vessels. Suppose the culture volume is V in millilitres. The cell concentration C = N/V has units cells per millilitre. If the mass is expressed in grams, the biomass concentration X = M/V may be reported in grams per millilitre, or more conveniently in grams per litre after conversion. When the same volume basis is used, X = C m̄. The product has the correct dimensions: cells per unit volume multiplied by mass per cell leaves mass per unit volume. It is not necessary to know the detailed chemical composition of the cells to establish this relationship, although such composition matters when interpreting the mass itself.

Worked example 1. Imagine a teaching example in which each of one million cells has a dry mass of 0.4 picograms. One picogram is 10⁻¹² grams. The combined dry mass is 10⁶ × 0.4 × 10⁻¹² g = 4 × 10⁻⁷ g, or 0.4 micrograms. If those cells synthesize an equal quantity of additional material without dividing, the total biomass becomes 0.8 micrograms but the population still contains one million individual cells. If all then divide symmetrically with negligible material loss during separation, there will be two million cells with an average dry mass of 0.4 picograms and the biomass will remain 0.8 micrograms immediately after division. The large increase in mass occurred before the large increase in number. Counting alone misses the first event; weighing alone misses the second.

Worked example 2. The same argument can be applied to two cultures whose masses are measured at a common time. Suppose each contains 2 mg of cellular dry material. Culture A has an average cellular mass of 0.5 pg; culture B averages 1 pg. Under the stated assumptions the first contains 2 × 10⁻³ g / (0.5 × 10⁻¹² g per cell) = 4 × 10⁹ cells, whereas the second contains 2 × 10⁹ cells. Their biomass values are identical although the cell counts differ twofold. A comparison of growth based on dry mass may therefore rank two cultures differently from a comparison based on reproductive units.

The mass of a microbial cell varies with species, growth conditions, cell-cycle stage and its intracellular reserves. In many bacteria, nutrient-rich conditions favour relatively large cells, but the relationship is organism dependent and cannot be used as a universal conversion rule. A nutrient limitation may reduce the rate of synthesis without preventing every completed division. A division perturbation can produce elongated cells that accumulate mass without proportionately increasing the number of separate compartments. During a short period of such uncoupling, the average mass per countable unit rises. A student should resist the temptation to interpret a rising optical signal immediately as a faster division rate.

Worked example 3. Population density and total population size must also be distinguished. If a suspension contains 5 × 10⁶ cells/mL in 20 mL, there are 10⁸ cells in the vessel. Addition of 20 mL of cell-free medium with no immediate division or cell loss reduces the concentration to 2.5 × 10⁶ cells/mL while leaving the total number unchanged. Evaporation can have the opposite effect by concentrating the existing cells. A rise in the measured concentration is evidence for net accumulation only when the relevant volume is stable or its changes are accounted for. This consideration becomes important when comparing cultures over time or comparing samples that were taken in different volumes.

An analogous distinction applies when a fraction of a population is lost from the measured suspension by settling or attachment to the wall of a vessel. A count of the liquid can fall while the total count of the entire vessel remains unchanged. In principle one could measure both attached and suspended material, but a routine sample usually represents only a specified compartment. Defining which compartment is sampled is part of defining the biological question.

It is useful to compare wet mass and dry mass even before examining weighing methods. Much of a living cell is water. A change in osmotic conditions may alter its water content, while storage compounds or new macromolecules change its non-water material. Wet mass includes both, so an increase may not demonstrate biosynthesis. Dry mass removes much of this ambiguity but still includes nonliving cell remnants and retained noncellular solids. Both mass measurements are operational and require an appropriate definition of the material being weighed. These distinctions will later explain why a culture can appear to grow in one assay while another assay appears unchanged.

3. Living, dead, intact, active and culturable are distinct properties

The most familiar laboratory demonstration of microbial multiplication is the appearance of colonies on a solid nutrient medium. A colony is a visible accumulation of descendants that arose from one founding reproductive unit or a small group of associated units. It is evidence that at least one cell in that unit was able to carry out sustained multiplication under the particular conditions of the test. A cell that fails to form a colony, however, does not provide evidence of sustained multiplication to the detectable endpoint under those conditions. This negative observation is not logically equivalent to demonstrating that every essential cellular function has ceased irreversibly.

In an unstressed, readily culturable population, most intact cells may produce colonies. It is then convenient to use colony counts as an estimate of viable population density. The term viable is often used in textbooks to mean capable of reproduction. Other contexts use viability in a broader physiological sense, including the retention of certain functions and the potential to resume growth after appropriate recovery. The intended meaning should be stated. An assay that measures reproductive success directly is different from an assay that reports membrane integrity or metabolic activity, and one should not silently transfer the definition of viability from one method to another.

A healthy cell normally maintains a selectively permeable membrane that separates its contents from the surrounding environment. It must regulate solute exchange and preserve electrochemical gradients. Severe membrane injury can impair these functions; a loss of integrity is therefore useful evidence of damage. Yet the exclusion of a membrane-impermeant dye shows that a barrier remains sufficiently intact for that dye under the measurement conditions. It does not prove that the DNA can be copied, the cell can divide or the necessary enzymes can operate. Conversely, limited membrane injury may be reversible. A binary label such as intact or permeable is not a complete description of all cellular functions.

Metabolic activity refers to chemical transformations associated with a biological system. A population can consume a substrate, release a product, or retain measurable enzyme activity without increasing its cell number. Maintenance reactions may use energy to replace damaged molecules, maintain gradients and repair structures. Some cells have low detectable activity yet remain capable of resuming multiplication. Conversely, residual enzyme activity in a damaged cell does not necessarily indicate sustained viability. The relevant test must be interpreted in the context of the function actually measured and the time at which the measurement was performed.

A dead cell need not disappear instantaneously. Its envelope and much of its cytoplasmic material can remain intact for a period after irreversible loss of reproductive capacity. Such a particle can scatter light, be recognized under a microscope and contribute to dry mass. Lysis means rupture or breakdown of the cell structure with release of material. Death and lysis are separate events; lysis may occur after death, but many nonviable cells remain recognizable. This is why a total microscopic count can stay high while the colony-forming count falls. It also explains why a decrease in optical density is not an obligatory sign of killing.

The category viable but nonculturable, abbreviated VBNC, is used for certain cells that fail to grow on the conventional medium or under the usual conditions for their organism while retaining evidence consistent with viability. Environmental stresses can be associated with the appearance of this state. The important teaching point is that culturability is defined relative to a specified recovery test; failure under one condition does not establish that recovery is impossible under every condition. The VBNC concept has been discussed extensively in microbiology because the operational definitions of viability differ across assays. Oliver's review (2005) describes evidence for such states and the difficulty of interpreting a simple culture-negative result.

A strong claim of recovery of previously nonculturable cells requires more than observing new colonies after a change of environment. A small undetected population of cells that remained culturable could multiply and account for the later colonies. To distinguish that explanation from resuscitation of formerly nonculturable cells, investigators need evidence that addresses the identity or prior state of the cells and the possibility of outgrowth of survivors. At examination level, recognize the logic of the alternative explanation rather than assuming that a particular dye or a new plate can settle the entire question.

Dormancy, injury and VBNC should not be treated as interchangeable words. A dormant cell may show very low metabolism and may be able to germinate or resume growth when conditions become appropriate. An injured cell may recover if its cellular machinery remains repairable. A VBNC cell is defined in relation to conventional cultivation and evidence of viability, not by any single metabolic rate or mechanism of injury. An organism that has never been grown on a chosen medium is not automatically a formerly culturable cell that entered a VBNC state. Detailed stress mechanisms belong to the later stress-response nodes; here the distinctions are essential because each state can give a different combination of counting results.

Worked example 4. Suppose a sample contains one hundred distinguishable intact bacterial cells. Eighty form separate colonies under a stated assay, ten are intact but fail to form colonies for reasons not established, and ten are irreversibly nonviable. A microscope might detect all one hundred. A plate may yield eighty. Without additional evidence, the ten cells of uncertain status cannot be classified conclusively as dead or VBNC. Even the observed eighty colonies correspond to eighty founding units only if the founders were independent and each produced exactly one visible colony. The apparent 80 percent reproductive recovery is meaningful under that assumption; it is not an absolute measurement of all forms of viability.

This is the central conceptual contrast for the next sections. The total microscopic count, the colony count, the optical density and the dry mass are not competing answers to exactly the same question. They observe different aspects of the population. Agreement between them can be valuable when a homogeneous population is actively multiplying, but disagreement can be even more informative when the physiology changes.

4. From a whole culture to a representative measurement

Before comparing counting methods, it is necessary to understand what is actually sampled. A millilitre is one thousandth of a litre, and a microlitre is one thousandth of a millilitre. Many microbial measurements begin with a small fraction of a vessel because counting every cell in a large population is impractical. A sample is useful only if the intended inference from that fraction to the rest of the culture is defensible. A perfectly calibrated instrument cannot compensate for a sample taken from the wrong region or a population that was distributed unevenly.

In a dispersed and well-mixed suspension, a small randomly selected volume can be regarded as having approximately the same expected concentration as the entire liquid. This is a statement about expectation, not a guarantee of identical counts in every aliquot. Cells are discrete objects and a finite sample can contain more or fewer than the expected number. The relative fluctuations are particularly important when the expected number is small. Larger effective sample volumes or independent replicate measurements can improve the precision of the estimate, provided the method remains appropriate for the density of the sample.

If cells occur in aggregates, chains or floating and settled groups, different aliquots may contain different numbers of individuals and different numbers of reproductive units. Aggregation is itself a biological property in some microbes, and the physical association can persist during measurement. Treating an aggregate as a single countable event can be appropriate when the aim is to count independent units, but it is inappropriate when an answer is being interpreted as the number of individual cells. In an exam problem, first determine whether the question has given the number of cells per aggregate or has merely stated that the cells occur in groups. Without additional information one cannot convert group counts into exact cell counts.

There is also a distinction between independent and technical replication. Repeated measurements of the same sampled suspension can reveal fluctuations associated with counting, delivery of volumes or the instrument. Samples from independently grown cultures capture additional biological variation. Three readings from one culture do not demonstrate that three independently grown populations have the same growth characteristics. A comparison of growth across conditions should match the biological unit of replication to the question asked. This principle is central to reasoning about experimental controls and error bars, even though detailed statistical design belongs to the methods unit.

A blank is a sample designed to measure the background signal of an assay rather than the cellular contribution. For an optical measurement, the cell-free medium may contribute colour, scattering or absorption; an appropriate blank helps account for that contribution. A negative control in a culture-based observation can help detect unintended growth or contamination. A positive control with a known detectable population helps establish that the measurement system can register the property of interest. These controls serve distinct logical roles. An absence of growth in a test sample is ambiguous if a positive recovery control also fails. Conversely, growth in a negative control undermines attribution of a count to the intended sample.

A dilution changes the concentration of the sample rather than the properties of each cell by definition only in an ideal calculation. In actual biological measurement, the conditions of dilution may affect integrity, aggregation or the ability to recover. The algebra developed in the next sections assumes that viable founding units are conserved during sampling and dilution, that the chosen volume is known, and that the preparation does not create or remove countable units. When those assumptions fail, correcting the measured count by the dilution factor will not restore the lost biological information.

There are two broad classes of measurement error. Random variation causes repeated estimates to scatter around an expected value. Systematic error shifts an estimate consistently away from the intended value. For example, random differences in the number of cells within equal sampled volumes can produce different colony counts even if the true concentration is unchanged. A counting chamber with an incorrectly assumed depth gives systematically wrong concentrations, even if a large number of replicate counts are in perfect agreement. Measuring precision is therefore not the same as establishing accuracy.

The most useful starting point for a numerical question is to identify three things: the measured object, the actual sampled volume and the relationship between that sample and the original material. A measured object might be a cell, a colony-producing unit, an optical signal or a weighed residue. A volume can be the microscopic volume of a grid, the portion of a diluted sample represented by a plate, or the total liquid from which solids were collected. Once these are clear, units and conservation relationships often lead directly to the appropriate calculation. The following section applies this approach to a direct observation of cells.

5. Direct microscopic counts: converting a visible grid into a concentration

The development of microscopy made it possible to observe microbial cells individually rather than infer their presence solely from the visible appearance of a culture. Counting cells within a known volume extends that observation into a quantitative measurement. The essential requirement is surprisingly modest: a reliably defined region of space in which recognizable objects can be counted. A microscope provides a magnified image, but magnification alone does not establish how much original liquid is represented by that image. A calibrated counting chamber supplies the missing geometric information.

A conventional counting chamber consists of a ruled glass surface and a cover glass separated by a defined depth. The ruled surface divides the viewing area into squares whose areas are known. The region above a square is a shallow rectangular prism of liquid. If the square has area A in square millimetres and the chamber has depth h in millimetres, the volume associated with the square is A h cubic millimetres. Counting cells within multiple squares gives a known total volume. A Petroff–Hausser bacterial counting chamber, for example, commonly has a depth of 0.02 mm; this is a particular chamber geometry and not a constant for all counting chambers. A different type of chamber can have a different depth or grid size, so the dimensions supplied in a question take precedence over any memorized conversion factor.

To derive the cell concentration, imagine that n cells are observed in a total of k equal squares. The effective volume examined is k A h mm³. One cubic centimetre is one millilitre, and one cubic centimetre contains one thousand cubic millimetres because each linear centimetre contains ten millimetres. Hence 1 mm³ = 10⁻³ mL. The observed volume is k A h × 10⁻³ mL. Dividing the observed count by this volume gives the estimated concentration in the chamber suspension, n/(k A h × 10⁻³) cells/mL. If the suspension examined was diluted to fraction d of the original concentration, the original concentration is n/(k A h × 10⁻³ d) cells/mL. The denominator is the original-suspension volume effectively represented by the observed cells.

The distinction between total count and average count per square is vital. If n is the total across k squares, use the total volume of those k squares. If n̄ = n/k is the average count per square, use the volume of one square. Mixing the total count with the volume of just one square overestimates the concentration by a factor of k. Mixing the average count with the total volume underestimates it by the same factor. Both correct expressions lead to the same answer because the number and volume refer to the same region of liquid.

Worked example 5. Consider a hypothetical chamber with A = 0.04 mm² and h = 0.02 mm. One square represents 0.0008 mm³ or 8 × 10⁻⁷ mL. Five squares contain a total of 100 recognizable cells. The represented volume is 4 × 10⁻⁶ mL. The estimated concentration in the chamber is 100/(4 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.5 × 10⁷ cells/mL. If the counted suspension is one fifth of the original concentration, d = 0.2 and the original concentration is 1.25 × 10⁸ cells/mL. Checking the result in reverse, the original equivalent of the microscopic sample is 8 × 10⁻⁷ mL; multiplying that by 1.25 × 10⁸ cells/mL returns the observed 100 cells. No assumptions about the growth rate or ability to divide were necessary.

How should a cell touching a boundary between two adjacent squares be treated? If all boundary cells are counted in both squares, some cells enter the tally twice. If all boundary cells are excluded from every square, some are never counted. A consistent convention includes cells touching two adjacent designated borders and excludes cells touching the opposite two borders. The particular choice of sides is less important than consistent application. The purpose is to assign a boundary object to only one neighbouring region. A student reconstructing a counting-grid diagram should show the included and excluded edges distinctly, rather than assuming every object that overlaps a line is counted twice.

A direct microscopic estimate is often called a total cell count because the routine observation of recognizable cellular profiles does not independently test their reproductive ability. Living and dead cells can have similar appearances. In a clean, dispersed suspension of readily distinguishable organisms, the method can give a reasonable estimate of total recognizable cells per unit volume. In an environmental sample, debris may resemble cells, very small cells may be difficult to resolve, and cells in clumps may overlap. Highly motile cells can make it difficult to associate a particular object with a particular grid region. Uneven settling can make the average concentration within the chamber unrepresentative. These errors arise from biological properties and optical resolution as well as from the physical dimensions of the device.

The expression direct count does not mean the result is automatically an exact count of all microbes in the original vessel. It means the measured events are observations of individual objects in the sample rather than a signal converted indirectly into abundance. The inference from the sample to the original culture still relies on the sample volume, dilution and representativeness. Nor does a larger number of squares remove a bias caused by incorrectly distinguishing debris from cells. A large precise tally of the wrong object is still the wrong measurement.

The useful density range of a chamber count is restricted at both ends. When a sample contains very few cells, a small observed volume can contain no cells even when the culture is not sterile. If many cells overlap in a field, the difficulty is no longer the rarity of objects but the inability to separate them reliably. Dilution can make a dense population more countable, though it adds a scaling operation and may alter fragile cells. For sparse populations a larger volume or a different method may be needed. In both cases the problem is the relationship between the expected event count and the amount of liquid actually represented. This same relationship reappears in colony counts.

Other direct counting approaches use electronically detected events. For instance, an instrument may register changes in electrical or optical properties as particles pass through a sensing region. These methods can register many events rapidly, and some distinguish different classes of particles by size or signal. But an event must still be interpreted: a clump may be one event rather than several cells, a noncellular particle may generate a signal, and a dead intact cell may remain detectable. A fluorescence-based approach can combine cell identification with a probe that reports a particular physiological feature, but detection of that feature does not automatically establish reproductive success. The underlying lesson remains the same as for a ruled chamber. Define the object, define the volume, and distinguish what is detected from the biological property inferred.

6. Colony-forming units: why a visible colony is an operational reproductive unit

A single bacterial cell is normally too small to be observed with the unaided eye. Its descendants, when retained together on or within a solid supporting medium, can eventually form a visible colony. This turns a microscopic reproductive event into a macroscopic observation. Solid media and colony isolation were important developments in classical bacteriology because they allowed distinct growing units to be distinguished in a population instead of observing only a collective cloudiness. The key measurement principle is the spatial separation of founding units so that the resulting populations can be recognized as separate colonies.

If a dispersed suspension contains individual cells capable of reproduction, and each individual is physically separated from its neighbours, each may give rise to one visible colony under appropriate conditions. Under this ideal model, the number of colonies equals the number of founding culturable cells represented in the sample. The colonies observed at the end are not themselves single cells; each colony may contain a very large number of descendants. Counting the colonies estimates the number of successful starting units, not the number of descendants produced by the time the observation was made. A large colony does not automatically represent more starting cells than a small colony. It may simply reflect a different lag time, growth behaviour, morphology or local nutrient access.

A colony-forming unit, abbreviated CFU, is the operational unit inferred from a separate visible colony. It is possible for a group of associated cells to generate one colony. A pair of daughter cells may not separate after division; cocci can remain in chains or clusters; extracellular material may hold cells together; several independent founders may happen to be close enough that their expanding colonies merge. Under these circumstances the count is no longer an exact count of individual reproductive cells. The notation CFU deliberately avoids that stronger claim.

Worked example 6. Consider an idealized chain of four cells that remains intact during sampling and yields one colony. If all four were individually capable of multiplication, the chain still contributes only one CFU. Ten such chains contribute ten CFU but contain forty potentially culturable individual cells. If the chains break into separate cells without growth or killing, the number of colony-forming units can increase to forty although the total cell number and dry mass remain the same. This illustrates how a physical change in the distribution of cells can imitate population growth when only CFU are examined. The converse is also possible: an increasing tendency to aggregate can lower the number of colony-forming units without a corresponding loss of individual viability.

A group may contain both viable and nonviable cells. Imagine a chain of six recognizable cells, one of which can produce descendants and the others cannot. If the viable member starts a colony, the chain produces one CFU. A second chain of six cells, all capable of reproduction, can also produce one CFU. These two cases give identical plate counts and identical chain lengths, but their fractions of reproductive individual cells differ. Thus multiplying CFU by an estimated mean chain length is a valid estimate of reproductive cell concentration only under a strong assumption, such as all contributing chains being composed entirely of reproductively competent cells and each counted chain yielding one colony. Without such information the correction is not determined.

Culture conditions determine whether the founding cells can produce the visible endpoint. A medium must supply suitable resources and allow the organism to carry out the processes required for division. Oxygen availability, temperature, the physical state of the medium and the observation interval can influence the outcome. A population containing microbes with diverse requirements is unlikely to have all members represented equally under one set of conditions. A medium used to favour one organism may restrict others. Hence a CFU concentration should be described in relation to the specified culture conditions, particularly when interpreting environmental or stressed samples. It is not a universal enumeration of everything alive in the sample.

Two standard spatial separation principles are the spread plate and the pour plate. In the former, the founding units are distributed over a solid surface; in the latter, units are distributed within solidifying medium as well as potentially near its surface. Their common logic is that separated founders yield separate colonies. Differences in exposure to oxygen, location in the medium and other features can influence the recovery of particular organisms. A comparison between these methods must therefore distinguish the mathematical correction for represented volume from the biological recovery associated with the conditions. No fixed superiority applies to all organisms.

Membrane filtration is another conceptual variant of viable enumeration. A defined volume of liquid is represented by material retained on a membrane, and retained culturable units may be detected as colonies under suitable recovery conditions. Its value is clearest when the concentration of reproductive units in the original liquid is low: representing a larger volume increases the expected number of detected units without requiring the same volume to fit into an ordinary small plate. The interpretation depends on retention, recovery and the ability to distinguish resulting colonies. It is not valid to assume that every organism passing through an assay will be retained and subsequently detected. Detailed instrument and cultivation procedures are not needed to understand this general relationship.

A colony-based count integrates several processes. First, a founding unit must be represented in the sample. It must survive any changes imposed by sample handling. It must be able to initiate growth under the recovery conditions. It must produce enough descendants to become detectable, and the resulting colony must remain distinguishable from neighbouring colonies. Failure at any one of these stages can lower the observed count. The inference of reproductive ability is therefore positive and conditional: the unit gave rise to a visible colony under the circumstances of the test. This interpretation is more precise than saying the plate measured all living cells.

The value of CFU measurements is that they report a biologically significant functional endpoint. For many readily culturable populations, comparisons made under matched conditions give valuable information about the capacity for continued multiplication. If a perturbation leaves the number of recognizable cellular profiles unchanged but reduces the number of CFU, the plate count supplies evidence that reproductive recovery has changed. It does not by itself distinguish irreversible killing from reversible inhibition, injury, association or altered recovery requirements. The quantitative method developed next is designed to estimate the density of these founding units while preserving that important limitation.

7. Dilution as conservation of countable units

A sample can contain so many colony-forming units that individual colonies cannot be distinguished after growth. Dilution lowers the expected number of founding units within a measured volume, making spatial separation more plausible. The arithmetic is a conservation argument. Suppose a volume v of the original suspension has concentration C₀, expressed as CFU/mL. Its expected number of founding units is C₀v. If it is combined with cell-free liquid to make a final total volume V, and no units are gained or lost, the number is still C₀v. The new concentration is therefore C₁ = C₀v/V. The dimensionless dilution fraction d = v/V is less than or equal to one in an ordinary dilution, and C₁ = d C₀.

The reciprocal of the dilution fraction is commonly called the dilution factor F = 1/d = V/v. Terminology varies among textbooks, so writing the fraction explicitly is safer than relying on the phrase dilution factor. A tenfold dilution has d = 10⁻¹ and F = 10. A hundredfold dilution has d = 10⁻² and F = 100. Multiplying an observed diluted concentration by F reconstructs the original concentration, while multiplying the original concentration by d predicts the diluted concentration. Confusing these two directions produces errors of multiple orders of magnitude.

A serial dilution is a sequence of dilution operations in which the output of one operation becomes the input to the next. If the first dilution fraction is d₁ and the second is d₂, the resulting concentration is C₂ = d₂ C₁ = d₂ d₁ C₀. By the same reasoning, after k steps the final dilution fraction relative to the original sample is the product d₁d₂...dₖ. Equal tenfold steps have a total fraction 10⁻ᵏ. The reciprocal factors also multiply, so three tenfold steps give a thousandfold dilution rather than a thirtyfold dilution. This calculation does not require any assumption about the generation time of the microorganisms. It is simply the consequence of redistributing conserved material over known volumes.

Worked example 7. Not every dilution series consists of equal decimal steps. In a teaching example, the successive fractions are 1/4, 1/5 and 1/10. The final fraction is 1/(4 × 5 × 10) = 1/200. A concentration of 4 × 10⁶ units/mL would then become 2 × 10⁴ units/mL under ideal conservation. If the fractions had been added instead, the result would make no physical sense because the second operation acts on an already diluted sample. At each step it is the concentration in the preceding sample that is multiplied by the new fraction.

Volume language can be a source of confusion. The statement that a sample occupies one part of a final ten-part mixture describes d = 1/10. The statement that one part of sample is added to ten parts of diluent describes eleven total parts and hence d = 1/11. Some shorthand conventions use a colon to refer to sample and final volume, while others describe the relative volumes of sample and diluent. When a problem provides explicit volumes, calculate the final volume and do not rely on an ambiguous shorthand notation. Also ensure that both numerator and denominator are in compatible units before taking the ratio.

Worked example 8. The dilution described on a tube label is not necessarily the fraction of original material represented in the final counting event. The observation might involve only a portion of that diluted suspension. For instance, if the final dilution fraction is 10⁻⁵, each millilitre of the diluted suspension represents 10⁻⁵ mL of the original under the ideal model. An observed portion of 0.1 mL of the diluted suspension represents 0.1 × 10⁻⁵ = 10⁻⁶ mL of original suspension. The smaller represented volume does not mean that the concentration of the tube has undergone another tenfold dilution. It means that only a tenth of a millilitre of that concentration was observed. Distinguishing concentration dilution from sampled volume will prevent one of the most common errors in CFU calculations.

Worked example 9. A dilution series can amplify inaccuracies. If a step intended to have fraction 0.1 actually has fraction 0.11 and all later steps are as assumed, the final sample contains ten percent more units than would be predicted using the nominal dilution. If three successive steps each have an actual fraction 0.11 rather than 0.1, the ratio of actual to nominal final concentration is (1.1)³ = 1.331. The resulting concentration estimate using nominal fractions would be about 33.1 percent above the original concentration, even if the colonies were counted without error. Repeating the same systematic error cannot remove it.

The more serious biological departures from the dilution model involve a change in the number of units itself. If associated cells disperse into separate founders, the number of CFU may increase as the dilution proceeds. If some cells fail to recover after exposure to the dilution environment, the number of units detected may decrease. If a suspension is uneven, the portion transferred may not represent the preceding concentration. The multiplication of stated dilution fractions remains mathematically correct, but the original biological quantity can no longer be recovered from it without additional evidence. A useful series of counts should therefore behave consistently with the dilution model within the expected sampling uncertainty; departures from this pattern can indicate a physical, physiological or measurement problem.

8. Deriving and applying the CFU concentration equation

The number of colonies on an ideal countable plate is related to the number of reproductive units represented by that plate. Let C₀ denote the original concentration in CFU/mL, let d denote the cumulative dilution fraction of the sample represented, let v denote its volume in mL, and let n denote the observed number of colonies. The expected colony count is C₀ d v. Setting the observed count equal to this expected value for purposes of estimation and rearranging gives the estimate Ĉ₀ = n/(d v). This equation has a clear physical interpretation: divide the observed number of colonies by the equivalent volume of the original suspension represented by the observation. The units are CFU per millilitre.

Worked example 10. For a basic worked example, a plate represents 0.1 mL of a suspension diluted to 10⁻⁶ of the original. It contains 75 distinguishable colonies. The equivalent original volume is 0.1 × 10⁻⁶ mL = 10⁻⁷ mL. The estimated original concentration is 75/10⁻⁷ = 7.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL. The diluted suspension itself is estimated to contain 750 CFU/mL, which when multiplied by 10⁶ gives the same answer. The numerical consistency between these two routes is a useful check. Reporting 7.5 × 10⁷ would omit the plated volume correction; reporting 7.5 × 10⁻⁷ would apply the dilution in the wrong direction.

Worked example 11. For a problem involving unequal dilution steps, suppose the cumulative fraction is (1/4)(1/5)(1/10) = 1/200, the represented volume is 0.2 mL, and the observed count is 120. The equivalent original volume is 0.2/200 = 0.001 mL. Dividing 120 by 0.001 gives 1.2 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. The same expression applies to decimal or nondecimal dilution because the denominator always represents a volume of original material. If the total original suspension occupies 50 mL and its concentration is representative, the estimated total number of reproductive units is 1.2 × 10⁵ × 50 = 6 × 10⁶ CFU. The concentration and the total quantity answer different questions and must carry different units.

Worked example 12. The equation can also be used in reverse to predict expected counts from a specified concentration. Suppose the estimated original concentration is 8 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. A represented original volume of 10⁻⁶ mL should have an expected count of 80. If the represented original volume is ten times larger, the expected count becomes 800, assuming the founding units remain independent and recover equally. The mathematical expectation does not guarantee that 800 separately distinguishable colonies will actually be visible; at high colony density, merging and other interactions can undermine the correspondence. This contrast between expectation and detectability explains why a mathematically correct prediction may not produce a useful measurement.

Worked example 13. Repeated plates representing equal original volumes can be combined by averaging their counts and dividing by that common volume. If two plates each represent 2 × 10⁻⁶ mL of original culture and contain 96 and 104 colonies, the mean is 100. The concentration estimate is 100/(2 × 10⁻⁶) = 5 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. Equivalently, sum the 200 colonies and divide by the total represented volume of 4 × 10⁻⁶ mL. This form generalizes to different valid represented volumes and is conceptually more transparent than memorizing a special weighted-average formula.

Worked example 14. When several independent valid observations of the same concentration represent different volumes, write eᵢ = dᵢvᵢ for the equivalent original volume of observation i. The expected combined count is C₀ times the sum of all eᵢ because expectation is additive. A pooled concentration estimate is therefore Σnᵢ/Σeᵢ. For the ideal sampling model it uses all represented material. Consider two observations with counts 180 and 24, representing respectively 10⁻⁵ mL and 10⁻⁶ mL. If both are judged valid under the stated counting criterion, the combined estimate is 204/(1.1 × 10⁻⁵) = approximately 1.85 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. Averaging the separately calculated concentrations, 1.8 × 10⁷ and 2.4 × 10⁷, would instead give 2.1 × 10⁷ and place equal weight on observations containing very different amounts of information. The pooled expression gives greater influence to the observation representing a larger volume. An official reporting method may prescribe particular plate-selection rules, which must be followed when given in a question; the general pooled calculation assumes that all included counts are valid and comparable.

Worked example 15. Sometimes the original sample is a solid rather than a liquid. The relevant denominator may then be an effective mass of the original material, producing CFU/g rather than CFU/mL. A hypothetical homogeneous suspension contains one gram equivalent of a solid sample per 10 mL of suspension. A counting observation represents 0.001 mL of that suspension. The original solid mass represented is 0.001 × 0.1 = 0.0001 g. If it yields 60 colonies, the inferred density is 6 × 10⁵ CFU/g, subject to the assumptions that the suspension represents the solid and that the units were recovered comparably. Converting from a liquid suspension to solid material requires knowledge of the original mass-to-volume relationship, not a mere change of the unit label.

All these examples share the same denominator. Counted colonies are divided by the original material represented by them. A correctly written dimensional expression makes most arithmetic errors visible immediately. If the desired answer is CFU/mL but the denominator is a pure number, a volume factor is missing. If a result for the number of units in a whole vessel is reported as CFU/mL, the conversion from concentration to total has not been made. The mathematical correction reconstructs a concentration from observable counts; it does not reconstruct individual cells hidden inside reproductive groups or those unable to grow under the recovery conditions.

9. Countability, detection limits and uncertainty in colony measurements

A valid mathematical correction is not sufficient to make every plate informative. Imagine a suspension whose concentration is estimated from a single observed colony. If one additional colony had appeared, the estimated concentration would double. If the observed colony had failed to become detectable, the estimate based on the count would become zero. The problem is not that the division by represented volume was performed incorrectly. The count itself is too small to provide a precise estimate. At the other extreme, a plate with densely merged colonies may contain a large number of founding units but give no reliable way to identify each individual colony. A useful colony-counting range balances these competing limitations.

Many textbook examination problems adopt a range of 30 to 300 colonies per plate as a conventional criterion. It is important to understand the reason rather than treat the numbers as universal physical limits. The lower boundary reflects the large relative variation when few units are detected. The upper boundary reflects crowding, merging, competition and limitations of visual enumeration. Different validated methods prescribe different ranges. For example, the US FDA's 2025 Aerobic Plate Count chapter discusses different accepted ranges used by different organizations. In a question specifying a particular range, apply that range consistently. A plate with 25 colonies might be acceptable in one stated method and outside the preferred range in another.

Crowding introduces a qualitatively different problem from random fluctuation. The number of initial reproductive units may be high, but two or more of them can produce a merged visible structure. Local resource depletion or interaction among growing colonies may also affect the number that reach the detectable endpoint. The apparent count is then biased relative to the founding-unit number. A formula that multiplies a crowded count by a reciprocal dilution does not undo the merging. It simply gives a precisely scaled version of an unreliable observation. The notation too numerous to count describes an inability to distinguish and enumerate the colonies reliably; it is not a numerical count.

The randomness of low-count observations can be understood from an idealized probability model. Suppose a large suspension contains independently distributed reproductive units at constant concentration C, and a very small randomly selected volume e of original liquid is represented. Each unit has a small chance of appearing in that observation. Under independent random sampling and equal recovery, the number detected can be approximated by a Poisson distribution with mean λ = Ce. Its variance also equals λ. Therefore the standard deviation of the count is √λ. The relative standard deviation is √λ/λ = 1/√λ. This is a statement about ideal counting variation, not a full account of all sources of experimental uncertainty.

Worked example 16. For an expected count of 25, the standard deviation is 5 and the relative standard deviation is 20 percent. At an expected count of 100, the corresponding values are 10 and 10 percent. At 400 independently resolvable events, the relative standard deviation would be 5 percent. However, a plate containing 400 colonies may violate a stated crowding criterion; the improvement predicted by the simple probability model would not justify using an invalid observation. Combining independent acceptable observations can increase the total number of counted events without placing all of them on a single crowded surface.

Worked example 17. In the ideal model, a concentration estimate from count n and represented volume e has estimated counting standard error approximately √n/e for sufficiently informative counts. For example, suppose n = 100 and e = 10⁻⁶ mL. The estimated concentration is 10⁸ CFU/mL, and the approximate counting-only standard error is 10⁷ CFU/mL. That number does not include dilution error, loss of reproductive ability, aggregation, between-culture variation or an incorrect assumption about recovery. It is useful for comparing the information content of two observations, but it is not a guarantee of the accuracy of the result.

Worked example 18. Consider four valid observations each yielding about 100 independently sampled colonies and representing 10⁻⁶ mL of original suspension. Their pooled count is about 400 and their total represented volume is 4 × 10⁻⁶ mL. The concentration estimate remains about 10⁸ CFU/mL, while the relative counting uncertainty is approximately 1/√400 = 5 percent instead of 10 percent. If the four plates all originated from a single preparation with an erroneous dilution fraction, the pooled estimate can still be biased by that shared error. Independence of individual sampled units does not imply independence of all stages of an experiment.

The absence of detected colonies presents a particularly important reasoning challenge. A zero count does not imply an absolute concentration of zero unless an exhaustive, perfectly sensitive observation of the entire relevant population is assumed. If a very small fraction of a culture is represented, there may simply have been no culturable units in that fraction. The expected count in the ideal model is λ = Ce, and the probability of observing zero is exp(−λ). If λ is one, the probability is about 0.368, so observing no colonies is not an unlikely event. A culture can contain reproductive units and nevertheless produce a zero observation.

One detected unit corresponds numerically to a concentration of 1/e. This is a useful reporting-scale concept, often described informally as the single-event detection limit. It should not be confused with a concentration that is detected with certainty. At the concentration 1/e the expected count is one, but the probability of detection is only 1 − exp(−1), about 63 percent under the model. To find the concentration associated with a 95 percent probability of detecting at least one unit, set exp(−Ce) = 0.05. Taking the natural logarithm gives Ce = −ln(0.05) ≈ 2.996. The corresponding concentration is approximately 3/e. This is also the usual one-sided ideal Poisson upper confidence bound for a zero observation. It is not a claim that the actual concentration has a 95 percent posterior probability of lying below that bound.

Worked example 19. As an example, suppose the total volume of original material represented by several zero observations is 0.02 mL. The single-event reporting scale is 1/0.02 = 50 CFU/mL. The ideal Poisson 95 percent upper bound is about 3/0.02 = 150 CFU/mL. The distinction is useful in exam questions that ask whether a sample has been proved sterile. Even this upper bound is conditional on recovery of culturable units with the assumed probability and the random-sampling model. Organisms unable to grow under the assay conditions are not constrained in the same way by the absence of colonies.

Consistency across a dilution series is another powerful source of evidence. Suppose observations representing equal physical volumes at two adjacent tenfold dilutions produce 160 and 17 colonies. The ratio of approximately 9.4 is reasonably consistent with a tenfold concentration change, considering random sampling. If the counts instead are 160 and 140, the more dilute observation has nearly the same number of colonies despite representing only one tenth as much original liquid. That discrepancy calls for an explanation. Possible reasons include an erroneous dilution, unequal sampling, misidentification, crowding at the higher density, altered aggregate dispersion or inhibition of reproductive recovery in the less diluted material. A discrepancy is a signal to investigate, not proof of any one mechanism.

A good analytical answer distinguishes precision, validity and biological meaning. Precision concerns reproducibility and random counting uncertainty. Validity concerns whether the count corresponds to the object and volume specified by the model. Biological meaning concerns what that object tells us about the population. A plate can have an acceptable number of colonies and low sampling uncertainty, yet still report only the subset of microorganisms able to recover under the chosen conditions. The count should be recorded with its original material basis and the relevant recovery assumptions. The next method offers a different solution to the problem of counting large populations: instead of waiting for distinct descendants to form colonies, it detects the collective effect of particles on a light beam.

10. Turbidity and optical density: interpreting transmitted light

The cloudiness of a suspension is a consequence of the interaction between light and the material it encounters. A cell contains water, macromolecules and membranes whose optical properties differ from those of the surrounding liquid. When a beam of light passes through a suspension, some of its energy continues along the original direction while some is redirected by scattering. Some light may also be absorbed by cellular pigments or medium constituents. As the number and properties of suspended particles change, the intensity of light reaching a detector can change. This provides the basis of an indirect growth measurement.

A spectrophotometer is an instrument that measures transmitted light at a selected wavelength relative to a reference. In an idealized description, let I₀ be the reference transmitted intensity and I be the intensity registered through the sample after suitable referencing. The transmittance T = I/I₀ is dimensionless. When less light reaches the detector, T becomes smaller. Optical density, often displayed as absorbance by the instrument, is defined as OD = −log₁₀(T) = log₁₀(I₀/I). A reading of zero corresponds to the same measured transmitted intensity as the reference. The formula does not say that zero optical density guarantees the absence of all cells; a small cellular contribution may lie below the sensitivity of the measurement.

Worked example 20. This logarithmic definition has a useful physical meaning. If half the reference intensity is measured, T = 0.5 and OD ≈ 0.301. If one tenth is measured, T = 0.1 and OD = 1. If one hundredth is measured, OD = 2. Optical density is not a percentage of light lost. For example, OD = 0.3 does not mean 30 percent of the reference light has disappeared from the detected beam. The corresponding transmitted fraction is 10⁻⁰·³ ≈ 0.501, close to 50 percent. Similarly, doubling optical density does not mean that the transmitted intensity is halved; the intensity ratio is exponential in optical density.

The choice of wavelength is part of the measurement definition. OD₆₀₀ denotes a reading at a wavelength of approximately 600 nanometres, a visible-light wavelength commonly used for bacterial and yeast suspensions. The number 600 specifies wavelength, not a concentration or a cell diameter. The reading depends on the scattering and absorption properties at that wavelength. If a culture contains pigments that absorb strongly in the selected spectral region, its optical density may differ from that of an equally numerous nonpigmented culture. Wavelength choice is therefore not interchangeable across every organism or medium.

A common misconception is that a microbial suspension obeys the ordinary Beer–Lambert relationship exactly in the same way as a transparent solution of absorbing molecules. In an ideal absorbing solution, each thin layer removes a fraction of the incident intensity proportional to the amount of absorber present in that layer. If the attenuation coefficient is α, a thickness increment dx changes intensity by dI = −α I dx. Integrating for a homogeneous sample over path length l gives ln(I/I₀) = −αl, or OD = αl/ln(10). For a simple absorbing solute in the relevant regime α is proportional to solute concentration, leading to the familiar linear relationship between absorbance, path length and concentration.

A dilute suspension of independently scattering particles may exhibit an approximately analogous linear relationship if scattered light is mostly excluded from the detected beam and particle properties remain constant. But the effective attenuation depends on scattering geometry, and not solely on molecular absorption. At higher particle densities, light can scatter several times, and the detector may collect some redirected light. Instrument geometry, sample path length and the distribution of particles affect the recorded signal. Consequently, the ordinary Beer–Lambert equation cannot be assumed to provide a universal exact cell count from OD. Its value in teaching is to explain why a roughly linear low-density range can exist and why the logarithmic scale is appropriate for attenuation, not to replace the need for calibration.

Optical density is especially useful because a reading can often be obtained rapidly without waiting for cells to reproduce on a solid medium. A growing culture may be observed repeatedly by a series of measurements, and in suitable ranges the optical signal can act as a convenient proxy for changing biomass or cell concentration. The method has lower sensitivity for sufficiently dilute populations because a small difference between reference and sample intensity is hard to distinguish from background and instrument noise. At high densities its usefulness is limited by nonlinear response and reduced ability to relate the measured signal to the underlying particle concentration.

A blank is essential because the medium itself may absorb or scatter light. The reference intensity I₀ in the equation is the intensity registered for the chosen reference, typically a matched cell-free medium, rather than an assertion that all the light emitted by the source reaches the detector. For a clear medium and a homogeneous suspension, referencing against the same medium without cells helps isolate the additional attenuation associated with the suspended material. If the medium changes colour during metabolism or forms noncellular precipitates, the initial blank may not capture all background changes during growth. An apparent increase in OD can then be caused partly by chemical changes outside the cells. A coloured extracellular product, an insoluble precipitate or bubbles can contribute to a signal without an increase in the number of microorganisms. The question is not simply whether the instrument was functioning, but whether its signal is specific to the property being inferred.

Dead cells can also scatter light as long as their structures persist. Therefore a high optical density is compatible with a low colony-forming count. A treatment that stops reproduction without promptly disrupting cell envelopes may leave turbidity nearly unchanged. A treatment followed by extensive lysis may instead reduce optical density, although fragments and released material can contribute to the residual signal. Cells that enlarge or elongate may scatter light differently from smaller cells even before population number changes. Changes in cell shape, aggregation or composition can alter the optical signal per cell. This is one reason why optical density is best understood as an optical measurement of the suspension, not a direct count of living cells.

Optical density and turbidity are closely related but not identical terms. Turbidity broadly describes cloudiness associated with scattering by suspended particles; optical density describes a particular logarithmic measure of transmitted intensity. Some turbidity instruments measure light scattered at an angle instead of attenuation in a transmitted beam. Their readings use a different instrument scale. A numerical turbidity reading from one type of instrument cannot automatically be substituted into an OD-based concentration equation established for another. In an examination problem, use the stated measurement definition and calibration.

The scientific advantage of an indirect optical method lies in its responsiveness to changes that can be measured conveniently. Its limitation lies in the interpretation of those changes. An observed increase is evidence that the optical properties of the measured suspension have changed. To translate that change into a quantitative statement about cell concentration or biomass, one must establish and validate the relationship between the instrument signal and the desired biological quantity. That relationship is the subject of calibration.

11. Calibration, linear response and quantitative use of optical density

Calibration establishes an empirical relationship between an observed signal and a reference measurement. For a microbial suspension, the reference can be a direct count, a colony count or a dry-mass estimate, depending on the biological quantity one wishes to infer. These alternatives produce different calibrations. A curve relating OD to total recognizable cells is not automatically a curve relating OD to colony-forming units, even when both measures correlate well during a particular growth condition. If the fraction of culturable cells changes, the two relationships may diverge.

To construct the conceptual model, imagine a series of suspensions containing different known concentrations of the same population. Each is measured with a consistent optical setup and appropriate blank. If the particles remain similar across the observations, a plot of OD against reference concentration may initially show an approximately straight line. The slope describes the change in OD per unit concentration. An intercept may reflect imperfect background correction or other properties of the empirical measurement. In the simplest blank-corrected model, OD = kC, where k is the calibration slope and C is the reference concentration. The slope has units reciprocal to those of C because OD is dimensionless. Rearrangement gives C = OD/k.

Worked example 21. It is often convenient to express the calibration in the opposite direction. Suppose the stated calibration is C = a OD, with a = 4 × 10⁸ cells/mL per OD unit, applicable only over a specified linear range of 0.05 to 0.40 for this instrument and cell population. At OD = 0.25, the estimated reference concentration is 10⁸ cells/mL. This is not a universal value for bacteria at OD 0.25; it follows from the explicit hypothetical calibration. A different cell size, instrument geometry or reference assay may yield a different conversion. The student should always write the calibration units and the reference quantity in the answer.

Worked example 22. A calibration may include an intercept. Suppose a hypothetical reference-count relationship is OD = 0.02 + (2 × 10⁻⁹ mL/cell) C. A measured OD of 0.22 then corresponds to C = (0.22 − 0.02)/(2 × 10⁻⁹) = 10⁸ cells/mL. Ignoring the intercept would give 1.1 × 10⁸ cells/mL, a ten percent overestimate in this case. If the OD is close to the intercept, a small uncertainty in the background has a large relative effect on the inferred concentration. A fitted line with an intercept should not be extrapolated to negative cell concentrations; that is a sign that the empirical relationship has reached its limit of interpretation.

Worked example 23. A common analytical task concerns a suspension whose OD is outside the calibrated range. Direct use of the linear formula may then give a biased result. An appropriate reading from a diluted portion can be translated back to the original concentration if the dilution preserves the relevant particle properties and the diluted observation lies within the established range. Suppose a sample is observed at a one-fifth dilution and its blank-corrected OD is 0.30. With the hypothetical calibration C = (4 × 10⁸ cells/mL per OD unit) × OD, the diluted suspension contains 1.2 × 10⁸ cells/mL and the original estimate is 6 × 10⁸ cells/mL. Multiplying 0.30 by five gives a linear-equivalent OD of 1.50, but this is a reconstructed linear proxy. It does not establish that the undiluted suspension would actually produce an instrument reading of 1.50.

Worked example 24. This distinction can be tested by comparing several represented dilution fractions. Suppose a culture measured at one quarter of the original concentration gives a blank-corrected OD of 0.32 and at one eighth gives 0.16. The scaled values are 1.28 and 1.28, consistent with the assumed linear model. An undiluted reading of 0.93 would not be surprising if the high-density measurement were outside the useful range. The correct interpretation is not that the culture lost cells during the undiluted observation. The instrument response to the dense suspension may be nonlinear. Conversely, if the scaled values from valid diluted measurements disagree substantially, the linear model, dilution assumptions or sample uniformity may be questionable.

Worked example 25. The effect of path length is also worth understanding. In the ideal independent-attenuation model, optical density is proportional to path length for the same concentration. A sample that gives OD = 0.20 over a 1 cm path would give approximately OD = 0.10 over a 0.5 cm path if all relevant optical assumptions hold. The two readings do not indicate a twofold difference in concentration. Microplate readers can have effective optical paths that depend on the amount and geometry of liquid; cuvette measurements often use a fixed path. A calibration established on one instrument cannot automatically be applied to a different optical setup. At exam level, the key is the dependency on geometry rather than detailed instrument design.

The study by Stevenson and colleagues in 2016 examined calibration of microbial growth measurements made with microplate readers. It is an instructive example because it addressed a widespread assumption: that a convenient OD reading can always be converted into cell concentration using a simple common factor. The work emphasized the importance of optical geometry, response at different densities and calibration against appropriate reference measurements. Their comparisons used both microbial suspensions and particles with defined properties, allowing changes in scatterer characteristics to be separated conceptually from changes in population number. If two equally concentrated suspensions contain particles with different dimensions or optical properties, identical OD readings are not guaranteed. A comparison against independent particle or cell concentration data makes departures from a simple linear model visible; measurements of OD alone would not establish which part of the apparent trend arose from instrument response. This evidence explains why a conversion obtained during ordinary growth can become inappropriate after a change in cellular morphology. It does not imply that calibration is impossible; it identifies conditions under which the calibration must be reconsidered. The lesson for data interpretation is that calibration is not a formality performed after the biological analysis. It is part of defining the measured quantity. If a treatment changes cell shape or viability, a reference curve established for a different physiological state may no longer justify the same inferred population numbers.

Worked example 26. An optical estimate can be compared with a biomass calibration. Suppose a given reference system has X = (0.45 g/L per OD unit) × OD over its validated range. At OD = 0.40, X = 0.18 g/L. This estimate concerns dry biomass concentration, not the number of cells. If the mean mass per cell is independently known to be 0.5 pg for the same material, the corresponding number concentration would be 0.18 g/L divided by 0.5 × 10⁻¹² g/cell = 3.6 × 10¹¹ cells/L, or 3.6 × 10⁸ cells/mL. That secondary conversion assumes that the mass consists of the counted cells and that the mean mass is appropriate. If cells accumulate reserves or become larger, the mass estimate may remain useful while the inferred number becomes inaccurate.

It is possible to use relative optical readings without converting them into absolute concentrations. If blank-corrected OD rises from 0.10 to 0.30 within the same validated linear range and the optical properties per cell are stable, the reference concentration has approximately tripled. The actual conversion coefficient cancels when the two estimates are divided. However, if the response has a nonzero intercept, the appropriate ratio is the ratio of the corrected signal above that intercept. If cell morphology changes or the reading becomes nonlinear, even a relative inference may be invalid. In the next chapter on batch growth, optical curves will be interpreted with precisely these conditions in mind; the formal calculation of specific growth rate belongs to the kinetics chapter.

A calibration curve is therefore a conditional bridge between an easily measured physical signal and a biological reference. The bridge is useful only as long as the conditions that support it remain sufficiently similar. For some organisms or growth situations, the desired reference is total dry biomass rather than cell number. We now turn to that measurement directly, including why a mass measurement is particularly useful for populations that cannot be separated into discrete, uniform cells.

12. Dry cell mass: measuring accumulated biological material

A cell count is most useful when the population can be divided into identifiable units and those units are the subject of the investigation. Some growth processes are better described by the amount of material produced. Filamentous fungi and certain filament-forming bacteria illustrate the reason. A growing network can extend, branch and accumulate cellular material while the number of independent fragments changes irregularly. Dividing the network into pieces does not necessarily represent production of new biomass. A measure based on total dry cellular material avoids the need to define every fragment as an independent individual.

The basic idea of dry mass measurement is to distinguish cellular solids from the surrounding liquid and remove water from the collected material until the resulting mass can be interpreted reproducibly. The difference between the mass of a container with the dried material and the mass of the empty container gives the apparent mass of the retained solids. The empty-container mass is known as the tare. If the retained solids consist of the cells of interest, the corrected mass estimates dry cellular biomass. The expression is simple, but the biological meaning depends on which material was retained and how much of the unwanted background contributes to the result.

A suspension is composed of cells, solvent and dissolved or suspended extracellular substances. Measuring the dry residue of an entire culture without distinguishing these components can include unused nutrients, salts and secreted products. The resulting mass may not be an estimate of cellular biomass. Collection of cellular material therefore aims to separate the cells from most of the surrounding liquid. A matched cell-free background can be used conceptually to assess any residual mass associated with noncellular material. If dissolved salts remain associated with the retained fraction and crystallize as water is removed, they add mass without representing cellular growth. Insoluble extracellular polymers can also be retained together with cells, particularly in populations that form aggregates or biofilms.

Worked example 27. Consider a hypothetical measurement in which the tare mass of the collection container is 1.200 g and the mass of that container plus retained dry material is 1.235 g. The apparent retained mass is 0.035 g. A matched background correction indicates 0.005 g of noncellular residue. The corrected dry biomass is 0.030 g. If the measured material originated from 60 mL, or 0.060 L, of representative culture, the concentration is 0.030/0.060 = 0.50 g/L. This worked example emphasizes both subtractions and the conversion of volume units. Using 60 as the denominator without converting millilitres would give 0.0005 g/mL, which is the same concentration expressed in different units, not 0.0005 g/L.

Dry mass gives a population-level measure that is closely related to net biosynthesis. When cells assimilate nutrients into proteins, nucleic acids, membranes and storage substances, the mass of retained cellular material can increase. When cells lose material through degradation, release or lysis, the measured cellular fraction can decrease. However, the measured mass does not indicate how many cells were capable of reproduction at the time of sampling. Nonviable but structurally intact cells contribute material, and certain fragments may remain in the collected fraction. An increase in biomass is not by itself evidence of an increase in the number of viable individuals.

The term dry cell weight is conventional in microbiology even though the experimentally reported quantity is a mass, often in grams or grams per litre. Wet mass, in contrast, contains variable intracellular water and extracellular liquid trapped among the cells. Even two preparations with the same cell composition can have different wet masses if they retain different amounts of liquid. Osmotic swelling can change wet volume and wet mass without an equivalent increase in macromolecular material. Conversely, changes in the amount of dry intracellular storage compounds can produce real biomass changes without a proportional change in cell number. Dry mass removes much of the variability caused by free water, but it does not make every remaining molecule part of the viable reproductive population.

In a very dilute suspension, the mass of the cells may be small compared with the tare or the residual background. The desired biological quantity is obtained by subtracting two larger measured masses. If the measurement uncertainty of those masses is comparable with their difference, the relative uncertainty of the result is high. For example, a corrected mass of 2 mg cannot be interpreted as precisely as a corrected mass of 200 mg when the uncertainty of the weighing system is on the scale of 1 mg. Increasing the represented culture volume can increase the measurable mass, provided collection remains representative and background contributions are accounted for. This is the mass analogue of improving the information content of a low-count observation.

A dry-mass determination is generally destructive for the collected cells, unlike a routine optical reading of a suspension. It gives an endpoint for the material represented by that sample, not a continuous record of the same living cells. In a time series, material may be removed at several times from a culture or from matched cultures, and the concentration estimates must be compared on a consistent basis. If a significant fraction of a vessel is removed, the consequences for total biomass and subsequent culture behaviour must be distinguished from changes in the concentration of the remaining liquid. The appropriate comparison depends on whether the question concerns total production or concentration.

Biomass concentration can provide the reference for a later yield calculation. Yield describes how much biomass was formed relative to substrate consumed and is a different question from how quickly cell numbers increased. Suppose a culture accumulates 0.6 g/L of cellular dry material while its substrate concentration falls. The biomass measurement establishes the numerator of a possible yield calculation; the consumed substrate must also be measured before the yield can be determined. A large final biomass does not establish high yield unless the initial biomass and resource consumption are known. This concept is developed quantitatively in the dedicated chapter on growth yield. For now, the essential insight is that mass, cell number and reproductive capacity answer different physiological questions.

13. Using complementary measurements to identify biological changes

A strong experiment often measures the same population through two or more independent biological properties. Each method has its own ambiguities, but the combination can restrict the possible interpretations. If an optical signal increases, there may be more particles or a change in their properties. If the total microscopic count increases at the same time, evidence for an increase in recognizable cell number becomes stronger. If the colony-forming count also increases proportionately, the findings support an increase in culturable reproductive units. Agreement is most informative when the methods are sensitive to different sources of error.

Worked example 28. Consider a synthetic example involving two observations of an initially dispersed population. At the first observation, the direct count is 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL, the viable count is 1.8 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, and the blank-corrected OD is 0.10. At the second observation, the direct count is 6 × 10⁷ cells/mL, the viable count is 5.4 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, and the OD is 0.30 within the same validated range. Both numerical measurements have tripled, as has the optical signal. The apparent ratio of colony-forming units to recognizable cells is 0.9 CFU/cell at both times. Under the stated dispersion and recovery assumptions, the data support numerical growth of a population with a stable high reproductive recovery fraction. They do not prove that every cell divided threefold or that every individual cell had the same generation time.

Now change the second observation. The direct count remains 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL, the dry biomass concentration doubles and the OD increases. The viable count remains near its initial value. This pattern is consistent with increased average mass per cell or a change in the retained cellular material without substantial net numerical growth. A cell-division perturbation can produce such uncoupling because synthesis and envelope expansion may continue during a period in which the formation of new separate cells is limited. But the data alone do not identify the molecular target of the perturbation. An observation of cellular morphology, evidence that the retained mass is cellular and a suitable comparison culture would strengthen the interpretation.

Worked example 29. In a third example, a culture initially contains 10⁸ recognizable cells/mL and 9 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. After a perturbation it still contains about 10⁸ recognizable cells/mL but only 9 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. The colony count has decreased one hundredfold while the number of detectable cellular profiles has changed little. The apparent reproductive recovery ratio falls from 0.9 to 0.009 CFU/cell. If all units remain independent, the recovery fraction falls from approximately 90 percent to 0.9 percent, a difference of 89.1 percentage points. The stable direct count is consistent with persistence of cellular structures. The result strongly supports a loss of ability to produce colonies under the tested conditions, but does not on its own distinguish killing from reversible injury or a change in culturability.

An optical measurement in that third situation might remain close to its initial value because the particles that scatter light have not disappeared. If the data instead show a marked decrease in optical density and direct count together with the loss of CFU, structural disruption or removal of cells from the sampled compartment becomes a stronger possibility. A decrease in biomass could support the loss of retained cellular material. Nevertheless, one must still distinguish lysis from sedimentation, attachment to surfaces or other physical redistribution of material. A cell that leaves the sampled liquid is not necessarily a cell that has died.

Worked example 30. Aggregation produces another characteristic pattern. Suppose direct observation resolves ten million cells per millilitre both before and after the formation of groups. At first, individual cells produce approximately ten million CFU/mL. Later the same ten million cells are distributed into groups of five, each group gives one colony, and all individuals remain capable of reproduction. The colony count becomes two million CFU/mL, and the apparent CFU-to-cell ratio becomes 0.2. It would be incorrect to conclude that only twenty percent of cells are alive. The ratio has changed because the counted reproductive unit has changed. Comparison of the distribution of cells in direct observations can reveal the association and motivate a revised interpretation.

The reverse pattern can occur when existing aggregates are dispersed. A rapid rise in CFU that is not accompanied by an increase in cell number or dry mass is consistent with an increase in the number of independently counted founding units. This possibility is especially important when comparing a suspension before and after any physical change that might modify association. If the observed increase occurs too quickly to be plausibly explained by substantial cell reproduction, the case for altered counting units becomes stronger. The timing of an observation can provide additional evidence, but only relative to the known physiology and measurement conditions of the population.

A useful way to interpret apparently discordant assays is to write the relationship between them explicitly. For a dispersed suspension of individual recognizable cells, assume that a fraction p forms visible colonies under the stated conditions. The expected viable count concentration is approximately pC, where C is the total recognizable cell concentration. Thus the ratio of CFU/mL to cells/mL estimates p only if a successful individual contributes one distinguishable colony, the measurements represent the same population, and both assays are valid. With aggregation, the count is instead related to the number of successful independent groups, so the simple relationship to individual cell viability fails. The ratio is always calculable when the data permit it; its interpretation as a probability is conditional.

Worked example 31. Suppose a direct count is 5 × 10⁷ cells/mL but the plate-derived estimate is 6 × 10⁷ CFU/mL. In the ideal one-cell, one-colony model, the inferred p would be 1.2, which is impossible as a probability. This does not mean that cells are 120 percent viable. It indicates that at least one assumption or measurement is not consistent with the simple model. Random uncertainty might explain a modest discrepancy, especially with low counts. Alternatively, microscopic undercounting, inaccurate dilution correction, different aliquot composition or a different definition of countable objects may be involved. The apparent impossibility is useful because it exposes the limitations of interpreting ratios mechanically.

Paired measurements can also help distinguish metabolic activity from net population growth. If a population continues to consume an energy source but neither mass nor cell number increases detectably, maintenance metabolism is one plausible explanation. Energy may be used to retain gradients, replace damaged molecules or support other functions without net accumulation. However, a measured chemical change could also have a noncellular source; an appropriate control is required before attributing it to metabolism. Detecting a metabolic product is evidence of a transformation, not automatically evidence of multiplication. A student who can explain the intervening biological steps is better prepared to interpret later questions about starvation and antimicrobial stress.

14. Selecting a measurement and reasoning from experimental evidence

The choice of assay depends on the intended biological conclusion. To determine whether a suspension contains more recognizable cells after a change in conditions, a direct counting method is appropriate if the cells can be distinguished reliably. To investigate whether those cells can produce descendants under defined recovery conditions, a colony-based measurement is more directly relevant. To follow relative changes rapidly in a homogeneous culture, optical density can be a convenient proxy after calibration. To compare accumulation of cellular material in a filamentous population, dry mass may be particularly useful. None of these choices implies that the other methods are unimportant. A combination is often required when the distinction between number, mass and reproductive capacity is itself the subject of the study.

In environmental microbiology a direct observation may reveal substantially more cells than conventional culture-based enumeration. Part of the difference can reflect the diversity of nutritional and environmental requirements among organisms. Some observed cells may be dead, some may be metabolically active but not recover under the chosen conditions, and some may belong to organisms for which the assay was never suitable. The discrepancy between direct and culture-based observations therefore cannot be assigned entirely to a single physiological state. The classical observation that only a fraction of environmental cells are readily recovered on particular media illustrates why culture counts should not automatically be generalized to the entire microbial community.

Staley and Konopka's 1985 review of microbial activities in natural habitats discussed the striking discrepancy between direct observations and cultivation-based counts, often called the great plate-count anomaly. The comparison was valuable precisely because its two methods did not depend on the same property. Microscopy could register recognizable objects whether or not they grew on the available medium; cultivation required those objects to generate detectable descendants. A difference of several orders of magnitude was not a counting paradox once the different biological requirements were recognized. The review also concerned environments with different recoveries, so a single percentage should not be generalized to all habitats. The observation established the limitations of cultivation as a census, not the claim that every non-recovered cell was alive, dormant or in a VBNC state. To investigate the physiological state of particular cells, additional evidence of cellular function and recovery would be necessary.

In studies of a defined population, a known starting organism and matched recovery conditions make some comparisons more straightforward. Even then, perturbations can alter the very property that connects the assay to the biological quantity. A change in cell size alters an OD-to-number calibration; a change in association alters the relation between CFU and individual cells; a change in recovery ability alters the fraction that forms colonies. An assay that accurately represents a population at the beginning of an experiment may become a poor proxy for the same quantity later. This is why measurements should be interpreted in light of the state of the population rather than only the identity of the organism.

Imagine two cultures compared after equal observation intervals. Culture A has a larger increase in direct cell concentration, while culture B has a larger increase in dry biomass concentration. There is no necessary contradiction. If the samples refer to the same material and volumes, culture B may have accumulated more cellular mass per counted individual. If the examiner asks which grew more rapidly, the word growth must be specified: the relative rate of increase in population number and the relative rate of accumulation of biomass need not have the same ranking. One cannot infer resource conversion efficiency from either observation without knowing the amounts of substrate consumed. These distinctions prevent the student from conflating the different quantities developed in the next three nodes.

Another experimental question asks whether an observed decrease in CFU is reversible. The relevant comparison is between the same population's recovery under matched conditions and its ability to recover under a changed condition. If some colonies appear after the change, the result is consistent with the presence of recoverable cells. However, a small surviving culturable subpopulation may also have expanded between observations. The stronger inference that previously nonculturable cells themselves recovered requires evidence that addresses that alternative. The reasoning is an example of a general scientific rule: when two hypotheses predict the same final observation, a useful additional measurement must distinguish their predictions rather than merely repeat the endpoint.

Controls should be selected with the hypothesis in mind. If increased optical density could be caused by a noncellular precipitate, a cell-free comparison that experiences the relevant medium conditions may help assess this explanation. If reduced CFU might reflect a problem with the recovery conditions, evidence that a known culturable comparison population can form colonies under those conditions is valuable. If aggregation is suspected, a direct observation capable of distinguishing group structure is more informative than another optical reading alone. The choice of control follows from the mechanism being proposed, not from a fixed list of laboratory steps.

The evidence required for a conclusion also depends on its strength. An observation that OD rose is directly supported by the measured optical signal. A conclusion that cell concentration increased needs a defensible calibration or independent count. A conclusion that reproductive population size increased needs a suitable functional measure. A claim that a particular molecular mechanism caused the change would require additional mechanism-specific evidence. It is a common examination trap to move from an observed correlation directly to a strong causal explanation that has not been uniquely demonstrated.

Finally, consider what will be plotted in a growth experiment. The horizontal axis usually describes elapsed time, while the vertical axis must identify the actual measured quantity and its units. A plot of CFU/mL, a plot of cells/mL and a plot of OD do not show exactly the same biological variable. A logarithmic axis changes how proportional increases appear but does not alter the underlying definition of the measured quantity. A point with no detected colonies is a censored or below-detection observation for many purposes; it cannot simply be plotted as log zero, because the logarithm of zero is undefined. Labeling a vertical axis merely as growth can conceal the assumptions that matter most for interpreting the curve.

The conceptual foundation of microbial physiology is therefore a disciplined distinction between what the population does and how that behaviour is observed. Cells may build new material, divide, remain metabolically active without dividing, lose reproductive ability, associate into groups or disintegrate. Different observations respond differently to these events. A growth curve becomes biologically meaningful only after the reader knows which population property it describes. The next chapter develops the time-dependent behaviour of a closed batch culture, using this distinction to explain why the curves of biomass, total count and reproductive recovery can diverge.

15. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original examination-style practice. Choose the best answer before opening the reasoning.

B1. Which event is sufficient to demonstrate an increase in individual cell number?

  1. Water uptake by existing cells
  2. Synthesis of cellular proteins without division
  3. Completion of division into separate daughter cells without compensating loss
  4. An increase in absorbance of cell-free medium
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. A completed binary division adds one individual when no other cell is lost. Water uptake and protein synthesis can change size or mass without adding individuals. A cell-free optical change cannot demonstrate cell multiplication.

B2. Which is the most accurate definition of a colony-forming unit?

  1. One cell visible under a microscope
  2. A founding unit that yields a separately detectable colony under specified conditions
  3. One dead cell that retains its envelope
  4. All cells in a visible colony
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The measured unit is a successful independent founder, potentially a group. Microscopy does not test colony formation; a dead envelope cannot establish reproduction; descendants in the visible colony are not the original count.

B3. What does OD₆₀₀ specify?

  1. A cell diameter of 600 nm
  2. 600 cells per millilitre
  3. A wavelength of approximately 600 nm
  4. A universal biomass calibration
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The subscript is the optical wavelength. It does not identify diameter, concentration or a universal conversion coefficient.

B4. Which statement about a routine unstained total microscopic count is valid?

  1. It counts all living cells and excludes all dead cells
  2. It measures recognizable cells in a defined sample volume
  3. It is independent of optical resolution
  4. It establishes the generation time of each cell
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Recognizable profiles are counted in a calibrated volume. Dead intact profiles may be detected; resolution affects detection; a single concentration measurement does not establish generation time.

B5. Which method best describes accumulated material in a filamentous population whose fragments do not correspond to equivalent individual cells?

  1. A dry biomass determination with background correction
  2. An uncalibrated colony count converted directly to individual cell number
  3. A count of all fragments interpreted as divisions
  4. The wavelength of the spectrophotometer
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Dry material measures accumulation without requiring a consistent fragment-based individual. Colony and fragment counts require additional assumptions; wavelength alone is not a biological measurement.

B6. A nonculturable cell must be classified as dead because it does not form a colony. Which assessment is correct?

  1. Always correct
  2. Correct only if its optical density is high
  3. Incorrect: the result establishes failure to form a colony under the tested conditions, not necessarily irreversible death
  4. Correct because all living organisms grow on the same medium
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Culturability is conditional. OD does not settle viability; microbial requirements vary. Additional evidence is needed to classify irreversible death.

B7. Which statement about a tenfold dilution is correct under ideal conservation?

  1. Concentration becomes ten times larger
  2. The represented fraction is 0.1
  3. A second tenfold step gives a twentieth of the original concentration
  4. A dilution always preserves CFU even if clumps break
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. A tenfold dilution gives d = 0.1; two steps yield d = 0.01. The inverse is used to estimate original concentration. Association and recovery can change countable units.

B8. Why can a crowded plate underestimate founding units?

  1. Each colony necessarily contains exactly one cell
  2. The dilution fraction becomes greater than one
  3. Growing colonies can merge so their founders cannot be distinguished
  4. The medium no longer has a volume
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Crowding disrupts the correspondence between distinct colonies and independent founders. Colonies contain descendants, fractions do not become greater than one merely through crowding, and the sample volume remains defined.

B9. A spectrophotometer reads OD = 1 for a referenced sample. What is the transmitted fraction?

  1. 100%
  2. 90%
  3. 50%
  4. 10%
Answer and reasoning

Answer: D. T = 10⁻ᴼᴰ = 0.1. A 100% fraction gives OD 0; 50% gives about 0.301; 90% gives about 0.046.

B10. Assertion: intact nonviable cells may contribute to an optical signal. Reason: optical scattering does not require successful cell reproduction.

  1. Both are true and the reason explains the assertion
  2. Both are true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Remaining structures can scatter light irrespective of reproductive capacity, so the reason directly explains the assertion. Neither statement is false; this is not an unrelated coincidence.

B11. Assertion: CFU-to-total-cell ratio is always the fraction of living individuals. Reason: a group of several viable cells can produce one colony.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but reason unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

Answer: D. The reason identifies a counterexample to the assertion. A group may give one CFU despite containing several reproductive individuals. The ratio estimates a fraction only with valid one-cell/one-colony assumptions.

B12. Which experimental comparison best tests whether a rise in optical signal could reflect noncellular precipitation?

  1. Repeat the same OD measurement on the same suspension only
  2. Measure a suitable cell-free comparison exposed to the relevant medium conditions
  3. Assume all particles are viable
  4. Use the OD wavelength as a cell count
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. A matched cell-free comparison can reveal noncellular contributions; repeated readings improve precision but do not establish specificity. The remaining choices confuse optical observations with biological identity.

16. Part C — Quantitative and experimental reasoning

Original problems; all numerical datasets are hypothetical teaching examples, not published experimental measurements or past examination questions.

C1. A chamber has depth 0.02 mm and five counted squares of area 0.01 mm² each. The diluted sample contains 80 observed cells; d = 0.25. Estimate original cell concentration.

  1. 8 × 10⁷ cells/mL
  2. 3.2 × 10⁸ cells/mL
  3. 4 × 10⁶ cells/mL
  4. 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Total volume = 5 × 0.01 × 0.02 × 10⁻³ = 10⁻⁶ mL. Diluted density = 80/10⁻⁶ = 8 × 10⁷; original = 8 × 10⁷/0.25 = 3.2 × 10⁸. A omits dilution, C and D use incorrect scaling.

C2. A count of 112 colonies represents 0.2 mL of a 10⁻⁵ dilution. What was the original concentration?

  1. 5.6 × 10⁷ CFU/mL
  2. 1.12 × 10⁷ CFU/mL
  3. 2.24 × 10⁶ CFU/mL
  4. 5.6 × 10⁵ CFU/mL
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Original equivalent volume = 0.2 × 10⁻⁵ = 2 × 10⁻⁶ mL. 112/(2 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.6 × 10⁷. B omits the volume correction; C and D do not divide by the proper original equivalent.

C3. Two valid observations represent original volumes 2 × 10⁻⁶ and 4 × 10⁻⁶ mL and yield 44 and 76 colonies. What is the pooled concentration?

  1. 2.05 × 10⁷ CFU/mL
  2. 2 × 10⁷ CFU/mL
  3. 6 × 10⁷ CFU/mL
  4. 1.2 × 10⁸ CFU/mL
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Pool counts and volumes: (44+76)/(6 × 10⁻⁶) = 2 × 10⁷. A is the unweighted average of separate estimates (2.2 and 1.9 × 10⁷). C and D divide by incorrect represented volumes.

C4. A hypothetical original volume of 0.005 mL is represented by a zero colony count. Which interpretation follows from ideal independent Poisson sampling?

  1. Original culture is proved sterile
  2. One-event scale is 200 CFU/mL; ideal 95% upper bound is about 600 CFU/mL
  3. One-event scale is 0.005 CFU/mL
  4. There is a 100% probability of detection at 200 CFU/mL
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 1/e = 200; −ln(0.05)/e ≈ 599. Zero is possible when units are rare. At 200 the expected count is one and detection probability is about 63%, not 100%. The bound concerns culturable units under stated recovery assumptions.

C5. Four independent valid plates have 100 colonies each. Under the ideal Poisson model, compare pooled counting relative standard deviation with that of a single plate.

  1. 10% versus 5%
  2. 5% versus 10%
  3. 25% versus 100%
  4. No change
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Pooled count 400 gives 1/√400 = 0.05. One count of 100 gives 0.10. The other values do not follow square-root scaling; a shared systematic bias is not corrected by pooling.

C6. A hypothetical OD calibration is C = (6 × 10⁸ cells/mL per OD unit) × OD within the validated range. A one-quarter dilution reads 0.20. Estimate the original cell density.

  1. 1.2 × 10⁸ cells/mL
  2. 3 × 10⁷ cells/mL
  3. 4.8 × 10⁸ cells/mL
  4. 6 × 10⁸ cells/mL
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Diluted estimate = 6 × 10⁸ × 0.20 = 1.2 × 10⁸. Divide by 0.25 to recover 4.8 × 10⁸. A gives diluted concentration; B applies dilution in the wrong direction; D ignores OD.

C7. An empirical relation is OD = 0.04 + 3 × 10⁻⁹ C, with C in cells/mL. OD = 0.19. Find C.

  1. 5 × 10⁷ cells/mL
  2. 6.33 × 10⁷ cells/mL
  3. 1.5 × 10⁸ cells/mL
  4. 0.15 cells/mL
Answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Subtract the intercept: 0.19 − 0.04 = 0.15. Divide by 3 × 10⁻⁹ to obtain 5 × 10⁷. B ignores the intercept; C uses a wrong slope; D leaves the optical signal unconverted.

C8. A corrected dry biomass of 0.048 g was collected from 80 mL. What is its concentration?

  1. 0.0006 g/L
  2. 0.6 g/L
  3. 6 g/L
  4. 60 g/L
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. 80 mL = 0.080 L; 0.048/0.080 = 0.6 g/L. A is the numerical value in g/mL mislabeled as g/L. C and D introduce extra powers of ten.

C9. Culture A has 0.3 g/L dry cell mass and 10⁹ cells/mL; culture B has 0.3 g/L and 5 × 10⁸ cells/mL. Assuming all retained material is cellular, compare mean cell masses.

  1. B has half A's mean mass
  2. B and A have equal mean mass
  3. B has twice A's mean mass
  4. Mean mass cannot be calculated even with these assumptions
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. At equal biomass concentration, mean mass is inversely proportional to cell concentration. Convert counts to cells/L: A = 10¹² and B = 5 × 10¹¹. A = 0.3 pg/cell, B = 0.6 pg/cell. Thus A and B are not equal; B is not smaller; the data suffice under the assumptions.

C10. A population has stable direct counts, twice the dry biomass and increased OD, while CFU remains stable. Which explanation is best supported?

  1. Net numerical population has doubled
  2. Average retained cellular mass may have increased without net numerical growth
  3. All cells are necessarily dead
  4. The OD calibration must be universal
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. If the dry material is cellular, stable N and doubled M imply doubled mean mass. The numerical count did not double; stable CFU is evidence of reproductive recovery; no universal calibration follows. A molecular cause requires more evidence.

C11. Direct counts are constant at 8 × 10⁷ cells/mL. CFU falls from 8 × 10⁷ to 2 × 10⁷. Microscopy now shows groups of four; each group is assumed entirely culturable. What follows?

  1. 75% of individuals died
  2. The expected CFU decrease can be explained by grouping without loss of individual reproductive capacity
  3. Dry biomass must quadruple
  4. The original cells multiplied fourfold
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The same 8 × 10⁷ individuals form 2 × 10⁷ independent four-cell groups, exactly the new CFU density. Death, extra mass and numerical multiplication are unnecessary explanations.

C12. An optical reading is unchanged after a treatment but CFU decreases by 99%. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. Treatment has no effect because OD is unchanged
  2. All cells are lysed
  3. Reproductive recovery is greatly reduced; persistence of scattering particles is compatible with the unchanged OD
  4. Exactly 99% of all individual cells are irreversibly dead under every condition
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. OD reflects optical attenuation and may remain high when nonculturable or dead cells persist. CFU loss establishes reduced recovery under the tested conditions, not an exact individual-cell death fraction or universal irreversible loss. Complete lysis is not demonstrated.

C13. A previously culture-negative suspension later produces colonies after a change in environment. What alternative must be addressed before claiming resuscitation of formerly nonculturable cells?

  1. The original colonies were visible to the unaided eye
  2. A small previously undetected culturable subpopulation may have multiplied
  3. The sample contained no particles
  4. The optical wavelength changed the definition of CFU
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. Outgrowth of rare survivors can produce the same endpoint as resuscitation. Earlier zero counts are not proof that no culturable units existed. The other options do not identify a competing causal explanation for later colony formation.

C14. A diluted culture gives OD 0.24 at fraction 1/4 and 0.12 at fraction 1/8; undiluted OD is 0.70. Both diluted measurements fall in a validated linear range. What is the best conclusion?

  1. The original measured OD must be 0.96 exactly
  2. The diluted estimates disagree by a factor of two
  3. The scaled linear-equivalent signals agree at 0.96; the undiluted signal may be nonlinear
  4. All cells must be viable
Answer and reasoning

Answer: C. 0.24 × 4 = 0.96 and 0.12 × 8 = 0.96. Their agreement supports the linear-equivalent estimate, not equality with the observed undiluted signal. OD does not establish viability.

C15. In the normalized time-course diagram in section 13, the total-cell curve remains at 100% while the CFU curve reaches 1%. If each curve initially represents 4 × 10⁷ units/mL, which inference is supported?

  1. Final total-cell count is 4 × 10⁵ cells/mL.
  2. Final CFU is 4 × 10⁵ CFU/mL; the recognizable-cell count remains 4 × 10⁷ cells/mL.
  3. Exactly 99% of all individual cells must have lysed.
  4. The optical density must have fallen to 1%.
Answer and reasoning

Answer: B. One percent of 4 × 10⁷ is 4 × 10⁵; the stable normalized total-cell curve corresponds to the initial total-cell concentration. A reverses the curves. C conflicts with retention of recognizable profiles and confuses loss of colony recovery with lysis. D predicts a different, unmeasured physical property.
