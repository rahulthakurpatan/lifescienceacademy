1. Why does a cell need a mitochondrion?

Imagine two living cells of similar size. One is an epithelial cell that is temporarily resting; the other is a heart muscle cell that must contract, relax and restore its ion gradients repeatedly. Both require a supply of chemical energy, but the second consumes it at a far higher sustained rate. Merely placing additional glucose in the cytoplasm will not solve every energy problem. The cell must have a means of releasing energy from nutrients in controlled steps and transferring a useful fraction into ATP, the immediate energy donor for most cellular work. Mitochondria provide a specialized physical setting for this conversion. Their contribution is not simply that they contain respiratory enzymes; rather, their architecture allows the reactions of respiration to be coupled across a membrane.

ATP is not a permanent energy reservoir. A cell continually hydrolyzes ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pᵢ), for example during motor activity, active transport and biosynthesis. To sustain these processes, ADP must be phosphorylated again. Some ATP is formed directly by reactions such as those of glycolysis in the cytosol. In aerobic cells, however, much of the ATP supply can arise from a different mechanism: electrons released during nutrient oxidation are transferred to an electron acceptor, and the energy of that transfer is used to establish a proton electrochemical gradient. ATP synthase then converts the energy stored in the gradient into chemical energy. A lipid bilayer with controlled permeability is indispensable to this mechanism.

The earlier study of peroxisomes provides a particularly useful comparison. Both organelles contain reactions that oxidize substrates, yet a peroxisome has no conventional respiratory electron-transport chain connected to ATP synthase. An oxidation reaction does not automatically mean that oxidative phosphorylation is taking place. To distinguish the two compartments, ask where the electrons go, what happens to oxygen, whether an energy-storing membrane gradient exists and how the oxidation products enter the rest of metabolism. The answer depends on the arrangement of the enzymes, not just their names.

Mitochondria also participate in metabolite synthesis, calcium regulation and the cellular response to damage. These activities are connected to energy conversion because metabolism and ion transport depend on the composition and electrical state of the matrix. In addition, mitochondria are not fixed objects distributed randomly through a cell. Their shape, location, number and internal membranes change as cells grow, differentiate or experience stress. A useful model of this organelle must therefore explain its spatial organization, its energy function, its partial genetic independence and the way it renews itself.

Learning objectives

By the end of the chapter, explain the topology of both mitochondrial membranes, locate each major respiratory and metabolic process, reason from a loss of membrane integrity or transport function, interpret basic respiratory and fractionation experiments, distinguish organelle genomes from nuclear control, and predict the effects of changes in mitochondrial fusion, fission or distribution.

2. From visible threads to energy-converting compartments

The first observations of mitochondria came from light microscopy of stained cells. Their name, introduced by Carl Benda in 1898, reflects their frequently thread-like and granular appearance. These observations established that the cytoplasm contained recognizable structures, but staining did not reveal their function. It was possible to associate the abundance of these structures with particular tissues without knowing whether they were responsible for energy production or merely happened to be present where metabolism was active.

A more powerful approach became available when cell homogenates could be separated by differential centrifugation. After gentle disruption of cells in a protective medium, large structures sediment at relatively low centrifugal force, and smaller structures require higher forces or longer periods. A fraction enriched in mitochondria could then be examined for both appearance and biochemical activity. Work on isolated mitochondria in the mid-twentieth century established that respiration and ATP generation could be studied outside the original cell, provided that the organelles retained their functional integrity. This was an important step from correlation to localization. It did not show that every component in a mitochondrial fraction belonged exclusively to mitochondria: contamination and membrane damage remain experimental possibilities.

Electron microscopy revealed the crucial structural distinction. The organelle is surrounded by two membranes, and the inner one forms elaborate folds. The classic work of George Palade and others in the 1950s demonstrated the internal membrane organization. There was an obvious functional question: why would an organelle devoted to metabolism invest so heavily in membrane surface? A soluble enzyme can be accommodated by increasing volume, whereas a membrane-bound process is constrained by available area. Repeated folding suggested that some of the reactions depended directly on a surface.

Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic proposal in 1961 connected the structure to a physical mechanism. Instead of assuming that a freely diffusible chemical intermediate necessarily linked respiration to phosphorylation, the proposal identified an electrochemical proton gradient across an intact membrane as the coupling intermediate. Experiments in which respiration and ATP formation became separated after dissipation of the gradient provided a way to discriminate between the models. Today this explains why a mitochondrial preparation may retain respiratory proteins yet fail to make ATP efficiently if the inner membrane becomes permeable to protons.

These discoveries illustrate a general principle of cell biology: images locate structures, biochemical assays identify activities, and controlled perturbations test causal mechanisms. A convincing identification of mitochondria uses complementary evidence. Neither a dark oval in a micrograph nor oxygen consumption in a mixed fraction alone establishes every aspect of mitochondrial function.

3. The double-membrane architecture and its compartments

In a textbook drawing, a mitochondrion often looks like a bean a few micrometres long. Real organelles can be small ellipsoids, long tubules, branched networks or interconnected structures. Their dimensions depend on the cell, its metabolic state and the balance of fission and fusion. A thin electron-microscope section through a single long tubule can even create several apparently independent profiles. For this reason, the number of profiles visible in a two-dimensional micrograph is not necessarily the number of independent mitochondria.

Four structural regions organize our understanding: the outer mitochondrial membrane, the inner mitochondrial membrane, the intermembrane space, and the matrix. The outer membrane forms the boundary against the cytosol. Inside it lies an aqueous compartment, the intermembrane space. The inner membrane encloses the matrix, a separate aqueous compartment containing enzymes, mitochondrial DNA and ribosomes. Where the inner membrane folds inward, it encloses an extension of the intermembrane space called the crista lumen. A crista is not a solid projection of matrix material. It is an infolding of a membrane, with the matrix surrounding its outer, matrix-facing surfaces.

The two membranes are neither interchangeable nor redundant. The outer membrane enables controlled exchange with the cytosol, while the inner membrane is the main permeability barrier required for energy transduction. Both are lipid bilayers, but their proteins, lipid composition and transport properties are different. The inner membrane itself includes an inner boundary region, which runs roughly parallel to the outer membrane, and the folded crista membrane. These are continuous parts of one membrane connected through crista junctions. They are not equivalent to the two membranes of the mitochondrial envelope.

This topology determines how molecules move. A cytosolic metabolite entering the matrix must cross the outer membrane and then the inner membrane. A proton moved out of the matrix by a respiratory complex reaches the intermembrane-space side, including the lumen of a crista, depending on the complex location. A matrix enzyme remains separated from a cytosolic substrate until the appropriate membrane transport steps have occurred. When reading diagrams, trace the complete route instead of treating all space inside the outer membrane as one compartment.

4. Outer membrane: controlled communication with the cytosol

The outer membrane contains channels of the voltage-dependent anion channel family, often called VDAC or mitochondrial porins. These channels allow many relatively small water-soluble metabolites and ions to pass between the cytosol and the intermembrane space. Thus the concentration of many small solutes in the intermembrane space can approach that in the cytosol. A channel does not turn the whole membrane into an unlimited opening: channel gating, charge and solute size matter. Proteins and large macromolecular assemblies require different pathways.

The distinction between the outer and inner membrane becomes apparent when ADP is supplied to an isolated mitochondrion. ADP may reach the intermembrane space through VDAC, but it is charged and cannot cross the inner lipid bilayer freely. A dedicated inner-membrane carrier must exchange ADP with ATP. Similarly, inorganic phosphate needs a transport mechanism to reach the matrix, and pyruvate needs its own carrier. The outer membrane therefore acts as a first level of communication, but selective entry into the matrix is controlled largely at the second membrane.

The outer membrane also carries protein import receptors and machinery, lipid-metabolic enzymes and proteins involved in the remodeling of mitochondrial shape. The TOM complex, named for the translocase of the outer membrane, recognizes and translocates many proteins synthesized on cytosolic ribosomes. A protein that is destined for the matrix cannot be expected to enter through VDAC simply because it is dissolved in water. The protein import system must recognize information in the precursor and coordinate passage through two membranes without destroying their integrity.

Selective damage to the outer membrane has a different immediate effect from damage to the inner membrane. For example, release of a soluble protein of the intermembrane space can occur while much of the inner membrane remains intact. Cytochrome c, a small electron carrier associated with the outer face of the inner membrane, may be lost under such conditions. Its loss can compromise the respiratory chain; its appearance outside mitochondria also has a signaling role in programmed cell death. The mechanisms of cell-death regulation are treated separately in Subunit 2.D. The important point here is that a protein's location determines what its release means.

5. Inner membrane: selective permeability and respiratory surface

The inner membrane is unusually rich in proteins, many of which are dedicated to electron transfer, proton translocation, ATP synthesis and solute exchange. Its lipid composition includes cardiolipin, a characteristic phospholipid with four acyl chains. Cardiolipin helps establish the physical environment of respiratory membrane proteins and supports the organization of several of their assemblies. It is not a respiratory enzyme and does not synthesize ATP by itself. Its presence is one illustration of the cooperative relationship between membrane lipids and membrane proteins.

For chemiosmotic coupling, the inner membrane must separate charge and maintain differences in proton activity. A bare lipid bilayer is poorly permeable to charged species, and the particular composition of the respiratory membrane supports a low passive proton permeability. This allows proton pumping to produce a potential difference instead of being immediately neutralized. The membrane is not absolutely impermeable. Controlled transporters carry nutrients, products and ions; a limited leak also exists. The physiological gradient reflects the balance between pumping, ATP synthesis, solute transport and leakage.

Respiratory complexes I, II, III and IV, mobile electron carriers, and ATP synthase are associated with the inner membrane. Complex II is unusual because it participates in both the citric acid cycle and electron transport. Its catalytic domain faces the matrix; it introduces electrons into the chain but is not a proton pump. ATP synthase spans the inner membrane with a catalytic head facing the matrix. The location of the catalytic head is critical: newly formed ATP is released to the matrix before export to the cytosol.

Some protein import occurs preferentially at contact regions between the membranes; respiratory machinery is enriched in crista membranes. These distributions are useful generalizations, not impermeable partitions between inner membrane domains. The lipid bilayer remains continuous. The distribution of proteins can change with the structure and metabolic state of the organelle. An experimental perturbation that alters membrane architecture may thus change energy function even without removing every respiratory protein.

This gives a practical interpretation of a common question: why are isolated inner-membrane vesicles used to study respiratory coupling? A sufficiently sealed vesicle can maintain a gradient and contain respiratory enzymes. Its orientation, however, must be established before deciding which side contains the catalytic sites, where protons accumulate and which substrates are accessible. A membrane fragment that is open to the medium may retain enzymatic activity without retaining coupled phosphorylation.

6. Cristae: why folding, shape and connectivity matter

Cristae solve a geometric problem. If a mitochondrial envelope enclosed only a smooth inner membrane, its respiratory surface would be limited by the organelle's size. Inward folds add membrane area while allowing the organelle to occupy approximately the same volume. More membrane can accommodate more electron-transport machinery and ATP synthase if appropriate protein density is maintained. In cells with high sustained oxidative demands, mitochondria often have a large crista membrane area. Cardiac myocytes are a familiar example. The relationship is functional rather than an absolute rule: mitochondrial size, protein composition, substrate availability and cellular work also matter.

Cristae vary in shape. Lamellar cristae are sheet-like, whereas tubular cristae are tube-like. Some steroid-producing cells possess abundant mitochondria with tubular or vesicular-looking cristae. Such cells perform mitochondrial steps in steroid synthesis as well as energy conversion. The geometry seen in a thin section depends partly on how the section cuts the structure. A circular profile may be a cross section through a tubule rather than a disconnected vesicle. The morphology must therefore be interpreted in the context of the three-dimensional organelle.

The narrow junctions connecting crista lumens to the peripheral intermembrane space are significant. They do not make cristae entirely separate membrane-bound organelles. Rather, they restrict the geometry through which molecules exchange and help establish characteristic membrane domains. Proteins that organize these junctions, including the MICOS complex, and the curvature associated with ATP synthase assemblies contribute to the organization of crista membranes. The GTPase OPA1 also participates in inner-membrane organization and fusion. It is sufficient for the present purpose to understand these as cooperating structural influences, not to memorize their individual subunits.

Membrane area should not be confused with proton-storage volume. A fold creates more membrane surface, but the ability to store an electrochemical gradient is primarily a matter of selective permeability, charge separation and proton activity. Increasing area can increase the capacity for pumping and phosphorylation, and can also increase passive leak if membrane quality changes. If a membrane becomes leaky, abundant cristae cannot by themselves rescue energy coupling. Structure contributes to function only when the underlying chemical and electrical conditions are favorable.

Consider two cells with mitochondria of the same apparent size. One has densely packed cristae with functional respiratory machinery, while the other has sparse cristae. At equal respiratory complex density per unit membrane area and with no limiting substrate, the first may have a greater maximum respiratory capacity. It does not necessarily consume oxygen faster at rest. If both cells have the same low ATP demand, the gradient can limit electron transfer. Distinguish a capacity from an actual flux whenever a question relates crista abundance to respiration.

Worked example — surface capacity

An idealized organelle has 24 µm² of inner-membrane area. Remodeling increases the area to 36 µm² while functional complex density remains 500 complexes per µm². The original count is 24 × 500 = 12,000; the remodeled count is 36 × 500 = 18,000. The potential capacity increases by (18,000 − 12,000)/12,000 × 100 = 50%. This calculation assumes uniform functional density and ignores substrate supply and membrane leakage. It does not establish a 50% increase in resting ATP production.

7. Matrix and intermembrane space: distinct chemical environments

The matrix is not an empty space within the cristae. It is a concentrated aqueous environment containing metabolic enzymes, cofactors, ions, nucleotides, mitochondrial nucleoids and ribosomes. The enzymes that oxidize pyruvate to acetyl-CoA are located here, as are most enzymes of the citric acid cycle and much of the machinery of mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation. In the citric acid cycle, carbon skeletons are oxidized and energy is transferred to electron carriers such as NADH and FAD-linked enzymes. These reducing equivalents supply the inner-membrane respiratory machinery. The exception to the general localization of the citric acid cycle is succinate dehydrogenase (complex II), which is membrane-bound and catalytically faces the matrix.

The matrix compartment can maintain an ion composition and pH different from the cytosol because its surrounding membrane is selectively permeable. During active respiration, the matrix is usually electrically negative relative to the intermembrane space and somewhat more alkaline. The precise values are not universal. They depend on tissue, substrates, transport rates, energetic state and how the potential is measured. A chemically favorable environment for one enzyme system is maintained through a dynamic balance of reaction and transport, not simply by the presence of a membrane.

The intermembrane space contains proteins with particular functions, including cytochrome c and enzymes such as adenylate kinase in many mitochondria. Cytochrome c transfers electrons between complexes III and IV by interacting with their intermembrane-space-facing domains. Because the outer membrane allows many small ions through, the peripheral intermembrane space exchanges readily with the surrounding cytosol for many metabolites. This does not imply that the crista lumen must be uniformly identical to cytosol at every instant: geometry and local reaction rates can produce local differences. The gradient that powers ATP synthase is across the inner membrane, not across the outer membrane.

A useful method for learning locations is to follow a carbon atom and an electron separately. Carbon from cytosolic pyruvate enters the matrix, is processed and may leave as carbon dioxide. Electrons released from its oxidation are transferred to respiratory carriers; they do not follow carbon dioxide out of the organelle. Oxygen accepts electrons at the respiratory chain, where water is formed. ATP is synthesized on the matrix side and is then exported. Keeping these three paths separate prevents the mistaken notion that oxygen is directly attached to glucose at the cristae to make ATP.

8. How architecture couples respiration to ATP production

We can now connect the structural compartments to the energy problem posed at the start. The respiratory chain is a set of membrane-associated electron carriers. The reactions release free energy as electrons move from suitable donors to oxygen, the terminal electron acceptor in conventional aerobic mitochondrial respiration. Rather than converting this energy directly into ATP in a single reaction, some respiratory complexes use it to transfer protons from the matrix to the other side of the inner membrane. The inner membrane's selective permeability preserves the resulting electrochemical difference.

Electrons entering through complex I normally come from matrix NADH. Electrons from succinate oxidation enter through complex II. Both paths can deliver electrons to ubiquinone, a lipid-soluble carrier in the inner membrane. From reduced ubiquinone, electrons pass to complex III, then to cytochrome c on the intermembrane-space side, and finally to complex IV, where oxygen is reduced to water. Complexes I, III and IV contribute to net outward proton translocation, whereas complex II does not. Accordingly, electrons that enter at complex II bypass one energy-conserving step. This is why their ATP yield is typically lower than that of electrons delivered through complex I, under otherwise comparable conditions.

The respiratory complexes are oriented. Proton removal and proton release occur on specified sides of the membrane. If the complexes were all mixed in a single aqueous compartment, electron transfer might still release energy, but the organelle could not maintain the same transmembrane gradient. Separation of the matrix from the intermembrane space is thus a physical requirement for storing respiratory energy. Pumping generates both a difference in proton concentration and a difference in electrical potential, since positively charged protons are displaced outward.

ATP synthase provides a controlled route for protons to move back down their electrochemical gradient. The membrane-embedded F₀ sector forms the proton-conducting rotary machinery, while the F₁ catalytic head projects into the matrix. Rotation changes conformations at catalytic sites and supports phosphorylation of ADP. The important organizational consequence is that both the respiratory pumps and ATP synthase operate across the same inner membrane, but their directions of proton movement are opposed. They form a coupled circuit rather than two unrelated pathways.

Oxygen consumption reports electron transfer to oxygen. ATP production reports useful phosphorylation. These outputs can vary independently when coupling changes. If protons leak across the inner membrane through another route, electron transfer may accelerate as the opposing gradient falls, but fewer of the returning protons pass through ATP synthase. Oxygen consumption and heat release can therefore increase without a corresponding increase in ATP synthesis. Conversely, blocking proton return through ATP synthase can cause the gradient to rise and restrain further pumping, slowing oxygen consumption.

The detailed chemical intermediates and phosphorylation stoichiometry are developed in Unit 1. Here the essential skill is to locate the donor reactions, the respiratory chain, the proton gradient, ATP synthesis and nucleotide exchange, and to predict what happens when one physical connection is interrupted.

9. The proton electrochemical gradient: a quantitative model

The expression proton-motive force describes the energy available when a proton moves across a membrane. To see why both pH and electrical potential are necessary, consider movement from the intermembrane space (i) into the matrix (m). For a solute of charge number z, the molar free-energy change for transfer equals the chemical contribution plus the electrical contribution. For a dilute solution, with activities approximated by concentrations, the expression is

ΔG = RT ln([X]ₘ/[X]ᵢ) + zF(ψₘ − ψᵢ).

Here R is 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is the absolute temperature, F is approximately 96,485 C mol⁻¹, and ψ is electrical potential in volts. Since one coulomb multiplied by one volt is one joule, the electrical term has the same units as the chemical term. For H⁺, z = +1. Define Δψ = ψₘ − ψᵢ and ΔpH = pHₘ − pHᵢ. The definition pH = −log₁₀(aH⁺) implies ln(aH⁺ₘ/aH⁺ᵢ) = −2.303 ΔpH. Substitution gives

ΔG(H⁺ inward) = FΔψ − 2.303RTΔpH.

This equation states exactly which movement we have chosen. If the matrix is negative, Δψ is negative, and positive charge moving inward is electrically favored. If the matrix is more alkaline, ΔpH is positive, and the chemical term is also negative. Both terms favor proton entry. Pumping a proton outward under those same conditions requires the opposite free-energy change. Dividing by F expresses the inward change as an equivalent voltage; some textbooks define the magnitude of the proton-motive force using the opposite sign. A clear direction convention is more important than memorizing a sign without a definition.

The equation applies to a single ionic species between specified compartments. It does not predict ATP production by itself. To obtain an ATP yield, one would additionally need the proton requirement of the synthase and the energetic costs of transporting substrates and products. Nor does the equation require large differences in the total concentration of all ions. A relatively small separation of electrical charge near the membrane surfaces can generate a significant potential while the bulk solutions remain approximately electroneutral.

The matrix is normally more negative, but this is a steady-state property sustained by respiration and dissipated by ATP synthesis, leakage and transport. Removing oxygen eventually prevents conventional respiratory electron flow from maintaining the gradient, although other mechanisms, including reversal of ATP synthase supplied with ATP, can transiently support membrane potential in some circumstances. A measured potential is therefore a strong indicator of energetic state, not conclusive proof of net ATP synthesis.

Worked example — free energy of inward proton transfer

At 310 K, suppose the matrix potential relative to the intermembrane space is −0.150 V, matrix pH = 7.8 and intermembrane-space pH = 7.2. Then ΔpH = 0.6. The electrical term is 96,485 × (−0.150) = −14,472.75 J/mol. The chemical term is −2.303 × 8.314 × 310 × 0.6 ≈ −3,561 J/mol. Total ΔG ≈ −18.03 kJ/mol of protons. The equivalent inward voltage is about −0.187 V. If an illustrative process uses three protons per ATP, the gradient could supply about 54.1 kJ/mol ATP before other energetic costs. The assumption of three is for this problem, not a universal mitochondrial stoichiometry.

A second limiting case

If ΔpH becomes zero while Δψ remains −0.150 V, inward proton movement still releases 14.47 kJ/mol. If Δψ becomes zero but ΔpH remains 0.6, it releases about 3.56 kJ/mol. Eliminating only one contribution does not necessarily remove the entire gradient. In real organelles the two contributions influence one another, so they cannot always be experimentally varied independently.

10. How metabolites and ATP cross the inner membrane

A strong gradient would be useless to the cell if mitochondria could not obtain the molecules required to make ATP or export the ATP after it was made. The outer membrane is relatively permeable to many small metabolites, but the inner membrane requires selective transport. This makes mitochondrial metabolism a coordinated system of reactions and membrane transport. Even a perfectly functional respiratory chain cannot sustain ATP synthesis when the matrix runs out of ADP or phosphate.

The mitochondrial ADP/ATP carrier, also known as adenine nucleotide translocase (ANT), exchanges matrix ATP for ADP on the intermembrane-space side. For the simplified charge states ATP⁴⁻ and ADP³⁻, exporting one ATP and importing one ADP transfers one net negative charge outward. This is favored by the negative matrix potential. Both nucleotides cannot simply diffuse through the inner bilayer because they are highly charged. Exchange couples the direction of nucleotide traffic to the electrical state of the organelle and the relative nucleotide activities. It does not directly hydrolyze a separate ATP molecule to move each nucleotide.

The phosphate carrier supplies inorganic phosphate required to form ATP. A common description is phosphate–proton cotransport or the equivalent phosphate–hydroxide exchange. Its operation therefore draws on the chemical part of the gradient. Pyruvate also enters via a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier, and numerous additional carriers exchange metabolites such as dicarboxylates. Long-chain fatty acids enter the matrix oxidation pathway through an appropriate transport system involving carnitine; the detailed reactions belong to Unit 1. The inner membrane is not a barrier that has to be removed before metabolism occurs; its controlled permeability is part of the metabolic mechanism.

Cytosolic NADH presents a different problem. It carries reducing equivalents from glycolysis but does not normally cross the inner mitochondrial membrane freely. Shuttle systems transfer its reducing equivalents through coupled reactions and metabolite movement. The malate–aspartate shuttle can produce matrix NADH, whereas the glycerol phosphate shuttle transfers electrons to the ubiquinone level via a mitochondrial enzyme. The individual steps are taught with metabolic pathways; the relevant structural lesson is that moving reducing power is not equivalent to moving a whole NADH molecule.

Imagine an inhibitor that prevents ADP from entering the matrix while leaving the respiratory complexes and ATP synthase intact. ATP production for cytosolic work falls as usable matrix ADP is depleted. Proton return through ATP synthase slows, the gradient may rise, and the respiratory chain can slow as a consequence. The inhibition is not at an electron-transfer complex, even though oxygen consumption may decrease. Interpretation requires a measurement of transport, membrane potential and respiration rather than a single assay.

11. What respiration experiments reveal about intact mitochondria

Isolated mitochondria permit a particularly informative experiment. Suspend a preparation in a suitable medium containing oxygen, respiratory substrate and inorganic phosphate. Monitor oxygen consumption continuously. When ADP is scarce, ATP synthase has limited substrate and proton return is relatively slow. The gradient rises and restrains respiratory pumping, so oxygen consumption is relatively low. Addition of ADP provides the synthase with substrate, increases proton return and permits faster electron transport. This rapid stimulated state is classically called state 3 respiration. After the added ADP has largely been converted to ATP, respiration returns to a slower state, classically state 4, in a suitable experimental design.

The ratio of state 3 to state 4 oxygen consumption is the respiratory control ratio, RCR. A well-coupled isolated preparation often has a relatively high ratio because ADP availability strongly affects the rate. A damaged or leaky inner membrane may have increased resting respiration and a reduced ratio, because the proton gradient is continually dissipated. A low ratio may also arise if the stimulated rate is poor because of substrate limitations or damage to respiratory complexes. An RCR is thus an indicator of the preparation's functional condition, not a uniquely specific diagnosis.

A second experiment distinguishes electron transport from ATP synthase function. Oligomycin inhibits the proton-conducting sector of ATP synthase. In a coupled preparation its addition reduces ATP formation and usually reduces respiration because the gradient builds up. A protonophore such as FCCP can then dissipate the gradient without reopening ATP synthase. Electron transfer and oxygen consumption may accelerate, but phosphorylation through the inhibited synthase remains low. This sequence demonstrates that the effect of oligomycin on respiration was largely mediated by the gradient rather than by direct inhibition of every respiratory complex.

Rotenone inhibits complex I. With a suitable substrate supplying electrons via complex II, respiration may continue through the remaining pathway, as long as downstream components and other required conditions are intact. Antimycin A acts at complex III and interrupts electron transfer from the ubiquinone pool toward cytochrome c; conventional complex I- or II-linked respiration is therefore strongly inhibited. These contrasts allow us to infer the position of a block. Interpretation must state which substrates are present, because a pathway with an alternative point of electron entry can respond differently to an inhibitor.

The same reasoning applies to physiological energy demand. During contraction, ATP is used and ADP becomes available. The increased demand can stimulate respiration through the coupling system, although cellular control also includes regulation of substrate delivery and enzymes. This is a demand-responsive system rather than a power supply that runs at maximum rate continuously. Measurements in isolated organelles are especially valuable because they simplify the environment; conclusions about actual tissue physiology must account for the complexity of intact cells.

Worked example — respiratory control ratio

Preparation A consumes 120 nmol O₂ min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ in state 3 and 24 in state 4. RCR = 120/24 = 5. Preparation B has rates of 96 and 48 under the same conditions; RCR = 2. Neither conclusion is unique without further measurements.

12. Metabolism beyond ATP: the mitochondrion as a biochemical hub

Mitochondria are sometimes described only as ATP factories, but the label misses an important function. The citric acid cycle connects the breakdown of carbohydrates, lipids and amino acids with the production of intermediates needed for biosynthesis. A rapidly growing cell does not always oxidize every carbon atom completely to carbon dioxide. It may withdraw carbon skeletons to form other molecules. For instance, citrate exported from mitochondria can contribute to the cytosolic acetyl-CoA pool used in lipid synthesis. The cycle must be replenished when intermediates are withdrawn, or its capacity to oxidize substrates will decline. This is why the mitochondrial metabolic role remains important even in cells that use substantial glycolysis.

Mitochondrial fatty-acid oxidation produces acetyl-CoA and reducing equivalents, particularly from substrates that enter the mitochondrial pathway. Peroxisomal β-oxidation is especially important for shortening certain very-long-chain fatty acids. Peroxisomes can deliver shortened products to mitochondria for further oxidation. The two organelles are complementary rather than duplicates. A defect in one compartment may place a burden on the other but does not imply that all enzymes of one pathway can relocate spontaneously to replace the other.

Certain specialized biosynthetic pathways also depend on the organelle. In animal cells, early and late steps of heme synthesis occur in mitochondria, while intermediate reactions take place in the cytosol. Mitochondria also participate in the assembly of iron–sulfur clusters, which are important cofactors of numerous enzymes including respiratory proteins. In steroid-producing cells, the conversion of cholesterol to pregnenolone occurs in mitochondria; subsequent steps involve mitochondria and the ER, depending on the steroid. The relevance to cellular organization is that membrane transport and contact between compartments connect these activities. Detailed biosynthetic chemistry is outside this chapter.

Brown adipose tissue provides an example in which energy conversion itself is adapted to a different purpose. Uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) permits regulated proton conductance through the inner membrane. The gradient is partly dissipated without ATP synthesis, allowing more of the energy from substrate oxidation to be released as heat. This is regulated thermogenesis, not simply an accidental rupture of mitochondrial membranes. The ability of the same basic architecture to support ATP production or heat generation shows the importance of controlling proton return.

Electron transfer can also produce reactive oxygen species when a small fraction of electrons is transferred prematurely to oxygen. Mitochondrial defenses include superoxide dismutase and peroxide-removing systems. Reactive oxygen species at controlled levels may participate in cellular signaling, while excess production or inadequate removal can damage lipids, proteins and nucleic acids. The effect depends on production, localization and removal, not simply on whether oxygen is present. Peroxisomal catalase is characteristic of the preceding node; mitochondrial antioxidant defenses should not be confused with a peroxisomal pathway merely because both handle products of oxygen chemistry.

Finally, mitochondria participate in programmed cell death through changes in membrane permeability and the release of proteins such as cytochrome c. That role is structurally understandable now: an intermembrane-space protein gains access to the cytosol when its normal boundary is compromised. The detailed apoptotic cascade belongs to the cell-cycle and cell-death chapter. The mitochondrial compartment is therefore a meeting point of energy metabolism, metabolite production, redox regulation and cellular signaling.

13. Calcium buffering and metabolic communication with the ER

Calcium ions are important intracellular signals. Their free concentration in the cytosol is normally maintained at a low level, and transient rises in cytosolic calcium can activate processes such as secretion and contraction. The ER serves as a major intracellular calcium store in many cells. Mitochondria do not replace the ER as the general-purpose store, but they can take up and release calcium and thereby affect the duration and spatial distribution of calcium signals.

The driving force for calcium entry into the matrix comes primarily from the negative electrical potential of the inner membrane. Calcium has charge number +2, so the electrical free-energy term for a given potential difference is twice that for a monovalent cation. The mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU) complex provides a regulated route across the inner membrane. The outer membrane's porins permit calcium access to the intermembrane space. Uptake is especially effective in local regions where cytosolic calcium becomes elevated, such as close to sites of calcium release from the ER. The existence of physical contacts between ER and mitochondrial membranes helps bring these locations near one another without requiring the membranes to fuse.

Once calcium enters, some binds to matrix molecules or participates in buffering reactions, so the amount of total calcium accumulated need not equal the increase in free matrix calcium concentration. Calcium can stimulate particular matrix dehydrogenases directly or indirectly, helping to match nutrient oxidation to cellular demand. In a contracting cell, a calcium rise can thus communicate that energy-consuming work is occurring. This is a feedback mechanism involving metabolism, not a claim that calcium itself provides the energy used to synthesize ATP.

Calcium must also leave the organelle. A mitochondrial sodium/calcium exchanger is an important efflux mechanism in many animal cells, and other pathways contribute in different contexts. A steady-state matrix calcium level reflects the balance between influx, binding and release. If uptake greatly exceeds removal for a prolonged period, excessive matrix calcium can promote dysfunction, including loss of membrane potential and increased permeability in stressed mitochondria. These outcomes depend on the physiological context and should not be treated as an inevitable consequence of any calcium entry.

A loss of the membrane potential can therefore have consequences even before every matrix enzyme becomes inactive. It reduces the electrical driving force for calcium entry, changes ATP/ADP exchange and can interfere with protein import. This explains why a mitochondrial perturbation can alter cytosolic calcium signals even when the plasma membrane calcium channels themselves are normal. The organelle is embedded in a network of interacting compartments.

Worked example — calcium electrochemical driving force

For transfer from intermembrane space into matrix, assume free Ca²⁺ activity in matrix is ten times that outside, Δψ = −0.150 V and T = 310 K. Chemical term = RT ln(10) ≈ +5.94 kJ/mol. Electrical term = 2F(−0.150) ≈ −28.95 kJ/mol. Total ≈ −23.01 kJ/mol. Entry remains energetically favorable in this illustrative condition despite the opposing concentration difference. Actual flux depends on channel gating, buffering and the local calcium activity.

14. Mitochondrial DNA, nucleoids and ribosomes

A remarkable feature of mitochondria is that they contain their own genetic system. DNA is located in the matrix in protein-associated structures called nucleoids. Many animal mitochondrial genomes are circular double-stranded DNA molecules, and there can be multiple genome copies within a cell, often distributed among multiple nucleoids. The familiar human mitochondrial genome is approximately 16.6 kilobases long. It encodes 37 genes: 13 protein-coding genes, 22 transfer RNA genes and two ribosomal RNA genes. The 13 proteins are subunits of the oxidative phosphorylation machinery. These figures apply to human mtDNA, not to all eukaryotic mitochondrial genomes.

The mitochondrial genetic system has its own transcription and translation machinery within the matrix. Mitoribosomes translate mitochondrial messenger RNAs, and the encoded hydrophobic respiratory membrane proteins can be inserted into the inner membrane. In mammals the mitoribosome is commonly described as a 55S particle, composed of 28S and 39S subunits. Sedimentation coefficients do not add arithmetically because they depend on particle shape, mass and interactions with the solvent. Mitochondrial ribosomes in other lineages can differ considerably in composition and sedimentation behavior. They are evolutionarily related to bacterial ribosomes, not universally identical to present-day bacterial 70S ribosomes.

Mitochondrial gene expression also differs from nuclear gene expression in certain details. For example, the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code assigns UGA to tryptophan rather than a termination signal. Genetic code differences are lineage-dependent. The enzymes that replicate, transcribe and process mitochondrial DNA and RNA are mostly nuclear-encoded and imported. Similarly, almost all of the organelle's ribosomal proteins are made from nuclear genes. The presence of DNA and ribosomes does not mean that a mitochondrion can synthesize all the proteins it needs.

Consider respiratory complex II in mammals. Its subunits are nuclear-encoded. In contrast, complex IV contains essential mitochondrial-encoded subunits together with nuclear-encoded ones. Thus a mutation in nuclear DNA can cause a mitochondrial respiratory defect, and so can a mutation in mtDNA. Failure to assemble a complex can arise from loss of one constituent even when the other genome expresses its own components normally. Functional organelles depend on coordination between two genetic systems.

Mitochondrial genomes vary across eukaryotes. Plant mtDNA can be much larger than mammalian mtDNA and occurs in complex molecular forms, including recombining molecules; its complete physical organization is not always represented by a single stable circle. Some protists have unusual mitochondrial genomes or reduced mitochondrial-related organelles. Many lineages require imported tRNAs or use distinct RNA processing mechanisms. For examination questions, identify the organism before using a particular genome size, shape, gene number or genetic code.

Multiple mitochondrial genome copies create the possibility of heteroplasmy, the coexistence of distinct mitochondrial DNA variants in one cell or organism. Homoplasmy refers to a uniform state at a specified variant or locus. During growth and division, the relative proportions of variants can change. Detailed pedigree patterns, segregation and threshold effects belong to the genetics unit; at this stage the important conclusion is that organelle copy number and mtDNA copy number are different quantities. One large connected mitochondrion may contain many nucleoids, and a newly divided fragment may receive different numbers of nucleoids.

Worked example — genome copy number

A cell has 240 mtDNA molecules and 60 separately resolved nucleoids. The average is four genome molecules per resolved nucleoid. This average does not demonstrate that every nucleoid contains exactly four, nor does it reveal the number of physically separate mitochondria. If 48 of the 240 copies contain a variant, the bulk variant fraction is 20%; the location of those copies among organelles remains unknown.

15. Biogenesis and protein import: why mitochondria are semi-autonomous

Most of the proteins needed to enlarge and maintain mitochondria are encoded in the nucleus. They are translated on cytosolic ribosomes and subsequently delivered to the correct mitochondrial compartment. This differs from the route by which many secretory proteins enter the ER. A matrix-directed mitochondrial precursor generally does not enter the lumen of the ER and travel to mitochondria in a conventional secretory vesicle. Instead, information in its amino acid sequence directs it to the mitochondrial protein import machinery.

Many matrix precursors contain an amino-terminal targeting sequence that forms an amphipathic helix with positively charged residues and hydrophobic residues arranged on different faces. Cytosolic chaperones help maintain many precursors in an import-competent, not fully folded state. Receptors of the TOM complex recognize appropriate signals and deliver the chain through the outer membrane. A matrix precursor then engages the TIM23 complex of the inner membrane. The negative electrical potential on the matrix side favors movement of its positively charged targeting sequence. Matrix Hsp70 and associated machinery use ATP to facilitate translocation and prevent backward movement of the incoming chain.

A matrix processing peptidase often removes the targeting sequence after entry. Chaperones then assist folding or assembly into functional complexes. Other classes of mitochondrial proteins have internal targeting information and use different routes. For example, many multi-pass inner-membrane carriers enter through TOM and use the TIM22 pathway; some inner-membrane proteins can be inserted from the matrix side by OXA1-related machinery. Outer-membrane β-barrel proteins are assembled through a distinct sorting pathway. The names help distinguish general destinations, but not every mitochondrial protein follows the same route or has a cleavable amino-terminal presequence.

Protein import establishes a causal link between energy and maintenance. A decrease in membrane potential may inhibit the entry of matrix-directed proteins that require it. Consequently, prolonged energetic dysfunction can also impair the replacement of enzymes and membrane components. A mitochondrion with intact DNA cannot solve this problem by translating all the missing nuclear-encoded proteins locally. It remains dependent on the cell and on the energetic state of its own membrane.

The logic of targeting sequences can be tested experimentally. Attach a candidate mitochondrial targeting sequence to a normally cytosolic reporter protein and examine its location. If the reporter enters mitochondria, the sequence may be sufficient to direct it there under the test conditions. Deleting the signal from its normal precursor and observing failed targeting supports necessity. In an import experiment with isolated mitochondria, a protease added externally should digest a precursor remaining outside, whereas a successfully imported matrix protein is protected by the membranes. Protease protection alone is not sufficient to distinguish matrix from intermembrane-space localization, because both can be protected while the outer membrane is intact. Selective outer-membrane permeabilization, followed by complete solubilization as a control, helps identify the destination.

This framework explains why mitochondrial biogenesis is not a simple form of independent bacterial multiplication. New membrane lipids and proteins must be supplied and assembled; mitochondrial DNA must be replicated and expressed; existing mitochondrial structures must grow and divide. The nucleus, cytosol, ER and mitochondria cooperate in maintaining the population. The term semi-autonomous captures this partial genetic and structural independence without suggesting that mitochondria are self-sufficient.

16. Endosymbiotic origin: what the evidence actually shows

Why does an organelle with so much dependence on the nucleus possess its own genetic system at all? The endosymbiotic model provides a coherent historical explanation. In a simplified account, a bacterial ancestor related to alphaproteobacteria established a long-term association with an ancestral host cell. Over evolutionary time, the association became obligatory, many genes were lost or transferred to the nuclear genome, and mechanisms for importing the encoded proteins back into the organelle evolved. The modern mitochondrion is therefore an integrated organelle rather than a free-living bacterium inside a cell.

Several independent lines of evidence support the model. Mitochondria possess a distinct genetic system and machinery evolutionarily related to bacterial translation. Their division by growth and fission recalls bacterial multiplication in a broad structural sense, although contemporary fission involves eukaryotic machinery. They possess two membranes and bacterial-related biochemical characteristics, including characteristic respiratory systems. Most decisively, comparisons of DNA and protein sequences place mitochondrial genes within bacterial evolutionary diversity, with a relationship to alphaproteobacteria. Each line has limitations, but together they are much stronger than a single superficial resemblance.

A double membrane alone does not prove an endosymbiotic origin: the nuclear envelope also has two membranes but has a different structural relationship to the ER and a different evolutionary history. Circular DNA alone is likewise insufficient because plasmids and other genetic elements may be circular. Sensitivity of mitochondrial translation to certain antibacterial drugs can support evolutionary relatedness but is not unique proof and varies with the drug and the organism. Sequence evidence and convergence of independent observations are more informative than memorizing a checklist of resemblances.

The precise evolutionary steps that generated the original mitochondrial partnership are not needed to understand current mitochondrial organization. Nor should each present-day mitochondrial membrane be assigned a simple historical identity without qualification. Membrane composition, protein assembly and host interactions have been extensively remodeled since the ancient association. For examination purposes, distinguish the established bacterial ancestry from speculative details about the original ecological relationship.

Gene transfer explains the division of labor between the nucleus and the organelle. When a gene product could be produced in the cytosol and imported successfully, nuclear encoding became possible. A small organellar genome remained in most mitochondrial lineages, preserving local synthesis of certain respiratory components. Why particular genes were retained is not reducible to a single universal explanation; local assembly and regulation of hydrophobic membrane proteins are among the relevant considerations. The semi-autonomous organelle thus reflects evolutionary integration rather than incomplete modern function.

17. Fission, fusion and the changing mitochondrial network

A mitochondrion is often drawn as a single oval because that is convenient for showing compartments. Living-cell microscopy reveals a more dynamic arrangement. Mitochondrial tubules move, join together and separate. In some cells a branched network extends through much of the cytoplasm; in others mitochondria remain as many relatively short structures. The pattern depends on the rates of membrane remodeling, the cytoskeleton, metabolic demands and cellular condition. Shape is a result of continuing processes, not necessarily a permanent category of organelle.

Fusion combines the membranes and contents of mitochondrial structures. In mammalian cells, mitofusins (MFN1 and MFN2) are dynamin-family GTPases involved in outer-membrane fusion. OPA1 participates in inner-membrane fusion and crista organization. These steps must be coordinated because fusing only the outer membranes does not establish continuity between the matrices. A complete fusion event permits mixing of many matrix components and exchange of some membrane components. It can help distribute resources and partially complement defects in a population. It does not guarantee the complete mixing of all mtDNA nucleoids or correction of every genetic defect.

Fission separates a mitochondrial structure into smaller units. In mammals, the dynamin-related GTPase DRP1 is recruited from the cytosol to sites on the outer membrane. It assembles into a constricting machinery, and membrane division results through coordinated events affecting both membranes. Contacts with the ER can help define division sites; other factors contribute to the remodeling process. The essential principle is that a double-membrane organelle must be divided without leaving both daughter structures open to the cytoplasm or losing the separation of their compartments.

Fission is useful during cell division, redistribution of mitochondria to narrow cellular regions and segregation of portions of a network. Fusion supports network continuity and the sharing of resources. Neither process is inherently good or bad. Excessive fragmentation may indicate stress or a failure of fusion, but fragmentation is also a normal physiological response in some circumstances. Conversely, extreme elongation can reflect failure of division rather than unusually healthy mitochondria. A static image is not enough to distinguish an increase in fission from a decrease in fusion.

The balance between these processes can be analyzed using time-lapse imaging. Fluorescent markers targeted to the mitochondrial matrix reveal the outline of the network, and a locally activated matrix marker can reveal content mixing after fusion. If inhibiting DRP1 produces elongated structures, the result is consistent with a reduction in fission; if deleting a mitofusin produces many disconnected structures, it is consistent with impaired fusion. Appropriate controls are needed because altered metabolism, expression of the marker or an effect on mitochondrial turnover may independently affect morphology. The rate of actual fusion or fission events provides stronger evidence than the endpoint shape alone.

The mitochondrial population also undergoes selective turnover. Damaged portions can sometimes be separated by fission and subsequently delivered to the lysosomal system for degradation through mitophagy. Fusion and fission contribute to this quality-control process, but neither automatically determines the fate of every individual organelle. The molecular regulation of autophagy and cell-death decisions is taught in Subunit 2.D. The structural lesson is that mitochondria can be remodeled and removed while new material is incorporated into the remaining population.

18. Organelle growth, distribution and inheritance during cell division

Mitochondrial biogenesis includes expansion of membranes, synthesis and import of proteins, replication of mtDNA and assembly of functional complexes. Fission increases the number of separate structures when it divides an existing structure, but it need not increase the total mass of mitochondrial material. Growth can increase mass without increasing the count of discrete objects. These variables must be distinguished when considering how a cell adapts to changing energy requirements.

As a cell prepares to divide, mitochondrial material and mitochondrial DNA must be distributed to daughter cells. This distribution does not follow a simple rule of attaching every mtDNA molecule to a mitotic spindle in the same manner as a nuclear chromosome. Mitochondrial positioning, fission, transport and cell geometry can influence the partition. Many organisms have specific mechanisms that regulate mitochondrial distribution. Multiple genome copies and organelle structures allow a daughter cell to receive a functional complement even though exact numerical equality is not guaranteed.

Mitochondria are usually inherited from the egg in mammals, a consequence of the relative gametic contributions and mechanisms that eliminate or exclude paternal mitochondria. Exceptions and organism-specific patterns exist, and maternal inheritance does not mean that all mitochondrial proteins are maternally encoded. Nuclear genes supplied by both parents govern most mitochondrial proteins. Also distinguish inheritance at fertilization from partitioning during ordinary mitotic cell division. Full extranuclear genetics is treated later in the course.

Position is particularly important in large or polarized cells. Mitochondria can move along cytoskeletal tracks and accumulate where they are needed. In neurons, transport along microtubules helps supply distal axons and synaptic regions. In muscle, arrangements near contractile structures and calcium-handling membranes enable the coordination of energy supply and demand. The role of motor proteins and microtubule polarity is developed in Nodes 2.B.09 and 2.B.10; here their relevance is the placement of mitochondrial function in three-dimensional space.

Consider a neuron with normal mitochondrial respiration measured in a homogenate but a defect in movement of mitochondria toward a distal region. The local region may have insufficient ATP or altered calcium buffering even when the total mitochondrial enzyme activity of the cell looks normal. A biochemical measurement of the whole cell cannot identify every spatial defect. A complementary imaging approach is necessary to test whether organelles actually reach the region where they are required.

Mitochondrial abundance, volume fraction, network length and respiratory capacity may therefore give different answers about the same cell. An increased count of fluorescent mitochondrial objects might reflect fragmentation, an increase in mass, or a change in the imaging threshold. Measuring total mitochondrial volume, respiratory capacity normalized to an appropriate quantity, and time-dependent morphology provides a more informative description than using a single measure of number.

Worked example — distinguishing partition from replication

A cell contains 100 arbitrary units of mitochondrial material. Ten fission events increase the count of resolvable structures from 20 to 30 without synthesis or degradation. The average material per structure falls from 5 to 3.33 units; total mass is unchanged. If daughter cells receive 55 and 45 units, the two add to 100. A 55:45 partition does not itself imply unequal replication of the nuclear genome or that either daughter received a precise proportion of every mtDNA variant.

19. Mitochondrial diversity and important biological exceptions

In most aerobic eukaryotic cells mitochondria are central to oxidative metabolism, but their abundance and organization differ. A mature mammalian erythrocyte has no mitochondria: it loses its organelles during differentiation and relies on glycolysis for ATP. This does not mean that every cell capable of glycolysis lacks mitochondria. Many cells use glycolysis and respiration concurrently. The mammalian erythrocyte is a specific differentiated exception; mature erythrocytes of many non-mammalian vertebrates retain organelles.

Some eukaryotes adapted to low-oxygen environments have mitochondrion-related organelles, such as hydrogenosomes and mitosomes. Hydrogenosomes can perform anaerobic energy metabolism in certain lineages, whereas mitosomes generally have highly reduced functions and do not produce ATP through conventional oxidative phosphorylation. Their existence demonstrates that descent from a mitochondrial ancestor is not synonymous with possession of a conventional oxygen-respiring organelle. Bacteria, by contrast, lack membrane-bounded mitochondria and can carry out respiratory electron transport using their own cell membranes or specialized membrane systems.

Plant cells possess mitochondria as well as plastids. Photosynthesis does not eliminate the need for respiration. A photosynthetic cell requires mitochondria for metabolism and ATP supply under a range of conditions, including darkness. In plant mitochondria, alternative oxidase can provide an electron-transfer route from the ubiquinone pool to oxygen that bypasses complexes III and IV. It does not conserve energy by pumping protons in the manner of those complexes; ATP yield from such electron flow is lower. Detailed regulation and the physiological roles of alternative respiration belong to Unit 6.

These examples sharpen the definition of an organelle. It is not identified only by one visible property, a particular ATP output or the presence of a single DNA molecule. Its evolutionary identity, molecular organization and functional context must be considered together. A cell that produces ATP without mitochondria is not a contradiction of chemiosmosis, since ATP can also be generated by substrate-level phosphorylation and bacterial respiratory membranes can support chemiosmotic energy conversion.

When comparing tissues, do not assume that higher mitochondrial number invariably means higher ATP synthesis at the instant of measurement. Heart muscle typically has abundant mitochondria because it performs sustained oxidative work; steroid-producing cells require mitochondria for both respiratory and specialized biosynthetic roles; brown fat intentionally dissipates part of its gradient for heat. The meaningful biological comparison is between the structure, the set of molecular functions and the demands of that cell.

20. Mitochondria, peroxisomes and chloroplasts: compare the topology

The peroxisome studied in the previous chapter confines oxidative reactions within a single membrane. Mitochondria and chloroplasts possess double envelopes and their own genetic systems, reflecting bacterial ancestry. However, the internal energy-transducing surfaces of the two are arranged differently. In mitochondria, cristae are continuations of the inner membrane, with their lumens communicating with the intermembrane space. In chloroplasts, the thylakoid membrane encloses a distinct lumen and is not simply an inward fold continuous with the inner envelope. The stroma surrounds the thylakoids and contains the chloroplast genetic system.

The direction of proton accumulation is analogous in function but different in compartment names. Respiratory electron transfer moves protons from the mitochondrial matrix to the intermembrane-space side; ATP synthase releases ATP toward the matrix as protons return. In chloroplasts, light-driven reactions promote accumulation of protons inside the thylakoid lumen; ATP synthase permits return toward the stroma, where ATP is produced. Both systems rely on a selectively permeable energy-transducing membrane, but their electron donors, terminal electron acceptors and energy inputs differ. The photosynthetic mechanisms are developed in the following node and plant physiology.

Mitochondrial oxidative metabolism generally consumes oxygen, whereas oxygenic photosynthesis can release oxygen by water oxidation. In a plant cell these reactions may occur at the same time in different organelles. Net oxygen exchange from a leaf is therefore not a direct measurement of mitochondrial respiration alone. This illustrates why organelle-specific measurements and correct compartment maps are necessary.

A student should now be able to identify an unfamiliar organelle from combined structural and functional clues. A single membrane, catalase activity and hydrogen peroxide-producing oxidation suggest a peroxisome. A double membrane, folds continuous with the inner membrane, a matrix and oxygen-dependent ATP synthesis suggest a mitochondrion. A double envelope with separate internal thylakoids and light-dependent electron transfer indicates a chloroplast. No individual feature is always sufficient; the pattern provides the strongest inference.

21. Recognizing mitochondria and interpreting experimental evidence

A thin section viewed by transmission electron microscopy can show the double envelope and cristae. If the internal folds are absent, a damaged organelle or a particular section plane may be responsible. Serial sections or three-dimensional imaging can clarify continuity. Conventional light microscopy usually cannot resolve the two closely spaced mitochondrial membranes. Fluorescence imaging instead uses labeled proteins or dyes to reveal location, distribution and changes in shape. A fluorescent image provides a particular kind of evidence, and its interpretation depends on the marker.

A genetically encoded matrix-targeted fluorescent protein can label mitochondrial morphology if its targeting and folding are validated. Potential-sensitive dyes accumulate differently depending on membrane potential and experimental conditions; a reduced signal may reflect depolarization, altered organelle mass, dye concentration or other factors. A second marker that reports mitochondrial material independently of potential can help distinguish a true loss of potential from a decrease in mitochondrial amount. It is also necessary to control dye loading, illumination and detector settings. The general reasoning is more important than specialized imaging protocols.

Differential centrifugation offers another route to organelle identification. An enriched mitochondrial fraction is expected to contain markers such as succinate dehydrogenase or cytochrome c oxidase activity. Catalase enrichment suggests peroxisomes, and acid hydrolases suggest lysosomal material. Because mitochondria, lysosomes and peroxisomes may sediment in overlapping fractions, enrichment is not the same as purity. Density-gradient separation can improve resolution by exploiting different buoyant densities. A reliable localization uses multiple markers and compares total recovered activity, specific activity and contamination.

Consider an illustrative fraction containing a high proportion of the recovered succinate dehydrogenase activity but also substantial catalase. The result is consistent with mitochondrial enrichment accompanied by peroxisomal contamination. If the fraction also consumes oxygen, we cannot assign all oxygen consumption automatically to mitochondria without testing coupling and appropriate respiratory inhibitors. Peroxisomal oxidases consume oxygen too. In an intact preparation, ADP stimulation and sensitivity to the mitochondrial coupling system give functional evidence in addition to enzyme markers.

Membrane fractionation and selective permeabilization allow finer localization. A protein accessible to an externally added protease in intact mitochondria may face the cytosol. An intermembrane-space protein may become accessible when only the outer membrane is opened. A matrix protein should remain protected until the inner membrane is also disrupted, provided that the protease is active and the protein is digestible. Controls using known markers and complete solubilization are essential. Otherwise resistance to protease could be mistaken for enclosure when it reflects an intrinsically resistant protein.

A useful examination strategy is to ask what variable an experiment directly measures and which hypothesis that variable can discriminate. Oxygen use measures electron transfer to oxygen under the specified conditions; membrane potential reports an electrical difference; ATP assays report phosphorylation or ATP abundance; electron microscopy shows structure. Together these measurements can distinguish defects in energy conversion, membrane integrity, protein targeting, organelle amount and distribution. One measurement rarely establishes every causal step.

Homogenate SDH specific activity = 200/100 = 2 U/mg. In the enriched fraction it is 120/10 = 12 U/mg, a six-fold enrichment. SDH recovery = 120/200 × 100 = 60%. 22. Integrating structure, function and dysfunction

The organelle can now be understood as a system of interacting constraints. Respiratory enzymes require substrate and electron acceptor; the inner membrane must maintain a gradient; ATP synthase requires ADP and phosphate; carriers must exchange metabolites; the mitochondrial genome and imported proteins must support assembly; and organelle shape and location must be appropriate for the demands of the cell. Defects in any of these conditions may reduce the ATP supply, but they do so through different immediate mechanisms.

Suppose an inner-membrane proton leak develops. ATP synthesis per oxygen consumed falls and resting respiration may increase if the chain has spare capacity. In contrast, inhibition of complex IV directly reduces conventional respiration and the ability to maintain the gradient. Inhibition of ATP synthase can initially raise the gradient while slowing electron transfer. Depletion of mtDNA can gradually impair complexes containing its gene products, while complex II can retain its nuclear-encoded subunits, although overall mitochondrial metabolism may be secondarily impaired. A defect in protein import can affect numerous proteins even with normal mitochondrial DNA. Each prediction follows from the structural model, not merely from the name of the affected component.

A cell may compensate for mitochondrial dysfunction by changing glycolytic flux, altering organelle turnover or adjusting metabolic demand. Whether compensation is sufficient depends on cell type and the particular defect. High-energy tissues are often particularly sensitive to respiratory defects, but not every clinical feature can be deduced from ATP concentration alone because mitochondria have other functions. The combination of redox changes, abnormal calcium handling, defective biosynthetic reactions and altered organelle distribution may also contribute to cellular dysfunction.

Three reasoning distinctions are worth retaining. First, an organelle's shape is not its capacity, and capacity is not its actual metabolic rate. Second, oxidation, oxygen consumption, proton pumping and ATP formation are related but distinguishable processes. Third, a genome and ribosomes provide genetic autonomy only in part; biogenesis still depends heavily on nuclear genes and protein import. These distinctions explain the most common conceptual mistakes encountered in questions about mitochondria.

The next chapter examines plastid diversity and chloroplast organization. The mitochondrial model provides a useful starting point: a distinct genetic system, selective envelope transport, growth and division, and a membrane gradient coupled to ATP synthase. The next conceptual advance is to recognize how a different internal membrane topology and a different energy source change the organization of these same general principles.

23. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Question 1

Where is the ATP-producing F₁ catalytic head located?

A. Cytosol; B. Matrix side of inner membrane; C. Crista lumen; D. Outer membrane

Answer and explanation

B. The head projects into the matrix, where ATP is released. A and D place synthesis at the wrong membrane; C confuses proton accumulation with the catalytic side.

Question 2

A crista lumen is topologically continuous with which space?

A. Matrix; B. Cytosol directly; C. Intermembrane space; D. ER lumen

Answer and explanation

C. It communicates through crista junctions with the intermembrane space. A is separated by the inner membrane, B by the outer membrane, and D belongs to another organelle.

Question 3

Which respiratory component does not pump protons?

A. I; B. II; C. III; D. IV

Answer and explanation

B. Complex II transfers electrons from succinate to Q but does not pump protons. I, III and IV contribute to net outward proton translocation.

Question 4

Which statement about human mtDNA is correct?

A. Encodes all respiratory subunits; B. Encodes 37 genes including 13 proteins; C. Encodes only ribosomal proteins; D. Always one copy per organelle

Answer and explanation

B. Human mtDNA encodes 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs and 2 rRNAs. A and C contradict the dependence on nuclear genes; D ignores variable genome copy number.

Question 5

A typical nuclear-encoded matrix precursor enters by which route?

A. ER–Golgi–lysosome; B. TOM–TIM23; C. VDAC alone; D. Nuclear pore

Answer and explanation

B. TOM and TIM23 coordinate crossing of the outer and inner membranes. A is secretory traffic, C is a small-solute channel rather than a protein import route, and D is nuclear transport.

Question 6

Which molecule is directly exchanged by ANT?

A. Cytosolic NADH for matrix NADH; B. Matrix ATP for external ADP; C. Calcium for ATP; D. Oxygen for carbon dioxide

Answer and explanation

B. The carrier exchanges adenine nucleotides. A confuses redox shuttles with direct NADH transport; C and D are unrelated to its substrate specificity.

Question 7

A mature mammalian erythrocyte obtains its ATP mainly through:

A. Oxidative phosphorylation; B. Chloroplast ATP synthase; C. Glycolysis; D. Peroxisomal ATP synthesis

Answer and explanation

C. Mature mammalian erythrocytes lack mitochondria. A is therefore unavailable, B is not an animal organelle, and D is not a conventional ATP-generating respiratory system.

Question 8

Which proteins are most directly associated with mammalian outer-membrane fusion?

A. MFN1/MFN2; B. DRP1; C. MCU; D. VDAC

Answer and explanation

A. Mitofusins promote outer-membrane fusion. DRP1 contributes to fission; MCU transports calcium; VDAC facilitates small-solute exchange.

Question 9

Assertion: a high rate of oxygen consumption does not guarantee high ATP production. Reason: an uncoupler can dissipate the gradient without ATP synthesis.

A. Both true and reason explains; B. Both true but unrelated; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false

Answer and explanation

A. Proton conductance through an alternate route removes the coupling intermediate while electron transfer may continue. The reason directly explains the assertion.

Question 10

Assertion: mtDNA proves that mitochondria are fully autonomous. Reason: most mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded.

A. Both true; B. Both false; C. Assertion true, reason false; D. Assertion false, reason true

Answer and explanation

D. The organelle possesses a limited genetic system but depends on nuclear expression and import. The reason identifies why autonomy is partial.

Question 11

Which evidence most directly establishes bacterial evolutionary ancestry?

A. Oval shape; B. Two membranes alone; C. Phylogenetic relationships of conserved genes; D. ATP production alone

Answer and explanation

C. Sequence comparisons provide evolutionary information. A and B are nonunique structural observations; D occurs in many biological systems and does not establish ancestry by itself.

Question 12

In a chloroplast, where is the proton-rich compartment during light-driven electron transport?

A. Stroma; B. Thylakoid lumen; C. Cytosol; D. Matrix

Answer and explanation

B. Protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen and return toward the stroma through ATP synthase. A is the ATP-producing side; C and D belong to other contexts.

24. Part C — Experimental and quantitative reasoning

Question 1

An isolated mitochondrial preparation receives NADH-generating substrate, ADP and phosphate. Oligomycin reduces O₂ consumption; FCCP added afterward restores O₂ consumption but ATP production remains low. What is supported?

A. ATP synthase transfers electrons to O₂; B. A gradient couples electron transfer to phosphorylation; C. FCCP repairs ATP synthase; D. Respiration does not need an electron acceptor

Answer and explanation

B. Oligomycin blocks proton return and raises opposing gradient; FCCP dissipates it, enabling electron transport without restoring synthase function. A and D confuse chain components; C is contradicted by the low ATP output.

Question 2

At 310 K, Δψ (matrix minus outside) = −0.120 V and ΔpH = +0.5. Find ΔG for inward proton transfer.

A. −8.6; B. −14.5; C. +14.5; D. −11.6 kJ/mol

Answer and explanation

B. Electrical term = −11.58 kJ/mol; chemical term = −2.303 × 8.314 × 310 × 0.5/1000 ≈ −2.97. Sum −14.55. A uses opposing signs, C reverses transfer direction, D omits pH.

Question 3

An isolated matrix-targeted reporter is protected from protease in intact mitochondria, remains protected after selective outer membrane opening, and is digested after detergent. Which localization is best supported?

A. Cytosolic face of outer membrane; B. Freely soluble intermembrane space; C. Matrix or inner-membrane protected matrix-facing domain; D. ER lumen

Answer and explanation

C. Both outer and inner membrane integrity initially shield the reporter. A would be accessible initially; B would become accessible after outer opening; D is not established by a mitochondrial assay. Further extraction distinguishes soluble matrix from membrane-associated localization.

Question 4

A fraction contains 8 mg protein and 96 units of SDH; homogenate contains 80 mg and 160 units. What are enrichment and recovery?

A. 6-fold, 60%; B. 12-fold, 60%; C. 6-fold, 10%; D. 2-fold, 96%

Answer and explanation

A. Specific activities are 12 and 2 U/mg, so enrichment = 6; recovery = 96/160 = 60%. B reports specific activity as enrichment, C confuses protein recovery, D uses unrelated raw values.

Question 5

Two mitochondria have identical volume and equal respiratory complex density. Crista membrane area is 15 and 45 µm². What can be inferred under saturating substrates and equal coupling?

A. The second may have three times the respiratory complex capacity; B. Resting ATP flux must triple; C. The second has three times matrix volume; D. The first cannot respire

Answer and explanation

A. Area ratio = 45/15 = 3. B is not established because demand can limit resting flux; C contradicts equal volume; D lacks support.

Question 6

Rotenone blocks respiration supported by a complex I-linked substrate. Addition of succinate restores oxygen consumption in a suitably prepared system. Which inference is valid?

A. Complex II bypasses I and feeds Q; B. Complex II bypasses IV; C. Rotenone activates ATP synthase; D. Oxygen is no longer needed

Answer and explanation

A. Succinate supplies electrons through II to the ubiquinone pool; III and IV remain necessary for the conventional downstream route. B, C and D misidentify the chain.

Question 7

Mitochondrial fluorescent object count doubles after inhibition of a fusion factor. Total mitochondrial protein remains unchanged. What is the most defensible conclusion?

A. Mitochondrial mass doubled; B. mtDNA must have doubled; C. Fragmentation can increase count without mass gain; D. Respiratory capacity must double

Answer and explanation

C. A balance shifted toward fragmentation can create more resolved objects. A is contradicted by protein data; B and D require independent measurements.

Question 8

Free Ca²⁺ in matrix is five times outside; Δψ = −0.100 V at 310 K. Approximate ΔG for inward Ca²⁺ transfer.

A. +15.2; B. −15.2; C. −9.65; D. −23.4 kJ/mol

Answer and explanation

B. Chemical term RT ln5 ≈ +4.15 kJ/mol. Electrical term 2F(−0.100) ≈ −19.30. Sum ≈ −15.15. A reverses the sign; C uses a monovalent electrical term alone; D treats chemical term as favorable.

Question 9

A mammalian cell loses mtDNA expression while nuclear expression remains initially normal. Which complex is entirely nuclear-encoded?

A. I; B. II; C. III; D. IV

Answer and explanation

B. Complex II contains nuclear-encoded subunits only in mammals. I, III and IV contain mitochondrially encoded subunits. Secondary damage may eventually affect II as well.

Question 10

The membrane potential collapses while TOM receptors remain intact. A matrix precursor with a positively charged targeting sequence accumulates outside the matrix. Which explanation fits?

A. Targeting no longer needs a signal; B. TIM23 import loses an important driving force; C. VDAC becomes ATP synthase; D. All protein synthesis must stop instantly

Answer and explanation

B. Negative matrix potential favors import of the positively charged presequence across the inner membrane. A, C and D do not follow from loss of potential.

Question 11

Oxygen consumption in state 3 is 180 and in state 4 is 30 nmol/min/mg. A new treatment changes them to 150 and 75. What changes?

A. RCR 6→2, weaker control; B. RCR 6→5, unchanged leak; C. RCR 3→2, proven mtDNA deletion; D. RCR 30→75

Answer and explanation

A. The ratios are 180/30 = 6 and 150/75 = 2. Higher resting consumption is compatible with leak but not diagnostic. B miscalculates; C asserts a specific genetic cause without evidence; D gives state 4 rates.

Question 12

A neuron shows normal respiration in an organelle homogenate, but its distal axon has reduced mitochondrial fluorescence and local ATP. Which test most directly addresses the proposed cause?

A. Measure whole-cell DNA only; B. Image mitochondrial movement along the axon; C. Count nuclear pores; D. Measure extracellular chloride alone

Answer and explanation

B. Spatial imaging can test defective transport or positioning, a plausible cause of local dysfunction despite normal bulk respiration. A, C and D do not directly address location.

Question 13

A mutant has normal mitochondrial mass marker but lower potential-dependent dye signal. Which conclusion is justified?

A. It contains no mitochondria; B. It may have decreased membrane potential, requiring calibrated controls; C. All respiratory proteins are absent; D. Its mtDNA is necessarily wild type

Answer and explanation

B. A potential-sensitive signal can reflect depolarization if mass and loading controls are appropriate. A is contradicted, C and D are not measured.

Question 14

A plant leaf releases net oxygen in bright light. Can one conclude that mitochondrial respiration has stopped?

A. Yes, chloroplast ATP replaces every mitochondrial role; B. No, photosynthetic oxygen release may exceed simultaneous respiratory consumption; C. Yes, all mitochondria convert to chloroplasts; D. No, only peroxisomes consume oxygen

Answer and explanation

B. Net exchange combines oxygen production and consumption. A and C ignore distinct organelle functions; D ignores mitochondrial respiration.

Question 15

A drug increases proton permeability and respiration but lowers ATP per O₂. Which change best explains the result?

A. The gradient is dissipated through a nonproductive path; B. Complex II becomes a stronger proton pump; C. Matrix ATP freely crosses every membrane; D. mtDNA copy number must increase

Answer and explanation

A. Leak bypasses ATP synthase, reducing coupling. B invents proton pumping at II; C is unsupported; D is not established by a rapid physiological response.

Question 16

A cell has 400 mtDNA copies, of which 120 carry variant X. Following division one daughter receives 150 copies, 60 variant X; assuming no replication or loss, what fraction carries X in the other daughter?

A. 24%; B. 30%; C. 40%; D. 60%

Answer and explanation

A. Other daughter receives 250 total and 60 variant copies, so 60/250 = 24%. B is parent fraction, C is first daughter's fraction, D is a copy count not a percentage.

Question 17

An inner-membrane vesicle is sealed but inverted. Which statement is correct?

A. The F₁ head can face the surrounding medium; B. Crista lumen always faces F₁; C. The membrane cannot retain any gradient solely because it is inverted; D. The vesicle becomes an ER compartment

Answer and explanation

A. Inverted topology exposes the normally matrix-facing F₁ head. B reverses the usual topology; C overlooks the capacity of sealed vesicles to form a gradient; D is unrelated.

Question 18

A mitochondrial inhibitor blocks ADP/ATP exchange but does not directly inhibit respiratory complexes. Which immediate sequence is plausible?

A. Matrix ADP availability declines, ATP synthase slows, respiratory pumping is restrained; B. Complex II instantly becomes a channel; C. ATP freely enters through the lipid bilayer; D. The outer membrane becomes the respiratory membrane

Answer and explanation

A. Impaired substrate supply slows phosphorylation and can raise backpressure from the gradient. B, C and D contradict the molecular organization.
