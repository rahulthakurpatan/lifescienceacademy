1. The biological problem: copying is not enough

A dividing cell has two distinct tasks. First it must reproduce the molecular information in its chromosomes. Then it must distribute the resulting DNA molecules so that neither daughter nucleus lacks an essential chromosome. The second task is not solved automatically by the first. Imagine that a diploid cell has two homologs of a particular chromosome, one inherited from each parent. After DNA replication, four DNA duplexes represent those two homologs. If all four travel to one daughter, the total DNA in the cell has been conserved, but the other daughter lacks the chromosome entirely. Faithful inheritance demands that the distribution be organized chromosome by chromosome.

Mitosis is the process of nuclear chromosome segregation that normally produces two sets of chromosomes equivalent in number and chromosome identity to the original nuclear set. The term comes from the thread-like appearance of chromosomes during division. In a four-stage naming system, prometaphase is included in late prophase; the five-stage model treats it separately because envelope breakdown and spindle capture mark a distinctive transition. Interphase is not a mitotic stage, and cytokinesis is a related but distinct process. A diploid cell generally produces two diploid nuclei; a haploid cell can also undergo mitosis and produce two haploid nuclei. Mitosis is therefore described as equational division, meaning chromosome-set number is maintained, not that the process always starts with two sets. It does not guarantee absolute DNA sequence identity: replication errors, mutation or chromosome segregation errors can introduce differences. It is a mechanism for faithful partitioning, not a guarantee that every division is error-free.

M phase normally includes mitosis and cytokinesis, the latter being the physical partitioning of cytoplasm. Their timing overlaps in many organisms, but they are conceptually separable. A nucleus may divide without an immediately corresponding division of the cell body. In a multinucleate cell, for instance, several nuclei can share a continuous cytoplasm. Conversely, physical constriction does not prove that chromosomes have segregated correctly. Throughout this chapter, distinguish a cell, a nucleus and a future daughter chromosome group. The count assigned to one of these compartments may not describe the others.

Growth from an embryo, replenishment of short-lived epithelial cells, root elongation and asexual reproduction in many eukaryotes all rely on controlled mitotic divisions. The value of mitosis is not merely an increase in cell number. A newly formed nucleus must retain the genes needed for metabolism, gene expression and future responses. Cell-type differences are usually created by differential gene expression, not by selectively discarding the chromosomes containing unused genes. The mitotic mechanism makes it possible for different descendant cells to possess largely the same hereditary information while expressing different subsets of it.

The five named stages of mitosis are useful because each solves a different physical problem. Prophase makes replicated chromosomes manageable; prometaphase establishes their connection to a bipolar transport system; metaphase organizes them for partition; anaphase sends sisters apart; telophase restores nuclei capable of interphase functions. These stages form a continuous sequence rather than a collection of independent events. Their meaning becomes clear when we track the same chromosomes from one stage to the next.

2. The material entering mitosis: chromosomes, sisters and DNA content

Before the first visible changes of mitosis, a cell has already passed through S phase. DNA synthesis has produced a second DNA double helix for each original nuclear chromosome. A duplicated chromosome consists of two sister chromatids, each containing one continuous double-stranded DNA molecule in the usual model of a linear nuclear chromosome. Although both sister chromatids contain a copy of the centromeric sequence, counting chromosomes by centromeres in standard examination problems means counting joined sister centromeric units before disjunction and independent centromeres afterward. The convention does not imply that the two replicated centromeric DNA sequences are chemically fused. Sister chromatids are associated by protein complexes called cohesins; they are not two strands of a single DNA double helix. A DNA double helix contains two antiparallel strands, whereas a pair of chromatids contains two double helices. This difference explains why separating chromatids does not require melting the DNA double helix.

Homologous chromosomes and sister chromatids represent two different relationships. Homologs in a diploid organism contain corresponding gene loci, but may carry different alleles. Sisters arise by replication of one particular homolog. For a locus with maternal allele A and paternal allele a, DNA replication yields an A-bearing sister pair and an a-bearing sister pair. Mitosis normally gives each daughter one A-bearing chromatid and one a-bearing chromatid. It does not send the two maternal sisters together to one daughter and paternal sisters to the other. Such a pattern would fail to preserve the original chromosome complement.

The centromere is a chromosomal region at which the kinetochore, a protein-based structure connecting a chromosome to spindle microtubules, assembles. Each sister possesses its own centromeric DNA and kinetochore. When sisters are joined, their centromeric regions are associated, and the pair is conventionally counted as one duplicated chromosome. After disjunction, each former chromatid is an independent daughter chromosome. Counting individual kinetochore spots before separation would therefore give twice the standard chromosome number, not the number of independently segregating chromosome units. The familiar X shape represents a duplicated, condensed chromosome; it is not the permanent shape of every chromosome, and not every metaphase chromosome has arms of equal length.

The symbol n denotes the number of chromosomes in a single haploid set; C denotes the amount of nuclear DNA in one unreplicated haploid set. A diploid G1 cell is 2n, 2C. After complete DNA replication it remains 2n but is 4C. For a species with 2n = 8, G1 contains eight chromosomes and eight double-stranded DNA molecules; G2 contains eight duplicated chromosomes and sixteen duplexes. The extra eight duplexes have not created extra homologous sets in the ordinary ploidy sense. They are copies temporarily linked to their corresponding originals. The C value measures DNA amount, not chromosome identity or gene dosage for an individual chromosome.

DNA replication alone is also not enough to prepare for movement. Replicated DNA molecules may be topologically intertwined. Topoisomerase II helps unlink duplexes by transiently passing one DNA duplex through another through a controlled break and resealing reaction. At the same time, sister association must be retained where it is needed. Prematurely removing every link would allow sisters to drift before the spindle has oriented them. Failure to resolve entanglements would hinder separation even if the normal cohesion release signal arrived. The chromosome must therefore enter division both individually organized and reversibly paired with its sister.

The earlier chapters on chromosome architecture and chromatin packing provide structural foundations. The following mitotic sequence is a transformation of these same physical objects, not the creation of new chromosomes by condensation.

3. How chromosome movements became observable

The events of mitosis were originally inferred from microscopic preparations rather than from molecular knowledge of DNA. During the nineteenth century, Walther Flemming examined stained dividing cells, including salamander material, and described characteristic thread-like chromosomal configurations. His observations established an ordered morphological account of division. A fixed preparation freezes cells at different moments. By comparing numerous cells from proliferating tissue, one can infer a plausible sequence: diffuse chromatin becomes distinct threads, chromosomes form an equatorial arrangement, two groups separate and new nuclei appear. However, the fixed image does not itself demonstrate that a given cell progressed through all these configurations.

This limitation matters experimentally. Suppose a researcher sees many cells containing condensed but scattered chromosomes and only a few cells showing chromosome separation. The observation may reflect a normally short anaphase and a longer preceding stage. It could equally reflect arrest at the stage with scattered chromosomes, perhaps caused by treatment or poor physiological conditions. Neither explanation can be selected simply by counting fixed cells. Comparing treated samples with a matched untreated preparation helps identify an abnormal accumulation, while recording the same living cell repeatedly establishes whether the configuration persists or progresses.

Live-cell microscopy offers a different kind of evidence. If chromatin is labeled with a fluorescent histone marker and spindle microtubules with fluorescent tubulin, the observer can follow two components independently. One can see a chromosome change location while its attached spindle fibers change length or orientation. A labeled nuclear-envelope component can mark when the boundary surrounding chromosomes becomes discontinuous and when it returns. Such imaging tests the temporal order of events without implying that the labeled protein necessarily causes every event correlated with its movement. A useful control is to check that labeled cells divide at approximately normal rates and produce viable daughter cells, since a marker or illumination can itself perturb division. In a three-channel time-lapse, for example, define the time of envelope breakdown as the first loss of a continuous nuclear boundary, the time of metaphase as completion of congression, and anaphase onset as the first sustained sister separation. If the boundary is lost at minute 0, the last chromosome aligns at minute 11 and the first sisters separate at minute 18, the observed prometaphase interval is about 11 minutes and metaphase lasts about 7 minutes under those operational definitions. These invented times illustrate how a sequence is measured; they are not biological constants. An experiment using intervals of five minutes between frames could not resolve these transitions with single-minute precision.

Modern observations reveal that metaphase is not motionless: chromosomes make small excursions around their average positions. Nor is mitosis a set of instantaneous switches between perfectly drawn stages. Different chromosomes within one cell may be captured by the spindle at different times. The names are assigned to whole-cell configurations that dominate during successive intervals. To stage a microscope image, use more than one criterion, such as chromosome condensation, nuclear boundary, chromosome arrangement and separation into groups. A single condensed object could represent a normal chromosome, a damaged fragment or an overlapping projection of multiple chromosomes.

An onion root tip contains a meristem, a region in which many cells proliferate. A stained squash of the tissue is a useful teaching preparation because different mitotic stages can occur in adjacent cells. Mammalian cell cultures and living embryos provide complementary observations of spindle and chromosome dynamics. The classical drawings and modern live images support the same general sequence, but do not require every species to dismantle its nuclear envelope or use centrioles in the same way. These differences will become important when comparing mitotic apparatuses.

4. Prophase: reorganizing chromosomes for transport

During interphase, chromatin must permit DNA replication, transcription and regulated access to genes. A long DNA molecule is organized into loops and nucleosomes but does not generally appear as a separate compact chromosome in a routine light micrograph. Moving such extended DNA through a crowded nucleus would be problematic. Long interwoven polymers can become entangled, and a moving attachment point could drag a fragile length of DNA behind it. The first major transformation of mitosis is therefore to convert the replicated chromatin into discrete, mechanically manageable chromosome units.

Prophase begins with progressive chromosome condensation. Condensin complexes contribute to higher-order folding and chromosome organization, including the formation of loop-based structures along a chromosome axis. The result is shorter, thicker chromatids with better-defined individual boundaries. Condensation does not produce DNA or change a chromosome's gene content. The DNA is still wrapped around histones; a change in the organization of chromatin makes the same molecules more compact. Topoisomerase II helps resolve linkages between neighboring DNA molecules, allowing sisters to become distinct structures while remaining connected where necessary.

It is useful to separate three processes that often overlap in time. Compaction reduces the physical extent of each chromatid. Individualization distinguishes one chromatid or chromosome from the surrounding chromosome mass. Cohesion retains the relationship between replicated sisters. A protein defect could affect one without eliminating the others. For example, loss of sufficient condensin function can yield poorly organized chromosome masses even if DNA replication was completed normally. Premature cohesion loss can produce apparently discrete chromosomes that nonetheless lack the paired organization needed for accurate segregation. A normal total DNA amount cannot distinguish these defects.

In typical vertebrate somatic cells, much cohesin along chromosome arms is removed during prophase, whereas a centromeric fraction is protected until anaphase. This permits the arms of sister chromatids to resolve while keeping the sister centromeres associated. The extent and timing of cohesin removal vary among eukaryotes; the general point is that chromosome preparation must leave enough cohesion to hold sisters together until their appropriate release. At the light microscope level the resolved arms of some duplicated chromosomes appear as the familiar X. Short arms, eccentric centromeres and different viewing angles can create other outlines.

The nucleolus, a structure involved in ribosomal RNA production and ribosome assembly, commonly becomes less apparent as mitosis proceeds. Transcription of many nuclear genes decreases during mitosis, though transcription is not universally and completely absent. A nucleolus may disassemble before the nuclear envelope disappears. Its loss is therefore a useful supporting morphological cue but not an infallible boundary between stages. In early prophase of a typical open-mitotic cell, condensed chromosomes can still be enclosed in a recognizable nucleus.

The cytoplasm is being reorganized at the same time. Microtubules are polymers of tubulin with distinct plus and minus ends. Interphase microtubule arrays contribute to transport and cell organization; during mitosis their organization and dynamics change to construct the spindle. In a conventional animal somatic cell, the two centrosomes that developed during the preceding cell cycle separate. A centrosome is a major microtubule-organizing center, usually containing a centriole pair surrounded by pericentriolar material that supports nucleation. Centrosomes are not chromosomes and centrioles are not centromeres. Their locations help define the two sides of the future bipolar spindle.

Mitotic kinase activity helps coordinate chromatin compaction and cytoskeletal remodeling. The detailed activation and inactivation of cyclin-dependent kinases are studied separately in cell-cycle regulation. For present purposes, their significance is that chromosome preparation and spindle organization do not occur as unrelated events. The cell must bring the mechanical components and the genetic cargo into an appropriate state at overlapping times. As a conceptual distinction, a spindle pole is a region where microtubules are organized toward one end of a bipolar spindle, not necessarily a centriole-containing organelle. Kinetochore microtubules provide links to chromosomes, interpolar microtubules interact with microtubules of the opposite half-spindle, and astral microtubules in many animal cells contact the cell periphery. This elementary map explains why pole formation can occur before all chromosomes are attached and why disappearance of asters alone does not prove loss of all spindle function. By the end of prophase in an open-mitotic cell, the chromosomes are much easier to distinguish, and the spindle poles have begun to establish their separation, but many kinetochores have not yet established the attachments needed for segregation.

5. Prometaphase: opening access to kinetochores

A bipolar spindle cannot reliably move the chromosomes unless it establishes a physical connection to them. In open mitosis, this access becomes possible when the nuclear envelope loses its integrity. The envelope is a double-membrane boundary containing nuclear pore complexes, and in animal cells its inner surface is supported by a protein meshwork called the nuclear lamina. At mitotic entry, phosphorylation of lamins and other nuclear-envelope and pore-associated proteins contributes to their reorganization and disassembly. The barrier between nucleoplasm and cytoplasm is lost, and spindle microtubules can gain access to chromosomes.

Nuclear-envelope breakdown is often used as the transition into prometaphase. This does not mean that the membranes are destroyed and must be synthesized again from nothing. Nuclear-envelope membranes are continuous with the endoplasmic reticulum, and mitotic remodeling redistributes membrane material and associated proteins. Their later reassembly will depend on reversing aspects of the earlier mitotic state. In some fungi the nuclear envelope remains substantially intact throughout division; that alternative is closed mitosis. Thus, envelope breakdown is a landmark of the common open-mitotic model, not the definition of nuclear division in all eukaryotes.

Spindle microtubules undergo dynamic instability, alternating between growth and shortening. Growing ends can encounter chromosome surfaces and the protein structures at centromeres. A kinetochore can initially associate with the side of a microtubule, after which its attachment may mature into an end-on connection. This provides a mechanical link between the chromosome and the dynamic polymer. Microtubules can also be generated and organized near chromosomes or by existing spindle microtubules; a simple picture of every fiber growing directly from an animal centrosome is not a universal mechanism. The central requirement is an appropriately oriented connection within a bipolar apparatus.

Each duplicated chromosome has two sister kinetochores. An appropriate bipolar attachment, termed amphitelic attachment or sister bi-orientation, connects one sister kinetochore toward one pole and the other toward the opposite pole. Since cohesion still links the sisters, the forces exerted in opposite directions stretch the joined system instead of separating it immediately. This geometry prepares a chromosome so that later release of sister cohesion yields two separate movements. The target is not merely to attach every chromosome somewhere; it is to connect the two sisters in ways that will send them to opposite destinations.

Several alternative configurations illustrate why this distinction matters. A monotelic chromosome has an attachment at only one sister kinetochore. The second sister has no microtubule connection in this simplified classification. A syntelic chromosome has both sisters connected toward the same pole. In a merotelic attachment a single kinetochore receives microtubules from both poles. These are not normal final arrangements for accurate sister segregation. Incorrect attachments are often destabilized and re-established; a merotelic kinetochore may remain attached and escape efficient checkpoint detection, later creating a lagging chromosome. Detailed correction and control are the subject of the next chapter; here the geometry is enough to predict the danger. This prediction has direct experimental support. Cimini and colleagues (2001) examined mammalian tissue-culture cells by confocal immunofluorescence, distinguishing chromosomal material, kinetochores and microtubule bundles. They observed individual lagging chromatids whose kinetochore was connected by bundles directed toward opposing poles. Both control cells and cells exposed to a spindle-disrupting treatment were examined; lagging was not simply a defining feature of the treated sample. The geometry provided evidence that one attached kinetochore can still have the wrong directionality. Not every anaphase lag is necessarily merotelic, however, and a fixed image does not establish the complete preceding attachment history of an individual chromosome.

During prometaphase, chromosomes move in complex ways as attachments form, detach and reorient. One chromosome may reach the central region early while another remains near a pole. A cell with a dispersed set of condensed chromosomes and no continuous nuclear boundary is therefore not necessarily defective. It may be passing through a normal stage of alignment. If it remains in that condition for an abnormally long interval, the possibility of defective spindle attachment or chromosome motility becomes more plausible. Time-resolved evidence distinguishes transient disorder from a persistent block.

Congression is the process by which chromosomes approach and organize around the spindle equator. It does not result from gravity or from attraction to a pre-existing central membrane. Motor proteins and interactions involving dynamic microtubules contribute to chromosome movements; forces acting on chromosome arms and on kinetochores help determine position. The subsequent equatorial organization emerges from this evolving force system. When most or all chromosomes have attained a stable arrangement, the cell becomes morphologically recognizable as metaphase.

6. Metaphase: a dynamic arrangement before separation

At metaphase, duplicated chromosomes are organized around a plane approximately midway between the two spindle poles. This arrangement is called the metaphase plate. The word plate can be misleading: it is not a membrane, a disc of solid material or a cell wall. It denotes the position occupied by the aligned chromosomes. A side view may show a narrow band, whereas a view along the spindle axis may display a roughly circular rosette of chromosome profiles. Both are compatible with the same three-dimensional configuration. A stage-identification question can exploit this difference between projection and structure.

The most useful positional landmark is the relationship of centromeric regions to the spindle equator. Chromosome arms may extend in various directions because individual chromosome shapes and lengths differ. One does not require all telomeres to lie in a perfectly flat plane. In a routine micrograph, chromosomes can overlap and may appear as a dark mass at the cell center. If the image provides a spindle orientation, the distinction from prophase is easier: prophase chromosomes may be condensed but lack a systematic equatorial arrangement, whereas metaphase chromosomes are aligned relative to two poles.

Metaphase is a mechanically balanced but active state. Microtubules continue to exchange subunits and chromosome positions can fluctuate. Attachment of sister kinetochores to opposite poles can generate tension resisted by cohesion. If a chromosome moves slightly to one side, the forces on it can change so that it returns toward the equator. The chromosome's position is therefore an outcome of coordinated microtubule dynamics and motor-associated movements, not evidence that it has been immobilized. Even a stationary average position is compatible with continuous molecular activity and turnover.

Alignment is also not sufficient by itself to guarantee that separation can begin safely. A chromosome might appear near the equator without having formed the appropriate attachments. The spindle assembly checkpoint delays anaphase while unattached kinetochores generate an inhibitory signal. Once appropriate attachment and checkpoint satisfaction permit the transition, the cell can release the remaining sister cohesion. This distinction explains why a drug that compromises spindle function often increases the number of prometaphase-like or metaphase-like cells: cells may enter mitosis but spend longer waiting or failing to complete it. The detailed checkpoint network is treated in 2.D.03 and 2.D.05.

A metaphase chromosome contains two chromatids. Its two sister kinetochores face generally opposite poles in the conventional mitotic configuration. Homologous chromosomes align as individual duplicated chromosomes; they do not normally pair as the bivalents of meiosis I. A cell with 2n = 6 should therefore have six duplicated chromosomes at metaphase, not three paired homologous units. The arrangement and sister orientation make metaphase an effective stage for visualizing the chromosome complement.

Clinical and experimental cytogenetics often uses metaphase spreads because chromosome condensation makes individual chromosomes comparatively easy to recognize, measure and classify. A spread separates chromosomes physically on a slide, so the original spindle geometry is no longer available. This is useful for counting and comparing chromosomal morphology but does not demonstrate that the same cell would have performed an accurate division. A karyotype is an organized representation of a chromosome complement, often based on condensed chromosomes, not a movie of their behavior.

Metaphase establishes a final starting geometry for anaphase. If the same sister pair were still held together, opposing spindle connections could exert force indefinitely without generating two independent daughter chromosomes. An irreversible transition that removes the appropriate linkage at the right time is therefore the logical next requirement.

7. Anaphase: sisters become independently moving chromosomes

Anaphase begins when the sister chromatids of replicated chromosomes disjoin. A sudden release of the remaining cohesion permits the opposing spindle connections to drive them apart. In the usual animal-cell model, the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome helps trigger degradation of securin, freeing separase to cleave a cohesin subunit. This explanation is sufficient to connect the molecular event with the visible change: the links resisting sister movement are removed, so each former sister can travel as an independent chromosome. Full regulatory details and the distinction between cohesin protection and cleavage are developed in 2.D.05.

This transition changes chromosome number under the standard counting convention. Before disjunction, a pair of sisters is counted as one duplicated chromosome. Immediately after disjunction, the pair has become two daughter chromosomes, each consisting of one chromatid with its own kinetochore. No new DNA has been synthesized during the transition. In a cell initially having 2n = 10, there are ten duplicated chromosomes at metaphase and twenty single-chromatid chromosomes in the entire undivided cell after all sisters separate. Each future daughter group receives ten. Total nuclear DNA material remains 4C across the two groups until the cell is physically partitioned.

In conventional descriptions, anaphase movement has two distinguishable components. During anaphase A, chromosome-to-pole distance decreases. During anaphase B, the distance between the spindle poles increases. Both increase the separation between the two chromosome groups, and they may overlap in time. The classification refers to measured motions rather than a rigid rule that one process always finishes before the other. Shortening of kinetochore microtubules and changes in the positions of spindle poles are among the mechanisms responsible; the next chapter examines the spindle apparatus in greater detail.

For a typical monocentric chromosome, the kinetochore is the site through which spindle attachment transmits force. The centromeric region often leads poleward, with chromosome arms trailing toward the equator. The resulting V-, J- or rod-like profiles depend in part on centromere position and arm lengths. A metacentric chromosome can appear V-shaped during anaphase; a more terminal centromere can produce a more rod-like appearance. These are useful observations but not rigid shape rules, especially in a projected micrograph. Holocentric chromosomes, with kinetochore activity distributed along much of their length, do not necessarily show the same single-leading-centromere appearance.

A well-executed anaphase yields two complementary groups, each containing one copy of every chromosome present at the preceding G1 stage. The fact that two groups contain similar masses of DNA does not prove perfect chromosome identity. Imagine one group receives both sisters of chromosome 3 and the other both sisters of chromosome 4. If their sizes are similar, total fluorescence can look balanced even though the chromosome complements differ. Chromosome-specific labeling, individual counts or later karyotype analysis would provide more informative evidence.

Anaphase may expose earlier failures. A chromosome with an unresolved merotelic attachment may lag between the groups. A DNA connection that persists between separating masses can be visible as an anaphase bridge; unresolved DNA intertwining or fused chromosomes are possible explanations. A lagging chromosome and a bridge are morphological observations, not a unique diagnosis of the molecular cause. An acentric fragment may also fail to segregate normally because it lacks a typical localized kinetochore. Some displaced chromosomal material can later be enclosed in a small separate nuclear structure, a micronucleus. Centromere-directed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), in which a labeled nucleic acid probe hybridizes to a complementary chromosomal sequence, can help distinguish a micronucleus containing centromeric material from one containing a fragment without that sequence. A positive probe signal does not necessarily prove that a complete chromosome is present, and a negative result requires adequate probe and detection controls. This example shows why chromosome identity and physical appearance are distinct forms of evidence.

A checkpoint delay before anaphase helps prevent many attachment-related failures, but does not eliminate every source of error. Once sister cohesion has been released, the cell cannot simply reset the original duplicated configuration by waiting. Accurate chromosome preparation, attachment and correction must therefore precede disjunction. The mechanical success of anaphase provides the condition necessary for reconstructing two independent daughter nuclei.

8. Telophase: rebuilding two functional nuclei

Segregation by itself leaves two condensed chromosome masses without the usual architecture of interphase nuclei in an open-mitotic cell. These masses must now be converted back into structures that support gene expression, RNA processing and regulated exchange of molecules with the surrounding cytoplasm. Telophase is the period in which nuclear reformation and chromosome decondensation become prominent. Its purpose is not to copy the genome again, but to restore nuclear organization around the genome sets that have already been separated.

As mitotic kinase activity falls, many proteins modified during entry into mitosis are dephosphorylated. Nuclear-envelope components associate with the daughter chromosome surfaces, membrane material reorganizes around them, and nuclear pore complexes are rebuilt. In animal cells the lamina also reassembles. The resulting envelope is not a fragment of plasma membrane pinched around the chromosomes. It is a renewed nuclear boundary involving membranes associated with the endoplasmic reticulum and nuclear-specific proteins. Re-establishment of regulated nuclear import supports recovery of nuclear functions.

Chromosome decondensation follows or overlaps nuclear reconstruction. Chromatid organization becomes less compact, making individual chromosomes difficult to distinguish by ordinary microscopy. The chromosomes have not disappeared; their DNA remains present in interphase chromatin. Nucleoli reform around active ribosomal RNA gene regions as the relevant transcription and assembly processes resume. Nucleolar appearance and restoration of a continuous nuclear outline are therefore useful signs of late mitosis, though the exact sequence and timing depend on cell type and imaging markers.

During telophase, the central spindle and other microtubule structures can contribute to organizing cytokinesis. In an animal cell a constricting cleavage furrow may already be visible, because cytokinesis can begin during anaphase. In a plant cell a cell plate begins to form in the region between daughter nuclei. These structures establish separate cytoplasmic compartments, but nuclear division and cytoplasmic division are still distinct events. A cell with two reformed nuclei and no complete partition can be in late M phase, or it can be a persistent binucleate cell if cytokinesis fails.

A useful thought experiment is to allow chromosome segregation and nuclear-envelope assembly but prevent the final division of the cell body. For an initially diploid cell with 2n = 12, two nuclei each contain twelve single-chromatid chromosomes and 2C DNA. The one binucleate cell contains twenty-four such chromosomes across both nuclei and 4C DNA. It is misleading to call each nucleus tetraploid. If the nuclei subsequently fuse, or if a later cell cycle produces a single enlarged chromosome complement, a different ploidy situation may arise. The correct description depends on the compartments being counted.

The end of telophase is not always sharply separated from the beginning of G1. If nuclear envelopes have reformed but some chromatin remains condensed, an observer might classify the cell as telophase rather than interphase. Conversely, an early interphase cell may retain remnants of the previous spindle or a temporary cytoplasmic bridge. Recognizing the dominant chromosome and nuclear configuration is more reliable than expecting an instantaneous boundary between two named stages. The restoration of two interphase-like nuclei completes the nuclear objective of mitosis.

9. One segregation principle, several mitotic architectures

Textbook animal-cell drawings commonly depict two centrosomes with star-like arrangements of microtubules, called asters. The spindle has a long axis joining the poles, and the chromosomes lie between them. This organization is helpful for understanding bipolar segregation, but a centrosome with centrioles is not an indispensable feature of mitosis across all eukaryotes. The conserved requirement is that the replicated chromosomes form connections to an apparatus that can distribute sisters into opposite groups.

Most flowering plant somatic cells lack the typical centriole-containing centrosomes found in animal cells. They nonetheless organize a bipolar mitotic spindle using dispersed microtubule nucleation and self-organization mechanisms. Microtubules may originate around the nuclear envelope or within the assembling spindle, and they become organized into opposing arrays. Such a spindle is often described as anastral because it lacks the conspicuous centrosomal asters typical of many animal cells. The chromosomes still condense, attach through kinetochores, align, disjoin and enter daughter nuclei. Thus, the absence of an animal-style centrosome does not imply the absence of a spindle.

Plant cells also have rigid cell walls, which constrain their shape and prevent division by simply drawing the plasma membrane inward in the same way as a typical animal cell. Their later cytokinetic mechanism uses a growing cell plate associated with a phragmoplast, a microtubule-rich structure that organizes delivery of membrane and wall material between daughter nuclei. In many higher plant somatic cells a preprophase band of cortical microtubules marks a future division plane before disappearing. It is not itself the spindle or the cell plate. Detailed mechanisms are reserved for the next node. For stage identification, a dark transverse cell plate between two reforming nuclei supports late mitosis/cytokinesis in plant tissue.

A different kind of variation concerns the nuclear boundary. In budding yeast, spindle pole bodies are associated with the nuclear envelope, and a spindle forms inside the nucleus while the nuclear membrane is retained. This is a common example of closed mitosis. Chromosomes can segregate through the action of an intranuclear spindle without the envelope breaking down in the manner characteristic of vertebrate somatic cells. Some eukaryotes use intermediate or partially open arrangements. Therefore, a retained nuclear envelope does not by itself prove that a cell is in interphase or early prophase unless the species and division architecture are known.

The nature of the chromosomes also changes the appearance of stages. Species with very small chromosomes may show condensed dots rather than long X profiles. Polyploid cells can undergo mitosis while retaining their higher ploidy. Haploid fungi can perform mitotic division without homologous chromosome pairs in the same nucleus. In these situations, mitosis remains equational because it preserves the starting set number in the normal outcome. Prokaryotic binary fission is not mitosis: it lacks the eukaryotic nucleus and the mitotic chromosome-spindle sequence described here, despite achieving the general goal of genetic partitioning.

The most reliable comparison therefore emphasizes mechanism rather than superficial resemblance. A condensed chromosome array followed by separation into two groups, with inheritance of one copy of each replicated chromosome by each group, identifies the central mitotic logic. Whether the spindle contains astral arrays, whether the envelope disassembles and whether cytokinesis uses a furrow or a plate are properties of the particular cell architecture.

10. Recognizing stages from unfamiliar images

In a micrograph, begin by locating the cell boundary and establishing what the stain or fluorescent marker reveals. A DNA stain shows chromosome position and condensation; it does not directly reveal microtubules or membrane continuity. An image showing only tubulin cannot prove that all chromosomes have separated. If both labels are available, one can relate chromosome groups to spindle poles. If a nuclear-envelope marker is absent, an invisible outline may be due to staining rather than biological disassembly.

A cell with an extensive diffuse chromatin region and a recognizable nucleolus is consistent with interphase, assuming the stain and tissue permit visualization. Thick threads appearing within one recognizable nuclear boundary indicate prophase. Condensed chromosomes without a continuous envelope, distributed irregularly and not yet arranged at the equator, suggest prometaphase in an open-mitotic cell. A compact central arrangement perpendicular to the pole-to-pole axis suggests metaphase. Two distinct groups of condensed single-chromatid chromosomes moving away from the equator suggest anaphase. Two groups that are becoming diffuse within re-forming nuclear boundaries suggest telophase.

This reasoning is stronger than memorizing five isolated sketches. Consider a round image with many chromosome bodies arranged in a circle. It might appear to lack the characteristic straight metaphase line, but the observer may be looking along the spindle axis. A different optical section or a tubulin label would show whether the chromosome array lies between the two poles. Similarly, a view along the direction of chromosome movement can superimpose the two anaphase groups. Three-dimensional imaging or a series of focal planes may be needed to distinguish them.

A single picture containing two chromosome groups is not automatically anaphase. The groups might already have re-established their nuclear envelopes and begun decondensing, in which case telophase is a better interpretation. If two separated cells are visible, each with an interphase nucleus, the previous mitosis may already have finished. In a root-tip preparation, adjacent cells can be in different stages because the tissue is asynchronous. The proximity of two nuclei in a field is insufficient to conclude that they arose from the same mitosis unless a shared cell boundary or time-lapse lineage supports that interpretation.

Mitotic chromosome preparations also need distinction from meiotic ones. A metaphase-I meiocyte can contain paired homologous chromosomes, called bivalents, rather than independent duplicated chromosomes. Sister chromatid separation in mitosis and meiosis II can produce superficially similar configurations; starting ploidy, cell context, preceding chromosome behavior and homolog pairing history help discriminate them. Do not identify meiosis simply because chromosomes are visible, nor mitosis because a cell contains two poles. The characteristic event must be understood in its life-cycle context.

The following schematic panels offer practice in recognizing configurations without relying on cell colors. They are drawings rather than photographs. Real images are noisier, often partly out of focus and not necessarily oriented as conveniently. In an examination, explicitly state the observable evidence and acknowledge any missing feature that would prevent a unique stage assignment.

11. Chromosome bookkeeping: derive counts instead of memorizing

Begin any counting problem by writing the starting chromosome number and the DNA amount in G1. Let N be the number of chromosomes in that starting nucleus, whether the nucleus is haploid, diploid or polyploid. Let D be its total nuclear DNA amount before replication. For a normal mitotic cycle with one completed S phase, replication produces two chromatids for each of N chromosomes, so the number of duplexes becomes 2N and the DNA amount becomes 2D. Chromosome number remains N until sister disjunction. For the remainder of this derivation, assume no chromosome gain, loss or fragmentation.

During prophase, prometaphase and metaphase the chromosome count is N, the count of chromatids is 2N, and the total nuclear chromosome DNA amount is 2D. During anaphase, after complete disjunction but before cell division, the chromosome count in the whole cell is 2N and there are 2N DNA duplexes. Each chromosome now consists of one chromatid. Some textbooks reserve the term chromatid for the members of a duplicated chromosome; under that wording, the former chromatids are now daughter chromosomes. When a question asks for chromatid number in anaphase, clarify the convention rather than claiming that DNA molecules have vanished. A count of single-chromatid chromosomes or DNA duplexes is unambiguous.

Each daughter group normally receives N chromosomes and half the total replicated DNA, D. In telophase these groups acquire separate nuclear envelopes. A completed cytokinesis leaves one nucleus in each of two cells. The whole-cell count changes because the system boundary changes, not because DNA is degraded to restore the G1 value. In a diploid example the whole anaphase cell temporarily contains 4n independent chromosomes and 4C DNA, but this is not evidence that it has acquired a stable tetraploid nucleus. Each future daughter chromosome group is 2n, 2C. An examination question may use the wording 4n for chromosome count in the undivided anaphase cell; always specify the counted compartment.

Worked example 1 — A diploid cell with 2n = 14. In G1, N = 14 and DNA content is 2C. After S phase, metaphase has 14 duplicated chromosomes, 28 chromatids and 4C. At anaphase the undivided cell has 28 daughter chromosomes and 28 DNA duplexes. Each pole receives 14 chromosomes and 2C. At telophase the two nuclei each have 14 chromosomes; after cytokinesis the two cells each have 14. The number of chromosomes in the two-cell system is 28, while the number per cell returns to 14.

Worked example 2 — A haploid cell with n = 5 and 3 pg of DNA in G1. Its G1 content is 1C = 3 pg. S phase raises the DNA content to 6 pg = 2C without altering the chromosome count of five. At metaphase the cell contains five duplicated chromosomes and ten chromatids. At anaphase there are ten separate chromosomes in the undivided cell. Each daughter receives five and 3 pg. The two nuclei are haploid, showing that neither diploidy nor homolog pairing is a prerequisite for mitosis.

Worked example 3 — A tetraploid cell with four sets of seven chromosomes. The haploid set number is n = 7, so its starting chromosome count is 4n = 28. If C is the haploid DNA amount, its G1 DNA content is 4C, not 2C. Replication produces 56 chromatids and 8C. Following anaphase disjunction, 56 daughter chromosomes exist in the whole cell; each of the two future nuclei receives 28 and 4C. The ordinary mitotic outcome is still tetraploid. Equational refers to preservation of the parent ploidy, not necessarily formation of diploid nuclei.

Worked example 4 — An error in one sister pair. An initially diploid cell has 2n = 8. At anaphase seven duplicated chromosomes segregate correctly, while both sisters of chromosome 2 go to the same side. Each side receives seven chromosomes from the correctly segregating pairs. The affected side gains two copies from chromosome 2 and thus has nine; the other gains zero and thus has seven. The combined count is still sixteen. They are aneuploid groups with 2n + 1 and 2n − 1, respectively. Their DNA contents need not be exactly 2C because the extra or missing chromosome has a particular size. No inference about exact DNA mass is possible without knowing that size.

Worked example 5 — A measured DNA amount. A diploid species has 2n = 20 and a G1 nuclear DNA content of 10 pg. At metaphase it has 20 duplicated chromosomes, 40 duplexes and 20 pg of nuclear DNA. Anaphase creates 40 independent chromosomes in one cell without changing the 20 pg total. Each normally segregated telophase nucleus has 20 chromosomes and 10 pg. A binucleate cell resulting from failed cytokinesis contains 20 pg total nuclear DNA, not 20 pg in each nucleus. The calculation assumes that chromosome DNA replication was complete and that DNA mass from mitochondria and plastids is excluded.

These examples reveal a simple conservation rule: in the absence of new synthesis, degradation or loss, the sum of the DNA amounts in the daughter groups equals the total DNA amount at metaphase. But conservation of DNA mass does not guarantee the correct allocation of chromosome identities. Always pair a count or fluorescence measurement with a structural or genetic description when the problem concerns segregation accuracy.

12. From a population snapshot to mitotic timing

The mitotic index is the fraction of observed cells classified as mitotic, often expressed as a percentage. To calculate it, count the cells in mitotic stages and divide by the total number of cells sampled, including interphase cells. If M is the number of mitotic cells and T the total number observed, mitotic index = 100 × M/T percent. Both quantities are counts, so their ratio is dimensionless. If the study specifically counts M phase including late cytokinesis rather than nuclear mitosis alone, the scoring definition should be stated. In a tissue preparation, consistently classify late telophase and dividing cell boundaries to avoid counting one division as two different cells.

For an approximately steady asynchronous population in a simplified teaching model, a fraction of cells occupying a stage can be used as a rough indicator of the fraction of time a typical cycling cell spends there. This interpretation requires representative sampling, similar cycle lengths, negligible cell loss or stage-specific arrest, and a suitable age distribution. A population growing exponentially is not exactly uniformly distributed in cell age because young cells are more numerous. A tissue with quiescent cells also violates the assumption that every counted cell passes through the entire cycle at the same rate. Therefore, converting snapshot fractions to durations is a model-based approximation, not a generally exact measurement.

Worked example 6 — Stage frequencies. A root-tip field contains 500 cells: 425 interphase, 32 prophase, 18 prometaphase, 12 metaphase, 6 anaphase and 7 telophase. The number in mitosis is 32 + 18 + 12 + 6 + 7 = 75. Mitotic index = 100 × 75/500 = 15%. Metaphase represents 12/500 = 2.4% of all sampled cells but 12/75 = 16% of mitotic cells. The first percentage refers to the entire sampled population, the second to the stage distribution conditional on being mitotic.

Under the simplified uniform-age assumption and a 20-hour cycle length, estimated mitotic duration is 0.15 × 20 hours = 3 hours; estimated metaphase duration is 0.024 × 20 = 0.48 hours = 28.8 minutes. Using 16% of 3 hours gives the same 0.48 hours. These are hypothetical model estimates, not actual measured onion root-tip timings. If only 300 of the 500 sampled cells are cycling, the observed mitotic index for all cells is still 15%, but among the cycling subset it would be 75/300 = 25%. The denominator changes the biological interpretation.

Worked example 7 — Measuring anaphase movement. In a hypothetical time-lapse, two opposite chromosome groups have centromeres 3 μm apart initially and 13 μm apart 5 minutes later. Their separation increased by 10 μm, giving mean separation rate 10/5 = 2 μm/min. If the motions are symmetric around a stationary midpoint, each group moves 5 μm relative to that midpoint, or 1 μm/min. The symmetry assumption is essential. Without pole positions, one cannot distinguish movement of chromosomes toward their poles from separation of the poles themselves. Tracking chromosome coordinates and both pole coordinates over time resolves the ambiguity.

DNA-content flow cytometry offers another population measurement. A DNA-binding fluorophore can yield fluorescence approximately proportional to nuclear DNA content under controlled staining and instrument conditions. In a cycling diploid population, a 2C peak includes G1 and can include G0; cells traversing S phase occupy a range between 2C and 4C; a 4C peak includes G2 and mitotic cells before cytokinesis. This last peak cannot be equated with metaphase. A standard DNA-content histogram uses fluorescence intensity on its horizontal axis and event frequency on its vertical axis; it does not plot the stages as a time series. A mitotic chromatin marker, such as phosphorylation of histone H3 at a mitosis-associated site, can support classification in combination with DNA content and morphology. It does not alone establish a precise five-stage assignment, because marker levels change through mitosis and can have other context-dependent signals.

If a study finds a larger 4C fraction after treatment, several hypotheses remain: G2 delay, prometaphase or metaphase arrest, or accumulation of cells with a higher DNA complement after failed division. Morphology, mitotic markers and time-resolved division data help distinguish them. Aggregates of two G1 cells can also mimic a 4C event in a flow measurement if doublets are not excluded. A properly interpreted histogram reports measured fluorescence distributions first; phase labels are biological inferences supported by additional information.

13. Predicting the effects of disrupted mitosis

Experimental perturbations are especially useful when they are connected to the ordered tasks of mitosis. A treatment that prevents spindle microtubules from forming interrupts the connection between replicated chromosomes and their destinations. Chromosome condensation and nuclear-envelope breakdown may still occur if upstream cell-cycle entry is intact, but alignment and separation become defective. Microtubule-destabilizing agents such as colchicine or nocodazole are commonly used to disrupt spindle organization; their precise effects depend on exposure and cell context. A population can accumulate condensed chromosomes without normal alignment. Calling every such cell metaphase-arrested is too imprecise if many chromosomes remain scattered.

A drug that excessively stabilizes microtubules, such as paclitaxel, can also impair mitosis even though microtubules remain abundant. The spindle requires controlled remodeling, not merely the presence of polymer. This comparison teaches a general principle: both excessive instability and insufficient dynamics can disrupt a process that depends on changing attachments and force balance. A tubulin image may distinguish a severely depleted spindle from an abnormal polymer-rich spindle, whereas DNA-content measurement alone may show 4C cells in both conditions.

Consider a distinct perturbation in which chromosomes condense and align normally, but separase cannot cleave its relevant cohesin target. The expected primary defect is failure of sister disjunction. An aligned array might persist, or later mitotic events could become abnormal if regulation bypasses the block. The prediction differs from spindle loss because the cells can reach the alignment stage. Conversely, loss of cohesion too early allows sisters to become independent before correct bipolar organization. A cell containing prematurely separated chromosomes may have a total DNA amount of 4C even if its chromosome count is abnormal for the apparent stage. Counting chromatids and analyzing attachment morphology are more informative than DNA mass alone.

A nuclear-envelope perturbation can be interpreted according to the species. If an open-mitotic animal cell fails to dismantle the barrier between chromosomes and most spindle microtubules, spindle access is impaired, which can disrupt chromosome capture. The same reasoning cannot be applied unchanged to closed-mitotic budding yeast, whose spindle operates within the nuclear compartment. An examination question may specify a retained envelope as evidence of a defect, but the proper conclusion depends on the architecture of the cell being tested.

A different experiment might inhibit physical partitioning after normal chromosome movements. If two daughter nuclei reform with equal chromosome complements but remain in one cytoplasm, the primary observed defect is cytokinesis rather than failure of mitosis. A later round of replication or division in such a cell can cause more complex outcomes, so the immediate phenotype should be distinguished from possible consequences in subsequent cycles. Nuclear and cellular compartments must again be specified when assigning ploidy and DNA amount.

For a convincing experiment, compare a treated group with a vehicle-treated or otherwise matched control, record several time points, and evaluate more than one readout. An increase in condensed 4C cells may indicate an extended mitotic interval rather than increased entry into mitosis. Observing actual division completion, chromosome movement and daughter survival can distinguish accumulation from successful proliferation. Removing a reversible treatment and examining whether cells resume the expected sequence can provide further evidence, although recovery alone does not identify the exact molecular target. The interpretation should remain proportional to the observations.

Errors of chromosome segregation can contribute to aneuploidy and genomic instability, consequences important in development and cancer. Not every abnormal mitosis leads to a persistent abnormal lineage: some cells arrest, die or fail to proliferate. A visible lagging chromosome does not prove that a tumor will develop. The relevant link is mechanistic: errors can alter the chromosome complement, altering gene dosage and cellular function, and subsequent selection or surveillance influences the fate of the affected cells.

14. Consolidating the sequence and correcting misconceptions

A useful way to reconstruct mitosis is to ask what would go wrong if the next event occurred too early. If sister chromatids separated before chromosome capture, correct allocation would be much harder. If kinetochores attached while replicated DNA was poorly individualized, transport could drag entangled chromosome masses. If daughter nuclear envelopes reformed around one unsplit chromosome mass, two independent nuclei could not acquire complete complements. The normal order coordinates preparation, connection, orientation, release and reconstruction.

Several apparent contradictions disappear when these tasks are separated. DNA doubles in S phase but chromosome number does not, because replication creates paired sisters. Chromosome number doubles in the undivided anaphase cell without DNA synthesis, because sister pairs become independent chromosomes. The DNA amount per daughter nucleus returns to its G1 value without DNA loss, because the total replicated DNA has been distributed into two compartments. The same logic applies to haploid, diploid and polyploid cells.

Chromosome appearance is also distinct from chromosome existence. Interphase chromosomes are generally difficult to identify as individual compact bodies, but the DNA molecules and chromosome identities persist. Condensation does not create new chromosomes, nor does decondensation destroy them. A centromere is not a centrosome, a kinetochore is not a centriole, and a chromatid is not one strand of a double helix. Confusing these words leads to wrong mechanistic predictions as well as wrong counts.

An equatorial arrangement is not a guarantee that every attachment is correct. A bipolar spindle is not necessarily centrosome-based. Nuclear-envelope breakdown is not universal. Mitosis does not require homolog synapsis or programmed crossing over, and it can occur in haploid cells. The distinction from meiosis becomes particularly clear in anaphase: mitosis normally separates sisters; meiosis I normally separates homologs while sister association is retained at centromeres. Detailed meiosis belongs to the later learning sequence.

Finally, distinguish a morphological observation from an inferred cause. A lagging chromosome is visible evidence of delayed segregation, not a direct assay of an individual regulatory protein. An increased mitotic index can reflect delayed exit rather than faster proliferation. A 4C fluorescence peak is not a specific metaphase marker. These distinctions are central to analytical examination questions because a good explanation should account for the data and identify what additional evidence could rule out competing interpretations.

With these principles, the next academic question is how the spindle, the molecular permission for cohesion release and the cytokinetic apparatus work together to produce a complete division. The next chapter develops that machinery while preserving the chronological picture established here.

15. Part B — Foundational concepts and assertion–reason

Original examination-style questions. Select the best answer before opening the explanation.

1. At which event does the count of independent chromosomes normally double in one undivided mitotic cell?

  1. DNA synthesis
  2. Chromosome condensation
  3. Sister disjunction
  4. Nuclear envelope breakdown
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Disjunction converts each paired unit into two independent chromosomes without DNA synthesis. S phase doubles duplexes, condensation changes packing, and envelope breakdown changes accessibility.

2. Which structure directly provides the chromosome–microtubule interface?

  1. Centrosome
  2. Kinetochore
  3. Nucleolus
  4. Telomere
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The kinetochore is a protein assembly at the centromeric region. A centrosome organizes microtubules, the nucleolus supports ribosome production, and a telomere protects an end.

3. A typical open-mitotic animal cell has condensed dispersed chromosomes and no continuous nuclear envelope. Which stage is most consistent?

  1. Early G1
  2. Early prophase
  3. Prometaphase
  4. Late telophase
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Prometaphase combines envelope breakdown with incomplete congression. G1 chromatin is dispersed; early prophase retains the envelope; telophase has separated groups and reformation.

4. Which statement about the metaphase plate is correct?

  1. It is a physical membrane
  2. It is an approximate equatorial arrangement
  3. It consists of paired homologous bivalents in normal mitosis
  4. It contains only unreplicated chromosomes
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The plate describes a position relative to poles. It is not a membrane; homolog pairing is not the normal mitotic arrangement; chromosomes remain duplicated.

5. What is expected in a normal haploid cell undergoing mitosis?

  1. Two haploid nuclei
  2. Two diploid nuclei
  3. Four haploid nuclei
  4. No segregation because homologs are absent
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Mitosis preserves starting ploidy. Two diploid nuclei would require altered chromosome complement; four nuclei are not the usual outcome; sisters provide the needed segregation partners.

6. Which observation best distinguishes telophase from early anaphase?

  1. The presence of replicated DNA somewhere in the cell
  2. The presence of two chromosome groups
  3. Reforming nuclear envelopes and decondensation
  4. The presence of microtubules
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Both stages can have two groups, replicated DNA in the original whole-cell sense, and microtubules. Nuclear reformation and decondensation distinguish telophase.

7. A flowering plant somatic cell lacks typical centriole-containing centrosomes. Which conclusion is justified?

  1. It cannot undergo mitosis
  2. It cannot have microtubules
  3. It must use bacterial binary fission
  4. It can organize a bipolar spindle by other mechanisms
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Plant cells organize microtubules without animal-style centrosomes. The other alternatives incorrectly equate one nucleation architecture with all spindle function.

8. Assertion: S phase doubles the nuclear DNA content. Reason: It generates paired sister DNA duplexes for each original chromosome.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Replication creates another duplex for each chromosome; DNA amount doubles. The reason is causal. Chromosome number remains unchanged until disjunction.

9. Assertion: Nuclear-envelope breakdown is indispensable for every eukaryotic mitosis. Reason: Some fungi form a spindle within a retained nucleus.

  1. Both true; reason explains assertion
  2. Both true; reason does not explain
  3. Assertion true; reason false
  4. Assertion false; reason true
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: D. Closed mitosis in fungi disproves the universal claim. The reason correctly gives a counterexample.

10. Which event is not normally part of mitotic chromosome behavior?

  1. Chromosome condensation
  2. Sister kinetochore bi-orientation
  3. Homolog synapsis into meiotic bivalents
  4. Daughter nuclear reformation
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Synapsis into bivalents belongs to meiosis I. Condensation, sister orientation and nuclear reconstruction are characteristic events of the usual open-mitotic sequence.

16. Part C — Mechanistic, quantitative and experimental reasoning

Original examination-style questions. Select the best answer before opening the explanation.

1. A diploid species has 2n = 18 and 6 pg nuclear DNA in G1. A cell has just completed sister disjunction, before cytokinesis. Which whole-cell count is correct?

  1. 18 chromosomes; 12 pg
  2. 36 chromosomes; 6 pg
  3. 36 chromosomes; 12 pg
  4. 18 chromosomes; 6 pg
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. S phase produced 36 duplexes and 12 pg. Disjunction makes 36 independent chromosomes in one cell, and DNA amount remains 12 pg. 18 chromosomes and 6 pg describe one normal daughter group; 18 and 12 pg describe metaphase, not completed disjunction.

2. A haploid yeast cell has n = 8. At metaphase, how many chromosomes and duplexes are present?

  1. 8 and 8
  2. 16 and 16
  3. 8 and 16
  4. 16 and 8
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Replication doubles duplex number to 16 but sisters remain paired as eight duplicated chromosomes. 8 and 8 is G1; 16 and 16 is the whole cell after disjunction; 16 and 8 cannot describe the normal configuration.

3. A diploid cell with 2n = 10 undergoes correct segregation, but cytokinesis fails. What is the immediate outcome after nuclear reformation?

  1. One 4n nucleus
  2. Two nuclei each 2n, 2C
  3. Two nuclei each 4n, 4C
  4. One 2n nucleus with 4C
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The two groups acquire separate envelopes, each with ten chromosomes and 2C. The combined cell has 4C, but this does not make either nucleus 4n. A single nucleus would require an additional event or failed nuclear separation.

4. In a cell with 2n = 12, both sisters of one chromosome move to the same pole; all other pairs segregate normally. What are the group counts?

  1. 12 and 12
  2. 14 and 10
  3. 13 and 11
  4. 13 and 12
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Eleven correct pairs provide eleven chromosomes to each side. The faulty pair adds two to one group and none to the other: 13 and 11, total 24. 14/10 requires a different error; 13/12 violates the conserved count.

5. A treatment raises the 4C flow-cytometry peak. Which additional finding most directly supports prometaphase accumulation rather than G2 arrest?

  1. Increased 4C DNA fluorescence alone
  2. Condensed scattered chromosomes, absent continuous envelope, mitotic spindle
  3. An intact interphase nucleus with diffuse chromatin
  4. A reduced number of mitochondria
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The joint morphology places cells after open-mitotic entry but before alignment. DNA content alone is shared by G2 and M; diffuse chromatin with intact nucleus supports G2; mitochondrial number is not a specific criterion.

6. One sister kinetochore is attached to both spindle poles while its partner is attached to one pole. Which interpretation is best?

  1. Normal amphitelic attachment
  2. Merotelic attachment that can cause lagging
  3. Normal homolog pairing
  4. Syntelic attachment necessarily involving both sisters toward one pole only
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. A single kinetochore attached toward both poles is merotelic. Amphitelic involves opposite attachments of the two sisters; homolog pairing is irrelevant; syntelic connects both sisters toward the same pole.

7. A cell is recorded with a normal equatorial array for an unusually long time after a cohesion-cleavage defect. Which process is most directly impaired?

  1. S phase initiation
  2. Kinetochore construction by definition
  3. Sister disjunction
  4. Nuclear DNA replication during metaphase
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. The cell reached alignment but cannot release the links needed for independent chromosome movement. S phase has already finished, kinetochore formation is not necessarily blocked, and replication is not normally part of metaphase.

8. A root sample contains 360 cells, of which 24 are prophase, 12 metaphase, 6 anaphase and 12 telophase (prometaphase scored with prophase). Which mitotic index and metaphase fraction of mitotic cells are correct?

  1. 15%; 22.2%
  2. 15%; 3.33%
  3. 54%; 22.2%
  4. 12%; 15%
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. M = 24 + 12 + 6 + 12 = 54. Index = 54/360 × 100 = 15%; metaphase conditional fraction = 12/54 × 100 ≈ 22.2%. 3.33% uses all cells; 54 is a count, not percent; 12 is the metaphase count.

9. Hypothetical measurements: pole separation stays 10 μm; distance of a chromosome to its attached pole decreases from 4 to 1 μm in 3 min. What is supported?

  1. Anaphase B alone
  2. A mean chromosome-to-pole movement of 1 μm/min, consistent with anaphase A
  3. DNA duplication at 1 μm/min
  4. A pole-separation rate of 3 μm/min
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: B. The measured chromosome-to-pole change is 3 μm in 3 min = 1 μm/min. The constant pole separation does not support B in that interval; distance is not DNA synthesis; the pole-separation rate is zero.

10. Two daughter groups display equal total DNA fluorescence after division. Which conclusion follows without additional evidence?

  1. All chromosome identities are correctly distributed
  2. Both groups must be diploid
  3. They have approximately similar DNA amounts under the measurement assumptions
  4. Every kinetochore attached properly
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Mass measurements do not resolve chromosome identities, starting ploidy or attachment history. Equal amounts support only similar measured DNA content; chromosome-specific labeling is needed to test identities.

11. A DNA bridge persists between daughter chromosome groups. Which interpretation is most defensible?

  1. All DNA replication was absent
  2. It proves one exact cohesin mutation
  3. It suggests a persistent chromosomal linkage; several causes remain possible
  4. It proves the groups are in G1
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: C. Anaphase bridges can reflect persistent DNA connections from different causes. Morphology alone does not identify a single mutation or prove replication failure; separated moving groups are not evidence of G1.

12. A population has more mitotic cells after treatment, but live imaging shows fewer completed divisions per hour. What best reconciles the findings?

  1. The treatment lengthens mitotic occupancy or causes arrest
  2. Mitotic index necessarily equals proliferation rate
  3. All cells have shorter mitosis
  4. DNA stains directly count completed divisions
Show answer and reasoning

Answer: A. Longer residence increases the observed fraction even if exit and division completion decline. Index is a snapshot, not a direct division rate; shorter mitosis alone does not explain accumulation; DNA staining does not record completed events.
