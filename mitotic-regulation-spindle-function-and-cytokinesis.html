1. Two separations, one division

A dividing cell must accomplish two physically different tasks. It must distribute its duplicated chromosomes so that each future nucleus receives a complete genome, and it must divide the surrounding cytoplasm so that the nuclei occupy separate cells. The first task is mitosis, or nuclear division; the second is cytokinesis. Two accurate daughter nuclei in a single cytoplasm do not constitute two daughter cells, and an accurately positioned division furrow cannot compensate for an incorrectly distributed chromosome. In most animal somatic cells the two processes overlap because the furrow starts during anaphase, before nuclear division has finished.

Consider a diploid cell whose DNA has already been replicated. Each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids, copies held together by a protein complex called cohesin. The centromere is a specialized chromosomal region; the kinetochore is the protein assembly formed upon it that provides an interface with microtubules. Each sister chromatid has its own kinetochore. The chromatid pair is consequently a mechanical object with two attachment sites and a temporary connection between them. The cell must establish a connection from each sister to a different end of the spindle, then release the sister linkage at a controlled time. Removing cohesion before proper attachment would turn one manageable pair into two independent objects that can be distributed incorrectly.

The physical problem is inseparable from the regulatory problem. Microtubules must be dynamic enough to explore the space and find chromosomes, yet a kinetochore connection must withstand forces without falling apart. An enzyme must remove cohesion, but its activity must be delayed until attachment is adequate. Finally, the spindle must help tell the cell surface where to divide. These requirements explain why the mitotic spindle is simultaneously a dynamic mechanical assembly and a signaling platform. Review chromosome architecture and molecular motors as prerequisites. We consider primarily conventional bipolar mitosis of monocentric chromosomes in a vertebrate somatic cell and then contrast plant division.

2. The bipolar spindle: poles and microtubule classes

A spindle is an organized assembly of polarized microtubules, associated proteins and motors. Microtubules consist of alpha- and beta-tubulin heterodimers arranged in a tube. Their ends are structurally different: the plus end generally undergoes more rapid growth and shortening than the minus end. In many animal somatic cells, minus ends are concentrated near spindle poles and plus ends extend toward chromosomes, the opposing half-spindle or the cortex. Individual microtubules need not extend all the way from pole to target, so this is a description of overall organization, not an invariant for each filament.

The centrosome is a major microtubule-organizing center in animal cells. It includes centrioles and surrounding pericentriolar material, where gamma-tubulin complexes help nucleate microtubules. Nucleation means starting a polymer, not elongating an existing polymer. Centrosomes duplicate during the cell cycle and separate to establish two organizing regions. Motor-driven movement, microtubule growth and connections between microtubules help form and maintain the two poles. A centrosome is not the same as a spindle pole: the latter is the region where spindle minus ends are focused. Some cells establish poles without centrioles or typical centrosomes.

Three functional groups make the spindle understandable. Kinetochore microtubules connect to chromosomes through kinetochores; bundles of such fibers in many animal cells are called kinetochore fibers or K-fibers. Interpolar microtubules extend into the central spindle, where plus-end regions from opposite sides overlap in an antiparallel arrangement. Astral microtubules radiate outward from the poles and interact with the cortex, the protein-rich region beneath the plasma membrane. These populations differ by connections and functions, not because they are made of chemically different tubulins.

Kinetochore fibers transmit forces that move chromosomes and help maintain their position. Interpolar fibers provide a framework for motors and crosslinkers that maintain bipolarity and later increase the distance between poles. Astral fibers transmit forces between poles and cortex, assisting spindle positioning and orientation relative to cell shape. A dividing epithelial cell must not merely form two poles; it must orient the spindle in relation to tissue geometry so that daughters are placed appropriately. An intact kinetochore fiber population cannot guarantee a correct division plane.

Mitotic cyclin-dependent kinase activity helps reorganize the interphase microtubule network. In open mitosis, nuclear envelope breakdown permits spindle access to chromosomes. Microtubules may also be nucleated near chromatin and amplified within the spindle. Higher plant cells organize bipolar spindles without the usual animal centrosome and often without prominent astral arrays. The conserved requirement is two functional chromosome-segregating directions, not a pair of centrioles.

3. Dynamic instability and spindle assembly

A growing microtubule can encounter a chromosome or continue elsewhere. Microtubules repeatedly alternate between growth and rapid shortening. This behavior, dynamic instability, arises in part because GTP-bound tubulin is added to the growing polymer and GTP hydrolysis changes stability of the lattice behind the tip. Loss of a stabilizing tip can produce catastrophe, a switch to shortening; a shortening microtubule can undergo rescue and resume growth. Repeated changes in length and direction of exploration through nucleation and turnover allow a population of microtubules to sample intracellular space without needing a preassembled path to every chromosome.

The search-and-capture model explains part of chromosome attachment. A microtubule contacting the outer kinetochore can initially bind along its side, allowing motor-assisted movement. Such contacts can mature into end-on attachments where the kinetochore remains associated with the dynamic plus-end region. Chromatin-associated nucleation and local spindle assembly improve the efficiency of the search; search-and-capture from centrosomes alone is therefore not a complete universal account. Protein complexes at the outer kinetochore interact with the microtubule lattice and end so that polymerization and depolymerization can occur without loss of all contact.

The poles must remain separated while microtubules explore. In many animal cells kinesin-5, a bipolar motor, crosslinks antiparallel microtubules and moves toward the plus end of each track. Because the plus ends face the middle, this slides microtubules apart relative to one another and favors pole separation. Other motors and crosslinkers can exert opposing forces, and dynein contributes to minus-end focusing and cortical forces. An assembled spindle is a balance of activities, not an immutable structure.

Inhibition of kinesin-5 in many mammalian cells permits inward activities to dominate and can yield a monopolar spindle. Chromosomes might make attachments, but a monopole cannot provide two opposed directions for sister separation. Nocodazole depolymerizes microtubules, removing attachment tracks. Paclitaxel stabilizes microtubules and suppresses their normal dynamics; it too can disrupt congression and segregation despite persistence of polymer. Opposite biochemical effects can produce related cell-cycle outcomes because what matters is not simply the amount of polymer but its ability to construct and use a dynamic bipolar apparatus.

Some oocytes organize acentrosomal spindles and budding yeast undergoes closed mitosis using spindle pole bodies, retaining its nuclear envelope. The common principles of bipolarity, attachment and controlled cohesion release remain applicable. An observation in mammalian somatic cells must not be generalized to every organism without checking spindle architecture.

4. Kinetochore attachment, congression and bi-orientation

Each replicated chromosome must become bi-oriented: the two sister kinetochores attach to opposite poles. In conventional monocentric chromosomes the geometry of the centromeric region favors sisters facing opposite directions. Cohesin maintains the connection that allows forces on one sister to be resisted by the other. Without this coupling, pulling forces could move both chromatids before appropriate allocation has been secured.

In amphitelic attachment, the two sisters connect to opposite poles; this is the desired configuration. In monotelic attachment, only one sister is attached, leaving the other free. In syntelic attachment, both sisters attach to the same pole. In merotelic attachment, one kinetochore connects to microtubules from both poles; the other sister may also be attached. The crucial distinction is between two sister kinetochores attaching to opposite poles and an individual kinetochore receiving fibers from both poles. The latter can generate competing forces on a single chromatid.

Location alone does not reveal attachment geometry. During congression chromosomes move near the spindle equator. Forces generated at kinetochores interact with forces acting on chromosome arms, including polar ejection forces associated with microtubule interactions and motors on chromosomes. Kinetochore-associated motors such as CENP-E help some chromosomes reach the equatorial region. The metaphase plate is a spatial arrangement, not a cellular membrane or a literal plate. Chromosomes can oscillate around the equator while remaining bi-oriented.

When both poles pull on a bi-oriented pair, the sisters resist separation because cohesin links them. The centromeric region stretches, generating tension. Equal opposing forces can produce little net movement despite appreciable tension. If both sisters attach to the same pole, there is generally insufficient appropriate intersister tension because attachments pull in the same overall direction. The cell exploits differences in geometry and mechanical state to favor productive connections. Bi-orientation establishes the directions of subsequent segregation before the linkage is removed; the cell does not sort individual DNA sequences at anaphase.

5. Error correction: attachment is not enough

If a kinetochore could bind a microtubule only once, early mistakes would persist until anaphase. Attachments instead turn over and can be remade. Aurora B kinase, part of the chromosome passenger complex, phosphorylates outer-kinetochore components including the Ndc80 attachment complex. This can reduce stability of kinetochore–microtubule interactions, allowing weak or inappropriate connections to release and undergo another attempt.

In a simplified spatial-separation model, Aurora B activity concentrated near the inner centromere acts on outer-kinetochore targets. Poor tension leaves targets relatively accessible to the kinase. Correct bi-orientation stretches the centromeric region and changes relative positions and regulation of attachment proteins. Phosphatases remove phosphate groups and help stabilize appropriately regulated contacts. Attachment permits force, force and geometry influence attachment stability, and repeated correction favors configurations sustaining productive opposing forces. This model is useful but not a complete description of every tension-responsive mechanism.

A syntelic pair initially connects leftward, creating an attached chromosome that cannot send one sister to the right. Destabilization releases improper connections, making a new contact with the right possible. If an amphitelic arrangement forms and sustains tension, its contacts become more stable on average. Error correction changes probabilities over repeated attempts; the kinase does not directly sort chromatids. Merotely poses a different problem: one kinetochore can be pulled in opposite directions despite being attached. Merotelic chromosomes may align and evade the spindle checkpoint. A connection persisting into anaphase may leave a lagging chromatid. Neither visible alignment nor checkpoint satisfaction is proof that every attachment is correct.

A cell with weakened Aurora B-mediated correction may retain abnormal attachments despite a spindle and apparent metaphase alignment. A cell lacking a functional checkpoint may proceed while a kinetochore is still unattached. Both may missegregate chromosomes, but the immediate defects differ. A final abnormal chromosome number cannot alone identify which mechanism failed.

6. Spindle assembly checkpoint and the timing of anaphase

The spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) connects attachment to timing. It is not a stage of mitosis. During early mitosis unattached kinetochores recruit signaling components, including Mps1 kinase and Mad and Bub proteins. These promote formation of an inhibitory assembly, the mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC). In vertebrates its principal components include Mad2, BubR1, Bub3 and Cdc20. The common outcome is inhibition of premature anaphase-promoting complex activity, even though molecular details differ across organisms.

The APC/C, or anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome, is an E3 ubiquitin ligase. It promotes attachment of ubiquitin chains to selected substrates, allowing degradation of many by the proteasome. Its mitotic activator Cdc20 assists substrate recognition and activation. By inhibiting APC/C–Cdc20, the MCC delays destruction of proteins whose removal is needed for anaphase and mitotic exit. As long as sufficient checkpoint signaling persists, sisters remain connected even if most chromosomes are aligned.

The signal originates at an attachment site but acts on a regulator distributed through the cell. Separating each sister pair independently as soon as it attached would remove the mechanical and regulatory context needed for orderly division. A common inhibitory signal allows the chromosome set to wait for a remaining problem. There can be a delay after the final attachment while inhibitory complexes are disassembled and APC/C activity rises. The checkpoint is primarily sensitive to kinetochore attachment. Tension matters through attachment stability and correction, but it is misleading to equate the SAC with a universal direct tension meter. Error correction can release syntelic connections and expose unattached kinetochores, while merotely may evade the SAC.

Classical live observations by Rieder and colleagues showed a relationship between the final monooriented chromosome acquiring bipolar attachment and anaphase timing. In a subsequent laser-ablation experiment, destruction of the remaining unattached kinetochore allowed progression without that site acquiring normal attachment. This distinguished a signal generated at the unattached site from the idea that every chromosome must physically reach a particular position before progression is possible. Ablation did not repair the chromosome or prove the daughters had correct genomes. The permission to proceed and physical correctness are distinct.

Upon productive attachment, checkpoint signaling diminishes and existing inhibitory complexes turn over. APC/C–Cdc20 can target its substrates. Under prolonged spindle disruption, some cells eventually exit mitosis without proper segregation through mitotic slippage associated with gradual loss of cyclin B despite checkpoint signaling. Others die or remain arrested. The checkpoint delays progression but is not an indefinitely reliable guarantee of viability.

7. Cohesion release and the anaphase switch

Cohesin establishes temporary association between sister chromatids during replication. Its components include SMC1 and SMC3 linked by a kleisin subunit, Scc1 in budding yeast or RAD21 in vertebrates. Cohesin is distinct from condensin, which helps organize chromosomes into their mitotic form. A compact chromosome can fail to separate if its sisters remain connected.

In vertebrate mitosis much arm cohesin is removed in prophase through a regulated pathway involving WAPL, while centromeric cohesin is protected until anaphase by mechanisms involving shugoshin and PP2A. The remaining cohesin holds sisters together against spindle forces. The amount and location removed before anaphase vary among organisms. The fundamental requirement is sufficient linkage maintained until separation is appropriate.

Separase is a protease that cleaves the kleisin component of cohesin. Before anaphase it is inhibited importantly by binding securin; in vertebrates inhibitory association with cyclin B–CDK1 also contributes. When the checkpoint no longer restrains APC/C–Cdc20, securin is ubiquitinated and degraded by the proteasome. Separase is released from restraint and cleaves remaining cohesion. The forces already acting on the kinetochores can now separate chromatids. APC/C is not itself the protease: it promotes substrate ubiquitination, the proteasome degrades securin, and separase cleaves cohesin.

APC/C also promotes cyclin B degradation. Falling cyclin B–CDK1 activity allows phosphatases to reverse many mitotic phosphorylations, contributing to spindle reorganization, chromosome decondensation and nuclear envelope reassembly. Later APC/C activity associated with Cdh1 helps maintain a low mitotic-cyclin state in many cells. Cohesion release permits disjunction; decline in kinase activity permits exit. Protein destruction makes the transition effectively directional within the current cycle: reversing phosphorylation cannot instantly recreate securin or cleaved cohesin.

A cleavage-resistant cohesin subunit illustrates signal versus execution. Even if APC/C is active and securin degraded, resistant kleisin can keep sisters connected and lead to chromosome stretching or bridges. Premature removal of cohesion can instead separate sisters before the establishment of proper attachments. A normal decrease in securin does not prove cohesion was released, and separase activity alone does not prove chromosome movement is correct.

8. Anaphase A and anaphase B

At anaphase, former sisters become individual daughter chromosomes. In anaphase A the distance between a daughter chromosome and its own pole decreases. In anaphase B the distance between the two poles increases. Both can move chromosome groups apart and often overlap. Their relative contributions depend on the cell and organism. The labels describe components of movement, not invariably consecutive stages.

In A, kinetochore microtubules typically shorten as chromosomes move poleward. Tubulin can be removed at the kinetochore-associated plus end or at pole-associated minus ends as part of poleward microtubule flux. Coupling proteins maintain contact with a shortening end, allowing movement as the lattice disassembles. ATP-dependent motor activity can also contribute. Depolymerization releases stored chemical and conformational energy from the polymer, so a model in which a motor walks the entire distance along a fixed track is incomplete.

In B, crosslinked antiparallel microtubules slide relative to each other through motor action. Their growth or rearrangement helps maintain overlap while poles move farther apart. In many animal cells cortical dynein anchored to the cortex interacts with astral microtubules. Because dynein tends to move toward minus ends, an anchored motor can pull the attached microtubule and its pole toward the cortex. Pushing and pulling can act simultaneously.

Fluorescent markers for chromosomes and poles distinguish these movements. If chromosome-to-pole distance falls while pole separation is unchanged, A occurs without detectable B during that interval. If chromosome-to-pole distance remains fixed while poles separate, the increased distance between chromosome groups arises from B. Measuring chromosomes relative to the microscope frame alone does not distinguish the two, because a moving pole can carry its chromosome farther from the center without changing the chromosome-to-pole segment.

A defect in B can leave chromosome groups unusually close despite normal shortening of kinetochore fibers. A defect in A can leave chromosomes near the midzone even when poles separate. Bridges or lagging chromosomes can complicate this model. A visually long spindle is not evidence that segregation succeeded.

9. Quantitative interpretation of anaphase

Let L be pole-to-pole distance in micrometers, dL the distance of the left chromosome group from the left pole, and dR the distance of the right group from the right pole. If both groups lie between the poles on the spindle axis and are properly ordered, the separation S between the groups is L − dL − dR. The identity comes from dividing the pole-to-pole segment into three consecutive pieces: dL, S and dR.

The change is ΔS = ΔL − ΔdL − ΔdR. During A, changes in dL and dR are negative; subtracting them adds positive contributions. During B, ΔL is positive. For symmetrical approach by distance a on each side, ΔS = ΔL + 2a. Asymmetric spindles require separate terms. The equations apply to axial projections, not arbitrary three-dimensional trajectories.

Worked example 1. Pole separation is initially 12 µm and each chromosome group lies 5 µm from its pole. Four minutes later pole separation is 18 µm and each group lies 2 µm from its pole. Initial S = 12 − 5 − 5 = 2 µm; final S = 18 − 2 − 2 = 14 µm. The increase is 12 µm: 6 from B and 3 + 3 = 6 from A. Mean approach speed is 0.75 µm/min on each side and pole-separation speed is 1.5 µm/min. If the spindle center remained fixed, each pole moved outward at half that rate. These are hypothetical teaching values.

Worked example 2. Pole distance increases from 14 to 20 µm over three minutes. Left chromosome-to-pole distance falls from 6 to 4 µm and right distance stays 6 µm. Initially S = 2 µm; finally S = 10 µm. The increase of 8 µm consists of 6 from B and 2 from left-side A. The right side contributes zero detectable approach. This does not identify the defective molecule; attachment, turnover or opposing forces could produce similar measurements.

Worked example 3. Of 240 scored cells, 36 are visibly mitotic. Mitotic index = 36/240 × 100 = 15%. An increase after spindle disruption can be consistent with increased residence time in mitosis, not necessarily increased proliferation. If entry continues while exit slows, mitotic cells accumulate. Changes in entry rate, death or a non-steady-state population prevent simple inference of phase duration from a snapshot.

10. Animal cytokinesis: specifying the division plane

An animal cell must partition the cytoplasm between separating chromosome groups. A cleavage furrow is an inward deformation of the plasma membrane driven by a contractile cortical structure. In typical symmetrical division it lies near the equator, roughly perpendicular to the spindle's long axis. An eccentrically positioned spindle can yield unequal daughter sizes without necessarily distributing chromosomes unequally.

Classical manipulations of dividing echinoderm eggs by Rappaport established that spindle-associated structures can specify furrow location. In appropriately shaped cells, a furrow could form between adjacent asters that did not have a chromosome group between them. The position signal therefore need not come directly from chromosomes at the furrow. This did not mean that chromosomes were dispensable for genome inheritance or that all cells use asters in exactly the same way. In many animal cells signals from both astral microtubules and central spindle cooperate.

As mitotic CDK activity decreases at anaphase, microtubules reorganize to form a central spindle between chromosome groups. Antiparallel overlaps provide a site for centralspindlin, a complex containing a kinesin motor component and a regulatory GAP-family component. Its association with the exchange factor Ect2 supports local activation of the small GTPase RhoA at the equatorial cortex. A GTPase switches between GDP-bound and GTP-bound states; an exchange factor promotes GDP replacement by GTP, enabling the active state.

Active RhoA recruits and activates factors that assemble actin and stimulate myosin II. Formins promote actin polymerization; Rho-associated kinase contributes to phosphorylation of myosin regulatory light chains, favoring contraction. Local activation produces a contractile zone rather than global cortical contraction. A globally contracting cortex could deform the cell without making an appropriate partition. Timing is equally important: a furrow that starts before chromosome segregation is complete can damage DNA, while one that never begins leaves both nuclei in the same cell.

11. Contractile ring and furrow ingression

The ring assembles on the cytoplasmic side of the plasma membrane. It is a dynamic network of actin, non-muscle myosin II and scaffolding proteins that organize and connect it to the cortex. Myosin II motor heads bind actin and use ATP hydrolysis to generate relative movement. Because neighboring filaments can have different orientations, motor action can draw parts of the network together. Circumferential contraction decreases ring radius and draws the attached surface inward.

The purse-string analogy explains why shortening a circular structure constricts an opening. It is misleading if it suggests a permanent rope pulled by one motor. Actin and myosin exchange with cytoplasm; filaments assemble and disassemble as the circumference decreases. Cortical flow, membrane attachment and trafficking help accommodate the changing surface geometry. A ring containing actin is not necessarily mechanically competent: organization, motor activity and membrane coupling matter.

Myosin II is not the principal motor of chromosome movement along kinetochore microtubules. Inhibiting actin polymerization or myosin II can allow recognizable segregation but prevent furrow ingression. A microtubule perturbation before anaphase may instead prevent the signal that initiates the ring, even when cortical actin machinery remains intact. The timing of an inhibitor is part of the interpretation.

Worked example 4. Approximate the ring as a circle of radius r. Circumference is 2πr. If radius falls from 8 to 2 µm over six minutes, circumference falls from 16π to 4π µm, a reduction of 12π or 37.70 µm. The fractional reduction is 75%; mean circumference decrease is 6.28 µm/min. Mean radial decrease is 1 µm/min, a different quantity. A real furrow need not be circular or constant-speed; circumference change does not equal speed of a myosin head.

As the furrow deepens, daughter cell bodies remain connected by a narrowing cytoplasmic bridge. Two rounded bodies seen by microscopy do not necessarily indicate completed cytokinesis. A distinct membrane-remodeling event must sever the remaining connection.

12. Midbody and final membrane scission

During late cytokinesis the central spindle becomes compacted in the intercellular bridge. Its dense region is the midbody, containing bundled microtubules and associated proteins. It organizes later division events. The midbody is not the metaphase plate; it forms after segregation and ingression, between daughter cell bodies.

Abscission is the final membrane scission. In many animal cells proteins of the ESCRT machinery, especially ESCRT-III, remodel and narrow the bridge membrane. Associated processes remove or sever microtubules near the constriction; spastin contributes in some systems. Membrane fission ends the continuous cytoplasmic connection. The midbody remnant may be inherited by one daughter, released or otherwise processed.

Inhibiting myosin II can prevent adequate constriction, whereas interfering with late scission can permit accurate segregation and deep ingression while leaving a persistent bridge. A bridge may eventually regress, returning the two daughter bodies to one binucleate cell. An endpoint picture of binucleation cannot tell whether the furrow never formed or formed and regressed. Time-lapse imaging distinguishes these histories.

When chromatin remains in the bridge, immediate membrane scission can damage DNA. An Aurora B-associated abscission checkpoint can delay final separation in response to such bridges in animal cells. This late safeguard is distinct from the SAC: it delays the membrane cut rather than re-establishing securin or returning chromatids to metaphase. It does not guarantee successful bridge resolution.

Some developmental programs intentionally modify cytokinesis. Developing animal germ cells can retain bridges that share cytoplasmic contents. Early Drosophila embryogenesis includes multiple nuclear divisions in a common cytoplasm before cellularization. Nuclear division without immediate separation is not always pathological; the developmental context determines its meaning.

13. Plant cytokinesis: division-plane marking and phragmoplast

A higher plant cell has a plasma membrane enclosed by a mechanically resistant wall. Rather than draw the external wall inward like an animal furrow, it builds a new internal partition. The structure starts near the center and expands outward until it meets the parental membrane and wall. The strategy requires spatial guidance and delivery of new membrane and wall material.

In many dividing plant somatic cells a preprophase band, a transient cortical array of microtubules and associated actin, marks the future division site. It predicts where the partition will eventually meet the parental wall. The band disappears around mitotic onset, while cortical molecular cues preserve information about the site. It does not physically deposit the cell plate. Some plant cells divide without a detectable preprophase band, so it is not a universal prerequisite.

After chromosome segregation, microtubules and associated proteins between the daughter nuclei reorganize into the phragmoplast. It contains two opposing microtubule arrays whose plus-end regions face the central division plane, together with actin and associated proteins. The central region receives vesicles derived predominantly from the secretory pathway, including the Golgi and trans-Golgi network. Transport, tethering and fusion concentrate membrane and wall precursors.

As the center becomes occupied by the developing cell plate, old microtubule arrays reorganize and assembly continues at the expanding outer region. This centrifugal expansion advances the region of vesicle delivery and plate formation. Guidance toward the cortical division zone allows the plate to join the parental boundary in the correct position. The preprophase band marks the site, the spindle distributes chromosomes, and the phragmoplast guides construction. The cell plate is the membrane and wall precursor compartment being constructed, not another name for the phragmoplast.

A plant cell with normal spindle function can have an abnormal plate if fusion or phragmoplast organization is impaired. A guidance defect can produce an incorrectly oriented cross-wall even with working vesicle fusion. These phenotypes distinguish positioning from construction.

14. Cell plate fusion and membrane topology

Vesicles delivered into the division plane fuse into a membrane-bound tubulovesicular network. Continued delivery and fusion enlarge it. Fusion requires proteins that bring opposing bilayers together. KNOLLE is a cytokinesis-associated syntaxin in Arabidopsis, belonging to the SNARE proteins involved in membrane fusion. Mutations affecting KNOLLE or its partners can leave vesicles accumulated at division sites and cause incomplete partitions. Transport to the correct site is not sufficient for a continuous plate.

Wall components accumulate in the lumen. Callose, a beta-1,3-glucan, is an important transient component in many higher plants and supports the developing compartment. Remodeling follows as the wall matures. Pectins, hemicelluloses and cellulose contribute to mature wall structure, with timing and composition varying. Neither callose alone nor cellulose alone describes the entire process.

Topology matters. Each vesicle's membrane has a cytoplasmic face exposed to cytosol and a luminal face toward its contents. Fusion produces a flattened compartment with opposite membrane surfaces separated by a lumen. At the periphery the plate fuses with the parental plasma membrane. The lumen becomes continuous with the extracellular wall space, while the opposite membrane surfaces become the facing plasma membranes of daughters. No membrane turns inside out. The new partition is a wall between two plasma membranes.

Some endoplasmic reticulum strands may be retained across the developing wall, contributing to primary plasmodesmata. These channels allow regulated communication between adjacent plant cells. Their presence does not mean division failed. The daughters remain distinct compartments while maintaining selective symplastic connections.

Animal furrowing moves inward from the cortex, decreasing the opening between daughters. Plant plate formation moves outward from the interior, increasing partition area. Both use cytoskeletal organization and membrane dynamics, but their main apparatus and geometry differ. A phragmoplast is not an actomyosin purse string and an animal midbody is not a growing cell plate.

15. Division failure, aneuploidy and DNA accounting

Abnormal division can originate at several levels. A monopolar spindle prevents normal bipolar attachment. Persistent attachment errors may leave lagging chromosomes or send both sisters to one pole. Premature anaphase may begin with unattached kinetochores. Failure of cohesion release can produce bridges despite sufficient attachments. Cytokinesis failure may occur despite accurate chromosome separation. Each disrupts a different intermediate in the pathway.

A lagging chromosome remains behind the main groups during anaphase. It may eventually enter the correct nucleus, segregate incorrectly or be enclosed separately in a micronucleus. Nondisjunction is failure of copies that should separate to reach separate nuclei. Aneuploidy means gain or loss of individual chromosomes relative to the normal set; polyploidy is an increase in complete sets. A micronucleus is a morphological observation and does not establish exact chromosome complement without identification of its DNA.

Worked example 5. A diploid cell has 2n = 8. Before replication it has eight chromosomes and 2C DNA, where C is the DNA amount of one unreplicated haploid genome. At metaphase it has eight duplicated chromosomes, sixteen chromatids and 4C. Immediately after normal disjunction the undivided cell has sixteen daughter chromosomes and 4C in total; each group has eight chromosomes and 2C. If mitosis finishes but cytokinesis fails, one cell contains two diploid nuclei, each with eight chromosomes and 2C. The cell has sixteen chromosomes distributed between two nuclei and 4C total. It is binucleate with tetraploid-equivalent total genome content, not necessarily one tetraploid nucleus. If both genomes later contribute to one nucleus without loss, it could be 4n = 16, 4C before another S phase.

Worked example 6. In the same species one replicated chromosome undergoes nondisjunction. The other seven pairs segregate normally. The left nucleus gets seven plus two = nine chromosomes; the right seven plus zero = seven. These are 2n + 1 and 2n − 1. Total count remains sixteen, so conservation of total chromosome number does not prove equal inheritance. DNA content of each differs from 2C by the DNA amount of the missegregated chromosome.

Cytokinesis failure can alter the centrosome complement and affect later spindle organization. A subsequent division may form a multipolar spindle or cluster extra centrosomes into a bipolar-looking arrangement that has had abnormal attachment opportunities. These processes can contribute to genomic instability, but failure does not automatically cause cancer or ensure continued proliferation. Cells may arrest, die or undergo specialized differentiation.

16. Interpreting experimental observations

A useful experiment follows more than one structure through time. Fluorescent tubulin indicates spindle and bridge microtubules, a chromatin marker reveals chromosome groups, and a membrane marker reveals furrows and bridges. Additional labels can report checkpoint protein recruitment or active RhoA. Time series allow attachment, anaphase, ingression and abscission to be scored separately. A fixed image cannot always distinguish a normal transient intermediate from a persistent arrest.

Consider four hypothetical matched groups of cultured animal cells. Vehicle-treated cells establish a baseline. A second group receives a microtubule-depolymerizing treatment before metaphase; a third receives a myosin II inhibitor shortly after anaphase begins; a fourth receives a late ESCRT perturbation. In the first treatment, many cells have unattached kinetochores and fail to initiate normal anaphase. In the second, many segregate chromosomes but fail to constrict. In the third, furrows ingress but bridges remain connected for unusually long periods. Applying the same drug at a different time could alter its phenotype.

Compare the earliest event that diverges from control. Loss of kinetochore fibers can cause secondary failure of furrowing. Loss of cortical contraction after accurate segregation points to a different immediate defect. If both contraction and ingression occur yet the connection persists, late scission becomes a plausible hypothesis. Drug specificity is not assumed; independent genetic perturbation and rescue by a functional target protein strengthen causal inference.

An unattached kinetochore retaining Mad2 is consistent with local checkpoint signaling but does not directly measure whole-cell APC/C inhibition. Persistence of securin suggests delayed destruction, which could also arise from changed synthesis, proteasome function or altered substrate recognition. Combining time-resolved markers and perturbations distinguishes these alternatives. Similarly in plants a correctly oriented phragmoplast with unfused vesicles points toward fusion, whereas an expanding phragmoplast aimed at the wrong cortical site suggests guidance failure. Transport, fusion, expansion and boundary attachment are separate observable events.

Worked example 7. A hypothetical control sample of 100 cells contains eight persistent bridges at a specified observation time; a treated sample of 100 contains 56. Observed fractions are 8% and 56%, the difference is 48 percentage points and ratio is 7. This describes prevalence at that time, not a sevenfold increase in probability of permanent failure. Some bridges may resolve. Matched conditions, repeated samples and time-lapse duration measurements are necessary to interpret the underlying rate.

17. Integrating the mechanisms and avoiding misconceptions

Follow the entire division as a sequence of permissions and physical changes. Replicated chromosomes retain sufficient cohesion while a bipolar spindle establishes connections. Error correction favors bi-orientation and the SAC delays progression when unattached sites persist. APC/C-mediated protein destruction permits separase activation and declining mitotic CDK activity. Microtubule dynamics and motors separate chromosome groups. Division-plane signaling organizes animal furrowing or plant plate construction. Finally the physical partition matures. The events overlap but their causal order matters.

Securin inhibits separase, not cohesin itself. Separase cleaves kleisin, not securin. APC/C is a ubiquitin ligase, not a microtubule motor or a protease. Aurora B contributes to attachment regulation and also to a later abscission safeguard, but these functions differ. Actomyosin drives much of animal furrowing whereas spindle microtubules determine chromosome movement and assist spatial specification. Phragmoplast means a cytoskeletal guide; plate means the partition under construction.

Always specify the counting compartment and time. A metaphase cell with 4C DNA is normally diploid, not necessarily tetraploid. Chromosome number temporarily doubles in the undivided anaphase cell because sisters now count individually. This does not change ploidy of each daughter nucleus. A binucleate cell may have twice the DNA of a normal G1 cell without a tetraploid nucleus. A 4C flow cytometry peak alone cannot distinguish a normal G2 cell, a binucleate cell after cytokinesis failure or certain tetraploid G1 cells.

In meiosis the logic changes at the first division: homologous chromosomes separate while sisters are normally co-oriented and centromeric cohesion is retained. Meiosis II separates sisters. Thus regulated attachment geometry plus timed cohesion release determines the segregating unit. In the next study of meiosis this distinction will explain why the first division reduces ploidy. To solve a mitotic perturbation problem, ask which structures exist, which distances change, which signals remain active, whether cohesion has been released and whether the membrane boundary is complete.

18. Part B — Concepts and assertion–reason

Original practice questions. Choose the best answer before opening the explanation.

1. Which is the kinetochore?

  1. A DNA region
  2. Protein assembly binding spindle microtubules
  3. Pole nucleation site
  4. Cortical actin ring
Answer and explanation

B. The kinetochore is the protein interface. A is the centromere, C an organizing site, D the cytokinetic ring.

2. Which fibers contact chromosomes directly?

  1. Astral
  2. Interpolar
  3. Kinetochore
  4. Actin
Answer and explanation

C. Kinetochore fibers transmit chromosome-directed force. Astral fibers contact cortex; interpolar fibers overlap; actin is a different polymer.

3. One kinetochore contacts both poles. Name the attachment.

  1. Amphitelic
  2. Syntelic
  3. Monotelic
  4. Merotelic
Answer and explanation

D. Merotely is one kinetochore bound to both poles. Amphitelic is opposite-pole attachment of distinct sisters, syntelic both sisters to one pole, monotelic one sister unattached.

4. What does separase directly do?

  1. Ubiquitinate cyclin B
  2. Cleave cohesin kleisin
  3. Polymerize actin
  4. Hydrolyze tubulin GTP
Answer and explanation

B. Separase is a protease targeting kleisin. APC/C promotes ubiquitination; actin polymerization and tubulin dynamics are separate.

5. Which event directly lowers mitotic CDK1 activity?

  1. Cyclin B degradation
  2. Securin binding
  3. Actin depolymerization
  4. Alignment alone
Answer and explanation

A. Cyclin B loss reduces CDK1 activity. Securin regulates separase; actin and alignment do not directly remove cyclin B.

6. Poles move apart, chromosome-to-pole distances fixed. Which movement?

  1. A only
  2. B
  3. Neither
  4. Cohesion formation
Answer and explanation

B. Pole separation defines B; A requires decreasing chromosome-to-pole distance. C overlooks movement and D is unrelated.

7. Main animal furrow motor?

  1. Kinesin-5
  2. CENP-E
  3. Myosin II
  4. Securin
Answer and explanation

C. Myosin II acts on actin. Kinesin-5 and CENP-E act on microtubules; securin regulates separase.

8. Which guides plate assembly?

  1. Persistent preprophase band
  2. Phragmoplast
  3. Midbody
  4. Centromere
Answer and explanation

B. Phragmoplast guides delivery; the band disappears before plate assembly. Midbody is an animal structure and centromeres organize attachment.

9. Assertion: an aligned chromosome can have abnormal attachment. Reason: merotelic attachments can permit alignment and evade SAC.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and explanation

A. Merotely provides a mechanism for alignment without correct topology or persistent unattached-site signal. Both statements are true and related.

10. Which most directly suggests delayed abscission?

  1. Unreplicated DNA
  2. Monopolar spindle
  3. Persistent bridge after deep ingression
  4. No condensation
Answer and explanation

C. A bridge after ingression indicates delayed final separation. Other choices concern earlier stages.

19. Part C — Analytical and quantitative reasoning

Original practice questions. Assume conventional monocentric mitosis unless specified.

1. A drug causes monopolar spindles, unattached kinetochores and persistent securin. Explain.

  1. APC/C activation
  2. SAC-dependent APC/C inhibition
  3. Premature separase
  4. Isolated ESCRT failure
Answer and explanation

B. Unattached kinetochores promote MCC formation, inhibiting APC/C–Cdc20. Increased APC/C or separase would favor separation; ESCRT acts late.

2. A cleavage-resistant kleisin remains despite normal securin degradation. Predict.

  1. Sisters may remain linked
  2. All microtubules disappear
  3. DNA necessarily fails to replicate
  4. Segregation must be normal
Answer and explanation

A. Cohesion cleavage is downstream of securin loss. A resistant linkage remains; no evidence supports B or C, and D confuses permission with execution.

3. 2n=12; normal mitosis with failed cytokinesis. Immediate nuclear organization?

  1. One 4n nucleus necessarily
  2. Two nuclei each 2n=12 and 2C
  3. Two nuclei each n=6 and 4C
  4. One 2n nucleus and 2C total
Answer and explanation

B. Normal nuclear separation gives two diploid nuclei, total 24 chromosomes and 4C. A assumes fusion, C halves ploidy and D loses a genome.

4. 2n=10; one sister pair nondisjoins. Daughter counts?

  1. 10 and 10
  2. 12 and 8
  3. 11 and 9
  4. 20 and 0
Answer and explanation

C. Nine normal pairs contribute nine each, and the abnormal pair two and zero. B requires two such events; A and D do not match.

5. Pole distance increases 10 to 15 µm; left and right d fall 4 to 2 and 4 to 1. Increase in S?

  1. 5 µm
  2. 7 µm
  3. 10 µm
  4. 15 µm
Answer and explanation

C. Initial S=2, final S=12, increase 10=5+2+3. A counts B only, B omits right approach, D is final pole separation.

6. Circular furrow radius falls 6 to 3 µm. Which statement?

  1. Circumference falls 75%
  2. Circumference falls 50%
  3. Circumference falls 25%
  4. Myosin head speed equals radial speed
Answer and explanation

B. Circumference is proportional to radius: 12π to 6π. Area falls 75%, not circumference. Molecular speed is not uniquely determined by radial speed.

7. 45/150 treated cells and 15/150 controls are mitotic. Justified conclusion?

  1. 30% versus 10%, ratio 3
  2. Proliferation increased threefold
  3. Mitosis lasts 30 min
  4. Every treated cell arrested
Answer and explanation

A. Observed fractions are 30% and 10%. A snapshot cannot establish proliferation, duration or universal arrest.

8. Chromosome lags despite alignment and securin destruction. Which attachment particularly explains it?

  1. Normal amphitelic
  2. Merotelic
  3. Normal ring
  4. Normal centrosome duplication
Answer and explanation

B. Merotely creates opposing forces on a single kinetochore. Normal amphitelic attachment favors disjunction; ring and centrosome duplication alone do not explain competing forces.

9. Furrow ingresses but daughters remain connected for hours. Which target?

  1. S-phase polymerase
  2. ESCRT-mediated scission
  3. Initial kinetochore capture
  4. Preprophase band
Answer and explanation

B. Late bridge persistence suggests abscission. A and C act earlier; D is a plant structure. Exact target needs further evidence.

10. Plant mutant has normal phragmoplast positioning but unfused plate vesicles. Which defect?

  1. Cytokinetic SNARE such as KNOLLE
  2. Failure of all vesicle transport
  3. Premature anaphase
  4. Myosin II ring
Answer and explanation

A. Vesicles arrived, favoring fusion failure. B conflicts with their presence; C is not a fusion explanation; D imports the animal apparatus.

11. Laser destruction of last unattached kinetochore permits anaphase. What follows?

  1. Normal attachment restored
  2. That site supplied inhibitory signal
  3. All DNA repaired
  4. SAC dispensable universally
Answer and explanation

B. Removing a signal source can permit anaphase without restoring attachment. A and C are not demonstrated; D generalizes beyond evidence.

12. A checkpoint-deficient cell progresses with unattached kinetochores; another cell with a functional SAC has merotelic lagging. Best principle?

  1. SAC detects every error
  2. Attachment monitoring and correction are distinct
  3. Cohesin unnecessary
  4. Chromosome count uniquely identifies defect
Answer and explanation

B. SAC controls timing, while merotely may evade it. A contradicts observations; C is unrelated; D ignores multiple causal routes.
