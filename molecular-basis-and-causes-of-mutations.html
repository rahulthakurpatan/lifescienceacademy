Molecular Basis and Causes of Mutations

What changes when a gene mutates?

Every generation of cells faces two competing requirements. Its DNA must be copied accurately enough to preserve thousands of interdependent biological functions, yet the same hereditary material must occasionally acquire new sequence variants. Without the first property, stable inheritance would be impossible. Without the second, alleles could not arise through sequence change and evolution would have no new hereditary variation on which to act. The molecular study of mutation begins with this tension between faithful copying and occasional change.

A mutation is a change in the nucleotide sequence of genetic material. In this chapter we focus primarily on cellular DNA and changes that affect individual nucleotides or relatively short stretches of sequence. A change does not need to alter an observable trait in order to qualify as a mutation. It may occur outside a coding sequence, alter a codon without changing its encoded amino acid, or have no measurable effect in the environment being studied. Whether a change is advantageous, harmful or approximately neutral is a question about its biological consequence, not its molecular definition.

The word allele describes a sequence version at a locus. A mutation is an event that can produce a new allele. Once a new sequence has been copied into a stable DNA duplex, its descendants may carry that allele even if the process that originally produced the change is no longer present. In a diploid organism the homologous chromosome may retain the original sequence, producing a heterozygote. In a haploid cell the change can be present in the only copy of the relevant locus. Neither condition by itself determines the severity of a phenotype.

An important distinction is that a difference from a reference sequence is not necessarily a newly observed mutation. A reference is an agreed comparison sequence, not an ancestral genome or a definition of perfect biological function. Suppose a population contains two common alleles of a metabolic gene. Sequencing an individual whose allele differs from the reference reveals a variant; it does not establish when the change originated, whether it occurred in that individual, or whether it has any functional consequence. Evidence from a family, lineage or ancestral comparison can help identify a newly arisen change.

The molecular change and its phenotypic effect should be described separately. A single substitution might interrupt an essential enzyme, subtly affect RNA stability, or leave a protein sequence unchanged. Conversely, distinct sequence changes can disrupt the same pathway and yield similar phenotypes. The complementation logic of the preceding chapter can group recessive functional defects, but does not tell us whether two mutants have identical DNA changes. Sequence analysis answers a different question.

A second distinction concerns the biological level of change. A nucleotide substitution is a local alteration in sequence. A deletion can affect one nucleotide or extend across an entire chromosomal segment, so the same word can refer to very different scales. Changes involving the structure or number of whole chromosomes belong mainly to subunit 8.F. Here we examine how smaller changes arise and how their consequences can be predicted from a gene model. This boundary helps connect the chemistry of DNA with classical inheritance without confusing sequence mutations with chromosomal segregation errors.

From DNA damage to a stable sequence change

DNA damage is a chemical or structural disturbance to the molecule. A base can lose an amino group, a nucleotide can lose its base, and adjacent bases can become covalently linked. None of these events automatically produces a stable alternative sequence. A cell may restore the original structure through repair, tolerate the lesion without copying it, stop dividing, or die. Only some damage events lead to a heritable sequence change in the descendants of the affected cell.

Consider a normal duplex carrying G opposite C. During replication, a polymerase accidentally places T opposite template G. The result is a G:T mismatch rather than two complementary copies of the original sequence. If the incorrectly inserted T is removed and replaced by C, the original sequence is restored. If the mismatch persists until another replication, one daughter duplex can carry G:C and the other A:T. The latter is now an ordinary complementary base pair, despite its difference from the starting sequence. A mechanism that recognizes mismatches has no obvious structural mismatch to recognize in that fully paired mutant duplex.

This reasoning illustrates what geneticists mean when they speak of fixation of a mutation at the molecular level. The word fixation is also used in population genetics when an allele reaches frequency one in a population. The two meanings should not be confused. A sequence change can become established in a single DNA lineage without spreading through the entire population. Population fixation requires a separate history of reproduction, selection, drift and other evolutionary forces.

Repair is a competition with other cellular processes, rather than a guarantee of a particular outcome. The lesion must be recognized before it is copied or processed into a different structure. Repair must remove or reverse the relevant damage without introducing a replacement error. The cell must retain sufficient genetic integrity to reproduce if the outcome is to be transmitted. Thus the probability of a stable sequence change depends on damage formation, lesion persistence, copying behavior, repair efficiency and survival.

The chemistry of DNA provides opportunities for both accuracy and error. Complementarity makes a second strand available as a template when one strand has been damaged. Nevertheless, complementarity alone cannot distinguish a newly synthesized incorrect base from the correct template base in every context. Cells use information about replication, DNA structure and repair intermediates to make that distinction. Detailed enzyme pathways are covered in Unit 3; the genetic principle needed here is that repair modifies the probability that a mismatch or lesion will become an enduring variant.

A mutation can also arise without a prior chemical lesion. A polymerase copying intact DNA sometimes chooses the wrong nucleotide. Repeated sequences can promote changes in alignment between the template and newly synthesized strands, changing the number of repeated units copied. These errors belong to the spontaneous mutation category when they arise without deliberate external mutagenic exposure. The term spontaneous does not mean causeless. Every event has a physical or biochemical origin; the category simply identifies the circumstances in which it arose.

For interpretation of experiments, keep three observations separate: the number of lesions measured shortly after a damaging event, the proportion of surviving cells with sequence changes, and the long-term frequency of those variants among descendants. A treatment that produces more lesions may also kill more cells, leaving relatively few mutants to be measured. A repair defect can affect lesion clearance, survival and mutation frequency differently. The count of one observation cannot substitute for the others.

Classifying base substitutions

DNA has four standard bases. Adenine and guanine are purines with two fused rings; cytosine and thymine are pyrimidines with one ring. Replacement of one purine by another purine, or one pyrimidine by another pyrimidine, is called a transition. Replacement across these two chemical classes is called a transversion. The classification describes the identity of the initial and final bases, not the magnitude of the functional consequence.

For a particular original base there is one possible transition and two possible transversions. For example, A to G is a transition, while A to C and A to T are transversions. Across all four starting bases there are four directed transition changes and eight directed transversion changes. If all twelve directed possibilities were equally likely, the ratio of transitions to transversions would be one to two. Actual mutation spectra often depart from that idealization because DNA chemistry, neighboring sequence, replication and repair make some events much more likely than others.

A stable base-pair substitution can be written in terms of the nucleotide on either strand. An A:T to G:C event is a transition when described as A to G on one strand and also when described as T to C on the complementary strand. These are two descriptions of the same molecular event, not two independent mutations. If a strand is reported without specifying orientation, a student may mistakenly double-count the change. Sequence comparison conventionally states which strand and reference position are being compared.

Transitions do not always have smaller phenotypic effects than transversions. The chemical similarity of two bases does not tell us whether their replacement creates a stop codon, removes a splice signal or changes an essential protein residue. To predict an effect, one must combine the sequence alteration with the location and role of the altered nucleotide. A transition in a catalytic codon may have a greater effect than a transversion at a weakly constrained position.

Mutation spectra are often simplified into six complementary substitution classes by describing each event from the pyrimidine-containing reference strand. For instance, G to A and C to T belong to the same complementary class if strand information is not retained. This convention is useful for comparing classes of change but may obscure differences between the two DNA strands when transcription or replication is relevant. Therefore the counting rule and strand convention must be stated before spectra are compared.

Imagine an illustrative set of independently observed substitutions consisting of 60 transitions and 40 transversions. The transition fraction is 60 divided by 100, or 0.60, and the transition-to-transversion ratio is 60 divided by 40, or 1.5. These measures are not the same. In an equal-alternative model the ratio is 0.5, so the observed set is relatively enriched for transitions compared with that particular model. This calculation does not identify a specific cause. Base composition, detection bias, sequence context and sampling error could all influence the observed distribution.

The distinction between mutation spectra and functional classification becomes especially useful in examination problems. A question may report C to T changes at many sites, then ask which chemical mechanism could plausibly explain the excess. Another question may show a single A to G change and ask for the translated product. The first problem concerns origin; the second concerns consequence. Neither is answered by memorizing the word transition alone.

Reading coding consequences from DNA sequence

A protein-coding sequence is interpreted by the translation machinery in sets of three RNA nucleotides called codons. The coding strand of DNA has the same sequence as the corresponding RNA except that T in DNA is replaced by U in RNA. The template strand is complementary and antiparallel to that RNA. Before translating a DNA sequence in a problem, establish whether the sequence supplied is the coding strand or the template strand, which direction it is written in, and where the reading frame begins.

The standard genetic code associates most codons with amino acids and three codons with translation termination. Several codons specify the same amino acid, a property known as degeneracy. Degeneracy means that some substitutions change the codon without altering the encoded residue. It does not mean that one codon is arbitrarily interpreted as several different amino acids in the ordinary code. Some organellar codes differ from the standard code, so the assumed code is another condition that must be clear when solving a translation problem.

For an example, consider a short coding strand written five prime to three prime as ATG GAA TTT CCG TAA. Its RNA sequence is AUG GAA UUU CCG UAA. If translation starts at the first codon, the encoded peptide segment is methionine, glutamate, phenylalanine and proline, followed by termination. The termination codon is a signal, not a fifth amino acid. In this example GAA and GAG both encode glutamate. Changing the final A of GAA to G is a transition and is synonymous at the amino acid level.

Now change GAA to GCA. The original second codon specifies glutamate and the altered codon specifies alanine. This is a missense substitution, because the resulting peptide differs at that position. The change from A to C is a transversion. Glutamate has an acidic side chain, while alanine has a small nonpolar side chain. A substitution could change interactions in an active site or at a protein surface. Yet the position and local structural environment determine whether such a change is well tolerated.

Changing GAA to TAA creates UAA in the RNA. Translation beginning at the original start codon would encounter a termination signal at the second codon. This is a nonsense substitution, also termed a stop-gain variant. The original coding strand change is G to T, a transversion. In a real gene, the abundance of the mutant RNA may also be affected by quality-control pathways. It is therefore insufficient to assume that every premature stop simply produces a stable truncated protein.

The positions of nonsense changes matter. A premature stop near the beginning of a gene can remove most encoded domains, while a stop near the normal end can leave much of the protein sequence intact. A truncation may remove a short region that is indispensable for localization or regulation. Conversely, some terminal truncations retain substantial biochemical activity. A sequence prediction is a necessary first step, but evidence about RNA, protein and cellular function is needed to establish the phenotypic result.

The term synonymous refers to the amino acid identity of a translated codon under a specified code and frame. Such a substitution may nonetheless alter RNA secondary structure, splice-regulatory motifs or the efficiency and timing of translation. In a simple exam question asking only for the peptide encoded by two codons, it is appropriate to call a synonymous change silent at the protein-sequence level. In a question asking about RNA expression or phenotype, assuming complete neutrality is unjustified.

Not all substitutions within a coding region fall comfortably into the familiar three categories. A change can remove the normal termination signal, potentially extending translation until a later stop; a change in an initiation codon can impair initiation or shift use to an alternative site. The resulting effects depend on the transcript and cellular translation system. In particular, a start-loss mutation should not automatically be described as a missense mutation merely because an AUG codon has changed. Its role as an initiation signal is the feature that needs to be evaluated.

Insertions, deletions and the reading frame

A substitution changes base identity without changing the number of nucleotides at the affected site. An insertion adds nucleotide sequence; a deletion removes it. The combined term indel is convenient when referring to short length-changing variants without specifying the event direction. Direction depends on the comparison: if two alleles differ by three bases, an ancestral reference or other evidence is needed to determine whether one acquired an insertion or the other acquired a deletion.

In a protein-coding region, a length change that is not divisible by three shifts the reading frame downstream of the altered sequence, provided the original initiation frame remains in use. This can change many successive amino acids rather than merely adding or removing one residue. Eventually a termination codon may be encountered in the altered frame, although the distance to that codon cannot be predicted without examining the sequence. A length change divisible by three preserves the downstream phase of translation and is called in-frame.

The earlier illustrative coding sequence ATG GAA TTT CCG TAA makes the difference visible. Inserting C immediately after ATG changes the grouping to ATG CGA ATT TCC GTA, with the final remaining nucleotide not constituting a complete codon in this fragment. Beginning at the same start, the encoded sequence starts with methionine, arginine, isoleucine, serine and valine rather than methionine, glutamate, phenylalanine and proline. The original stop codon is no longer read as a stop in its former frame. The fragment is too short to establish the eventual termination position.

By contrast, inserting the complete triplet GCT immediately after ATG produces ATG GCT GAA TTT CCG TAA. Under the standard code, alanine is inserted into the peptide while the downstream codons retain their original grouping. This is an in-frame insertion. It need not be harmless; an additional amino acid can disrupt a tightly packed protein structure or a precise sequence motif. The preservation of the reading frame says nothing definitive about protein folding.

An in-frame deletion need not remove only intact preexisting codons. If three consecutive nucleotides are deleted across a boundary between two codons, the remaining fragments of those codons may join to form a different codon. The downstream frame is preserved, but a local amino acid substitution can accompany the loss of one residue. Students should therefore translate the new sequence rather than merely dividing the length of the deletion by three and predicting that many intact amino acids are lost.

Several nearby changes can interact. Suppose a coding sequence acquires a one-nucleotide insertion followed later by a one-nucleotide deletion. After the insertion, the reading frame changes; after the deletion, the original downstream phase can be restored. The intervening peptide sequence may still be altered, and a premature termination signal might occur before the frame is restored. Thus a net length change of zero does not prove that the resulting protein is unchanged. The order and locations of individual events are essential.

A frameshift has meaning only with respect to a translated reading frame. Inserting one nucleotide into an intron is not automatically a frameshift in the mature messenger RNA, because the intron is normally removed. The insertion could instead alter splicing, or have no measurable consequence. Similarly, an indel in a promoter might alter transcription-factor binding or spacing without directly changing the coding sequence. Identify the functional region before applying a coding-specific label.

Splicing, regulatory DNA and repeat expansions

In many eukaryotic genes the DNA sequence that is initially transcribed includes exons and introns. RNA processing removes introns from a precursor and joins retained exons to form a mature transcript. The protein-coding portion of that transcript may differ from the genomic DNA by many intervening segments. A sequence change near an exon boundary can therefore change the mature RNA even when it does not directly replace a coding base.

Splice donor and acceptor signals contribute to recognition of the appropriate intron boundaries. Mutations can weaken a normal signal or make an alternative signal more competitive. Outcomes can include loss of an exon from the mature transcript, retention of intronic sequence, or selection of a nearby alternative splice site. Whether the resulting coding sequence retains its original frame depends on the number and position of nucleotides gained or lost in the mature RNA. Different tissues may use different splice patterns, so a genomic variant can have different consequences in different expression contexts.

For example, if skipping a wholly coding exon removes 99 nucleotides, the downstream frame remains unchanged because 99 is divisible by three. If exon skipping removes 100 coding nucleotides, the downstream phase changes. These predictions assume no compensatory RNA processing and that translation starts in the same frame. They also distinguish preservation of the downstream frame from preservation of protein function: an in-frame missing exon may encode an indispensable protein domain.

Promoters and enhancers regulate transcription through sequence-dependent interactions with regulatory proteins and through chromatin context. A substitution at a regulatory motif can change binding affinity, altering transcription without changing the encoded protein sequence. A decrease in transcript abundance may reduce the amount of otherwise normal protein. A regulatory variant can also increase expression or affect when and where a gene is expressed. These outcomes require information about the regulatory element and the cells in which it functions.

A repeat expansion is an increase in the number of adjacent copies of a sequence motif. Repetitive DNA can form misaligned intermediates during DNA metabolism. A length difference may then be established when the intermediate is copied or processed. A repeat expansion can occur inside or outside a protein-coding region. Triplet repeats located within the coding frame can preserve that frame while adding repeated amino acids; expansions outside a coding region can instead affect transcription, RNA processing or RNA-mediated cellular function.

Huntington disease provides a well-known example of a coding CAG repeat expansion in HTT, producing an expanded glutamine tract. Fragile X syndrome provides a contrasting example involving a CGG expansion in the five-prime untranslated region of FMR1, where full expansions are typically associated with transcriptional silencing. These examples establish why the number of repeated nucleotides alone cannot predict mechanism: the location, sequence and biological processing of the repeat are crucial. Clinical inheritance and disease classification are treated separately in subunit 8.D.

Repeat changes also illustrate why an allele can have a degree of instability that depends on its existing sequence. In certain disorders, changes in repeat length across generations contribute to anticipation, in which onset may become earlier or severity may increase. This is not a universal property of repeats, nor is every repeat expansion inevitably pathogenic. Repeat size, interruptions, parental transmission and other biological context influence the outcome. The molecular principle is that local sequence structure can alter the probability of future sequence change.
