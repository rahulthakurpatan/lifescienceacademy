Why geneticists need molecular markers

A chromosome contains many DNA sequences that cannot be distinguished by ordinary observation of an organism. Classical genetics follows visible differences such as flower colour, seed shape or a nutritional requirement. These differences can provide excellent landmarks, but there are relatively few readily scored traits, and each may depend on the environment, developmental stage or several genes. Molecular markers solve a different problem: they allow geneticists to follow inherited differences in DNA itself, even when the difference has no visible biological consequence. A marker therefore need not encode a protein or cause a phenotype. Its value is that its alternative forms can be distinguished reliably and can be followed through a suitable genetic experiment.

Consider two plants whose chromosomes contain different DNA sequences at a particular site. If a cross produces offspring in which one of the two parental sequence forms can be identified, the investigator can determine which parental chromosome contributed that region. At another location, a sequence difference may mark a second region. Following both sites in many offspring reveals how frequently inheritance separates the two parental combinations. These observations are the molecular counterpart of the phenotype-based testcross developed in classical linkage analysis. The crucial innovation is the ability to observe differences that are ordinarily invisible, not a replacement of the underlying laws of chromosome inheritance.

A locus is a defined genomic position or region; an allele is one of the alternative forms found at that locus. A polymorphism is sequence variation among individuals or chromosomes in a population. The operational definition used in a marker experiment depends on the assay: the actual difference might be a nucleotide substitution, an insertion, a deletion, a change in repeat number or a rearrangement affecting a larger fragment. If every individual in a particular cross carries the same marker allele, that marker is monomorphic within that experiment, even if it is highly variable in other populations. Informative polymorphism is thus a relationship between a marker and the biological material under study.

It is useful to separate the DNA variant from the assay used to observe it. For example, a single nucleotide substitution might eliminate a restriction site, making two alleles distinguishable by restriction fragment length. The same substitution could instead be detected by sequencing or by an allele-specific assay. The biological variant is unchanged; the laboratory observation differs. Conversely, two fragments that migrate to the same position in a gel may have different sequences. An assay distinguishes the classes it was designed to distinguish, not necessarily every possible sequence at the locus.

The essential logic of mapping is to connect an observation to an inference. An assay provides an observable signal, which is interpreted as a genotype. A collection of genotypes is interpreted through an inheritance model. A mapping analysis then estimates the position or relationship of a locus. Each inference introduces assumptions. Incorrect scoring, unknown parentage, a duplicated sequence or a biased sample can weaken the conclusion even when the DNA band itself is real. A good geneticist therefore asks not only whether a marker can be detected but whether its segregation can be interpreted unambiguously.

Dominant and codominant molecular scoring

Diploid organisms generally have two homologous copies of an autosomal locus. If an assay can distinguish both alleles in a heterozygote, the marker is scored codominantly. Suppose one allele produces a fragment of 150 base pairs and a second allele produces a fragment of 158 base pairs. The two homozygotes have their respective fragment lengths, while a heterozygote has both. The observable genotype classes can therefore be recorded as AA, AB and BB. In an F2 from two homozygous parents, those classes are expected in proportions one quarter, one half and one quarter when Mendelian segregation occurs without viability or scoring biases.

A dominant marker assay distinguishes the presence of a signal from its absence without distinguishing the two genotypes that carry at least one signal-producing allele. Suppose D produces a band and d does not. DD and Dd both score as present; dd scores as absent. In an F2 the expected observable ratio is three present to one absent under the same assumptions. This is dominance of the scored marker signal, not necessarily dominance of a biological phenotype. The underlying alleles may have no measurable physiological effect.

Dominant markers can be informative in a carefully chosen cross. In a Dd by dd testcross, band-positive offspring are expected to be Dd and band-negative offspring dd. The two classes reveal the allele contributed by the heterozygous parent. In an F2, however, the band-positive class combines two genotypes and loses information. Treating all band-positive F2 individuals as heterozygotes would create false segregation patterns and may distort estimates of linkage.

The distinction also affects evaluation of marker quality. A marker allele at a known locus may fail to produce a detectable band because an assay does not amplify it, a phenomenon often described as a null allele in PCR-based scoring. That is not automatically a loss-of-function mutation of a biological gene. A true heterozygote carrying one visible allele and one nonamplifying allele may be scored incorrectly as a homozygote. Replicate assays, family segregation, alternative primers and independent genotype checks can help distinguish assay failure from genuine homozygosity.

An informative marker is not simply one with many alleles. A multiallelic marker that is identical in both experimental parents cannot track their different ancestry. A biallelic SNP can be completely informative when the parents are fixed for different alleles. For a population with allele frequencies p_i, expected heterozygosity under random mating is one minus the sum of squared allele frequencies. If two alleles each occur at frequency 0.5, expected heterozygosity is 0.5; if four alleles each occur at frequency 0.25, it is 0.75. These values describe population-level diversity and must not be mistaken for the probability of informative segregation in a specific family.

Restriction fragment length polymorphism

Restriction enzymes recognize particular DNA sequences and cleave at defined positions relative to those sequences. A mutation can create or destroy a recognition site, or change the length of DNA between two sites. Consequently, homologous chromosomes may produce restriction fragments of different lengths when treated with the same enzyme. A restriction fragment length polymorphism, abbreviated RFLP, is the inherited difference in fragment size revealed by such an analysis.

Imagine a genomic region bounded by two conserved restriction sites 8 kilobases apart. One allele contains an additional site 3 kilobases from the left boundary, whereas the other does not. Complete digestion yields fragments of 3 and 5 kilobases from the first allele and one fragment of 8 kilobases from the second. A probe complementary to a unique sequence lying entirely within the left 3-kilobase interval detects the 3-kilobase fragment in one allele and the 8-kilobase fragment in the other. It does not detect the 5-kilobase fragment because that fragment lacks the complementary sequence. A heterozygote therefore displays the 3- and 8-kilobase signals. The sum of detected band lengths need not equal the total genomic region length, because the probe selectively reveals fragments containing its target.

Classical genomic RFLP analysis separates restriction fragments by electrophoresis and detects selected fragments through Southern hybridization. A genome contains a very large number of restriction fragments; detecting every fragment as a simple collection of DNA bands would not normally identify a particular locus. Hybridization supplies sequence specificity. A unique probe gives a much more interpretable pattern than a repetitive probe that binds many unrelated regions. For an appropriate single-copy locus, the two homologous allele forms can often be distinguished codominantly.

The 1980 proposal by Botstein and colleagues showed how restriction fragment polymorphisms could provide landmarks for constructing human linkage maps without requiring each marker to produce a visible trait. The logic was especially important for human genetics, where controlled crosses cannot be performed. DNA polymorphisms could be followed through existing pedigrees, and linkage between an unknown disease locus and informative markers could narrow the search for the gene. Linkage indicated a chromosomal neighbourhood, not a functional identity between the marker and the disease gene.

Incomplete restriction digestion is an important confounder. A partially digested sample may contain a larger fragment even when the corresponding fully digested allele lacks it. If an investigator calls the larger product a new allele without verifying digestion, the apparent genotypes can become systematically wrong. Multiple independent markers, complete-digestion controls and known reference genotypes provide complementary checks. RFLP approaches are generally locus-specific and reproducible when well designed, but classical Southern-based analysis is relatively labour intensive and requires suitable DNA and probes. PCR-based restriction assays can interrogate smaller predefined regions and should be distinguished from the original genomic Southern approach.

Random amplified polymorphic DNA

RAPD, or random amplified polymorphic DNA, uses short primers of arbitrary sequence to sample anonymous regions of a genome. A detectable amplification product requires compatible primer binding positions in opposite orientations and within an amplifiable distance. A nucleotide change affecting a primer-binding site, or a change in the length or arrangement of the intervening sequence, can alter the resulting band pattern. The investigator does not need to know the complete sequence of the target regions before selecting the arbitrary primer.

A single reaction may reveal several products from different genomic locations. Each reliably resolved band may be treated as an individual candidate marker, but the bands are not automatically alleles of the same locus. An individual producing four bands and another producing three have not necessarily gained or lost a whole gene. The observed difference might reflect a small primer-binding variant, an amplification competition effect or a genuine larger genomic alteration. Only segregation and additional characterization can establish how a particular band should be interpreted.

Most RAPD bands are scored as dominant presence or absence markers. If the homologous chromosome in a diploid lacks the amplifiable configuration, it may contribute no visible signal, while the other chromosome supplies the band. A heterozygote and a homozygote carrying the amplified allele then appear the same. Consequently, the choice of mapping population and segregation model becomes critical. The ease of surveying variation must be balanced against loss of genotype information.

RAPD profiles can be sensitive to technical conditions. A weak or inconsistent band is poor evidence of heritable polymorphism. If the same DNA sample yields different profiles in independent observations, a biological explanation is not warranted until technical variability has been excluded. Conversely, repeated observation of a band difference, followed by inheritance in the predicted classes, strengthens the case for a genetic marker. Conversion of an informative anonymous band into a sequence-characterized locus can improve specificity; that is a distinct follow-up strategy rather than an intrinsic property of every RAPD band.

Amplified fragment length polymorphism

AFLP combines restriction digestion with selective amplification of a subset of the resulting DNA fragments. Adaptors provide known sequence elements at restriction-fragment ends, allowing primers to target those fragments. Additional selective bases at primer ends reduce the number of amplified fragments, creating a reproducible subset of genomic products that can be resolved as a profile. This is a conceptual description of the relationship between fragmentation and detection, not a laboratory recipe.

The essential difference between AFLP and RAPD is the source of primer-defined specificity. RAPD relies on arbitrary primer-binding sites distributed in the genome. AFLP first defines a collection of restriction fragments, then selects subsets through adaptor-associated primers. Polymorphism may alter restriction sites, selective-primer matching or fragment lengths. Because many fragments can be surveyed in one profile, AFLP has been useful when researchers need genome-wide marker coverage without an existing detailed reference sequence.

AFLP bands are commonly scored as dominant markers. This does not mean codominant information is physically impossible; under some circumstances corresponding length alleles can be identified. Nevertheless, anonymous presence or absence scoring is typical, and one should not infer a three-genotype classification merely because the gel contains many bands. Like RAPD, coincident migration of distinct fragments can create apparent identity, especially when comparing evolutionarily distant genomes.

Vos and colleagues described AFLP in 1995. Its historical significance lies in combining the reproducible sequence recognition of restriction digestion with the efficiency of selective DNA amplification. A researcher comparing AFLP with an older RFLP system should distinguish the number of loci sampled per assay from the information available at each locus. A dense set of dominant AFLP markers may be more useful for a coarse genome survey than a handful of codominant markers, while an individual codominant marker can offer clearer inference of heterozygosity. Neither method is universally superior.

Simple sequence repeats and microsatellites

Simple sequence repeats are tandem arrays of short DNA motifs, often one to six nucleotides long. They are commonly called microsatellites or short tandem repeats. A locus containing eight copies of the motif CA differs in length from the corresponding locus containing twelve copies if the flanking regions are otherwise unchanged. Replication-associated slippage is an important source of variation in repeat number. Different repeat counts may persist as alternative alleles in populations.

A locus-specific assay ordinarily uses the relatively conserved sequences flanking the repeat. Suppose the total flanking contribution to an amplified fragment is 120 base pairs. The eight-repeat allele has length 120 + 2 × 8 = 136 base pairs, whereas the twelve-repeat allele has length 144 base pairs. The predicted heterozygote contains both products. The difference of eight base pairs corresponds to four repeat units, not eight repeat units. This simple calculation connects an electrophoretic size measurement with a molecular model of the allele.

Microsatellites are attractive in linkage experiments because one locus may contain several alleles. In a family where both parents have different repeat-length alleles, transmission of each parental copy can often be inferred. However, fragment length is not a unique sequence identifier. Independent changes can produce the same length, and an insertion in flanking DNA may alter product length without changing the repeat count. The size class must be interpreted as a measured marker allele rather than a complete sequence description.

PCR slippage can generate minor products called stutter peaks or bands, often differing by one repeat unit from a main product. A stutter product is not evidence of a third inherited allele in an ordinary diploid. Calling alleles requires a reliable interpretation of the signal pattern and awareness of the assay's resolution. Flanking-sequence variation can also lead to nonamplifying alleles. Such errors are especially important when an apparent homozygote has unexpected segregation among offspring. The biological model and the technical observation must be checked together.

Single nucleotide polymorphism

A single nucleotide polymorphism, abbreviated SNP, is a variable nucleotide position in a set of homologous sequences. For example, a reference sequence may contain an A at a position where another chromosome carries G. A diploid individual's genotype at that position could be AA, AG or GG, provided the locus is autosomal, diploid and uniquely assayed. A SNP is often biallelic, although more than two nucleotide states can exist in a population.

A SNP can be synonymous, nonsynonymous, regulatory or located in a region with no known direct function. The classification of the sequence difference depends on its genomic context, while its usefulness as a marker depends on whether the alternative forms can be scored and inherited informatively. A neutral SNP may be linked to a causal variant and can therefore track a trait. A functional SNP may have no detectable association with the trait under study. The word marker does not imply pathogenicity, benefit or functional neutrality.

Genotyping can be based on sequencing, allele-specific hybridization or other sequence-discriminating methods. Unlike fragment length scoring, the intended observation is the nucleotide identity at a defined position. A dense collection of SNPs can provide fine coverage of a genome. Nevertheless, a high number of markers does not create new meiotic crossovers. If several closely spaced SNPs are transmitted together in every sampled individual, they provide essentially the same segregation information for that family. They may delimit a physical interval but cannot resolve gene order within it by linkage data alone.

Missing genotype calls should be distinguished from confirmed absence of an allele. A sample with insufficient evidence for a genotype must not automatically be labelled homozygous reference. Systematic missingness in one genotype class can distort segregation ratios and create apparent linkage. When interpreting a genotype matrix, investigators need to distinguish biological variation from measurement uncertainty before calculating recombination frequencies.

From marker inheritance to genetic and physical maps

A genetic map represents relative positions inferred from inheritance, usually expressed in centimorgans. A physical map represents locations or distances along DNA, expressed in base pairs or their multiples. Although they describe the same chromosome, they are not proportional scales. Recombination probability varies along chromosomes because crossover events are not uniformly distributed. Two regions with the same physical length may therefore have very different genetic lengths.

Suppose a testcross has 500 offspring, of which 40 carry recombinant combinations at two codominant markers. The observed recombination fraction is 40 divided by 500, or 0.08. For a short interval this is approximately 8 centimorgans. The estimate assumes informative phase, accurate genotyping and no differential recovery of the parental and recombinant classes. A longer interval can contain multiple crossovers, including events that restore the parental arrangement at the two outer markers. Consequently, simple percentage recombination increasingly underestimates map distance as intervals become larger.

Consider an illustrative comparison in which markers A and B are 2 megabases apart but genetically separated by 1 centimorgan, while B and C are 2 megabases apart and genetically separated by 8 centimorgans. Both physical intervals are equal, but the inferred recombination rates are 0.5 and 4 centimorgans per megabase. The second interval has the greater observed rate. This does not demonstrate that every base in the second interval has an eightfold higher recombination probability; the average can reflect a localized hotspot, differences in chromatin or a variety of other factors.

Marker density and recombination sample size impose different limits. Increasing density supplies more landmarks at which to detect crossovers that actually occurred. Increasing the number of informative meioses increases the opportunity to observe crossovers within a short region. If no recombinants appear among 100 independent informative gametes, this is evidence that the fraction is low, but not proof of zero recombination or identical physical coordinates. Under a binomial model, the probability of zero observed recombinants at true fraction r is (1 − r)^100. At r = 0.01 this probability is approximately 0.366. A one-percent recombinant fraction is therefore quite compatible with the observation of zero in such a sample.

Marker mapping across plants, animals and bacteria

In plant genetics, crossing two lines with contrasting marker alleles and a trait of interest creates progeny in which recombination can separate the trait locus from adjacent markers. Selfing, backcrossing or the production of fixed mapping lines changes the distribution of genotype classes and the amount of recombination represented. The choice of population is addressed in the next node. For the present analysis, the key point is that the marker must distinguish parental ancestry and the inheritance model must correspond to the cross actually performed.

Animal linkage mapping may use controlled crosses in model species, existing pedigrees in humans or other documented family structures. A marker informative in one family may be uninformative in another, and sex-specific recombination rates may influence genetic distances. A rare trait can be followed with linked markers even if the trait's underlying molecular variant remains unknown. The association between a marker and a trait in a pedigree depends on the parental phase; the same marker allele can be associated with the alternate trait allele in another family.

Bacteria generally do not undergo the diploid meiosis on which conventional eukaryotic linkage maps are based. A molecular difference can still mark a chromosomal region, and sequence comparisons can establish physical coordinates. Transfer and recombination events may also reveal relationships between markers. The interpretation depends on the process supplying the inherited DNA, such as transformation, conjugation or transduction, each of which has its own mapping assumptions. A bacterial co-transfer fraction cannot simply be substituted into a eukaryotic centimorgan calculation. Subsequent nodes treat the distinct transfer models in greater depth.

Across all three systems, marker association is an observation that requires explanation. A marker may predict a phenotype because it is causal, because it is linked to a causal allele, because it is associated with ancestry, or because of sampling and scoring artifacts. An experimentally observed association is strongest when alternative explanations are addressed by the design. Linkage analysis can localize a gene, but identifying a causal change requires independent evidence connecting sequence, function and phenotype.

Somatic cell hybrid mapping and radiation hybrids

Somatic cell hybrid mapping uses a different type of genetic evidence from a meiotic cross. Cells from different species, classically human and rodent cells, can produce hybrids containing genetic material from both parental species. During propagation, different hybrid cell lines may retain different subsets of human chromosomes. If a human-specific gene product or DNA marker is detected only in lines retaining one particular human chromosome, the chromosome-retention pattern supports assignment of the locus to that chromosome.

Consider six hypothetical hybrid lines. Human chromosome 3 is retained in lines 1, 2 and 5; chromosome 7 in lines 1, 3 and 5; and the human-specific marker is detected in lines 1, 2 and 5. The marker and chromosome 3 have perfect concordance in this small panel: all three chromosome-positive lines are marker-positive and all three chromosome-negative lines are marker-negative. Chromosome 7 has two discordant lines: line 2 lacks the chromosome despite the marker being present, and line 3 retains the chromosome despite absence of the marker. The result favours chromosome 3, conditional on accurate chromosome and marker scoring.

Concordance is the proportion of lines in which marker and chromosome are both present or both absent. Discordance is the proportion in which their presence states differ. Here chromosome 3 has concordance 6/6 and chromosome 7 has concordance 4/6. If two chromosomes have identical retention patterns, both receive the same concordance score, and the panel cannot distinguish them. More independently informative hybrid lines or additional chromosomal information are needed.

A human chromosome assignment is not necessarily a precise gene position within that chromosome. Chromosomes can be lost, retained as fragments or rearranged, and gene-product expression can be influenced by the hybrid cellular context. A DNA-based assay of a human-specific sequence may avoid some expression-dependent ambiguity, but it does not eliminate errors arising from chromosome characterization. A strong interpretation requires evidence that the measured marker and retained chromosome segment are correctly identified.

Radiation hybrid mapping extends the retention principle to chromosome fragments. Chromosomal DNA is broken, and independently retained fragments create patterns of co-retention across hybrid lines. Nearby loci are more likely than distant loci to remain on the same unbroken fragment under the model, so patterns of joint retention can be used to infer relative proximity. Radiation hybrid distance depends on breakage and retention, not on meiotic crossover frequency. The centiray is a model-dependent radiation hybrid map unit; it must not be equated with the centimorgan or with a fixed number of base pairs. Radiation hybrid analysis provides historical and conceptual context for physical localization without requiring detailed computational treatment at this level.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. A molecular marker must encode a gene. A marker may be a noncoding variant with no known phenotypic effect. Its defining role is inherited detectability.
  2. A band-positive F2 individual is necessarily heterozygous. Dominant scoring combines both homozygous and heterozygous band-positive genotypes. The underlying 1:2:1 ratio becomes 3:1.
  3. Codominant marker scoring implies both alleles produce a physiological phenotype. It refers to detection of both alleles; it need not imply any physiological effect.
  4. A polymorphic marker is informative in every cross. The relevant parents must carry distinguishable alleles. Diversity in an unrelated population is insufficient.
  5. An RFLP probe detects every restriction fragment. A probe detects fragments containing a complementary sequence; most restriction fragments remain invisible.
  6. A larger restriction band proves an insertion. Loss of an internal restriction site can produce a longer detected fragment without changing total DNA length. Partial digestion can mimic it.
  7. RAPD products are all alleles of one locus. Different bands generally represent different amplified regions; assignment needs segregation or sequence evidence.
  8. AFLP is simply another name for RAPD. AFLP starts with restriction fragments and adaptor-associated selective amplification; RAPD uses arbitrary primer sites.
  9. An SSR product eight bases longer has eight additional repeats. For an unchanged dinucleotide-repeat locus, eight additional bases correspond to four extra repeats.
  10. Every minor SSR peak is an inherited allele. Stutter can result from amplification slippage. Diploid genotype calls must distinguish genuine alleles from technical products.
  11. All SNPs alter protein sequence. SNPs can occur in coding, regulatory or other regions; even a coding SNP may be synonymous.
  12. Missing genotype means homozygous reference. A missing call indicates inadequate evidence, not a confirmed genotype.
  13. One centimorgan always equals one megabase. Recombination varies with genomic region, organism, sex and other biological factors; no universal conversion exists.
  14. Zero observed recombinants proves zero distance. Finite sampling can fail to detect rare recombination. Additional informative meioses may reveal it.
  15. A bacterial cotransfer fraction can be converted directly to cM. Bacterial transfer mechanisms differ from diploid meiosis and require distinct mapping assumptions.
  16. Perfect somatic-hybrid concordance always identifies one chromosome. Two chromosomes with identical retention patterns are indistinguishable in that panel.
  17. A marker associated with a phenotype must be causal. Linkage or shared ancestry can generate association without a functional effect of the marker itself.

Revision and active recall

Molecular markers reveal inherited variation, but a marker's scoring properties depend on its assay and biological context. RFLP uses restriction-fragment length, RAPD arbitrary priming, AFLP selective amplification of restriction fragments, SSR repeat-length variation and SNP nucleotide identity. Genetic maps summarize recombination; physical maps locate DNA features; somatic hybrid mapping uses chromosome retention. None of these observations alone proves causation.

Active recall

  1. Explain the distinction between a marker allele, its detection method and a causal allele.
  2. Derive dominant and codominant F2 scoring ratios.
  3. Draw a restriction-site gain and predict the probe-positive bands.
  4. Explain the sequence requirements for a RAPD band.
  5. Contrast the sources of specificity in RAPD and AFLP.
  6. Calculate the repeat-number difference between two SSR alleles.
  7. Explain why a biallelic SNP can be informative in a cross.
  8. Distinguish physical distance from genetic distance.
  9. Predict how a finite sample can miss rare recombinants.
  10. Contrast eukaryotic linkage mapping and bacterial marker mapping.
  11. Construct a chromosome-retention concordance table.
  12. Explain why co-segregation is not sufficient evidence of causality.

Mastery checklist

  • Predict Mendelian marker scoring from genotypes.
  • Interpret restriction and repeat-length patterns.
  • Choose markers for a given cross and identify errors.
  • Calculate recombination fractions and retention concordance.
  • Distinguish linkage, physical location and causal evidence.

Original examination practice

These questions are original instructional exercises, not past examination questions. Numerical datasets are hypothetical.

Part B — concepts

1. Which feature makes a DNA polymorphism useful for linkage mapping?

  1. It always changes protein activity
  2. It is detectably inherited in the relevant family
  3. It is found only in exons
  4. It is always dominant
Answer and explanation

B. A marker must distinguish inherited chromosome forms in the family. Coding location, dominance and functional effect are not prerequisites.

2. Which observation distinguishes a codominant marker?

  1. Both alleles detectable in a heterozygote
  2. Only one genotype is viable
  3. Every genotype yields the same band
  4. The marker determines a dominant trait
Answer and explanation

A. Codominance in scoring means both allele forms can be observed. It need not determine a trait.

3. What is the expected observable ratio for a dominant band in a Mendelian F2?

  1. 1:1
  2. 1:2:1
  3. 3:1
  4. 9:7
Answer and explanation

C. DD and Dd are both band-positive, with combined probability 3/4. Only dd is negative.

4. In classical Southern RFLP analysis, what determines which fragments are visualized?

  1. Fragment size alone
  2. Hybridization to the probe
  3. Protein abundance
  4. Allele dominance
Answer and explanation

B. The probe reveals fragments carrying its complementary sequence, not all DNA fragments.

5. A restriction site is abolished between two unchanged flanking sites. What can happen?

  1. A longer restriction fragment appears
  2. All DNA becomes shorter
  3. The chromosome necessarily duplicates
  4. The marker must become lethal
Answer and explanation

A. The formerly adjacent fragments remain joined after digestion. Total length of the genomic region need not change.

6. Which is typical of RAPD?

  1. Known primers flanking a defined repeat only
  2. Arbitrary short primers sampling multiple loci
  3. Detection exclusively by Southern hybridization
  4. A requirement for a complete reference genome
Answer and explanation

B. RAPD surveys amplifiable regions using arbitrary primers; a finished reference genome is not required.

7. Which sequence configuration permits a conventional RAPD product?

  1. One isolated binding site
  2. Two compatible sites oriented toward one another
  3. Two sites oriented outward
  4. Only an RNA target
Answer and explanation

B. Opposing primer sites within an amplifiable interval define a product.

8. What differentiates AFLP from RAPD?

  1. AFLP uses restriction fragments and selective amplification
  2. AFLP cannot use PCR
  3. RAPD always requires a known locus sequence
  4. AFLP detects protein variants
Answer and explanation

A. AFLP defines fragments by restriction and adaptor-associated amplification; RAPD uses arbitrary priming.

9. What is a microsatellite?

  1. A tandem array of short DNA motifs
  2. A chromosome-specific protein
  3. A single restriction enzyme
  4. A whole mitochondrial genome
Answer and explanation

A. SSRs are tandem short sequence repeats, often polymorphic in copy number.

10. What is a common molecular origin of SSR length variation?

  1. Ribosomal translocation
  2. Replication-associated slippage
  3. Protein phosphorylation
  4. Independent assortment without mutation
Answer and explanation

B. Slippage during DNA replication can change repeat copy number. Assortment redistributes alleles rather than generating this change.

11. Which is a typical SNP genotype at a diploid A/G locus?

  1. AG
  2. AAGGG
  3. CA repeated twelve times
  4. An 8 kb Southern fragment only
Answer and explanation

A. AG is a heterozygous genotype at a biallelic nucleotide position.

12. Which marker class is commonly multiallelic at a single locus?

  1. SSR
  2. Typical biallelic SNP
  3. Dominant RAPD band
  4. Dominant AFLP band
Answer and explanation

A. Many repeat-length alleles can exist at one SSR locus.

13. What does a physical map measure?

  1. Distance in nucleotide units
  2. Fraction of heterozygotes
  3. Number of marker phenotypes
  4. Probability of dominance
Answer and explanation

A. Physical maps locate genomic sequence features in base pairs, unlike genetic maps in recombination-based units.

14. A centimorgan describes what type of distance?

  1. Recombination-based genetic distance
  2. Fixed number of base pairs
  3. Protein molecular mass
  4. Chromosome count
Answer and explanation

A. It is a genetic map unit; approximately 1% recombinant gametes only in a sufficiently short interval.

15. What is the usual scoring of anonymous RAPD bands?

  1. Dominant presence or absence
  2. Always fully codominant
  3. A four-allele genotype at every band
  4. A continuous enzyme assay
Answer and explanation

A. A single band generally cannot distinguish one versus two copies of its amplifiable allele.

16. Which feature commonly improves locus specificity in SSR scoring?

  1. Primers in unique flanking DNA
  2. No primers
  3. Random chromosome loss
  4. Ignoring product size
Answer and explanation

A. Defined flanking sequences target the repeat locus and permit allele-size comparisons.

17. Why does bacterial mapping require special assumptions?

  1. Bacteria generally lack diploid meiosis
  2. Bacterial DNA contains no markers
  3. All bacteria have identical sequences
  4. Bacteria cannot recombine
Answer and explanation

A. Marker relationships may be inferred from transfer and recombination or physical coordinates rather than conventional meiotic segregation.

18. Somatic cell hybrid mapping primarily uses which evidence?

  1. Chromosome-retention and marker concordance
  2. F2 dominance ratios
  3. SSR stutter intensity only
  4. Independent assortment in bacterial meiosis
Answer and explanation

A. Hybrid clones retain different chromosome subsets; marker concordance supports assignment.

19. What is a radiation hybrid map based on?

  1. Fragment breakage and co-retention
  2. Frequency of meiotic tetratypes
  3. Protein mass
  4. A universal cM-to-bp conversion
Answer and explanation

A. Nearby sequences tend to co-retain following fragment formation under the radiation hybrid model.

20. What does a missing SNP genotype call mean?

  1. Confirmed absence of every allele
  2. Insufficient evidence for a reliable genotype
  3. Confirmed reference homozygote
  4. A new deletion in every case
Answer and explanation

B. Missing calls are uncertainty, not established allelic state.

Part C — analytical reasoning

21. An F2 shows 26 AA, 48 AB and 26 BB at one SSR locus. Which inference is most appropriate?

  1. Grossly incompatible with Mendelian segregation
  2. Consistent with approximately 1:2:1 scoring
  3. Dominant presence/absence only
  4. Evidence of complete linkage to an unknown locus
Answer and explanation

B. The expected counts are 25, 50, 25. Chi-square = 0.04 + 0.08 + 0.04 = 0.16, so this sample gives little evidence against the expected single-locus ratio.

22. A dominant marker segregates 75 present and 25 absent in 100 F2 progeny. How many of the positive individuals are expected to be heterozygotes?

  1. 25
  2. 50
  3. 75
  4. 100
Answer and explanation

B. Among the expected 100 progeny, 25 DD, 50 Dd and 25 dd occur. The 75 positive individuals include 50 expected heterozygotes.

23. A Dd × dd testcross gives 52 positive and 48 negative progeny. What can the positive calls identify assuming error-free scoring?

  1. DD and Dd together
  2. Dd offspring
  3. dd offspring
  4. Neither parental allele
Answer and explanation

B. The dd parent always contributes d. Positive offspring must carry D from the other parent and therefore are Dd.

24. An 8 kb region is cut into 3 and 5 kb fragments in allele A, remains intact in B, and the unique probe lies in the 5 kb region. What appears in AB?

  1. 3 and 5 kb
  2. 5 and 8 kb
  3. 3 and 8 kb
  4. 8 kb only
Answer and explanation

B. The probe binds the 5 kb fragment from A and the intact 8 kb fragment from B. The 3 kb fragment lacks the probe target.

25. A known homozygous cut-site allele unexpectedly gives a faint intact fragment as well as its usual product. Which check should precede a heterozygote call?

  1. Confirm digestion completeness
  2. Assign a new gene immediately
  3. Assume linkage equilibrium
  4. Ignore controls
Answer and explanation

A. Incomplete digestion can preserve a fraction of intact DNA, mimicking an uncut allele. A controlled repeat is necessary before genetic interpretation.

26. An SSR assay has a constant 120 bp flanking contribution and a trinucleotide repeat. Alleles are 141 and 153 bp. What are their repeat counts?

  1. 7 and 11
  2. 21 and 33
  3. 7 and 15
  4. 47 and 51
Answer and explanation

A. Subtract 120 to obtain 21 and 33 bp of repeat DNA. Divide each by three, yielding seven and eleven copies.

27. Two SSR alleles are both 150 bp but sequencing finds different internal motifs. What does this establish?

  1. Equal size guarantees sequence identity
  2. The size assay may merge distinct sequence alleles
  3. Both alleles must be null
  4. SSR loci cannot be polymorphic
Answer and explanation

B. Equal electrophoretic lengths do not imply identical sequence; the assay distinguishes size classes rather than every sequence variant.

28. A four-allele SSR has frequencies 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125. Expected heterozygosity is?

  1. 0.34375
  2. 0.65625
  3. 0.75
  4. 0.875
Answer and explanation

B. The squared frequencies sum to 0.25 + 0.0625 + 0.015625 + 0.015625 = 0.34375. Subtraction from one yields 0.65625.

29. An SSR is highly variable globally, but both mapping parents are AA. A biallelic SNP is CC in one parent and TT in the other. Which is informative for parental ancestry?

  1. SSR only
  2. SNP only
  3. Both automatically
  4. Neither because SNPs are biallelic
Answer and explanation

B. The SNP distinguishes the two parental chromosomes; the identical SSR alleles cannot track parental origin.

30. A testcross records 170 AB, 170 ab, 30 Ab and 30 aB. What is the recombinant fraction assuming AB/ab phase?

  1. 0.075
  2. 0.15
  3. 0.425
  4. 0.85
Answer and explanation

B. Ab and aB are recombinant. The fraction is (30 + 30)/400 = 0.15; a simple 15 cM estimate is only approximate.

31. For 200 informative gametes and true recombinant fraction 0.005, what is the probability of observing none?

  1. About 0.367
  2. Exactly zero
  3. Exactly 0.005
  4. About 0.995
Answer and explanation

A. Under independent sampling, the probability is 0.995^200, approximately 0.367. No observed recombinants is compatible with low nonzero recombination.

32. Two intervals both span 1 Mb. Genetic lengths are 0.2 and 5 cM. Which inference follows?

  1. Physical map is necessarily erroneous
  2. Mean recombination rate differs 25-fold
  3. Both have equal genetic length
  4. Each has the same crossover probability per base
Answer and explanation

B. The average rates are 0.2 and 5 cM/Mb. Their ratio is 25; the observation does not specify individual base-level probabilities.

33. Three markers have identical segregation across every member of a cross. Which intervention directly increases opportunities for observing a separation?

  1. More informative meioses
  2. Renaming alleles
  3. Changing units to kb
  4. Discarding heterozygotes
Answer and explanation

A. More sampled meioses increase the chance of detecting recombinants. Marker renaming and unit changes add no biological information.

34. A RAPD band appears in one of three independent assays of the same DNA. What is the best initial interpretation?

  1. A stable inherited variant has been proven
  2. The band is technically unreliable pending validation
  3. A new chromosome arose
  4. The sample is always heterozygous
Answer and explanation

B. Repeated assays of the same template should support consistent scoring. Inconsistent bands cannot yet be used as stable genetic landmarks.

35. Two AFLP products from distantly related samples migrate to the same position. What remains uncertain?

  1. Whether they represent homologous genomic loci
  2. Whether they have finite length
  3. Whether they can migrate
  4. Whether DNA has nucleotides
Answer and explanation

A. Distinct fragments can co-migrate. Size agreement alone does not establish sequence homology or allelism.

36. A SNP panel has many missing calls exclusively in one parental genotype. What is the principal mapping concern?

  1. Biased recovery of genotype classes
  2. Increased crossover rate proved
  3. Universal physical map accuracy
  4. Loss of all polymorphism
Answer and explanation

A. Genotype-dependent missingness can distort segregation and estimated linkage. Missing calls should not be treated as a confirmed reference genotype.

37. In six hybrids, marker states are +,+,−,−,+,−. Chromosome X states are +,−,−,−,+,+. How many discordant hybrids occur?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

C. Lines 2 and 6 disagree; the remaining four agree. Discordance is 2/6.

38. Two human chromosomes have identical retention profiles in a hybrid panel, both matching the marker perfectly. What is required to distinguish them?

  1. Additional informative hybrids or independent chromosomal evidence
  2. An arbitrary choice of the smaller chromosome
  3. Convert concordance to cM
  4. Ignore marker-negative clones
Answer and explanation

A. Identical profiles contain no distinguishing evidence. An independent observation must break the tie.

39. A chromosome-positive hybrid lacks the expected human gene product but contains its DNA sequence. What explanation must be considered?

  1. Expression failure in the hybrid context
  2. Gene absence is definitively established
  3. A meiotic crossover necessarily occurred
  4. The marker is always causal
Answer and explanation

A. The DNA assay indicates retention of the sequence; expression may be reduced or absent. Product detection and DNA detection measure different properties.

40. A marker predicts a trait in one pedigree but the opposite marker allele predicts it in another. Which explanation is plausible?

  1. Different parental linkage phases
  2. The law of segregation never applies
  3. The SNP must change function between pedigrees
  4. Physical distance is always zero
Answer and explanation

A. A neutral marker can be in coupling with different causal alleles in different families. Marker association does not establish functional causation.

Additional applied and assertion–reason questions

41. Assertion–reason: A highly polymorphic SSR may be uninformative in a particular cross. Reason: Both mapping parents may have the same allele.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

A. Population variability does not guarantee parental contrast; identical alleles cannot distinguish ancestry.

42. Assertion–reason: Dominant marker scoring loses heterozygote information in an F2. Reason: DD and Dd can produce the same signal.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false
  4. Reason false
Answer and explanation

A. The observation merges two underlying genotype classes; this is the direct source of information loss.

43. Which pair is correctly matched?

  1. RFLP — fragment length after restriction
  2. SSR — antibody affinity
  3. SNP — chromosome number
  4. RAPD — obligatory Southern probe
Answer and explanation

A. RFLP detects restriction-fragment size differences. The other pairings confuse distinct methods or biological properties.

44. A marker and phenotype co-segregate in every offspring examined. Which next evidence most directly supports causality?

  1. Functional evidence linking the candidate variant to the phenotype
  2. Increasing the marker's font size
  3. Calling all offspring homozygous
  4. Converting physical distance into cM by a universal constant
Answer and explanation

A. Functional evidence can distinguish the causal variant from a nearby marker that merely travels with it.

45. A marker band segregates 1:1 in a haploid sample. Which conclusion is defensible?

  1. It may distinguish two haploid allele states
  2. It proves diploid codominance
  3. It proves all samples are heterozygous
  4. It proves a 3:1 F2 ratio
Answer and explanation

A. Haploids have one allele at a single-copy locus. A 1:1 segregation pattern does not demonstrate diploid scoring properties.

46. A plant researcher wants to recognize AA, AB and BB in an F2. Which is generally preferable if informative and reliable?

  1. Codominant SSR
  2. Anonymous dominant RAPD band
  3. An invariant SNP
  4. A missing call
Answer and explanation

A. A codominant SSR can separate the three genotypes. A dominant band merges AA and AB, and an invariant marker has no parental contrast.

47. A researcher compares 20 kb of DNA from two bacterial strains. What is the natural unit for the sequence distance between two identified sites?

  1. Base pairs
  2. Centimorgans inferred from diploid meiosis
  3. Centirays without hybrid data
  4. Phenotypic variance
Answer and explanation

A. Sequence coordinates define physical distance. The other units require different evidence or are unrelated.

48. A radiation hybrid map shows frequent co-retention of two loci. Which inference is conditional on the mapping model?

  1. The loci may be physically close
  2. The loci are always the same allele
  3. The loci are necessarily causally related
  4. The loci must have zero meiotic recombination
Answer and explanation

A. Co-retention suggests proximity given breakage and retention assumptions, but it is not proof of identity or causation.

49. A marker allele is detectable in a parent but absent from every offspring expected to inherit it. Which possibilities need investigation?

  1. Scoring error, non-Mendelian recovery or incorrect pedigree assumptions
  2. Only a universal change in genetic distance
  3. Only SNP causation
  4. No possible explanation
Answer and explanation

A. Unexpected segregation has multiple potential causes; assay validity, biological transmission and sample relationships must be checked.

50. Which approach best distinguishes a reproducible marker from a transient laboratory artifact?

  1. Independent scoring and consistent inheritance
  2. A single weak band
  3. Assuming all extra bands are genes
  4. Ignoring negative controls
Answer and explanation

A. Reproducibility and inheritance provide complementary evidence of a reliable genetic marker.
