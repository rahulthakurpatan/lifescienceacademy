From visible traits to molecular landmarks

A genetic map answers a question that a description of a phenotype cannot answer by itself: where in the inherited material is the difference that produces the phenotype? Consider two varieties of a plant that differ in flowering time. Their leaves, roots and flowers may contain thousands of inherited differences, most of which have no visible effect on flowering. A cross between the varieties creates offspring in which chromosome segments are redistributed. If the offspring are scored for flowering time alone, one can investigate the inheritance of the trait but cannot easily identify the chromosome segment responsible. If many identifiable DNA differences are also scored, the transmission of each segment can be followed. The relationship between flowering and the inheritance of a particular DNA difference is the beginning of molecular genetic mapping.

A locus is a position or defined region in a genome. Alternative sequence forms at that locus are alleles. A molecular marker is an experimentally recognizable DNA variation used to distinguish inherited genetic material. A marker may be a single nucleotide, a difference in repeat number, an insertion or deletion, or a restriction fragment length difference. The important feature is that the observed molecular state can be connected reliably to a defined inherited locus. A DNA sequence shared without detectable variation by all members of a cross can serve as a physical landmark but is not an informative segregating marker in that cross. This difference between a landmark and an informative allele is fundamental to the entire chapter.

The idea extends the linkage principles introduced in the earlier lessons. Mendelian inheritance allows a pair of homologous chromosomes to contribute alternative alleles to gametes. Crossing over between homologues can break associations between loci on the same chromosome. Therefore, a nearby marker and trait allele are usually inherited together, whereas a distant marker has more opportunities to become separated from the trait allele. This prediction is statistical, not absolute. One recombinant offspring can be a source of positional information rather than an exception to be discarded. The map emerges by comparing many independent transmissions.

Early genetic mapping relied heavily on visible mutant phenotypes, such as differences in eye or body colour. These traits provided useful landmarks but were unevenly distributed, sometimes difficult to score, and often influenced by environment or genetic background. DNA-based variation expanded the number of available markers far beyond the number of convenient visible traits. Botstein and colleagues proposed in 1980 that informative restriction fragment polymorphisms could provide a framework for mapping human inherited disease loci through family transmission. Their conceptual advance was to separate the ability to follow a chromosome segment from knowledge of the biochemical function of the gene located there.

A road atlas provides a limited analogy. A bridge can help identify a neighbourhood even when the bridge did not cause a particular event in that neighbourhood. A polymorphic DNA marker can similarly point toward a chromosome region without producing the trait under investigation. The analogy breaks down because the marker and trait are transmitted through a dynamic process involving recombination, and genetic distances measure that process rather than a fixed number of base pairs. Two physically identical distances may have very different genetic distances. A student must keep location, transmission and biological causation as separate ideas.

The transition from a phenotypic marker to a molecular marker changes the scale of an investigation but does not replace the rules of genetics. A phenotype such as a recessive white flower is a convenient marker if all homozygotes can be identified and if the trait has no major environmental variability. The use of a marker based on DNA bypasses the need for its expression. Even an intergenic polymorphism can reveal which parental chromosome segment entered the offspring. It is important to recognize that the marker itself is a phenotype of the laboratory assay: a detectable molecular measurement reflects a genotype through a known or tested relationship. It can still be affected by experimental conditions. A marker does not become infallible simply because it is molecular.

The first step in developing a marker for mapping is to establish the relevant biological comparison. Imagine that a researcher has DNA from two plants that are highly inbred and produce reliably different seed colours. The samples are screened for sequence variants. Many variants will be shared between them and cannot distinguish parental ancestry; a smaller set will differ. For each candidate marker, one must establish whether both parents possess a single interpretable allele at the corresponding locus, and whether the first-generation hybrid has the expected combined genotype. This first-generation verification catches problems that might otherwise be misinterpreted as recombination later. A marker at which the first-generation hybrid is unexpectedly scored as identical to one parent might be dominant, might have a null allele, or might have failed technically.

A genetic map is often initially represented as linkage groups rather than a named list of chromosomes. A linkage group is a set of markers connected by statistically supported patterns of cotransmission. Before physical anchors are added, the group may not be known to correspond to chromosome 3 or chromosome 7, and its direction may be arbitrary. Furthermore, an incomplete genetic map may contain several separate groups corresponding to different regions of a single chromosome when the available markers do not bridge the regions. An observed number of groups exceeding the haploid chromosome number does not by itself establish that the organism contains additional chromosomes. Physical assignments and more informative markers can connect the fragments.

A molecular marker is sometimes called anonymous when its functional role or exact genomic position is not known. Anonymous markers were particularly important before complete genome sequences became common. Such a marker can be valuable if its inheritance is reliably scored. To move from an anonymous linkage marker to a defined molecular coordinate, one may establish the sequence of the underlying fragment and identify a unique position in the genome. The qualification unique matters because repetitive sequences or paralogous loci can match at several sites. A map constructed from ambiguous sequence matches may appear precise while actually assigning a marker to the wrong copy.

A molecular genotype also differs from a phenotype that is used as a selectable marker. A selectable marker allows cells or individuals with a particular allele to survive or reproduce under a defined condition; a scorable molecular marker need not influence survival. Selection changes which individuals become available for analysis and can therefore change observed genotype proportions. If the goal is to estimate recombination, the investigator must know whether the recovery process favours one genotype. A marker that is simply detected after all offspring have been sampled generally avoids that particular source of bias, although missing calls can introduce a different form of biased recovery.

Allelic states and dominant versus codominant scoring

A molecular assay does not observe an abstract allele directly. It observes a signal, such as the length of a DNA fragment, a fluorescence intensity or a sequence read. The analyst must convert this signal into an allele call using the expected relationship between DNA structure and the measurement. A codominantly scored marker permits the two alleles of a diploid individual to be distinguished simultaneously. For a marker with alleles A and B, both homozygotes and the heterozygote are distinguishable. A dominant presence or absence marker generally cannot separate individuals carrying one detectable copy from those carrying two.

These terms describe the scoring properties of a marker system and should not be confused with dominance of a biological trait. If a repeat-length allele produces a fragment of 150 base pairs and another produces 158 base pairs, an AB heterozygote normally displays both fragment sizes. Neither DNA fragment must suppress the other. A disease trait linked to this marker could nevertheless be recessive, dominant or incompletely penetrant. The marker signal and the disease phenotype obey different measurement rules, even when their inheritance is correlated.

Consider a diploid cross AA × aa. All first-generation progeny inherit A from one parent and a from the other. With self-fertilization or crossing of equivalent heterozygotes, the F2 genotype probabilities are one-quarter AA, one-half Aa and one-quarter aa, assuming ordinary segregation and equal viability. A codominant marker reveals these three categories. If A produces a detectable band and a does not, AA and Aa are combined into the same presence class. The observed F2 ratio becomes three presence to one absence, without any change in the underlying genotype probabilities. A one-to-one backcross to aa would give distinguishable Aa and aa classes, so dominance does not make a marker useless in every cross.

A band is not automatically equivalent to a locus. A complex electrophoretic pattern may contain multiple fragments of similar length that migrate to the same apparent position. Conversely, a single locus may produce several bands because of the molecular structure of the assay. A useful marker is established by reproducible scoring and a defensible locus assignment, often supported by inheritance in a controlled family or by sequence information. The visibility of a band is also affected by DNA quality, amplification efficiency and detection thresholds. A missing band should be coded as missing data when the experiment cannot distinguish assay failure from a genuine absence allele.

Genotyping errors have consequences beyond the loss of a correct label. A single false genotype between two otherwise consistent flanking markers can appear to create two crossovers within a very short region. If errors are not recognized, maps become longer than the real pattern of transmission supports. Quality control includes checking the parent and first-generation patterns, comparing replicate assays, monitoring missing calls, and examining whether the observed offspring classes are plausible under the chosen cross. Distorted segregation can arise from genuine biology, such as differential survival or transmission, so a deviation from the expected ratio is a reason for investigation rather than automatic deletion of a marker.

Consider two codominant loci in a diploid cross, one measured by fragment length and the other by a nucleotide-specific signal. At a fragment locus, the two parents show products of 140 and 154 base pairs respectively. The hybrid produces two peaks corresponding to those lengths. At the nucleotide locus, one parent has C at the position of interest and the other has T. The hybrid carries both alleles. Despite the different molecular readouts, both marker loci can be encoded as parental homozygote 1, heterozygote, and parental homozygote 2. Encoding genotypes in this common format makes it possible to combine different marker technologies in one genetic analysis without implying that the molecular methods work identically.

The difference between signal intensity and allele number requires care. A heterozygous product may produce a weaker peak than the same product in a homozygote, but PCR efficiency, competition among templates, DNA loading and detection saturation complicate this relationship. A simple presence marker does not become codominant merely because some bands look brighter than others. Reliable dosage inference requires a validated quantitative measurement. In a diploid map the usual question is whether each homologous chromosome can be identified. In a polyploid plant several homologous chromosome copies may carry different marker alleles, so even a codominant marker may require allele dosage information to reconstruct inheritance accurately. This chapter assumes diploidy unless otherwise stated.

One of the most revealing genotyping checks is parental compatibility. If two homozygous parents carry alleles A and B and there is no mutation, a genuine first-generation offspring at that locus should be AB. A C allele in a supposed offspring should prompt a check of sample identity, marker specificity, contamination and pedigree records. A rare de novo mutation is possible but is not the first explanation to accept in a large dataset with several incompatible calls. Similarly, when the only expected F2 alleles are A and B, frequent third-allele calls may indicate that several loci are being detected together or that samples have been mixed.

In an illustrative F2 dataset, the codominant counts are 48 AA, 104 Aa and 48 aa. The expected values for a total of 200 under Mendelian segregation are 50, 100 and 50. The Pearson chi-square statistic is the sum of squared differences divided by expected counts. It is 4/50 + 16/100 + 4/50 = 0.32, with two degrees of freedom when the three class probabilities are fixed in advance. Such small deviations are compatible with ordinary sampling variation. Combining AA and Aa gives 152 band-positive and 48 band-negative observations, with expected counts of 150 and 50; the corresponding statistic is 4/150 + 4/50, approximately 0.107. The dominant assay has collapsed genotype information even though both sets of counts fit their expected ratios.

The same genotype labels mean different things in different experimental designs. In a backcross to the band-present parent, all offspring of Aa × AA carry at least one A allele, and a dominant marker cannot distinguish the two classes. Backcrossing to aa instead produces band presence or absence in a one-to-one expected ratio. A student should not memorize a fixed rule that dominant markers are suitable or unsuitable for backcross mapping. The tester genotype determines whether the variable gamete can be read from the assay. In a haploid segregant, a presence or absence state can identify the only copy of a locus, so the diploid distinction between one and two copies is not relevant.

Restriction fragment length polymorphism

A restriction endonuclease recognizes a particular DNA sequence and cleaves DNA at or near that sequence. The positions of its recognition sites determine the lengths of DNA pieces obtained after digestion. A nucleotide substitution may remove an existing site or create a new site. Insertions or deletions can change the distance between unchanged sites. In either case, DNA molecules from different alleles can generate fragments of different lengths. Restriction fragment length polymorphism, abbreviated RFLP, describes this detectable difference rather than a particular mutation mechanism.

Imagine that two constant restriction sites enclose a 900-base-pair segment. In allele R, an additional site 300 base pairs from one end divides the interval into 300- and 600-base-pair fragments. In allele r the internal site is absent and a 900-base-pair fragment remains. If a probe hybridizes exclusively within the 600-base-pair portion, the R allele produces a 600-base-pair detected signal and r produces a 900-base-pair signal. A heterozygote produces both detected sizes. The 300-base-pair piece is real DNA but is not detected by this particular probe. Examinations often ask about the visible bands, so the position of the probe must be read as carefully as the restriction map.

The classical genomic RFLP workflow separates a complex digest by gel electrophoresis, transfers the separated DNA to a membrane, and identifies complementary fragments using a labelled nucleic acid probe. In Southern hybridization, the DNA is the target on the membrane; a northern blot concerns RNA and a western blot concerns protein. The probe provides specificity because a complete genomic digest contains many fragments that would otherwise overlap in a stained gel. A suitable single-copy probe allows fragment length states to be related to one chromosomal region.

A related strategy is PCR-RFLP, in which a known region is amplified and the resulting product is examined after restriction digestion. Here a gel can often be interpreted without a genomic Southern blot because the starting amplicon is defined by primers. Suppose an amplicon has a length of 500 base pairs. One allele remains uncut and the other yields 200- and 300-base-pair fragments. Under complete digestion the heterozygote produces all three sizes. The classical probe example and the PCR-RFLP example are not contradictory: the former observes only fragments complementary to the probe, whereas the latter can observe all detectable digestion products within the amplified target.

Partial digestion is an important confounder. If a cutting allele fails to be fully digested, the uncut product may coexist with the expected smaller fragments, imitating a heterozygote. A complete digestion control and known parental patterns are therefore informative. A mutation outside the recognition site can also change a fragment length, so observing a length difference does not by itself identify the causal nucleotide change. Sequencing is needed when the precise variant is important.

Classical RFLPs were valuable for developing linkage maps before dense sequence-based genotyping was widely available. Their strengths include interpretable locus-specific inheritance and often codominant scoring. Their disadvantages include substantial sample and time requirements for classical Southern-based assays, low throughput relative to modern SNP genotyping, and the requirement for a detectable restriction fragment difference. A PCR-RFLP can remain a practical targeted assay when the relevant restriction polymorphism is known. The choice between methods depends on the question, not solely on the age of a technology.

Restriction mapping and restriction fragment polymorphism are related but answer different questions. Restriction mapping seeks to locate recognition sites on a DNA molecule by comparing fragment lengths obtained with different enzymes or combinations of enzymes. RFLP scoring compares inherited fragment patterns among individuals at a locus. Suppose an investigator knows that an allele produces two pieces of a 1000-base-pair PCR product following digestion, of lengths 400 and 600 base pairs. Their sum agrees with the product length. This relationship supports the existence of one internal cut under the stated model, but it cannot tell whether the site lies 400 bases from the left end or from the right end without asymmetric information. The identity of a flanking primer or an additional restriction site can resolve orientation.

The interpretation of a Southern blot depends on hybridization specificity as well as fragment generation. A probe that spans a site and contains sufficiently long complementary segments on both sides can detect both fragments of a cut allele. A probe that lies entirely on one side detects only the corresponding fragment, provided the other pieces lack complementary sequences. A repeated probe might hybridize to multiple unrelated positions. Thus the genotype expected from an RFLP diagram cannot be inferred solely by counting all restriction fragments. Students should trace which fragments contain probe-complementary DNA before drawing the predicted gel.

A locus-specific fragment pattern can be complicated by restriction sites outside the region of immediate interest. Classical genomic RFLP analysis measures the fragment that contains the probe sequence, with endpoints at the relevant nearest sites in each direction. A mutation several kilobases away from the probe can change the measured fragment length even though the probe binding sequence remains invariant. This explains why a probe derived from a gene can reveal inherited variation that is not inside the coding sequence of that gene. The distinction is central when interpreting old linkage studies in which RFLPs were often described by the name of a nearby gene or anonymous cloned fragment.

The physical process of electrophoresis introduces another layer of interpretation. Negatively charged DNA migrates through a gel in an electric field, and smaller linear fragments generally move farther than larger ones under comparable conditions. A size standard provides reference migration positions. It is not safe to assume that equal spacing on the gel corresponds to equal differences in base-pair length; the relationship depends on the gel system and is often approximately logarithmic over a useful range. Two fragments of the same length can migrate together, making a single band brighter without changing its position. This is relevant in digestion problems when the two cut fragments happen to have equal lengths.

Consider a 600-base-pair product with a polymorphic site exactly in the middle. One homozygote shows only a 600-base-pair band; the other produces two fragments of 300 base pairs, which migrate together as a single size class. The heterozygote shows bands at 600 and 300 base pairs. There are two visible sizes but three types of molecular products counting the two equal-length cut fragments separately. If a question asks how many bands appear, the correct answer depends on resolved sizes rather than the count of molecules. If digestion is incomplete, a cut homozygote may also show the larger band; controls are needed to distinguish technical and inherited causes.

Not all restriction enzymes recognize the same number of nucleotides. Under a simplified model of independent, equally frequent bases, a particular nondegenerate six-base motif occurs with probability one in 4096 at a given start position, whereas a four-base motif occurs approximately one in 256. This provides intuition for why enzymes with shorter recognition sequences generally produce more fragments in random DNA. Real DNA has unequal composition, repeated motifs, structural constraints and sequence contexts that affect site frequency. Some enzymes also respond to DNA modification. The model is useful for explaining fragment abundance and not for predicting the exact digest of an unsequenced chromosome.

In mapping, the historical importance of RFLPs is not that restriction enzymes cause meiotic recombination. Digestion occurs in an assay on extracted DNA; the crossover being inferred occurred during the earlier biological transmission. The laboratory cleavage creates observable molecular fragments, whereas inherited chromosome exchanges determine which alleles occur together in the sampled offspring. Confusing these two processes leads to incorrect explanations of the relationship between restriction sites and genetic map distances.

Random amplified polymorphic DNA

PCR normally uses primers designed to flank a known region. Random amplified polymorphic DNA, or RAPD, instead uses an arbitrary short primer to amplify multiple genomic segments under conditions that permit that primer to bind at several suitable sites. A product can accumulate when two binding sites lie on opposite strands in an orientation that directs DNA synthesis toward the intervening segment, and the intervening distance is compatible with amplification. If a sequence difference prevents one binding event or alters the intervening segment, a visible product may change or disappear.

The apparent simplicity of the resulting pattern masks an important distinction. A RAPD band may represent a heritable DNA difference, but the usual presence or absence score cannot distinguish one amplifiable allele from two. In a diploid population a band-positive individual could be homozygous or heterozygous for the amplifiable form. A band-negative individual might be homozygous for the non-amplifiable form, provided the assay worked correctly. Replication and parental controls are particularly valuable because technical variation in primer binding or DNA quality can produce an apparent absence unrelated to inheritance.

RAPD was introduced by Williams and colleagues in 1990 as a way of recognizing DNA differences using arbitrary primers. Its appeal was that investigators did not first need to sequence the entire genome to develop potentially useful markers. This was especially useful in species for which little prior sequence information was available. A panel of primers could reveal multiple variable signals, which could be tested for inheritance and used to construct a map or distinguish accessions.

A strong band in one parent and its absence in another can be examined in offspring. If a cross yields 75 band-positive and 25 band-negative offspring out of 100 F2 plants, the observations are consistent with one dominant marker segregating at a single locus, although the fit alone is not proof of the molecular identity of that band. If two apparently identical bands in unrelated populations arose from different genome regions, treating them as the same allele would distort estimates of relatedness. The problem is less severe when a specific inherited band has been isolated and its sequence-based identity established. Sequence-characterized amplified region markers can be developed from useful anonymous bands to improve specificity.

RAPD is therefore best understood as a low-prior-information fingerprinting and marker discovery approach with limitations in reproducibility and locus interpretation. It is not a method for reading a genome sequence, and it does not normally distinguish the two alleles of a heterozygote. The fact that PCR produces a large amount of DNA does not make every amplified fragment a reliable marker.

A typical single-primer RAPD pattern depends on many potential priming sites distributed throughout the genome. To understand why a product has defined ends, trace the direction of polymerase extension. On one strand the primer must point toward the interval; on the complementary strand another primer-binding site must point back toward the first. Two sites in the same orientation do not define a conventional exponentially amplified product between them. The intervening distance must also be compatible with the assay. This reasoning explains why merely counting sites matching the primer sequence cannot predict the number of bands.

Loss of a product can be caused by a base change at a priming site, a deletion containing one of the sites, or a structural change that separates the sites too far for effective amplification. A new product can arise if a new compatible priming site appears. Length changes within a retained amplicon may produce a shifted band. These are alternative explanations of the observed pattern, not distinct genotypes that can be distinguished automatically by scoring a single gel. The strength of a RAPD marker lies in the reproducibility of its inherited pattern rather than in direct identification of the causal mutation.

In the early use of RAPD, a common goal was to screen many primers and identify bands specific to one parent in a mapping cross. A band that appears in all offspring regardless of parentage cannot inform linkage in that cross. A weak band that varies from replicate to replicate is also a poor candidate. A reliable polymorphic band can be followed through the progeny and compared with other markers. If the parental DNA samples and the first-generation hybrid are run repeatedly, stable polymorphic signals can be distinguished from reaction-dependent variability.

Suppose a dominant RAPD band P and a codominant marker Q are both heterozygous in a parent with haplotypes PQ/pq. The tester is pq/pq, so the observed presence of band P identifies the P allele from the heterozygote, and its absence identifies p if the assay is valid. The Q assay can distinguish the Qq and qq progeny. Under this design the combinations of these assay states directly identify the four gamete types. If the tester instead carried PP, all offspring would have the P band and that marker would lose its ability to report the variable transmission. The same assay can be informative or uninformative depending on the genetic design.

A fingerprint is useful for sample comparison but is not automatically a phylogenetic tree or a genetic map. To build a map one observes segregation across a biologically meaningful family or experimental population. To estimate relatedness one compares shared and differing marker states under a model appropriate to the marker and the sampled populations. An apparent shared RAPD band is less persuasive when fragments of the same mobility could arise independently. An anonymous marker identified within a particular biparental cross may be perfectly useful locally even if it cannot be confidently compared across distant species. These distinctions explain why choice of technology should follow the inferential question.

Amplified fragment length polymorphism

Amplified fragment length polymorphism combines restriction digestion with selective amplification of subsets of the resulting fragments. The original method described by Vos and colleagues in 1995 addressed a practical challenge: a genome contains too many restriction fragments for them all to be informative in one simple electrophoretic profile. By attaching defined adapter sequences to restriction fragment ends, the experiment provides recognizable primer-binding regions. Primers that extend into the adjacent genomic sequence select particular subsets of fragments. The result is a manageable collection of amplified fragments from a much larger pool.

The molecular causes of differences in AFLP profiles are varied. A restriction-site mutation can change the set of fragments available for amplification. A change in a nucleotide used for selective priming can alter whether a particular fragment enters the observed subset. An insertion or deletion within a fragment can shift its apparent length. These distinct events all can lead to a marker polymorphism, but a gel profile alone does not distinguish their causes. Inheritance tests and sequence analysis provide additional information when needed.

Selective amplification can be understood through a simplified probability model. If the four nucleotides occur with equal frequencies and independently, requiring one additional specified base at a fragment end retains approximately one-quarter of eligible fragments. Requiring three specified bases at one end retains approximately one in sixty-four. Specifying three at each end would retain approximately one in 4096 of the fragments that meet both end-type requirements. Real genomes are not random independent strings, restriction recognition motifs impose additional constraints, and amplification efficiency varies. The calculation explains the filtering principle; it is not a prediction of an exact number of bands.

AFLP profiles usually contain many more scorable signals per assay than a single locus-specific RFLP or SSR. This property can help with genetic map construction and diversity studies in genomes lacking extensive prior sequence resources. Standard AFLP scoring often treats a fragment as present or absent, so most markers behave as dominant scoring systems. Certain fragment patterns or quantitative approaches can permit more detailed genotype inference, but a student should not assume codominance merely because AFLP is based on restriction digestion.

Compared with RAPD, AFLP imposes more defined constraints on fragment generation and primer binding, usually giving better reproducibility under standardized conditions. It is also more complex and requires reliable fragment separation and consistent scoring across samples. The number of detected bands is not the same as the number of independent useful markers: two markers may be tightly linked, two fragments may overlap in apparent size, and some bands may be monomorphic. The quality of a map depends on accurate and informative markers distributed across the genome rather than on a large raw count of bands.

The logic of AFLP can be separated into three stages: generating fragments with reproducible boundaries, introducing known sequences at their ends, and selectively measuring a manageable subset. This is not simply a random PCR with longer primers. The restriction enzyme defines where DNA fragments begin and end, and the attached adapters provide a common sequence that can be recognized by designed primers. The selective bases add another requirement beyond the common adapter or restriction-derived sequence. A fragment must satisfy the relevant end constraints before it becomes a likely product in the selected subset.

Many AFLP designs use two types of restriction ends, arising from enzymes with different recognition-site frequencies. For intuition, imagine a fragment with one end from a relatively rare cutter and the other end from a relatively frequent cutter. Primers that recognize those end types preferentially amplify a subset of these mixed-end fragments. This controls the complexity of the profile. The exact number of amplified fragments depends on the genome, restriction motifs, fragment lengths, selective bases and analytical detection window. It is therefore misleading to claim that every AFLP assay detects a fixed number of genetic loci.

A restriction-site mutation in a region can affect several neighbouring fragments in principle. Loss of an internal restriction site joins two fragments into a larger one; depending on their end types and selected nucleotides, some original products may disappear and a new product may arise. This creates a possibility that two observed bands reflect alternative molecular consequences of a single event rather than independent variation at two freely segregating loci. Inheritance data can reveal strongly complementary patterns. When band states are coded independently without considering the biology, apparent marker counts can overstate the number of informative events.

In a diploid F2 population, a dominant AFLP fragment present in one parent and absent from the other commonly follows the same expected three-to-one pattern discussed for RAPD. If two fragments represent alternative lengths of one locus and can be demonstrated to segregate as alleles, codominant scoring becomes possible. Merely observing one band from each parent in a complex profile is insufficient to establish this relationship. They might belong to different loci. One must examine segregation, molecular identity or other appropriate evidence before treating them as an allelic pair.

The choice of selective primer combinations also affects ascertainment. A panel of markers generated by an AFLP assay is not a uniformly random sample of every nucleotide in the genome; it depends on the presence of suitable restriction sites and primer-selective sequence contexts. This does not invalidate its use in genetic mapping, but it matters when extrapolating marker variability to genome-wide diversity. The detected fragment distribution can favour certain genomic regions. Comparing marker density with physical coordinates becomes possible once fragment identities are established.

Relative to an SSR assay targeting one known locus, AFLP can generate a multilocus fingerprint with less prior locus-specific sequence information. Relative to a dense SNP array, the anonymous band patterns often require more work to assign exact physical positions. The distinction becomes important when the aim changes from detecting population differentiation to narrowing a trait to a small candidate region. The latter benefits from markers with defined sequence positions and unambiguous genotypes. A method that was excellent for building an initial map may not be the most efficient for the next stage.

Simple sequence repeats and microsatellites

A microsatellite or simple sequence repeat is a short DNA motif repeated in tandem. A sequence such as CACACACA contains four adjacent copies of the CA motif. Different inherited alleles at the same locus can contain different numbers of repeat units. During replication, transient misalignment between repetitive template and newly synthesized strands can contribute to expansions or contractions of repeat number. This process helps explain why some microsatellite loci have many alleles within a population.

An SSR assay usually amplifies the repeat-containing region using primers complementary to unique flanking DNA. When the flanks are constant, the amplified fragment length equals the sum of the constant sequence lengths and the repeat tract length. If the fixed contribution is 100 base pairs and a CA repeat occurs fifteen times, the product is 130 base pairs. A second allele with nineteen repeats produces 138 base pairs. A diploid heterozygote normally shows both products, and their eight-base difference corresponds to four CA repeat units. The observed difference is not eight repeat units.

The variability of SSRs is useful because a marker with several reasonably common alleles is more likely to distinguish homologues in a randomly selected individual than a marker with only two alleles. In a specific cross between two homozygous parents, however, only the parental alleles matter. An SSR with ten alleles in the broader species is entirely uninformative if both chosen parents carry the same allele. Conversely, a biallelic SNP can be perfectly informative for tracking the origin of a chromosome in that cross.

SSR scoring is not free of error. Polymerase slippage during amplification can produce smaller stutter peaks near the true allele. A sequence alteration at a primer-binding site may produce a null allele that fails to amplify. A heterozygote carrying one detectable allele and one null allele may therefore appear homozygous. An unexpectedly large fraction of apparent homozygotes, incompatibility with parental genotypes, or recurrent failures at one locus can motivate investigation of this possibility. In addition, different sequence arrangements can occasionally produce fragments of the same total length. Equal apparent size should not always be equated with identity by descent.

SSR markers are commonly useful in plant and animal pedigrees, identity studies and mapping. Their codominant inheritance is particularly attractive where both copies of a chromosome need to be followed. High-quality scoring requires sufficient fragment-size resolution to distinguish adjacent repeat alleles. The important exam-level relationship is that marker diversity arises from repeat-number or related length variation, while the assay measures length between flanking primers. The repeat itself need not lie inside a functional gene.

Repeat structure itself supplies biological information. A mononucleotide repeat changes length in increments of one base when units are gained or lost, a dinucleotide repeat in increments of two, and a trinucleotide repeat in increments of three, assuming perfect changes in unit number. Real microsatellites can have interrupted motifs or neighbouring insertion and deletion polymorphisms, so an unexpected allele length need not be an error. Sequencing a representative allele can establish the relationship between fragment length and repeat composition. In routine mapping, the fragment length may still serve as a reliable inherited identifier even when the detailed repeat structure is unknown.

A stable unique flanking region is essential for the usual locus-specific SSR design. If the primers match several genomic copies, multiple products can make diploid genotypes hard to interpret. If the flanks evolve between species, a primer pair developed in one species may fail in a related species despite retention of a similar repeat region. This is one reason marker transferability is a separate property from polymorphism. A highly variable marker within one species may be unsuitable for a different species because its detection method relies on nonconserved flanking DNA.

In an illustrative pedigree, a mother carries alleles of length 120 and 126 bases, and a father carries 132 and 138 bases. An offspring with products of 126 and 138 bases has received the 126 allele from the mother and the 138 allele from the father. Here each parental transmission can be assigned uniquely. If both parents instead carry 120 and 126, an offspring with both products cannot be assigned a unique maternal and paternal marker allele from the offspring genotype alone. The same highly polymorphic locus provides different linkage information in these two families, despite equally clear genotyping.

The phenomenon of stutter is especially relevant when adjacent alleles differ by one repeat unit. A stutter peak from a larger allele may coincide with the position of a genuine smaller allele. An analyst must compare peak patterns and validated calling rules instead of assuming every small peak represents an additional inherited allele. A consistent pattern in known homozygous controls provides evidence about assay behaviour. Diploid samples with apparent signals for more than two alleles at a single locus require scrutiny, because a true diploid genotype contains at most two copies at an ordinary single-copy autosomal locus. Mixtures, duplicated loci, stutter and unusual chromosome dosage are possible explanations.

A null allele can change both mapping and population analyses. Imagine that an SSR heterozygote with a 150-base-pair allele and a null allele is scored as a 150-base-pair homozygote. In a family, the apparent genotype may imply that a parent transmitted an impossible allele to an offspring. In a population survey, the same error reduces observed heterozygosity and can mimic biological inbreeding. Using a different pair of flanking primers or an independent assay may reveal the missing allele. The lesson is that a statistical deviation should be interpreted together with the molecular measurement process.

SSR loci can show high diversity because mutation processes create multiple repeat lengths. However, the same size can arise independently through expansion or contraction, a phenomenon that can complicate inference of distant relationships. In a short controlled mapping pedigree, the probability of such recurrent mutation during the observed transmissions is usually low compared with the usefulness of stable inherited fragment sizes. In a study of deep evolutionary relationships, the limitations of length identity become more consequential. The time scale and biological question determine how strongly a shared allele size supports shared ancestry.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms

A single nucleotide polymorphism is an inherited single-base difference at a defined genome position that varies within a population. For example, some chromosomes may carry A and others G at a particular site. A nucleotide substitution can be a transition or a transversion and may occur in coding DNA, noncoding regulatory regions or regions with no established functional role. A sequence difference can be used as a marker even when its biological effect is unknown. A SNP assay differs from a repeat-length assay because it must recognize base identity rather than relying necessarily on fragment length.

Several molecular readouts can distinguish SNP alleles. Allele-specific probes can be designed so that binding or signal production discriminates the alternative sequence states. Allele-specific amplification can exploit differences at primer-binding positions. Direct sequence reads identify bases at a mapped coordinate. A SNP that changes a restriction site can even be scored by PCR-RFLP, showing that a category of underlying sequence variation and the technology used to measure it are not mutually exclusive classifications.

In a diploid sample, a biallelic SNP may give AA, AG or GG genotypes when both alleles are detected reliably. In a sequence-based assay, an AG heterozygote may contribute reads containing both bases. The two read counts need not be exactly equal: sampling, alignment and amplification biases can influence their ratio. Very low coverage creates uncertainty, and a few reads supporting a rare alternate base may be due to sequencing error. The correct response to uncertainty is a cautious genotype call or a missing value rather than forced classification.

A single biallelic SNP has a theoretical expected heterozygosity no greater than one-half in a randomly mating diploid population, but SNPs are abundant and can be genotyped in large numbers. Dense SNP panels allow many inherited chromosome segments to be followed and can support fine-scale physical localization when sufficient recombination events are available. Their principal advantage for many modern mapping projects is scale and integration with genome sequence, rather than the informativeness of every individual SNP.

SNPs close together may be inherited as a haplotype, a combination of alleles along a chromosome. The genotype at two heterozygous SNP loci does not reveal haplotype phase by itself: Aa and Bb could represent AB/ab or Ab/aB. Family transmissions, molecular phasing or other evidence can resolve the ambiguity. A genotype matrix should not be interpreted as a set of known haplotypes without evidence for phase. This distinction connects molecular genotyping directly with the linkage-cross reasoning developed in the earlier node.

The discrimination of a SNP depends on the fact that complementary nucleic acid strands recognize nucleotide identity through base pairing. A probe intended to distinguish one allele must respond differently enough to the alternative allele to produce a reliable genotype signal. A mismatch can reduce binding stability, but the effect depends on surrounding sequence and assay conditions. This is why a well-characterized nucleotide-specific assay can distinguish alleles that have identical fragment lengths. Ordinary electrophoretic separation by length alone would generally not resolve a single-base substitution in equal-length products.

In a simplified fluorescent genotyping plot, the intensity for the A-specific signal is plotted horizontally and the intensity for the G-specific signal vertically. AA samples tend to cluster toward the A axis, GG samples toward the G axis, and AG samples in a region displaying both signals. Weak signals for both alleles may indicate insufficient DNA or assay failure. A genotype caller should not assign the weak central region to heterozygotes merely because the two intensities are similar. The magnitude and relative balance of signals are both relevant. Positive controls representing known genotypes and samples with no DNA help define expected signals and background.

In a hypothetical sequence dataset, three samples show A and G read counts of 29 and 0, 17 and 14, and 0 and 32. Under an ordinary diploid single-copy model these support AA, AG and GG respectively. A fourth sample has only one A read and no G reads. Its data are compatible with AA but also with AG if the G allele was not sampled. With unbiased sampling and exactly two reads from a heterozygote, the probability of observing two reads of only one allele is one-half: one-quarter for both A and one-quarter for both G. With ten independent unbiased reads, that probability is 2 times one-half raised to the tenth power, about 0.00195. This simple probability calculation explains why increased coverage can reduce uncertainty, but independence and equal sampling may be violated in actual data.

A marker panel designed from one set of individuals can be biased toward variants common in that discovery population. When applied to another population, some SNPs may be monomorphic or less informative. This ascertainment effect matters for population comparisons and selection of mapping markers. A SNP panel with many markers is not necessarily a representative sample of all variants in every population. For a controlled cross, screening the actual parents remains important even when a well-established commercial or research panel is available.

Dense marker data also contain redundancy. Imagine three nearby SNPs whose genotypes always occur together across the available progeny. They provide strong evidence for inheritance of a common segment, but treating each SNP as a separate independent meiotic event would exaggerate the amount of information. The independent observation is the inheritance of the segment in a particular informative transmission. A genetic analysis should account for the dependence between markers. The physical spacing of the SNPs can still be useful to describe the segment and locate candidates within it.

The boundary between a SNP marker and a functional variant is determined by evidence rather than assay design. A nonsynonymous substitution changes a codon so that a different amino acid may be incorporated, but that change might have little effect on the protein. A synonymous substitution does not change the encoded amino acid, yet may in some cases influence another aspect of gene expression. A regulatory substitution may affect the amount or timing of transcription. These possibilities show why a SNP's position or predicted consequence can motivate a functional hypothesis but cannot replace a direct test of the relationship to the trait.

Quantifying marker informativeness

Expected heterozygosity measures the probability that two independently sampled alleles differ, under a specified distribution of alleles. If allele i has frequency p_i, the probability that both samples are allele i is p_i squared. The mutually exclusive possibilities for a match sum to the sum of squared allele frequencies. The probability of a mismatch is therefore H = 1 minus the sum of p_i squared. In a randomly mating diploid population under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions this quantity also predicts the fraction of heterozygotes.

For a biallelic marker with frequencies 0.7 and 0.3, H equals 1 minus (0.49 + 0.09), or 0.42. Four equally frequent alleles give H = 1 minus 4 times 0.25 squared = 0.75. A locus with many alleles can have low heterozygosity if one allele overwhelmingly predominates. Ten allele names do not guarantee an informative marker. Observed heterozygosity, computed from actual heterozygote counts, may differ from expected heterozygosity because of inbreeding, population mixture, selection, genotyping error or sampling.

Polymorphism information content, often abbreviated PIC, has more than one usage in the literature. In the classical linkage-information definition for a codominant locus, PIC = 1 − sum of p_i squared − sum over i less than j of 2 p_i squared p_j squared. The additional subtraction represents combinations for which parental allele transmission may not be distinguishable in a linkage analysis. For two equally frequent alleles the formula gives 1 − 0.5 − 0.125 = 0.375, whereas expected heterozygosity is 0.5. Some marker studies use a diversity statistic labelled PIC that is simply 1 minus the squared-frequency sum. Always use the definition supplied by a question or clearly state the convention used.

In a controlled biparental cross the most relevant criterion may be parental differentiation rather than population heterozygosity. A marker with a rare allele present in one chosen homozygous parent and absent in the other can track ancestry extremely well. In a pedigree, informativeness instead depends on parental genotypes and whether a transmission can be assigned uniquely. A homozygous parent transmits the same marker allele to every offspring, so that parent's marker provides no evidence about crossing over between its homologues. A heterozygous parent can be informative if the offspring genotype and the other parent's genotype reveal which allele was transmitted.

A valuable marker panel combines appropriate informativeness, reliable scoring and genome coverage. Fifty markers within one small chromosome interval may be insufficient to discover linkage elsewhere. Conversely, widely spaced markers can identify a chromosome but leave the associated trait in a broad physical region. As a result, investigators often establish a framework map using reliable markers, then add more markers in regions where positional information is needed. This balances genotyping effort with the limits imposed by available meiotic events.

A useful exercise is to compare markers with different allele-frequency distributions. For a biallelic SNP with p = 0.95 and q = 0.05, the expected heterozygosity is 2pq = 0.095. For an equally frequent SNP it is 0.5. The first marker can still be excellent for a family in which the informative parent happens to be heterozygous, but on average it will be less likely to distinguish the two homologues of a randomly sampled individual. The expected heterozygosity describes a population sampling property, not an immutable performance rating of a particular assay.

For a three-allele marker with frequencies 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2, the sum of squared frequencies is 0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04 = 0.38. Expected heterozygosity is 0.62. In the classical PIC calculation the pair terms are 2(0.25)(0.09), 2(0.25)(0.04) and 2(0.09)(0.04), giving 0.045, 0.020 and 0.0072. Subtracting their sum from 0.62 yields 0.5478. The statistic is lower than heterozygosity because some matings involving heterozygous individuals do not allow the origin of a transmitted allele to be assigned uniquely. The calculation illustrates why PIC and heterozygosity should not be substituted without checking the definition.

At a dominant band marker, the population frequency of the absence allele cannot generally be obtained by treating every band-positive individual as carrying exactly one positive allele. Under Hardy–Weinberg assumptions, if the absence phenotype requires genotype aa, its frequency estimates q squared. With 36 absence observations among 100 sampled diploids, an estimated absence allele frequency is the square root of 0.36 = 0.6. The expected heterozygosity is then 2(0.4)(0.6) = 0.48. The procedure is model-dependent. If the sample consists of inbred lines, related individuals or a mixture of populations, the square-root inference may be inappropriate. By contrast, a codominant marker allows direct counting of both alleles from reliable diploid genotype calls.

The effective information content of a marker can be reduced by missing observations. Suppose 100 individuals are informative in principle, but 20 lack interpretable genotypes at one of two loci. A direct testcross estimate of recombination should use the 80 individuals with both calls, not all 100, provided the missingness is not related to recombinant status. If 8 of the jointly scored individuals are recombinant, the estimate is 0.10. If all failures occurred specifically in a particular recombinant genotype because the assay cannot amplify its allele, this estimate would be biased. Missing-data handling is therefore an inferential issue rather than a purely numerical housekeeping task.

Different loci can be selected for different purposes. A parentage analysis favours highly discriminating markers whose joint genotype probabilities are well characterized. A genome-wide linkage scan favours distributed markers that segregate in the available family. A fine-mapping experiment favours markers between previously identified boundaries. For a physical mapping panel, a unique sequence tag with reliable presence or absence scoring may be useful without allelic polymorphism. A single measure of marker quality cannot capture all four purposes. The correct comparison begins by identifying the specific uncertainty that the marker is intended to resolve.

Marker segregation and linkage inference

The basic mapping experiment requires two observations per informative transmission: the marker allele received and the state of the trait or second marker. In an appropriate testcross, offspring genotypes can directly report the gametes produced by a heterozygote. Suppose the heterozygote has haplotypes AB/ab, and a tester contributes ab in every gamete. The offspring classes reflect AB, ab, Ab and aB gametes from the heterozygote. With coupling phase established, AB and ab are nonrecombinant; Ab and aB are recombinant. If the observed counts are 88, 84, 15 and 13, respectively, the recombination fraction is 28/200 = 0.14.

For a short interval, the genetic distance in centimorgans is approximately 100 times the recombination fraction, giving about 14 cM in this illustrative dataset. The approximation becomes less accurate when multiple crossovers can restore the parental arrangement of endpoint markers. Under Haldane's model of independent crossovers, the relationship is d = −50 ln(1 − 2r) cM. At r = 0.14, the corrected estimate is approximately 16.43 cM. The calculation depends on a crossover model; it is not a direct measurement of DNA length. The previous linkage chapter develops mapping functions in detail.

An alternative F2 design combines two gametes from heterozygous parents and is not equivalent to a testcross. The observed diploid genotype can be compatible with more than one pair of gametes. Simply counting individuals with unusual combinations of alleles and dividing by the total is therefore not a generally valid estimator of recombination fraction. A mapping analysis must respect the probability model of the cross. In a doubled haploid population each homozygous line reflects a sampled haploid genome, whereas repeated inbreeding changes the relationship between observed recombinant line frequency and single-meiosis recombination.

Consider an informative testcross with a trait allele T associated with molecular markers M1 and M2. Among 300 individuals, 18 recombinants separate T from M1 and 42 separate T from M2. The observed fractions are 0.06 and 0.14. Under comparable conditions the first marker is more closely linked to the trait. The two pairwise fractions do not reveal whether M1 and M2 are on the same side of T. A joint three-locus analysis and knowledge of phase are needed to establish order. Choosing the marker with fewer recombinant individuals for prediction is reasonable, but identifying it as the causal allele is not.

Map resolution depends on observing enough transmissions that separate nearby loci. If the true recombination fraction between two loci is 0.002, the chance of observing zero recombinants in 100 independent transmissions is (0.998) raised to the power 100, approximately 0.819. Increasing marker density without increasing the number of informative transmissions may leave many markers with identical segregation patterns. These markers can be assigned to a shared map bin, but their order cannot be inferred from the available linkage evidence. A physical sequence may resolve their order even when the genetic map cannot.

To understand why a linkage group emerges from marker data, imagine four markers, A, B, C and D, scored in an informative backcross. A and B have a low recombinant fraction, B and C have a low recombinant fraction, and C and D have a low recombinant fraction. A and D may show a much higher recombinant fraction because several intervals separate them. Their endpoint recombination fraction may even approach one-half. Nevertheless, the chain of intermediate markers can connect them in a common linkage group. This is the practical advantage of adding markers between widely separated loci rather than attempting to construct an entire chromosome map from endpoint comparisons alone.

Pairwise linkage evidence does not always determine a unique order. When two markers cosegregate in every available individual, their order relative to one another cannot be inferred by counting recombinants. If a third marker lies outside that pair, both may have identical estimated distances from the third. A genetic map may display them at the same position with an explicit shared bin. Physical sequence positions may distinguish them, but importing that order into a linkage map should be identified as the use of supplementary information. It is not evidence for a crossover that was never observed.

Sampling uncertainty matters even in a perfectly genotyped experiment. With 20 recombinants among 100 informative gametes, the recombination estimate is 0.20. For independent Bernoulli transmissions the approximate standard error is the square root of r(1−r)/N, which in this case is the square root of 0.16/100 = 0.04. With the same proportion in 400 gametes the standard error is 0.02. Quadrupling the number of transmissions halves this sampling standard error. The formula is an approximation and does not account for uncertainty in phase, genotyping errors or correlations among observations. Near zero or one-half, more careful interval methods may be preferred.

The absence of recombinant observations requires a different interpretation from an observed zero distance. If a marker and trait do not separate in a family of 100, the estimate of r is zero, but the experiment has not demonstrated that recombination is biologically impossible. Under the independent-transmission model, the probability of zero recombinants is (1−r) to the power N. Setting this probability to 0.05 and solving gives r = 1−0.05 raised to the power 1/N. For N = 100, the upper one-sided 95 percent bound is approximately 0.0295. This illustrates why a zero-recombinant interval may still contain distinct loci that are several map units apart under the uncertainty of a small sample.

A genetic map can be inflated by erroneous singleton genotypes. Suppose a progeny chromosome has the parental pattern A A A A A across five ordered markers. One erroneous call at the third position creates A A B A A. Interpreted literally, this requires switches between the second and third markers and again between the third and fourth. A genuine double recombinant is possible, but its probability may be small if both intervals are short. The pattern warrants review of the raw marker signal, parental phase and neighbouring markers. It is not correct to automatically delete every double recombinant because legitimate rare crossover events supply important information. The decision should be based on assay evidence and the biology of the intervals.

Segregation distortion and linkage evidence must likewise be distinguished. A marker could show a large excess of one parental allele because an associated viability locus influences survival. A two-marker association can be altered when a specific genotype class is preferentially lost before scoring. Investigators may compare earlier and later developmental stages, reciprocal crosses, independent families or genotype-specific assay performance to assess the alternatives. The expected Mendelian ratio is a baseline model rather than a guarantee that every recovered experimental population will follow it.

A simple likelihood comparison provides another way of thinking about recombination evidence. In an informative two-locus family with known phase, the probability of observing R recombinants and N−R nonrecombinants is proportional to r raised to R times (1−r) raised to N−R. The binomial coefficient is the same when comparing candidate values of r for the same data. The estimated r is the value that maximizes this expression within the permitted range. A comparison to the independent-assortment value of 0.5 measures how much better a particular linkage model explains the data. The detailed LOD score methods belong to human genetics, but recognizing the role of competing probabilities helps a student understand why a low recombinant fraction from two offspring is not equivalent to the same fraction from two hundred.

Physical coordinates and genetic maps

A genetic map uses the frequency and pattern of inherited recombination to order loci and estimate distances in centimorgans. A physical map uses molecular evidence to place landmarks along DNA molecules, usually expressed in base pairs, kilobases or megabases. A reference genome assembly gives a coordinate framework, but the physical map is conceptually distinct from the linkage observations that may help validate it. One must not treat one centimorgan as a universal physical length.

Consider two intervals each 10 megabases long. If the first spans 2 cM and the second spans 20 cM, their average genetic-to-physical ratios are 0.2 cM/Mb and 2 cM/Mb respectively. The second interval exhibits ten times the genetic distance per physical length in the mapping context. Local crossover hotspots and cold regions, chromatin organization, proximity to centromeres, structural variation and sex-specific recombination contribute to variation in these relationships. The estimates also depend on the population and methodology used.

A physical interval can remain broad despite very tight genetic linkage if crossing over is suppressed. An inversion heterozygote, for example, can show reduced recovery of recombinant chromosomes in the inverted region. The physical genes have not necessarily moved closer together in base-pair distance. Their association in viable progeny reflects altered recombination or recovery. This is why genetic mapping must be interpreted together with biological context and independent sequence evidence.

Maps are complementary tools. A linkage analysis can place a trait in a region bounded by flanking DNA markers. If those markers have known sequence positions on a reference assembly, one can identify an approximate physical interval containing candidate genes. Additional recombinant chromosomes can narrow the interval. Structural differences between the parental chromosomes and the reference genome, missing sequence or incorrectly assembled regions may complicate the translation from genetic to physical order.

A marker that has no polymorphism in the studied family can still serve as a physical sequence-tagged site. Such a landmark can be located through direct sequence identity or other physical mapping approaches, although it cannot provide recombination evidence from that family. This distinction explains why physical and genetic maps contain overlapping but not necessarily identical sets of markers.

Physical mapping historically relied on approaches such as restriction maps, overlapping DNA clones and sequence-tagged landmarks. A clone-based physical map seeks to identify pieces of DNA that overlap along a chromosome, much as overlapping pages can help reconstruct a long document. A sequence-tagged site can provide an experimentally recognizable unique coordinate within those pieces. Modern sequence assemblies give direct base-pair coordinate systems, but the underlying challenge of establishing that different pieces represent the correct order and orientation has not disappeared. Repetitive DNA can complicate assembly, and biological genomes can differ from the chosen reference.

A map in base pairs should specify which genome assembly and which coordinate convention are being used if exact positions are important. The same named marker may be assigned different coordinates in different reference versions because a gap was filled or a segment was repositioned. In a learning problem the physical map is often treated as an accurate simplified reference, and the stated coordinate differences can be calculated directly. In an actual study, the genetic and physical orders may disagree because of errors in one map, structural differences between parents and reference, or genuine differences in chromosome organization. A discrepancy motivates investigation, not an immediate assumption that the genetic map is wrong.

The local ratio of genetic and physical distances can be used to estimate how much physical sequence remains in a candidate interval, but only with appropriate local evidence. Suppose marker X is at 5 Mb and Y at 15 Mb, and the genetic positions are 20 and 25 cM. The average ratio across this interval is 0.5 cM/Mb. If the trait maps roughly halfway between them genetically, it might be tempting to locate it at 10 Mb. That conclusion assumes a uniform recombination rate throughout the interval. If nearly all crossing over is concentrated near X, the physical location corresponding to the genetic midpoint could lie much closer to X. Physical coordinates of more closely flanking markers are more reliable than a linear conversion based on the full interval.

Chromosome ends and centromere-proximal regions often differ in recombination behaviour, but the precise pattern is species-specific and may differ between sexes. In some organisms a large physical region contributes little genetic map length, while another region contributes disproportionately. Mapping resolution measured in base pairs therefore varies across the genome. A trait in a recombination-poor region may remain associated with a long physical segment after many offspring have been genotyped. Additional marker density cannot by itself create the missing recombination events. Independent evidence or another genetic background may be required to resolve the responsible locus.

A marker-based positional map is also limited by the markers selected to bound the trait. If every verified recombinant excludes all positions to the left of marker L and all positions to the right of marker R under a single-locus model, the candidate interval is between those boundaries. If L and R are at 2.1 and 2.7 Mb on the reference, the interval spans approximately 0.6 Mb. That does not establish that every individual has exactly that physical sequence content; an insertion or deletion may alter the parental interval. It also does not establish how many genes are present without consulting the relevant annotation. The map provides a testable positional restriction rather than a final functional explanation.

Mapping in plants, animals and bacteria

Plant genetics often benefits from controlled crosses between divergent lines, selfing where biologically possible, and the production of populations that can be genotyped and phenotyped under defined environments. Molecular markers reveal the parental origin of chromosome regions. A disease-resistance trait in an F2 family can be scored alongside DNA genotypes to identify regions associated with resistance. The evidence is strongest when phenotypes are consistently measured and the expected inheritance model is considered. Environmental variation can blur the relationship between the marker and phenotype even when linkage exists.

Marker-assisted selection uses a marker associated with an advantageous trait to select individuals before the trait is fully expressed or when direct scoring is difficult. A marker can improve selection efficiency, but crossing over between marker and trait allele can generate false predictions. Flanking markers on either side of the trait can reduce uncertainty by showing the inheritance of a larger chromosomal interval. The effectiveness of such a strategy depends on genetic background, phase, marker reliability and the actual relationship between the marker and the target allele.

Animal mapping may use controlled breeding populations in model organisms or pedigrees in species where experimental crosses are impractical or inappropriate. In a pedigree, the inheritance of a marker allele can be followed through generations to determine whether it cosegregates with the trait. The number of informative meioses may be small, and incomplete penetrance or phenocopies can complicate phenotype interpretation. Locus-specific DNA markers allow chromosome segments to be traced without relying solely on visible characters.

Most bacteria are effectively haploid for chromosomal loci under ordinary genetic mapping conditions, although multiple chromosome copies can occur during growth and some taxa have more complex genome organizations. A standard diploid heterozygosity model is not automatically appropriate. Molecular markers can distinguish donor and recipient alleles, strains or lineages, and physical sequence coordinates provide a reference for locating differences. In bacteria the association of transferred markers may depend on the size of DNA transferred and the mechanisms by which it is incorporated, rather than on crossing over in a diploid meiotic cell.

In transformation and generalized transduction, two loci that are physically close may be transferred together more frequently because a single DNA fragment can encompass both. Conjugational mapping can use the order and timing of chromosome transfer. These methods depend on distinct biological processes and are developed in the following bacterial genetics nodes. Marker-based inference in bacteria therefore requires knowledge of the transfer mechanism, selection of recovered cells and the distinction between physical proximity and a measured co-transfer frequency.

Comparison across species highlights a general principle. Molecular marker detection is the measurement step; the biological design determines how those measurements become mapping evidence. Similar SNP or fragment assays can be used in very different organisms, but the appropriate probability model depends on how genetic material is inherited or exchanged.

Plant mapping offers a useful illustration of how marker scoring interacts with phenotyping. Consider a self-compatible diploid plant with two highly inbred parents differing in resistance. F1 plants carry one chromosome set from each parent and can be selfed to produce segregating F2 individuals. Genotyping the F2 at informative markers identifies inherited regions. If resistance is dominant and the causal locus is near a marker, resistant plants may carry either one or two copies of the resistance allele, while susceptible plants are expected to lack it under complete penetrance. Marker genotypes and phenotype classes must be analysed using the expected dominance model. A simple one-to-one count of resistant plants with a particular marker allele may confuse dominance with linkage.

In crop improvement, marker-assisted selection for a known major-effect allele can be distinguished from selection using markers throughout the genome to predict a complex phenotype. In the first case, the focus is on reliable inheritance of a particular chromosome region or functional variant. In the second, many markers collectively contribute predictive information and no single marker need have a large effect. Detailed quantitative genetic prediction belongs to later nodes. At this stage, the essential idea is that a marker close to a major locus may be useful for selection even when it has no direct biological effect on the selected trait.

A marker selected in one breeding family may not be equally reliable in another family. In the original family, allele M might lie on the chromosome carrying the resistance allele R; in another lineage M may be carried with susceptibility allele r. Marker phase is a property of the relevant chromosomes, not of the letter used to name an allele. Independent validation in the new genetic background or use of a well-established functional variant can reduce this problem. When a linked rather than functional marker is used, occasional recombinants must be anticipated even in the original family.

Animal family studies demonstrate why marker informativeness is tied to available meioses. If a parent affected by an inherited trait is heterozygous at several markers, the transmitted marker alleles may be reconstructed from offspring and partner genotypes. If the partner shares the same marker alleles, some offspring genotypes may be ambiguous. Small families can leave broad candidate regions because few informative transmissions are available. Combining data across appropriately characterized families may increase statistical support, but differences in genetic cause among families can complicate the analysis. A phenotype with similar appearance is not necessarily caused by the same locus in every family.

Bacterial genetic maps require a different model of inherited chromosome material. In an illustrative transformation dataset, suppose 100 recovered cells selected for donor marker A are examined for donor marker B. If 70 also carry B, the conditional cotransformation frequency is 0.70. If only 15 of another 100 A-selected cells carry marker C under comparable conditions, B may lie closer to A than C does. The comparison assumes similar opportunities for uptake, integration and detection of the unselected markers. It is not valid to interpret 70 percent cotransformation as 70 cM, because the event being measured is the joint recovery of a donor DNA segment rather than meiotic separation.

Sequence-based comparisons of bacterial isolates also raise the distinction between a physical map and a population history. A variant at coordinate 500,000 can be located on a reference chromosome without performing a genetic cross. When a group of isolates shares that variant and other nearby variants, their shared history might reflect descent from a common ancestor or acquisition of a segment through genetic exchange. The spatial arrangement of variants is not by itself evidence for one particular transfer mechanism. The subsequent nodes distinguish transformation, conjugation and transduction by the biological process and by the types of mapping evidence they generate.

In all three organism groups, the interpretation of an apparent marker association is strengthened by asking how the sampled individuals were obtained. A mapping population created through controlled crosses has a known recent pedigree. A natural population may have a complex demographic history. A panel of bacterial recipients recovered under selection is conditioned on survival or acquisition of a selected genetic state. These sampling differences define the denominator for a calculation and the alternative explanations for the observed marker patterns.

Somatic cell hybrid mapping and concordance

Somatic cell hybrid mapping exploits the fact that chromosomes from two different species can be retained or lost differentially in hybrid cell lines. A classical design uses human and rodent cells to produce hybrids, some of which retain different subsets of human chromosomes while retaining the recipient cellular background. If a human-specific marker or gene product is detected only in hybrid lines that retain a particular human chromosome, the concordance provides evidence assigning the locus to that chromosome.

The important distinction from meiotic linkage mapping is that the experiment does not count recombination between homologues during gametogenesis. The relevant experimental units are independently derived hybrid cell lines whose chromosome retention patterns differ. The assay may detect a human-specific enzyme isoform or a DNA sequence that can be discriminated from a corresponding rodent sequence. A signal present in the recipient parent alone is not evidence for retention of the human locus.

Consider the following illustrative panel. In five lines the human-specific marker is present in lines 1, 3 and 5, and absent in lines 2 and 4. Chromosome 7 is retained in lines 1, 3 and 5, while chromosome 12 is retained in lines 1, 2 and 5. Chromosome 7 agrees with the marker in all five lines. Chromosome 12 agrees in three lines: 1, 4 and 5. The two discordant observations are line 2, where chromosome 12 is present but the marker is absent, and line 3, where chromosome 12 is absent but the marker is present. Chromosome 7 is the better assignment under the simplest interpretation.

For a given chromosome, let n11 denote lines positive for both chromosome and marker, n00 lines negative for both, and n10 and n01 discordant lines. Concordance equals (n11 + n00) divided by the total number of informative lines. The formula counts agreements rather than measuring recombination. A score of 1 can be ambiguous if two chromosomes are always retained or lost together in the available panel. An additional line separating their retention states may be more informative than many lines duplicating the same pattern.

Chromosome fragmentation or rearrangement, detection errors and hybrid-specific suppression of a gene product can generate discordance. A gene product that is not expressed in a hybrid might be absent from the assay even though the human DNA remains present. DNA-based detection of a species-specific locus can help distinguish chromosome absence from altered expression. Conversely, a fragment of a chromosome may contain the locus even when the entire intact chromosome is scored as absent. Interpret the retention data according to the resolution and specificity of both measurements.

A somatic hybrid panel needs variable chromosome retention to provide informative comparisons. Suppose all six hybrid lines retain chromosome 4 and all six express a particular human enzyme. This pattern is compatible with assignment to chromosome 4, but it is also compatible with assignment to any other chromosome retained in all six lines. Negative hybrid lines are particularly important because the absence of a chromosome and the absence of the marker can support the assignment. A panel is not informative merely because its marker-positive lines are numerous.

Consider a ten-line panel in which a marker is present in six lines and absent in four. Chromosome X is present in those same six positive lines and absent in the four negative lines. Chromosome Y is present in all ten lines. Their concordances are 10/10 and 6/10. The comparison favours X. Yet if the marker were present in all ten lines, both the experimental design and the interpretation would change: Y would have perfect agreement and every uniformly retained chromosome would remain a possible candidate. Variation among lines, not the absolute number of positive cells, is the source of discrimination.

Missing data should be handled by comparing only pairs of reliable chromosome and marker calls. If a line lacks a reliable chromosome 7 result but has a marker result, it contributes no valid agreement or disagreement for chromosome 7. The denominators can therefore differ among chromosome comparisons. A candidate with perfect agreement across three lines may be less persuasive than one with nearly perfect agreement across thirty informative lines, especially if the small subset is unrepresentative. Reporting raw agreement counts alongside fractions prevents a deceptively precise comparison.

A high concordance score is not equivalent to a probability that the chromosome assignment is correct. The baseline level of agreement depends on how frequently marker and chromosome are retained. If both are absent in most lines, a high fraction of jointly negative lines can occur by chance. As an illustrative calculation, if two unrelated binary states are each present with probability 0.1, expected agreement under independence is 0.1 times 0.1 plus 0.9 times 0.9 = 0.82. This high expected agreement shows why panels need adequate positive as well as negative observations and why a rigorous assignment considers the distribution of retention patterns rather than only percent agreement.

The distinction between whole chromosome assignment and intrachromosomal order is important. A panel that differentiates chromosome 7 from chromosome 12 may locate a locus to chromosome 7 without distinguishing the short arm from the long arm or specifying a position within either arm. Panels with characterized chromosome fragments or rearrangements can provide additional regional information. Radiation hybrid mapping takes a systematic approach to the retention of smaller pieces. A sequence assembly can ultimately provide exact reference coordinates for a uniquely matched marker. Each method supplies information at a different scale.

Somatic hybrid mapping was especially valuable in the context of human genetics because deliberate experimental breeding cannot be used as it can in many plant and animal model systems. The technique uses cells as the units of analysis and exploits inherited donor chromosome retention rather than following generations of individuals. A gene can be assigned even if a convenient phenotypic polymorphism is not available in human families. Its limitation is that retention-based assignment does not directly measure disease linkage or reveal whether the gene causes a phenotype in an intact organism.

Radiation hybrid mapping

Radiation hybrid mapping refines the principle of differential retention by using a panel containing fragments of donor chromosomes rather than relying solely on whole-chromosome retention. Ionizing radiation generates chromosome breaks in donor cells. Donor fragments can be retained in viable hybrid cell lines. A panel is then tested for species-specific DNA landmarks. Two nearby markers are more likely to remain on a common retained fragment than markers separated by many potential break sites, all else being equal.

The critical event in this approach is a chromosome break, not a meiotic crossover. Radiation hybrid distances can be represented in centirays, associated with the radiation dose and a model of breakage and retention. Centirays are not centimorgans and cannot be converted universally to base pairs. Retention of fragments varies among lines, and selective survival can alter the observed frequencies. A raw proportion of discordant marker calls is not automatically a corrected radiation hybrid distance.

Cox and colleagues described radiation hybrid mapping in 1990 as an approach to constructing long-range mammalian chromosome maps. The method helped connect genetic and physical mapping by placing DNA landmarks even when the loci did not exhibit convenient polymorphism in a pedigree. An assay that distinguishes a donor sequence from recipient DNA can be useful for retention mapping regardless of whether the donor locus is variable among individuals.

If two marker presence patterns agree across almost all hybrid lines, one possibility is that they occupy nearby donor DNA regions. Another is that retention patterns are correlated for reasons unrelated to local physical proximity. Evidence improves when a large panel contains informative breakpoints and a statistical analysis accounts for retention probabilities. A single hybrid that retains one marker but not another may provide useful ordering information alongside other markers, but an isolated discordance can also reflect assay error. The conceptual strength of radiation hybrid mapping is that it samples a different process from meiotic linkage and can help localize markers where ordinary recombination evidence is limited.

It is helpful to compare radiation hybrid marker vectors with ordinary genotype vectors. In a linkage experiment, a marker value can indicate the parental allele inherited at a locus. In a radiation hybrid panel, a positive value typically indicates detectable donor DNA at the site, and a negative value indicates no detectable donor DNA at that site. These are fundamentally different observations. Two markers can agree because they are retained together on a fragment, whereas in a family they agree because their parental states have not been separated by recombination.

In an illustrative eight-line radiation hybrid panel, marker A is detected in lines 1, 2, 4 and 7, marker B in lines 1, 2, 4 and 8, and marker C in lines 3, 5, 6 and 7. A and B disagree in two lines; A and C disagree in six lines. Under comparable assay and retention conditions, A and B are better candidates for nearby sites than A and C. The exercise provides a qualitative comparison, not a full statistical map. A different overall frequency of marker retention, or correlated retention of unrelated fragments, could change the interpretation.

The effect of radiation dose can be understood conceptually. A panel generated with more chromosome breaks may provide finer discrimination between closely spaced loci, because there are more opportunities to separate them. But excessive fragmentation can reduce the availability of informative retained segments or complicate panel construction. The useful balance depends on the experimental system. The centiray distance is associated with a particular dose and map model, so a distance in one panel should not be compared directly with a distance in another without appropriate calibration. The dose dependence contrasts with base-pair coordinates, which are not defined by a radiation exposure.

Radiation hybrid and sequence-based physical maps can help resolve genetic-map discrepancies, because they do not depend on the same meiotic recombination process. If two markers have the same centimorgan position in a mapping family but are frequently separated in a suitable radiation hybrid panel, the latter supports their physical separation despite the absence of observed meiotic recombinants. Conversely, if both techniques support proximity, their convergence strengthens confidence in the localization. Agreement between independent evidence types is often more informative than repeatedly applying the same assay to the same biological sample.

From linked markers to candidate genes

A marker association means that marker alleles and phenotypes occur together more or less often than expected under a stated comparison. A marker may itself change the biological process that controls a trait. It may also be neutral but inherited with a nearby causal variant. In natural populations, common ancestry, population structure and linkage disequilibrium can create associations that do not directly identify a causal gene. Association is therefore evidence for a hypothesis about location or relationship, not a complete demonstration of molecular causation.

Linkage and linkage disequilibrium are related but different concepts. Linkage concerns the tendency of nearby loci to be transmitted together because recombination does not always separate them. Linkage disequilibrium concerns a nonrandom association of alleles in a population. Population history, mutation, selection, drift, migration and recombination influence disequilibrium. Two physically linked loci can have little disequilibrium if ancestral allele combinations have been reshuffled over many generations. Alleles at unlinked loci can show disequilibrium in a structured or recently mixed population.

Suppose a SNP G occurs in 80 percent of a group with a trait and in 40 percent of a comparison group. This observation alone cannot establish whether G is a functional allele. A nearby gene may contain the actual causal change, or the groups may differ in ancestry. In a controlled cross, segregation between a candidate marker and the phenotype in a verified recombinant individual may refute the claim that the marker perfectly predicts the trait under that cross model. Inconsistent penetrance or inaccurate phenotyping must also be considered.

A positional investigation begins with reliable mapping evidence, narrows the candidate region using additional informative recombinants or markers, and examines genes or functional elements within that interval. Evidence from sequence variation, biological function, expression and independent genetic tests can strengthen or weaken a candidate explanation. No one measurement is sufficient for every case. A robust conclusion combines positional and functional evidence while considering alternative explanations.

A good examination solution therefore states what the observations establish before describing what remains unresolved. A marker can be informative without affecting the phenotype; a marker may predict a trait in one family but fail in another; and a causal variant can be located within a region that was originally identified by an unrelated polymorphism. This disciplined distinction is the final conceptual bridge to mapping-population design and quantitative genetics.

Consider a controlled cross in which two markers and a trait are mapped in the order L–T–R. A recombinant chromosome that retains the resistance allele but carries the susceptible-parent allele at L can move the left boundary of the candidate interval inward. A different recombinant retaining resistance but carrying the susceptible-parent allele at R can move the right boundary inward. If the phenotype is accurately scored and a single causal locus explains the trait, the intersection of these constraints narrows the region. The marker alleles do not themselves define the biological mechanism of resistance. They constrain the position of the inherited factor that is consistent with the recombinant observations.

A candidate gene inside a mapped interval might encode a protein with a plausible biological function, but a plausible function is not proof. Other genes or regulatory elements in the same interval may be responsible. If a candidate substitution cosegregates perfectly with the trait in the available family, it becomes a stronger hypothesis but can still be linked to another unobserved causal variant. Evidence that the candidate sequence changes the relevant molecular activity, and that independent genetic observations agree with the proposed mechanism, provides a different type of support. Mapping and functional analysis answer complementary questions.

In population association studies, confounding can be illustrated using two groups with different ancestry. Suppose a marker allele is common in group 1 and rare in group 2. An environmental condition influencing the trait is also more common in group 1. In a pooled sample the marker may appear associated with the trait even if it has no biological influence and is not physically linked to a causal locus. Comparing appropriate subgroups, using family-based information or applying suitable statistical models can reduce such confounding. The example shows why observing a statistically strong association does not alone establish mechanism.

A practical distinction is that a useful diagnostic or breeding predictor need not be causal, but its predictive validity must be established in the population where it is used. A marker that is linked to a trait may predict the trait well within one pedigree. Recombination, allele-frequency differences or differences in genetic background can weaken prediction elsewhere. A functional variant may avoid uncertainty about recombination between marker and causal site, but its phenotypic effect can still depend on penetrance, environment and interaction with other genes. The strongest inference is specific about the population, phenotype and assumptions.

The final objective of marker-based mapping is therefore not simply to maximize the number of measured DNA differences. It is to extract reliable positional and inheritance information from those differences, while recognizing the limits of the experiment. A molecular signal must be converted into a defensible genotype; genotypes must be interpreted in the correct biological design; and positional evidence must be distinguished from causality. Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. A marker must cause the trait. A neutral sequence can be linked to a causal allele. Association locates a region; functional evidence is needed for causation. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  2. A monomorphic marker is always useless. It cannot distinguish parental alleles in this cross, but a unique sequence may anchor a physical or hybrid map. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  3. Marker dominance implies trait dominance. Dominant scoring merges genotypes with one or two visible marker copies; the trait has its own expression model. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  4. Every RFLP fragment appears on a Southern blot. Only fragments detected by the probe appear. Trace probe complementarity, not just cleavage. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  5. Three gel bands always mean three alleles. One cut allele can yield two visible sizes; an uncut allele yields a third. A diploid single-copy locus still has two alleles per individual. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  6. An uncut band proves a heterozygote. Partial digestion can preserve the large product in a cut homozygote. Check digestion controls. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  7. RAPD needs known flanking sequences. Arbitrary primers detect eligible genomic segments; locus-specific sequence information is not required initially. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  8. An AFLP band is always codominant. Presence/absence scoring is typically dominant. Allelic relationships require independent evidence. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  9. An SSR difference of 6 bp means six repeats. For a dinucleotide motif it usually represents three units, assuming unchanged flanks and perfect repeat-length variation. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  10. An SSR homozygote call proves both alleles amplify. A null allele can produce apparent homozygosity. Check family transmission and alternative assays. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  11. A SNP must change protein sequence. SNPs occur in coding and noncoding DNA; a synonymous or intergenic variant can serve as a marker. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  12. More alleles guarantee higher heterozygosity. Allele frequencies matter; one overwhelmingly common allele can lower diversity. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  13. PIC and expected heterozygosity are always identical. Classical linkage PIC subtracts additional ambiguity terms; use the specified definition. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  14. Zero recombinants proves identical positions. Finite sampling can fail to observe separation. Report uncertainty and use physical evidence. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  15. One cM equals a fixed number of bases. Recombination varies across chromosomes and biological contexts. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  16. Bacterial co-transfer percentage is a cM distance. The event and denominator differ from meiotic recombination; use mechanism-specific inference. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  17. Perfect hybrid concordance uniquely assigns a chromosome. Two chromosomes may share retention patterns. More discriminating lines are needed. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  18. Centiray is another name for centimorgan. Radiation hybrid mapping relies on breakage and retention, whereas genetic maps rely on meiotic transmission. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
  19. A large SNP association proves molecular causation. Linkage disequilibrium and population structure can produce association without a direct effect. In an examination, check the measurement model and the stated assumptions before selecting the inference.
