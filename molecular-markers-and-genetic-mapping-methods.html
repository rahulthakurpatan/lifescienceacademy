Why geneticists need molecular markers

A chromosome contains many DNA sequences that cannot be distinguished by ordinary observation of an organism. Classical genetics follows visible differences such as flower colour, seed shape or a nutritional requirement. These differences can provide excellent landmarks, but there are relatively few readily scored traits, and each may depend on the environment, developmental stage or several genes. Molecular markers solve a different problem: they allow geneticists to follow inherited differences in DNA itself, even when the difference has no visible biological consequence. A marker therefore need not encode a protein or cause a phenotype. Its value is that its alternative forms can be distinguished reliably and can be followed through a suitable genetic experiment.

Consider two plants whose chromosomes contain different DNA sequences at a particular site. If a cross produces offspring in which one of the two parental sequence forms can be identified, the investigator can determine which parental chromosome contributed that region. At another location, a sequence difference may mark a second region. Following both sites in many offspring reveals how frequently inheritance separates the two parental combinations. These observations are the molecular counterpart of the phenotype-based testcross developed in classical linkage analysis. The crucial innovation is the ability to observe differences that are ordinarily invisible, not a replacement of the underlying laws of chromosome inheritance.

A locus is a defined genomic position or region; an allele is one of the alternative forms found at that locus. A polymorphism is sequence variation among individuals or chromosomes in a population. The operational definition used in a marker experiment depends on the assay: the actual difference might be a nucleotide substitution, an insertion, a deletion, a change in repeat number or a rearrangement affecting a larger fragment. If every individual in a particular cross carries the same marker allele, that marker is monomorphic within that experiment, even if it is highly variable in other populations. Informative polymorphism is thus a relationship between a marker and the biological material under study.

It is useful to separate the DNA variant from the assay used to observe it. For example, a single nucleotide substitution might eliminate a restriction site, making two alleles distinguishable by restriction fragment length. The same substitution could instead be detected by sequencing or by an allele-specific assay. The biological variant is unchanged; the laboratory observation differs. Conversely, two fragments that migrate to the same position in a gel may have different sequences. An assay distinguishes the classes it was designed to distinguish, not necessarily every possible sequence at the locus.

The essential logic of mapping is to connect an observation to an inference. An assay provides an observable signal, which is interpreted as a genotype. A collection of genotypes is interpreted through an inheritance model. A mapping analysis then estimates the position or relationship of a locus. Each inference introduces assumptions. Incorrect scoring, unknown parentage, a duplicated sequence or a biased sample can weaken the conclusion even when the DNA band itself is real. A good geneticist therefore asks not only whether a marker can be detected but whether its segregation can be interpreted unambiguously.

Dominant and codominant molecular scoring

Diploid organisms generally have two homologous copies of an autosomal locus. If an assay can distinguish both alleles in a heterozygote, the marker is scored codominantly. Suppose one allele produces a fragment of 150 base pairs and a second allele produces a fragment of 158 base pairs. The two homozygotes have their respective fragment lengths, while a heterozygote has both. The observable genotype classes can therefore be recorded as AA, AB and BB. In an F2 from two homozygous parents, those classes are expected in proportions one quarter, one half and one quarter when Mendelian segregation occurs without viability or scoring biases.

A dominant marker assay distinguishes the presence of a signal from its absence without distinguishing the two genotypes that carry at least one signal-producing allele. Suppose D produces a band and d does not. DD and Dd both score as present; dd scores as absent. In an F2 the expected observable ratio is three present to one absent under the same assumptions. This is dominance of the scored marker signal, not necessarily dominance of a biological phenotype. The underlying alleles may have no measurable physiological effect.

Dominant markers can be informative in a carefully chosen cross. In a Dd by dd testcross, band-positive offspring are expected to be Dd and band-negative offspring dd. The two classes reveal the allele contributed by the heterozygous parent. In an F2, however, the band-positive class combines two genotypes and loses information. Treating all band-positive F2 individuals as heterozygotes would create false segregation patterns and may distort estimates of linkage.

The distinction also affects evaluation of marker quality. A marker allele at a known locus may fail to produce a detectable band because an assay does not amplify it, a phenomenon often described as a null allele in PCR-based scoring. That is not automatically a loss-of-function mutation of a biological gene. A true heterozygote carrying one visible allele and one nonamplifying allele may be scored incorrectly as a homozygote. Replicate assays, family segregation, alternative primers and independent genotype checks can help distinguish assay failure from genuine homozygosity.

An informative marker is not simply one with many alleles. A multiallelic marker that is identical in both experimental parents cannot track their different ancestry. A biallelic SNP can be completely informative when the parents are fixed for different alleles. For a population with allele frequencies p_i, expected heterozygosity under random mating is one minus the sum of squared allele frequencies. If two alleles each occur at frequency 0.5, expected heterozygosity is 0.5; if four alleles each occur at frequency 0.25, it is 0.75. These values describe population-level diversity and must not be mistaken for the probability of informative segregation in a specific family.

Restriction fragment length polymorphism

Restriction enzymes recognize particular DNA sequences and cleave at defined positions relative to those sequences. A mutation can create or destroy a recognition site, or change the length of DNA between two sites. Consequently, homologous chromosomes may produce restriction fragments of different lengths when treated with the same enzyme. A restriction fragment length polymorphism, abbreviated RFLP, is the inherited difference in fragment size revealed by such an analysis.

Imagine a genomic region bounded by two conserved restriction sites 8 kilobases apart. One allele contains an additional site 3 kilobases from the left boundary, whereas the other does not. Complete digestion yields fragments of 3 and 5 kilobases from the first allele and one fragment of 8 kilobases from the second. A probe complementary to a unique sequence lying entirely within the left 3-kilobase interval detects the 3-kilobase fragment in one allele and the 8-kilobase fragment in the other. It does not detect the 5-kilobase fragment because that fragment lacks the complementary sequence. A heterozygote therefore displays the 3- and 8-kilobase signals. The sum of detected band lengths need not equal the total genomic region length, because the probe selectively reveals fragments containing its target.

Classical genomic RFLP analysis separates restriction fragments by electrophoresis and detects selected fragments through Southern hybridization. A genome contains a very large number of restriction fragments; detecting every fragment as a simple collection of DNA bands would not normally identify a particular locus. Hybridization supplies sequence specificity. A unique probe gives a much more interpretable pattern than a repetitive probe that binds many unrelated regions. For an appropriate single-copy locus, the two homologous allele forms can often be distinguished codominantly.

The 1980 proposal by Botstein and colleagues showed how restriction fragment polymorphisms could provide landmarks for constructing human linkage maps without requiring each marker to produce a visible trait. The logic was especially important for human genetics, where controlled crosses cannot be performed. DNA polymorphisms could be followed through existing pedigrees, and linkage between an unknown disease locus and informative markers could narrow the search for the gene. Linkage indicated a chromosomal neighbourhood, not a functional identity between the marker and the disease gene.

Incomplete restriction digestion is an important confounder. A partially digested sample may contain a larger fragment even when the corresponding fully digested allele lacks it. If an investigator calls the larger product a new allele without verifying digestion, the apparent genotypes can become systematically wrong. Multiple independent markers, complete-digestion controls and known reference genotypes provide complementary checks. RFLP approaches are generally locus-specific and reproducible when well designed, but classical Southern-based analysis is relatively labour intensive and requires suitable DNA and probes. PCR-based restriction assays can interrogate smaller predefined regions and should be distinguished from the original genomic Southern approach.

Reading restriction-fragment patterns and evaluating evidence

The distinction between a genomic restriction map and an RFLP genotype is especially useful in examination problems. A restriction map specifies the positions of particular recognition sites along a molecule. A Southern RFLP assay reports the sizes of fragments that contain a probe-complementary region. If the probe location is unspecified, it may be impossible to predict which fragments will be detected. When a problem gives the complete restriction pattern, the position of the probe must be applied as a separate constraint rather than treating every digested fragment as a visible allele.

In a hypothetical 12 kb region, suppose conserved restriction sites lie at positions 0 and 12 kb. Allele X contains additional sites at 4 and 9 kb, yielding fragments of 4, 5 and 3 kb. Allele Y lacks the site at 4 kb but retains the site at 9 kb, yielding fragments of 9 and 3 kb. If the probe lies between positions 5 and 7 kb, it detects the 5 kb fragment in X and the 9 kb fragment in Y. An XY heterozygote has both detected bands. If the probe instead lies between positions 10 and 11 kb, the detected fragment is 3 kb for both alleles, and the assay cannot distinguish the polymorphism. The underlying restriction-site variation is real, but the second probe does not reveal it.

Suppose a third allele Z has an additional insertion of 2 kb between positions 5 and 7 of the X reference arrangement and retains the flanking restriction sites. Under a simplified assumption that the insertion introduces no new recognition site and does not disrupt the probe sequence, the middle fragment changes from 5 to 7 kb. A probe within an unchanged complementary segment will now distinguish X, Y and Z by 5, 9 and 7 kb products respectively. If both X and Z retain the same recognition-site pattern, the difference comes from the intervening distance rather than creation or loss of a restriction site. A marker family based on fragment size can therefore represent multiple molecular sources of variation.

The same problem illustrates why a size difference alone does not uniquely determine the molecular change. A 7 kb product could also result from a different restriction-site arrangement in another allele. To identify the causal structural change, one would need additional evidence such as another enzyme's restriction pattern, the sequence of the region or a targeted assay designed to distinguish the competing models. For mapping inheritance, the 7 kb marker class may still be useful if it is reproducible and follows the expected segregation. Mapping can proceed before the precise underlying mutation is characterized, provided the marker's operational identity is unambiguous in the cross.

Classical RFLP-based mapping provided a way to discover informative markers without prior knowledge of a trait's causal gene. Botstein, White, Skolnick and Davis proposed this approach in 1980, arguing that inherited DNA polymorphisms could provide widespread genomic landmarks. A linkage signal arises when particular marker alleles and trait alleles are transmitted together more often than expected under independent assortment. The crucial historical insight was to use naturally occurring DNA variation as a source of genetic landmarks, rather than waiting to identify enough observable biochemical or morphological phenotypes. The marker did not need to be within the gene determining the trait.

The logic also explains why an RFLP can be less informative in a particular family than in a population survey. Suppose the relevant parent has the same fragment allele on both homologs. Even if a different fragment allele is common elsewhere, the parent provides no marker segregation. One would seek another polymorphism in the same region, ideally one for which that parent is heterozygous. Conversely, if one enzyme and probe combination gives an invariant product, a different assay may detect a nearby sequence difference and provide the needed information. In this context the practical objective is not to maximize the number of bands but to identify marker alleles whose transmission can be recognized.

Random amplified polymorphic DNA

RAPD, or random amplified polymorphic DNA, uses short primers of arbitrary sequence to sample anonymous regions of a genome. A detectable amplification product requires compatible primer binding positions in opposite orientations and within an amplifiable distance. A nucleotide change affecting a primer-binding site, or a change in the length or arrangement of the intervening sequence, can alter the resulting band pattern. The investigator does not need to know the complete sequence of the target regions before selecting the arbitrary primer.

A single reaction may reveal several products from different genomic locations. Each reliably resolved band may be treated as an individual candidate marker, but the bands are not automatically alleles of the same locus. An individual producing four bands and another producing three have not necessarily gained or lost a whole gene. The observed difference might reflect a small primer-binding variant, an amplification competition effect or a genuine larger genomic alteration. Only segregation and additional characterization can establish how a particular band should be interpreted.

Most RAPD bands are scored as dominant presence or absence markers. If the homologous chromosome in a diploid lacks the amplifiable configuration, it may contribute no visible signal, while the other chromosome supplies the band. A heterozygote and a homozygote carrying the amplified allele then appear the same. Consequently, the choice of mapping population and segregation model becomes critical. The ease of surveying variation must be balanced against loss of genotype information.

RAPD profiles can be sensitive to technical conditions. A weak or inconsistent band is poor evidence of heritable polymorphism. If the same DNA sample yields different profiles in independent observations, a biological explanation is not warranted until technical variability has been excluded. Conversely, repeated observation of a band difference, followed by inheritance in the predicted classes, strengthens the case for a genetic marker. Conversion of an informative anonymous band into a sequence-characterized locus can improve specificity; that is a distinct follow-up strategy rather than an intrinsic property of every RAPD band.

What a RAPD fingerprint can and cannot establish

To understand why arbitrary priming can detect an inherited polymorphism, picture one arbitrary primer sequence written from its 5′ end to its 3′ end. A copy of that primer can bind a complementary segment on either DNA strand. On one strand, extension proceeds in one direction along the reference chromosome; on the complementary strand, extension proceeds in the opposite direction. If two compatible binding regions face one another, newly synthesized strands can eventually provide templates with defined primer-compatible ends. A discrete product may then accumulate. An arbitrary primer is not a primer without a sequence; its sequence is defined, but the genomic regions it will sample are not chosen in advance from known locus-specific information.

The geometry of those priming sites matters as much as their existence. Two sites that face away from one another do not normally define the interval between them as an exponentially amplified product. Two sites too far apart may fail to produce a detectable fragment even if their sequence matches are suitable. A mutation that reduces primer binding at one end can diminish or eliminate the corresponding band. A mutation in the interior may have no effect on product presence if it changes neither the primer sites nor the product's resolvable size. Thus a band-negative allele cannot be interpreted automatically as deletion of all DNA between the two primer sites.

In the work reported by Williams and colleagues in 1990, the central experimental question was whether amplification with a single arbitrary primer could detect sequence-dependent differences that behaved as inherited genetic markers. Detecting a different profile in two organisms establishes a candidate difference; demonstrating its transmission among their offspring is a stronger genetic test. A clear band in one parent but not the other can be followed through a segregating family. If the pattern matches the expectations for an inherited locus, the band becomes a candidate landmark for mapping. Repeated observation of the same product strengthens confidence that it reflects a reproducible property of the DNA rather than a transient analytical effect.

A simplified fingerprint comparison illustrates how to record such observations. Suppose parent P produces distinct bands at 300, 500 and 800 base pairs, while parent Q produces bands at 300, 650 and 800 base pairs. The 300 and 800 base-pair bands provide no observable parental contrast for this assay. The 500 and 650 base-pair bands are two candidate polymorphic characters. It is tempting to describe them as two alleles of a single length-variable locus, but that inference is premature. They could originate at distinct genomic regions. Their segregation patterns, and where necessary their sequences or mapped positions, can distinguish those possibilities.

If every descendant that has the 500 base-pair band lacks the 650 base-pair band, and every descendant with the 650 base-pair band lacks the 500 base-pair band, the pattern may suggest mutually exclusive inherited variants in the particular population. However, close linkage in opposite parental phases could produce much the same result in a finite sample. In a diploid F2, a simple model in which the two bands represent alternative length alleles of one codominantly detected locus would predict heterozygotes displaying both. The absence of that class would call for checking the inheritance model or scoring. In fixed haploid-derived lines, absence of double-positive genotypes may be perfectly compatible with alternative alleles at a locus. The population design changes what can be inferred from the same band observations.

A useful conceptual distinction is between a fingerprint and a genetic map. A fingerprint is a collection of measured features that helps distinguish samples. Even an anonymous band may contribute to identification or similarity assessment if it is reproducibly scored. A map additionally requires a defensible statement about genomic position or linkage relationship. A collection of differences between two parental fingerprints is therefore not yet a genetic map. Genotypes in descendants, informative recombination, or independent physical localization are needed before the candidate bands can be arranged as landmarks.

For an examination problem that describes an inconsistent RAPD profile, first determine whether the inconsistency is between biological samples or between repeat observations of the same sample. Differences between unrelated individuals may reflect genuine variation. Differences between measurements of the same DNA sample raise concern about measurement reliability, especially if the relevant bands are weak. A robust interpretation of absence depends on evidence that the overall assay functioned and that the specific target was capable of being detected when present. The methodological limitation is not that RAPD variation can never be real; it is that an anonymous band must earn its status as a genetic marker through appropriate evidence.

Amplified fragment length polymorphism

AFLP combines restriction digestion with selective amplification of a subset of the resulting DNA fragments. Adaptors provide known sequence elements at restriction-fragment ends, allowing primers to target those fragments. Additional selective bases at primer ends reduce the number of amplified fragments, creating a reproducible subset of genomic products that can be resolved as a profile. This is a conceptual description of the relationship between fragmentation and detection, not a laboratory recipe.

The essential difference between AFLP and RAPD is the source of primer-defined specificity. RAPD relies on arbitrary primer-binding sites distributed in the genome. AFLP first defines a collection of restriction fragments, then selects subsets through adaptor-associated primers. Polymorphism may alter restriction sites, selective-primer matching or fragment lengths. Because many fragments can be surveyed in one profile, AFLP has been useful when researchers need genome-wide marker coverage without an existing detailed reference sequence.

AFLP bands are commonly scored as dominant markers. This does not mean codominant information is physically impossible; under some circumstances corresponding length alleles can be identified. Nevertheless, anonymous presence or absence scoring is typical, and one should not infer a three-genotype classification merely because the gel contains many bands. Like RAPD, coincident migration of distinct fragments can create apparent identity, especially when comparing evolutionarily distant genomes.

Vos and colleagues described AFLP in 1995. Its historical significance lies in combining the reproducible sequence recognition of restriction digestion with the efficiency of selective DNA amplification. A researcher comparing AFLP with an older RFLP system should distinguish the number of loci sampled per assay from the information available at each locus. A dense set of dominant AFLP markers may be more useful for a coarse genome survey than a handful of codominant markers, while an individual codominant marker can offer clearer inference of heterozygosity. Neither method is universally superior.

Selective sampling and interpretation of AFLP profiles

A large genome contains many restriction recognition sites and therefore yields many fragments upon digestion. If all of those fragments were amplified indiscriminately and separated together, many would overlap and the resulting profile would be difficult to interpret. AFLP reduces this complexity by sampling an identifiable subset of fragments. The selectivity rests on known sequences at fragment ends and on additional nucleotide requirements near those ends. The added selectivity does not modify the inherited DNA sequence; it determines which members of the restriction-fragment collection contribute a detectable product.

A simple probability model helps explain the idea without substituting a model for a measured profile. Imagine that at the end of a candidate fragment one additional specified nucleotide must match the genomic base adjoining the primer-defined region. If the four bases are independently and equally likely, approximately one quarter of candidates match that requirement at that end. If a specified nucleotide is required independently at each of two ends, approximately one sixteenth of the candidates satisfy both conditions. For two specified bases at each end, the corresponding simplified fraction becomes one in 256. These fractions are illustrative expectations for a deliberately simple random-sequence model, not experimental yields or universal properties of genomes. Base composition, recognition-site distribution, fragment length and sequence dependencies all affect the actual collection of products.

The model shows why selective amplification can control profile complexity. More selectivity reduces the number of candidate fragments and can make individual bands easier to resolve. Less selectivity may increase the number of potential observations but can also increase overlapping products. If two fragments have similar sizes and migrate together, increasing the theoretical number of amplified loci does not necessarily increase the number of independently interpretable markers. The quantity of DNA features generated must be distinguished from the quantity of reliable genetic information obtained.

The primary work by Vos and colleagues in 1995 described the general principle of selective amplification of restriction fragments as a DNA fingerprinting technique. For mapping, an investigator can compare profiles of parents, choose reproducible polymorphic products and score their inheritance among descendants. The restriction-based definition of fragment ends is one source of the method's systematic character. A polymorphism affecting a restriction site may change which fragments are available, while a polymorphism at a selectively matched base may affect which fragment enters the detected subset. Neither scenario requires the entire genomic segment to be absent in the individual without the band.

Consider two marker bands scored in a set of fixed plant lines. Band X occurs in 39 of 80 lines and band Y occurs in 40. If the two bands segregated independently and their frequencies were near one half, all four combinations would be expected at roughly equal frequencies. Suppose the observed combinations are 36 lines with both bands, 3 with X only, 4 with Y only and 37 with neither. The pattern shows strong co-occurrence in this illustrative dataset. It suggests that the parental marker states are linked or otherwise nonindependently inherited, provided the sampling and scoring assumptions are sound. One must not automatically report 7/80 as a single-meiosis recombination fraction, because the fixed lines may have accumulated recombination through multiple generations. Their construction determines the mapping model.

The same observations could have a technical explanation if the two bands were nearly overlapping and systematically scored together. Their similar transmission is evidence of a relationship only if they are measured as distinct, reliable markers. It is also possible for two physically distant loci to show nonindependent ancestry in a selected or structured sample. Genetic mapping uses the expected segregation of a defined population to control these alternatives. It is not enough to compute a similarity coefficient from a fingerprint and name that value a recombination distance.

AFLP and RAPD therefore share the ability to generate anonymous multilocus information but differ in the mechanisms defining their profiles. The comparison is especially useful when the examination gives an assay description instead of its abbreviation. A reference to restriction fragments with attached known end sequences and selective primers points toward AFLP. A reference to one arbitrary primer recognizing distributed genomic sites points toward RAPD. A reference to a unique hybridization probe detecting inherited restriction fragments points toward RFLP. The same restriction enzyme may appear in more than one type of assay; the decisive feature is the overall relationship between fragment generation, signal detection and genetic interpretation.

Simple sequence repeats and microsatellites

Simple sequence repeats are tandem arrays of short DNA motifs, often one to six nucleotides long. They are commonly called microsatellites or short tandem repeats. A locus containing eight copies of the motif CA differs in length from the corresponding locus containing twelve copies if the flanking regions are otherwise unchanged. Replication-associated slippage is an important source of variation in repeat number. Different repeat counts may persist as alternative alleles in populations.

A locus-specific assay ordinarily uses the relatively conserved sequences flanking the repeat. Suppose the total flanking contribution to an amplified fragment is 120 base pairs. The eight-repeat allele has length 120 + 2 × 8 = 136 base pairs, whereas the twelve-repeat allele has length 144 base pairs. The predicted heterozygote contains both products. The difference of eight base pairs corresponds to four repeat units, not eight repeat units. This simple calculation connects an electrophoretic size measurement with a molecular model of the allele.

Microsatellites are attractive in linkage experiments because one locus may contain several alleles. In a family where both parents have different repeat-length alleles, transmission of each parental copy can often be inferred. However, fragment length is not a unique sequence identifier. Independent changes can produce the same length, and an insertion in flanking DNA may alter product length without changing the repeat count. The size class must be interpreted as a measured marker allele rather than a complete sequence description.

PCR slippage can generate minor products called stutter peaks or bands, often differing by one repeat unit from a main product. A stutter product is not evidence of a third inherited allele in an ordinary diploid. Calling alleles requires a reliable interpretation of the signal pattern and awareness of the assay's resolution. Flanking-sequence variation can also lead to nonamplifying alleles. Such errors are especially important when an apparent homozygote has unexpected segregation among offspring. The biological model and the technical observation must be checked together.

Reading microsatellite genotypes in families and populations

A microsatellite genotype is usually inferred from the lengths of products associated with one defined locus. Consider a hypothetical family in which a mother carries alleles of 142 and 150 base pairs and a father carries alleles of 146 and 154 base pairs. Their possible offspring genotypes are 142/146, 142/154, 150/146 and 150/154. Assuming ordinary segregation and no other differences in transmission, each combination has probability one quarter. Because all four parental allele sizes differ, an offspring's two product lengths identify which maternal and paternal allele were transmitted. This locus is informative for both parents.

Now change the father's genotype to 142/150. An offspring with products of 142 and 150 base pairs may have inherited either combination of parental copies. The same measured genotype can therefore correspond to two parental transmissions. An offspring with only a 142 allele would be expected to have inherited 142 from both parents if the assay is reliable and no nonamplifying allele is present. An offspring homozygous for 150 would similarly identify both transmissions. Thus a multiallelic marker can have partially informative genotype categories in a given family. The information value is determined by parental combinations and the specific offspring observation, not only by the number of alleles known in the wider population.

The expected heterozygosity of a locus provides one way to quantify allele diversity before a family or population is chosen. If two independently sampled gene copies have allele probabilities p₁ through pₖ, the probability that both copies have allele i is pᵢ². These alternatives are mutually exclusive, so the total probability of matching allele identities is the sum of the squared frequencies. Its complement gives the probability of different allele identities. In a randomly mating diploid population under the corresponding equilibrium assumptions, that complement equals the expected proportion of heterozygotes. This derivation shows exactly why uneven allele frequencies can reduce the information supplied by a nominally multiallelic marker.

For example, compare two hypothetical SSR loci. Locus A has three allele frequencies of 0.90, 0.05 and 0.05. Its expected heterozygosity is 1 − (0.81 + 0.0025 + 0.0025) = 0.185. Locus B is biallelic with equal frequencies, giving expected heterozygosity 0.50. Despite having more alleles, locus A is less variable by this measure because most copies in the population belong to the same allele. This does not make locus A useless: it could be fully informative in a cross between two individuals known to carry different homozygous alleles. Population diversity and cross-specific informativeness answer related but different questions.

Observed heterozygosity can differ from its expected value. Suppose 100 diploid individuals are genotyped at a biallelic SSR locus, with 40 AA, 20 AB and 40 BB. Both alleles have frequency one half, so the random-mating expectation is 50 heterozygotes among 100 individuals, whereas only 20 are observed. A deficit of observed heterozygotes may indicate population subdivision, inbreeding or technical problems such as allele dropout. It cannot be assigned to a single cause from these counts alone. The population-genetics treatment of such departures belongs to 8.E; here the example shows why the measurement process and the biological sampling scheme must be considered together when evaluating a marker.

Repeat-unit length also determines the interpretation of peak spacing. At a tetranucleotide repeat, neighboring alleles differing by one repeat are expected to differ by four base pairs if the flanks do not vary. A minor peak four base pairs shorter than a major product may be a stutter product, a genuine additional allele or another amplification artifact depending on the observed pattern and known assay characteristics. A heterozygote can have alleles one repeat apart, in which case a genuine shorter allele and a stutter product from the larger allele may overlap. Simply counting every peak above an arbitrary visual threshold can lead to incorrect genotype assignments.

Microsatellite length identity can also arise by independent mutational paths. One ancestral allele may gain repeats, while another loses repeats, and both can ultimately produce the same fragment size. This phenomenon, often discussed as size homoplasy, matters especially when geneticists compare distant populations or species rather than tracing a few generations within a known pedigree. The shared size state need not imply recent inheritance from the same ancestral chromosome. The locus may remain useful for mapping in a controlled family because the question is transmission over a limited pedigree, while its use for reconstructing deeper relationships requires greater attention to mutation and sequence context.

The principal exam skill is to treat an SSR signal as a locus-specific length measurement with an explicit biological model. Subtract constant flanking sequence before computing repeat counts, divide by the repeat-unit length, and check whether the result is an integer under the assumptions of the question. If the calculation yields a noninteger count, a variable flank, an incorrect locus model or a measurement discrepancy may be involved. If the question asks whether a length allele is informative, inspect the parental genotypes. If it asks whether two alleles are identical by descent, recognize that matching fragment lengths are not sufficient proof of their historical origin.

Single nucleotide polymorphism

A single nucleotide polymorphism, abbreviated SNP, is a variable nucleotide position in a set of homologous sequences. For example, a reference sequence may contain an A at a position where another chromosome carries G. A diploid individual's genotype at that position could be AA, AG or GG, provided the locus is autosomal, diploid and uniquely assayed. A SNP is often biallelic, although more than two nucleotide states can exist in a population.

A SNP can be synonymous, nonsynonymous, regulatory or located in a region with no known direct function. The classification of the sequence difference depends on its genomic context, while its usefulness as a marker depends on whether the alternative forms can be scored and inherited informatively. A neutral SNP may be linked to a causal variant and can therefore track a trait. A functional SNP may have no detectable association with the trait under study. The word marker does not imply pathogenicity, benefit or functional neutrality.

Genotyping can be based on sequencing, allele-specific hybridization or other sequence-discriminating methods. Unlike fragment length scoring, the intended observation is the nucleotide identity at a defined position. A dense collection of SNPs can provide fine coverage of a genome. Nevertheless, a high number of markers does not create new meiotic crossovers. If several closely spaced SNPs are transmitted together in every sampled individual, they provide essentially the same segregation information for that family. They may delimit a physical interval but cannot resolve gene order within it by linkage data alone.

Missing genotype calls should be distinguished from confirmed absence of an allele. A sample with insufficient evidence for a genotype must not automatically be labelled homozygous reference. Systematic missingness in one genotype class can distort segregation ratios and create apparent linkage. When interpreting a genotype matrix, investigators need to distinguish biological variation from measurement uncertainty before calculating recombination frequencies.

SNP marker panels, haplotypes and interpretation of genotype evidence

A single SNP usually provides a limited set of allele states, but collections of SNPs can represent the inheritance of long chromosome segments. The word haplotype describes a set of alleles arranged on one chromosome copy or DNA molecule. An individual's genotype at several SNPs lists which nucleotide states are present at those positions, but it does not necessarily identify how those states are arranged on the homologs. If the individual is heterozygous at two positions, alternative haplotype arrangements can generate exactly the same unphased pair of genotypes. This distinction is fundamental for understanding both the power and limitations of dense SNP data.

Consider two neighboring biallelic SNPs. At the first position the alleles are A and G; at the second they are C and T. An individual with A/G and C/T genotypes may carry AC and GT haplotypes, or AT and GC haplotypes. A controlled cross with known homozygous parents can determine the phase directly. In an unrelated individual, family information or other evidence may be needed. The genotype data at each locus alone do not distinguish the two arrangements. A haplotype assignment must therefore be supported by evidence rather than generated by placing all the first-listed alleles together by convention.

A marker panel can contain redundant information. Suppose ten adjacent SNPs always identify the same parental segment in every offspring of a cross. Adding them to a genetic map confirms that the segment contains multiple inherited landmarks but does not independently locate a crossover within it. If one progeny has the parental-A state at the first five SNPs and the parental-B state at the last five, the observed crossover breakpoint can be localized to the region between the fifth and sixth informative markers, subject to the model and absence of scoring errors. Additional informative markers within that region could narrow the physical interval. If no sampled progeny experienced a crossover in a particular region, however, merely increasing marker density does not create a linkage-based ordering signal within that unrecombined block.

Sequence specificity is a major advantage of SNP assays, yet even a well-defined nucleotide variant can become difficult to score in a genome containing closely related duplicated sequences. If an assay unintentionally measures two paralogous regions, different nucleotide states from the two loci might be misinterpreted as heterozygosity at one locus. Repeated apparent heterozygosity in otherwise fixed lines may be a clue that the measurement does not correspond to a single diploid locus. Independent sequence localization and consistent parental segregation can help resolve the discrepancy. This example shows why knowing the target nucleotide is not by itself sufficient; the assayed genomic location must also be understood.

For a simple two-allele detection system, the observation is often interpreted as supporting one of three genotype classes: homozygous for the first allele, heterozygous, or homozygous for the second allele. But experimental signals do not always fall into three perfectly distinct categories. Weak or ambiguous observations may be left uncalled. This is preferable to assigning genotypes without evidence, especially if a systematic error at a marker would introduce false changes of parental origin along a map. When a question supplies the genotyping outcome rather than the measurement process, one should use its stated assumptions, but in an experimental interpretation problem ambiguous data are themselves important evidence.

A SNP designated as the reference allele is simply the state recorded in the chosen reference sequence at the specified coordinate. It is not necessarily the ancestral allele, the most frequent allele, a wild-type functional allele or the allele with the most favorable phenotype. The alternate allele is defined relative to that reference. This nomenclature is particularly important when mapping results from different reference assemblies are compared. A coordinate without a specified assembly and sequence orientation may be ambiguous. Two descriptions using complementary strands can refer to the same substitution, even though the letter pairs differ. These are coordinate and representation issues, not biological changes in inheritance.

The density of SNP variation makes it possible to compare markers across genetic and physical maps in organisms with genome assemblies. If a genetic map orders three markers A, B and C but their assigned physical coordinates place C between A and B, the discrepancy warrants investigation. Potential explanations include insufficient linkage resolution, incorrect marker scoring, a physical assembly error or genuine structural differences between the reference and the mapping parents. A genetic map derived from one cross and a physical map derived from another genome are related but not automatically identical representations. Evidence from both types of map can reveal problems that neither would expose on its own.

Finally, the word association must be interpreted according to the source of data. A SNP associated with a trait in a controlled cross may mark a segment inherited from one of the parents. In an unrelated population, a similar association can arise because nearby variants are nonrandomly inherited together, or because the sampled groups differ in ancestry and phenotype for other reasons. The detailed population association and quantitative trait models belong to later nodes. The essential lesson at this point is that a highly significant SNP signal or a precisely reported physical coordinate does not itself establish the molecular mechanism of the trait. Localization guides the search for functional evidence.

Choosing an assay from the information required

The five conventional marker systems differ in what must be known about the target sequence, the number of loci sampled per observation, the number of distinguishable allele states and the types of error likely to affect interpretation. These are separate dimensions. A method with excellent reproducibility may interrogate few loci per assay. A method with many observable products may yield limited genotype information per band. A method that is optimal for distinguishing two inbred parents may be less useful for estimating diversity in a heterogeneous collection. There is therefore no general ranking that can substitute for defining the genetic question.

Suppose the objective is to establish a map in a species for which little sequence information is available. Anonymous fingerprinting methods can reveal variation without requiring a reference genome. RAPD offers a conceptually simple way to sample arbitrary genomic regions, although technical sensitivity and locus identity are important limitations. AFLP can provide numerous markers through selective sampling of restriction fragments and may offer more reproducible profiles under controlled conditions. These advantages must be compared with the information loss of typical dominant scoring. If the mapping population consists of fixed lines, each locus is generally represented by one homozygous allele state, so the inability to distinguish a heterozygote is less problematic than in a segregating F2.

If the objective is instead to distinguish all three genotype classes at a specific diploid locus, a codominant SSR or SNP is often attractive. An SSR can offer several alleles in a population, allowing the inheritance of parental copies to be followed. Its limitations include repeat-size ambiguity, nonamplifying alleles and the need for a locus-specific assay. A SNP usually has fewer alleles per locus but can be placed at a precisely specified sequence position. A set of many informative SNPs can characterize chromosomal ancestry at high density. For a simple cross between two fixed contrasting parents, even a biallelic marker provides the full three-genotype distinction when the heterozygote is detectable.

The ability to transfer a marker assay between species should not be assumed merely because a method has worked in one organism. SSR flanking regions may be sufficiently conserved in closely related species, but sequence changes can prevent amplification or lead to an unintended locus. Arbitrary-primer profiles can change with genome organization and may be difficult to compare when fragments have uncertain homology. SNP assays typically target a defined sequence and need appropriate sequence conservation or redesign. The relevant comparison is always between homologous loci, or between explicitly characterized features, rather than between unverified bands that happen to have the same size.

A molecular marker may also be chosen for its relationship to the biological target. A random anonymous marker far from a gene can help establish chromosome-wide map coverage but may provide little predictive information for a particular trait. A tightly linked flanking marker may track the trait well in a specific cross. An assay targeting an established causal variant can be more directly informative about that variant, but even then other genetic and environmental influences may affect phenotype. Marker selection thus progresses from broad mapping to increasingly specific inference as biological evidence accumulates.

This reasoning is particularly valuable in examination questions that describe a desired outcome rather than explicitly naming a technique. If an unknown species requires many anonymous polymorphic loci, ask which assays do not require detailed target sequences. If an F2 must be classified into AA, AB and BB, ask which scoring schemes resolve both alleles. If two populations contain variants of a known nucleotide position, a sequence-specific SNP assay addresses the question directly. If the data describe variable restriction fragments detected by a labelled probe, an RFLP explanation is appropriate. The most defensible method is the one whose measurement properties match the inference required.

From marker inheritance to genetic and physical maps

A genetic map represents relative positions inferred from inheritance, usually expressed in centimorgans. A physical map represents locations or distances along DNA, expressed in base pairs or their multiples. Although they describe the same chromosome, they are not proportional scales. Recombination probability varies along chromosomes because crossover events are not uniformly distributed. Two regions with the same physical length may therefore have very different genetic lengths.

Suppose a testcross has 500 offspring, of which 40 carry recombinant combinations at two codominant markers. The observed recombination fraction is 40 divided by 500, or 0.08. For a short interval this is approximately 8 centimorgans. The estimate assumes informative phase, accurate genotyping and no differential recovery of the parental and recombinant classes. A longer interval can contain multiple crossovers, including events that restore the parental arrangement at the two outer markers. Consequently, simple percentage recombination increasingly underestimates map distance as intervals become larger.

Consider an illustrative comparison in which markers A and B are 2 megabases apart but genetically separated by 1 centimorgan, while B and C are 2 megabases apart and genetically separated by 8 centimorgans. Both physical intervals are equal, but the inferred recombination rates are 0.5 and 4 centimorgans per megabase. The second interval has the greater observed rate. This does not demonstrate that every base in the second interval has an eightfold higher recombination probability; the average can reflect a localized hotspot, differences in chromatin or a variety of other factors.

Marker density and recombination sample size impose different limits. Increasing density supplies more landmarks at which to detect crossovers that actually occurred. Increasing the number of informative meioses increases the opportunity to observe crossovers within a short region. If no recombinants appear among 100 independent informative gametes, this is evidence that the fraction is low, but not proof of zero recombination or identical physical coordinates. Under a binomial model, the probability of zero observed recombinants at true fraction r is (1 − r)^100. At r = 0.01 this probability is approximately 0.366. A one-percent recombinant fraction is therefore quite compatible with the observation of zero in such a sample.

Recovering parental phase from a marker genotype matrix

The previous discussion introduced recombination as a measurable separation of parental alleles. Before counting recombinants, however, the investigator must establish which alleles were together in the parental chromosomes. The genotype AaBb tells us that the organism has an A allele and an a allele at the first locus and B and b at the second locus. It does not tell us whether A and B are on the same homolog. The arrangements AB/ab and Ab/aB have the same unphased genotype. Their parental and recombinant gamete categories are reversed. For this reason, the notation used for marker data must distinguish allele identity from haplotype whenever the question requires linkage inference.

Consider two inbred plant lines. One line has alleles A and B at two DNA markers; the other has alleles a and b. Because the parents are homozygous at both loci, their F1 has phase AB/ab without requiring an additional inference. If it is crossed to a double recessive ab/ab tester, the genotype of every offspring reveals the gamete of the F1. An A/a; B/b offspring received AB, an a/a; b/b offspring received ab, an A/a; b/b offspring received Ab, and an a/a; B/b offspring received aB. The observed diploid genotypes have been translated into haplotypes transmitted by one informative parent. This translation, rather than the name of the marker technique, is the basis of the mapping calculation.

In an illustrative sample of 250 progeny, suppose that 110 receive AB, 105 receive ab, 16 receive Ab, and 19 receive aB. The total recombinant count is 35, giving r = 35/250 = 0.14. The parental categories are both abundant, and the two reciprocal recombinant categories are broadly comparable in number. The small difference between sixteen and nineteen is not on its own a reason to infer an asymmetric molecular process; ordinary sampling produces unequal counts. If the reciprocal categories showed a strong and reproducible difference, one would investigate whether offspring survival or marker scoring differs between classes. The estimated r is conditional on the observed sample being representative of the gametes of the informative parent.

An F2 requires a different analysis. Suppose two F1 individuals both have the phase AB/ab. An offspring with genotype AaBb can be formed by an AB gamete together with an ab gamete, or by an Ab gamete together with an aB gamete. It can also be produced by reversing which parent contributed the two complementary haplotypes. Thus even if the offspring genotype is codominantly scored at both loci, the parental contribution is ambiguous in this particular category. It is not valid to classify every double heterozygote as a parental or recombinant offspring. One can infer recombination statistically using the expected genotype frequencies under the model, but a direct testcross counting rule cannot simply be transplanted to the F2.

This distinction is important when comparing marker density with mapping information. A dense data matrix may contain many thousands of entries, yet some parental transmissions may remain uninformative because the genotypes do not reveal which homolog was inherited. In a pedigreed animal family, if both parents are A/G at a SNP, an A/G offspring could have inherited A from either parent. If one parent is A/G and the other is A/A, the offspring genotype identifies which allele came from the heterozygous parent. A well-designed map uses those meioses for which the relevant transmissions can be identified or models the uncertainty explicitly.

The concept of phase is also central to marker-assisted tracking of a trait. Assume that a dominant resistance allele R is in coupling phase with marker allele M1 in a particular plant family. The susceptible allele r is linked to marker M2 on the other chromosome. If the marker-to-gene recombination fraction is 0.03, a fraction of gametes will transmit R together with M2 or r together with M1. Selecting every M1-bearing descendant can therefore recover many resistant individuals but need not recover all of them, and it need not exclude every susceptible individual. The marker is a probabilistic indicator of ancestry at the trait locus, not a guarantee that the resistance allele is present.

Using two flanking markers reduces some ambiguity because a crossover that separates one marker from the locus may be detected by the other. It does not eliminate uncertainty altogether. A double crossover within the flanking interval, a scoring error, or an uncharacterized causal variant can still disrupt predictions. The stronger the biological evidence that the assayed sequence itself is the causal variant, the less the selection depends on recombination between distinct loci. This is why genetic mapping and identification of a functional marker are related goals but not identical outcomes.

Interpreting marker data before estimating linkage

A mapping table is a measurement of biological samples, not a perfect transcription of the chromosomes they contain. Genotyping errors can mimic a recombination event because a wrongly called allele may appear to have changed parental origin between adjacent markers. Imagine three markers in the order A, B, C. The observed parental haplotypes are 111 and 222, with each numeral representing the parental origin of the allele. In a given gamete the marker calls are 121. This arrangement is compatible with a crossover in the A–B interval and another crossover in the B–C interval. But if both intervals are very short, an isolated 2 surrounded by a long run of 1 calls can also be a mistaken call at B.

One should not delete every biologically unexpected genotype. Genuine double crossovers do occur and are a central feature of linkage analysis. Instead, examine the strength of evidence. Does the raw signal at B support its reported allele? Is the locus independently scorable in both parents? Do other nearby markers support the same chromosomal segment? Are improbable double-crossover patterns unusually frequent at this one marker across many individuals? A localized excess of such patterns can indicate a scoring problem or an incorrectly ordered marker. The general rule is to test an alternative explanation using independent information rather than rewriting a dataset to match an expectation.

Consider an informative single-copy codominant marker scored in an F2. A sample of 120 individuals gives 44 AA, 32 AB and 44 BB. The Mendelian expectation is 30, 60 and 30. The chi-square statistic is (44−30)²/30 + (32−60)²/60 + (44−30)²/30 = 26.13, with two degrees of freedom if the expected proportions were specified in advance. Such a result is strong evidence against the simple error-free segregation model. The data show a deficit of heterozygotes, but the statistic does not identify the cause. A nonamplifying allele, allele dropout, unexpected population structure, nonrandom survival or a mistaken cross could produce departures from the assumed ratio. Follow-up checks are necessary before interpreting the marker as genetically distorted.

The pattern of departures is often more informative than the mere existence of a statistically significant result. In the preceding example the two homozygous classes are equally elevated while the heterozygote class is depleted. A genotype scoring process that sometimes miscalls heterozygotes as either homozygote might generate this symmetric pattern. By contrast, if BB were strongly depleted but AA and AB remained close to expectation, a genotype-specific survival effect or a failure to recognize one allelic class would be worth considering. These are hypotheses prompted by the observed pattern, not conclusions established by the chi-square test alone.

Missing observations require similar caution. Suppose a cross yields 200 individuals, but a marker pair is jointly scorable in only 180. If 18 of those 180 are recombinant, the observed fraction among jointly informative observations is 0.10. Dividing by all 200 individuals would silently assume that every unscored individual was parental, lowering the estimate to 0.09. If missingness is independent of true genotype, the 180 observations can provide an unbiased sample under that assumption. If recombinant genotypes disproportionately fail the assay, even the estimate based on 180 can be biased. The correct denominator follows from the observation model, not merely from the number of organisms initially collected.

The simplest checks are often powerful. A genotype should be compatible with the parental alleles except when a new mutation or a different biological model is justified. A known homozygous reference sample can establish expected allele size or sequence signal. An independently repeated measurement can reveal inconsistent scoring. A marker with strong evidence of a reproducible inherited polymorphism is preferable to a superficially impressive profile containing many weak products. In a multiallelic SSR assay, an offspring carrying an allele not observed in either parent may reflect a genuine mutation, but it also motivates inspection of stutter, calibration and sample identity. The frequency and pattern of anomalous observations determine which explanations deserve priority.

Reliable genetic inference requires documenting whether unusual observations were retained, treated as missing, or excluded for a stated reason. Removing data solely because they contradict a preferred mapping result biases the map. Conversely, including known invalid calls can inflate the apparent number of recombination events. The appropriate goal is not maximal data retention at any cost but an interpretable relationship between measurements, quality evidence and the inheritance model.

Genetic map resolution, physical coordinates and recombination models

A genetic map is constructed from relationships between inherited marker states. Its coordinate system is therefore tied to the probability distribution of recombination events, not directly to the length of a DNA molecule. To appreciate why two-point recombination fractions cannot be added like ordinary distances, imagine three ordered markers A, B and C. A crossover between A and B can change the association of their alleles. A crossover between B and C can change the association of that pair. If the same chromatid experiences an exchange in each interval, the outer alleles at A and C may return to their parental association while the middle allele reveals the intervening exchange. Consequently, an analysis that ignores the middle marker can miss evidence of multiple crossovers.

This phenomenon explains the difference between map additivity and recombinant fraction saturation. In an idealized short interval with rare multiple exchanges, the recombinant fraction approximates the expected number of relevant exchanges per chromatid. Over larger intervals, parity becomes important: an odd number of exchanges along a chromatid changes the outer-marker association, whereas an even number can restore it. Recombinant fractions therefore approach one half for widely separated linked loci under ordinary mapping models. A fraction near one half cannot distinguish loci on different chromosomes from very distant loci on the same chromosome without additional marker or physical evidence. A map distance, by contrast, can extend well beyond 50 centimorgans when estimated from a sequence of shorter intervals.

Under the Haldane model, exchanges along a chromatid are represented by a Poisson process with mean m, measured in morgans, and no crossover interference is assumed. The probability of observing k events is exp(−m)mᵏ/k!. Summing the probabilities of even values of k gives (1 + exp(−2m))/2; summing odd values gives (1 − exp(−2m))/2. The observed recombinant fraction r corresponds to the odd-event probability in this model. Rearranging yields 1 − 2r = exp(−2m), and taking the natural logarithm gives m = −ln(1 − 2r)/2. Multiplying by 100 gives the estimated distance in centimorgans. The formula is not a universal biological identity; it is a consequence of the chosen crossover model. Interference and other departures can require a different mapping relationship, as discussed in 8.C.01.

For the earlier illustrative testcross estimate r = 0.14, the Haldane model gives m = −ln(0.72)/2, approximately 0.1643 morgans or 16.43 cM. The short-interval approximation gives 14 cM. The difference arises from the probability of undetected even-number exchanges. At r = 0.01, the corresponding Haldane distance is approximately 1.01 cM, close to the simple approximation. At r = 0.40, the Haldane estimate is about 80.47 cM, much greater than 40 cM. The growing discrepancy is a mathematical consequence of the saturation of the two-point recombination fraction. At r = 0.5 the inverse formula is undefined as a finite distance, reflecting the loss of distance information at saturation.

A molecular marker can have a genetic map position even when its physical location is initially unknown. If its segregation connects it consistently to neighboring markers in a linkage group, its position can be estimated relative to those landmarks. This was particularly useful before whole-genome reference sequences were widely available. A physical map provides different evidence: a sequence or genomic fragment can be assigned to a chromosome and coordinate through the organization of DNA molecules or a reference assembly. When both map types are available, a sequence-defined marker acts as a correspondence between them. It identifies a location in base pairs and a location inferred from recombination.

Consider an illustrative chromosome region with four markers at reference positions 1, 3, 5 and 7 Mb. Their inferred genetic positions in one cross are 0, 2, 2.4 and 10.4 cM. The first interval spans 2 Mb and 2 cM, giving a mean of 1 cM/Mb. The second spans the same physical length but only 0.4 cM, giving 0.2 cM/Mb. The third again spans 2 Mb but covers 8 cM, giving 4 cM/Mb. No single conversion factor accurately describes all three intervals. If a trait is mapped between the second and third markers, the small genetic interval still corresponds to a substantial physical region. Conversely, an interval with high recombination can correspond to a relatively short physical distance even when its genetic length is comparatively large.

This relationship has practical implications for locating a gene. A trait mapped to a recombination-poor region may remain associated with many physical candidates because there are few informative crossover events to separate them. Increasing the number of markers within such a region can improve description of existing breakpoints, but the lack of crossovers remains a central limitation. Additional progeny, independent crosses or other forms of localization may be required to resolve the candidate interval. In a recombination-rich region, the same number of progeny may yield more informative breakpoints over an equal physical distance. Mapping resolution depends jointly on biological recombination, sample size and the availability of informative markers.

Sampling uncertainty can be quantified without knowing the complete crossover mechanism. For a direct testcross in which each of N informative gametes is independently classified as parental or recombinant, the recombinant count has a binomial distribution under the simple model. The standard error of the estimated fraction r̂ is approximately the square root of r̂(1 − r̂)/N, provided the sample is sufficiently large for the approximation. For 35 recombinants among 250, r̂ = 0.14 and the estimated standard error is about 0.0219. An approximate 95% interval using 1.96 standard errors runs from 0.097 to 0.183. This interval illustrates sampling variability rather than systematic errors arising from wrong phase, missing calls or selection. Close to zero or one, a more appropriate binomial interval should be used instead of relying blindly on the normal approximation.

If zero recombinant gametes are observed, the standard-error formula based directly on r̂ = 0 produces a misleading zero value. The sample provides a bound rather than proof that the true fraction is zero. In a sample of 300 independent informative gametes, a true recombination fraction of 0.01 would yield a probability of zero recombinants of 0.99 raised to the power 300, approximately 0.049. Thus zero observed events in this illustrative sample would be relatively unlikely if the true fraction were one percent. Solving (1 − r)^N = 0.05 gives a one-sided upper bound under the simple model; for large N and small r this is approximately 3/N. The result makes clear why many informative meioses are needed to support claims of very tight linkage.

Physical maps have their own uncertainties. Sequence assemblies may contain unresolved regions, repetitive sequences or reference-specific chromosome arrangements. A marker positioned at a particular coordinate in a reference genome can map differently relative to surrounding features in individuals carrying an inversion or another structural variant. A discrepancy between genetic and physical order is therefore not automatically a failure of one technique. It can reveal a genuine difference between the genomes compared, limitations of recombination-based ordering, or a measurement or assembly problem. A defensible interpretation explicitly identifies the source of the physical coordinate, the population used for the genetic map and the assumptions connecting them.

Marker mapping across plants, animals and bacteria

In plant genetics, crossing two lines with contrasting marker alleles and a trait of interest creates progeny in which recombination can separate the trait locus from adjacent markers. Selfing, backcrossing or the production of fixed mapping lines changes the distribution of genotype classes and the amount of recombination represented. The choice of population is addressed in the next node. For the present analysis, the key point is that the marker must distinguish parental ancestry and the inheritance model must correspond to the cross actually performed.

Animal linkage mapping may use controlled crosses in model species, existing pedigrees in humans or other documented family structures. A marker informative in one family may be uninformative in another, and sex-specific recombination rates may influence genetic distances. A rare trait can be followed with linked markers even if the trait's underlying molecular variant remains unknown. The association between a marker and a trait in a pedigree depends on the parental phase; the same marker allele can be associated with the alternate trait allele in another family.

Bacteria generally do not undergo the diploid meiosis on which conventional eukaryotic linkage maps are based. A molecular difference can still mark a chromosomal region, and sequence comparisons can establish physical coordinates. Transfer and recombination events may also reveal relationships between markers. The interpretation depends on the process supplying the inherited DNA, such as transformation, conjugation or transduction, each of which has its own mapping assumptions. A bacterial co-transfer fraction cannot simply be substituted into a eukaryotic centimorgan calculation. Subsequent nodes treat the distinct transfer models in greater depth.

Across all three systems, marker association is an observation that requires explanation. A marker may predict a phenotype because it is causal, because it is linked to a causal allele, because it is associated with ancestry, or because of sampling and scoring artifacts. An experimentally observed association is strongest when alternative explanations are addressed by the design. Linkage analysis can localize a gene, but identifying a causal change requires independent evidence connecting sequence, function and phenotype.

Using molecular markers to localize traits in plants and animals

A marker-based mapping experiment begins with a biological question that defines which inheritance observations are useful. Suppose two homozygous plant lines differ in a qualitative trait, and the investigator has evidence consistent with a single segregating locus. Parent P is resistant and parent Q is susceptible. The plants also differ at numerous neutral DNA markers. In the F1, the resistance allele and the parental marker haplotypes can be identified by their parental origin. In progeny of an informative cross, one asks which markers remain associated with resistance and which are frequently separated from it. This converts a phenotypic segregation question into a chromosome-localization question.

For an illustrative backcross to the susceptible parent, assume that resistance is fully penetrant, controlled by a dominant allele and scored accurately. A marker M has two distinct codominant alleles, M1 in the resistant parent and M2 in the susceptible parent. Among 200 backcross offspring, suppose 94 resistant individuals carry M1/M2, 6 resistant individuals carry M2/M2, 7 susceptible individuals carry M1/M2 and 93 susceptible individuals carry M2/M2. The parental combinations are resistance with M1 and susceptibility with M2. The recombinant classes consist of resistance without M1 and susceptibility with M1. The estimated recombinant fraction is (6 + 7)/200 = 0.065. The association is strong but imperfect, as expected for a marker linked to a distinct trait locus.

Now suppose a second marker N gives 80 resistant individuals carrying its resistant-parent allele, 20 resistant individuals lacking it, 22 susceptible individuals carrying it and 78 susceptible individuals lacking it. Its observed recombinant fraction is 42/200 = 0.21. Under comparable scoring and transmission assumptions, the first marker provides stronger evidence of proximity to the trait locus. However, the two recombinant fractions alone do not establish whether M and N lie on the same side of the trait gene or on opposite sides. A three-locus analysis or independent physical information is required to distinguish their order. This illustrates an important difference between ranking two markers by association and constructing an ordered map.

To narrow a candidate interval, a researcher may identify progeny carrying recombination breakpoints on either side of the inferred trait locus. If several independent resistant progeny retain the resistant-parent allele at a central marker but differ at more distant markers, their shared retained region becomes a candidate interval under the simple single-gene model. Susceptible recombinant individuals provide complementary information by identifying regions whose resistant-parent ancestry is not sufficient for the phenotype. Each informative breakpoint establishes a boundary condition, but the exact molecular change remains unidentified until the interval is further examined.

This inference becomes more complicated when the phenotype has incomplete penetrance or environmental variability. If an individual inherits the resistance allele but is classified as susceptible because of environmental conditions or variable expression, its genotype–phenotype pattern can resemble that of a recombinant. Conversely, an individual that lacks the resistance allele may show resistance for another reason. The genetic model of the trait must therefore be evaluated alongside the marker model. Repeated phenotyping or independent trait measurements can be more valuable than adding many markers if the principal source of uncertainty is phenotype classification. The next node addresses the design of mapping populations and the later quantitative-genetics node addresses traits influenced by several loci and environments.

In animal model systems, controlled crosses can supply similar evidence. The organism's reproductive biology may nevertheless alter the informative design. For example, recombination rates can differ between sexes, and some organisms lack ordinary meiotic crossing over in one sex. If a mapping population uses informative meioses mainly from a sex with suppressed recombination, the inferred map may contain large blocks of co-inherited markers even though the physical distances are substantial. The absence of recombination in those transmissions cannot be interpreted as proof that all markers occupy the same genomic position. Choosing an informative parental sex, where biologically and experimentally relevant, can therefore influence mapping resolution.

Human family mapping imposes a further constraint: investigators work with existing family structures rather than designing crosses to optimize segregation. Suppose one parent is heterozygous at a marker, and the other is homozygous. The marker genotype of each child can identify which allele came from the heterozygous parent. If the trait is transmitted from that parent and the family structure is sufficiently informative, co-segregation can support linkage. A family containing very few informative transmissions, however, can yield an apparently perfect association by chance. Multiple pedigrees and likelihood-based methods help evaluate how strongly the observations favor linkage over independent assortment. The formal LOD score treatment belongs to 8.D.02; it should not be replaced by a simple count of marker-positive affected relatives.

Phase can differ across families because a neutral marker allele can be associated with different nearby causal variants in different ancestral chromosomes. A marker allele that tracks a disease-associated allele in one family may track the unaffected allele in another. The difference does not require the neutral marker to change its own biochemical function. It follows from the arrangement of alleles on the inherited chromosomes. For this reason, family-specific linkage evidence and population-wide marker associations are distinct forms of genetic evidence. A mapping result in one family should not be generalized into a universal genotype–phenotype rule without relevant population and functional evidence.

The common principle across plant and animal mapping is that molecular markers make inherited chromosomal segments observable. The biological trait is localized by its relationship to those segments, and the interpretation rests on the actual crossing or family design. An apparent marker association becomes more credible when the underlying phenotypes are reliable, the parental phases are understood, the marker genotypes are reproducible and alternative explanations have been considered. The map is an intermediate scientific result that makes the search for a causal locus more focused; it is not a substitute for establishing that locus's biological function.

Sequence markers and linkage evidence in bacteria

A bacterium with one copy of a chromosomal locus generally does not require a diploid distinction between homozygous and heterozygous genotypes. A variant at that locus can be described directly as an allele or sequence state. This makes some forms of scoring simpler, but it does not mean that all markers behave identically in bacteria. Plasmids, multiple genome copies in growing cells, mixed cultures and duplicated loci can complicate the relationship between detected signal and chromosomal genotype. For an introductory mapping problem, one should identify whether the markers lie on the chromosome, on a plasmid or on another genetic element and use the stated assumptions about copy number and population purity.

A physical comparison between two bacterial chromosome sequences can identify a nucleotide substitution at a defined position. If several variants occur along a known reference, they can serve as markers that distinguish regions of different sequence ancestry. The coordinate relationship between two markers is measured directly along the chromosome, taking circular topology into account when relevant. A linear difference between positions on a circular chromosome may describe one of two possible arcs, so the question must specify which interval is meant or whether the shorter distance is required. For example, on a circular chromosome of length 4 Mb, markers at 0.1 Mb and 3.9 Mb are 3.8 Mb apart along one arc but only 0.2 Mb apart along the other. A physical map should not represent the artificial coordinate origin as a biological discontinuity.

Genetic relationships can also be inferred from the joint acquisition of marker alleles. If two chromosomal markers are often recovered together after a process that transfers limited stretches of DNA, their joint occurrence can support proximity, conditional on the mechanism of transfer and recombination. The basic logic resembles the use of finite fragments in physical localization: loci more likely to be carried within one transferred region can show stronger co-transfer. However, the underlying probability is not the probability of a recombinant gamete from a diploid meiosis. The direction of the relationship is also different: a high cotransfer frequency may support closeness, whereas a high recombinant fraction in a classical short-interval eukaryotic testcross indicates greater genetic separation.

Consider a hypothetical classroom dataset in which an analysis has selected for acquisition of marker X in a collection of bacterial recombinants. Among 100 selected individuals, 78 also carry donor marker Y, while only 12 carry donor marker Z. Assuming the biological mechanism, recovery conditions and marker detection are comparable, these observations are consistent with Y lying closer to X than Z does. The percentages are conditional on selection for X. They do not represent the unconditional probability that both markers enter every cell, nor do they establish a precise number of base pairs between loci. Selection, recombination and transfer-length distributions can affect observed recovery. The later transformation and transduction nodes derive the appropriate models for specific mechanisms.

A strain can also be identified by a pattern of molecular markers distributed across its genome. Such a pattern can distinguish two isolates, but a similarity pattern alone is not equivalent to an ordered genetic map. Two strains might share several marker alleles because of common ancestry, because of independent acquisition of similar DNA or because the chosen markers are widely distributed in the species. If the objective is chromosome mapping, physical position or well-defined transfer and inheritance evidence is required. If the objective is strain identification, reproducibility and discriminatory power of the profile may be the more immediate criteria. The same molecular measurements can be used to answer different biological questions, but their interpretation changes with the purpose.

This distinction is especially important for questions that juxtapose classical bacterial mapping with the use of modern DNA markers. A SNP, a restriction fragment or another sequence variant identifies a feature of the bacterial genome; transformation, conjugation and transduction provide different sources of genetic relationships among such features. The molecular marker is what is observed, while the transfer mechanism determines the inference that can be made from the observation. The remainder of subunit 8.C develops these mechanisms separately so that marker detection is not confused with the process by which DNA moves between bacterial cells.

Somatic cell hybrid mapping and radiation hybrids

Somatic cell hybrid mapping uses a different type of genetic evidence from a meiotic cross. Cells from different species, classically human and rodent cells, can produce hybrids containing genetic material from both parental species. During propagation, different hybrid cell lines may retain different subsets of human chromosomes. If a human-specific gene product or DNA marker is detected only in lines retaining one particular human chromosome, the chromosome-retention pattern supports assignment of the locus to that chromosome.

Consider six hypothetical hybrid lines. Human chromosome 3 is retained in lines 1, 2 and 5; chromosome 7 in lines 1, 3 and 5; and the human-specific marker is detected in lines 1, 2 and 5. The marker and chromosome 3 have perfect concordance in this small panel: all three chromosome-positive lines are marker-positive and all three chromosome-negative lines are marker-negative. Chromosome 7 has two discordant lines: line 2 lacks the chromosome despite the marker being present, and line 3 retains the chromosome despite absence of the marker. The result favours chromosome 3, conditional on accurate chromosome and marker scoring.

Concordance is the proportion of lines in which marker and chromosome are both present or both absent. Discordance is the proportion in which their presence states differ. Here chromosome 3 has concordance 6/6 and chromosome 7 has concordance 4/6. If two chromosomes have identical retention patterns, both receive the same concordance score, and the panel cannot distinguish them. More independently informative hybrid lines or additional chromosomal information are needed.

A human chromosome assignment is not necessarily a precise gene position within that chromosome. Chromosomes can be lost, retained as fragments or rearranged, and gene-product expression can be influenced by the hybrid cellular context. A DNA-based assay of a human-specific sequence may avoid some expression-dependent ambiguity, but it does not eliminate errors arising from chromosome characterization. A strong interpretation requires evidence that the measured marker and retained chromosome segment are correctly identified.

Radiation hybrid mapping extends the retention principle to chromosome fragments. Chromosomal DNA is broken, and independently retained fragments create patterns of co-retention across hybrid lines. Nearby loci are more likely than distant loci to remain on the same unbroken fragment under the model, so patterns of joint retention can be used to infer relative proximity. Radiation hybrid distance depends on breakage and retention, not on meiotic crossover frequency. The centiray is a model-dependent radiation hybrid map unit; it must not be equated with the centimorgan or with a fixed number of base pairs. Radiation hybrid analysis provides historical and conceptual context for physical localization without requiring detailed computational treatment at this level.

Chromosome assignment from hybrid concordance: resolving competing hypotheses

Somatic cell hybrid mapping was historically valuable because it supplied positional evidence without depending on informative families or ordinary meiotic recombination. The experimental logic begins with two cell populations that have distinguishable genetic characteristics. A hybrid cell contains genetic material from both parental populations; in classical human–rodent hybrid systems, different lines may lose different human chromosomes during propagation. By characterizing the remaining human chromosomes and measuring a human-specific DNA sequence or gene product, investigators can compare two binary profiles across a panel. The marker profile becomes an independent observation to be explained by the chromosome-retention data.

The critical comparison includes both presence and absence. If a chromosome carries the marker locus and is retained intact in a hybrid, the marker DNA should be detectable under an accurate assay. If that chromosome is absent and there is no retained fragment carrying the locus, the marker DNA should be absent. For an expression-based marker, detection additionally depends on the gene being expressed in the hybrid context. The most elementary chromosome-assignment model therefore assumes complete retention or loss of the relevant chromosome, reliable scoring and a marker whose presence can be detected whenever its genomic location is retained.

A useful way to formalize the observations is a two-by-two table. Let n₁₁ represent hybrid lines in which both the candidate chromosome and marker are present, n₀₀ those in which both are absent, n₁₀ those with the chromosome present but marker absent, and n₀₁ those with the chromosome absent but marker present. The total number of jointly scored lines is the sum of these four categories. The concordant fraction is (n₁₁ + n₀₀)/N, and the discordant fraction is (n₁₀ + n₀₁)/N. The two sum to one. This is a measure of agreement within the panel, not a meiotic recombination fraction or the probability of linkage in a population.

Suppose a hypothetical panel contains twelve hybrid lines. For chromosome A, the four counts n₁₁, n₀₀, n₁₀ and n₀₁ are 5, 6, 1 and 0. The concordant fraction is 11/12, about 91.7 percent. For chromosome B the counts are 4, 4, 3 and 1, giving concordance 8/12, approximately 66.7 percent. Both candidate chromosomes show some agreement with the marker, but chromosome A is the better match to the simple retention model. The single discordant line is not automatically grounds for rejecting chromosome A: the chromosome may be rearranged, its marker-containing segment may be missing, an expression-based marker may be silent, or one of the scores may be incorrect. Additional evidence is needed to distinguish those explanations.

A high concordance fraction can be misleading when one or both features are very rare. Consider an illustrative panel of 100 lines in which a marker is observed in only two, and a candidate chromosome is absent from every line. The two profiles agree in 98 lines because both are absent. A superficial ranking by agreement would award 98 percent concordance to a chromosome whose retention was never observed, yet the chromosome cannot explain either marker-positive line under the simple model. The lesson is to examine the actual contingency table, including positive concordance and the informativeness of retention variation. A high overall agreement score does not replace a biologically plausible pattern of co-retention.

Likewise, perfect agreement with two candidate chromosomes is not unique evidence for either. If the chromosomes always occur together in the sampled panel, their retention patterns cannot discriminate the hypotheses. A line retaining chromosome A but not B, or B but not A, would be especially informative. If the marker follows A in such a line, the ambiguity may be resolved. More clones with diverse and independently characterized chromosome complements increase the opportunity to distinguish candidate chromosomes. The number of measured hybrid lines matters, but so does the variation in their retained material. A large collection with nearly identical retention profiles can provide less discriminatory evidence than a smaller informative panel.

A marker-positive line scored as lacking the candidate chromosome invites a different class of explanation from a chromosome-positive, marker-negative line. In the former, a marker-containing chromosome fragment may remain integrated elsewhere, or the assay may detect a homologous nonhuman sequence. In the latter, the relevant chromosome segment may be absent despite apparent retention of the chromosome, or gene expression may be suppressed. DNA-based markers and species-specific assays can address some of these alternatives, but none eliminates the need to characterize the hybrid genome correctly. A thoughtful interpretation treats discordant observations as scientific evidence to investigate rather than as inconveniences to remove automatically.

Radiation hybrid mapping creates information at a finer scale by exploiting differences in retention of chromosome fragments rather than merely whole chromosomes. In conceptual terms, if two loci are separated by a short physical interval, a random break is less likely to occur between them than between loci far apart, all else being equal. The resulting fragments are retained in different combinations across hybrid lines. When the two loci are frequently retained together, the observations favor proximity under the assumed breakage and retention model. When they are frequently separated, the evidence favors a larger interval, although the inference also depends on the retention characteristics of the panel.

Cox and colleagues described radiation hybrid mapping in 1990 as a method for constructing maps of mammalian chromosomes. Its conceptual significance is the use of a fragmentation process as an alternative source of positional information. Unlike a meiotic genetic map, which depends on the occurrence and recovery of crossovers, radiation hybrid mapping depends on breakage and retention. Unlike a direct sequence map, it does not simply count bases between coordinate labels. A centiray expresses distance within a radiation hybrid mapping framework and depends on the particular panel and its breakage characteristics. Equal centiray distances in panels made under different conditions need not correspond to the same physical lengths.

At the NET/SET level, it is sufficient to distinguish the source of evidence in each approach. Whole-chromosome somatic hybrid analysis assigns markers through retention concordance; radiation hybrid analysis uses fragment co-retention to infer finer relationships; linkage mapping uses inherited combinations from informative meioses; physical sequence maps use positions along DNA. Combining these independent approaches can strengthen localization. If one approach yields an unexpected result, its measurement assumptions and biological sample should be compared with the other evidence. No single map type proves every aspect of chromosomal organization or gene function.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. A molecular marker must encode a gene. A marker may be a noncoding variant with no known phenotypic effect. Its defining role is inherited detectability.
  2. A band-positive F2 individual is necessarily heterozygous. Dominant scoring combines both homozygous and heterozygous band-positive genotypes. The underlying 1:2:1 ratio becomes 3:1.
  3. Codominant marker scoring implies both alleles produce a physiological phenotype. It refers to detection of both alleles; it need not imply any physiological effect.
  4. A polymorphic marker is informative in every cross. The relevant parents must carry distinguishable alleles. Diversity in an unrelated population is insufficient.
  5. An RFLP probe detects every restriction fragment. A probe detects fragments containing a complementary sequence; most restriction fragments remain invisible.
  6. A larger restriction band proves an insertion. Loss of an internal restriction site can produce a longer detected fragment without changing total DNA length. Partial digestion can mimic it.
  7. RAPD products are all alleles of one locus. Different bands generally represent different amplified regions; assignment needs segregation or sequence evidence.
  8. AFLP is simply another name for RAPD. AFLP starts with restriction fragments and adaptor-associated selective amplification; RAPD uses arbitrary primer sites.
  9. An SSR product eight bases longer has eight additional repeats. For an unchanged dinucleotide-repeat locus, eight additional bases correspond to four extra repeats.
  10. Every minor SSR peak is an inherited allele. Stutter can result from amplification slippage. Diploid genotype calls must distinguish genuine alleles from technical products.
  11. All SNPs alter protein sequence. SNPs can occur in coding, regulatory or other regions; even a coding SNP may be synonymous.
  12. Missing genotype means homozygous reference. A missing call indicates inadequate evidence, not a confirmed genotype.
  13. One centimorgan always equals one megabase. Recombination varies with genomic region, organism, sex and other biological factors; no universal conversion exists.
  14. Zero observed recombinants proves zero distance. Finite sampling can fail to detect rare recombination. Additional informative meioses may reveal it.
  15. A bacterial cotransfer fraction can be converted directly to cM. Bacterial transfer mechanisms differ from diploid meiosis and require distinct mapping assumptions.
  16. Perfect somatic-hybrid concordance always identifies one chromosome. Two chromosomes with identical retention patterns are indistinguishable in that panel.
  17. A marker associated with a phenotype must be causal. Linkage or shared ancestry can generate association without a functional effect of the marker itself.

Revision and active recall

Molecular markers reveal inherited variation, but a marker's scoring properties depend on its assay and biological context. RFLP uses restriction-fragment length, RAPD arbitrary priming, AFLP selective amplification of restriction fragments, SSR repeat-length variation and SNP nucleotide identity. Genetic maps summarize recombination; physical maps locate DNA features; somatic hybrid mapping uses chromosome retention. None of these observations alone proves causation.

Active recall

  1. Explain the distinction between a marker allele, its detection method and a causal allele.
  2. Derive dominant and codominant F2 scoring ratios.
  3. Draw a restriction-site gain and predict the probe-positive bands.
  4. Explain the sequence requirements for a RAPD band.
  5. Contrast the sources of specificity in RAPD and AFLP.
  6. Calculate the repeat-number difference between two SSR alleles.
  7. Explain why a biallelic SNP can be informative in a cross.
  8. Distinguish physical distance from genetic distance.
  9. Predict how a finite sample can miss rare recombinants.
  10. Contrast eukaryotic linkage mapping and bacterial marker mapping.
  11. Construct a chromosome-retention concordance table.
  12. Explain why co-segregation is not sufficient evidence of causality.

Mastery checklist

  • Predict Mendelian marker scoring from genotypes.
  • Interpret restriction and repeat-length patterns.
  • Choose markers for a given cross and identify errors.
  • Calculate recombination fractions and retention concordance.
  • Distinguish linkage, physical location and causal evidence.

Original examination practice

These questions are original instructional exercises, not past examination questions. Numerical datasets are hypothetical.

Part B — concepts

1. Which feature makes a DNA polymorphism useful for linkage mapping?

  1. It always changes protein activity
  2. It is detectably inherited in the relevant family
  3. It is found only in exons
  4. It is always dominant
Answer and explanation

B. A marker must distinguish inherited chromosome forms in the family. Coding location, dominance and functional effect are not prerequisites.

2. Which observation distinguishes a codominant marker?

  1. Both alleles detectable in a heterozygote
  2. Only one genotype is viable
  3. Every genotype yields the same band
  4. The marker determines a dominant trait
Answer and explanation

A. Codominance in scoring means both allele forms can be observed. It need not determine a trait.

3. What is the expected observable ratio for a dominant band in a Mendelian F2?

  1. 1:1
  2. 1:2:1
  3. 3:1
  4. 9:7
Answer and explanation

C. DD and Dd are both band-positive, with combined probability 3/4. Only dd is negative.

4. In classical Southern RFLP analysis, what determines which fragments are visualized?

  1. Fragment size alone
  2. Hybridization to the probe
  3. Protein abundance
  4. Allele dominance
Answer and explanation

B. The probe reveals fragments carrying its complementary sequence, not all DNA fragments.

5. A restriction site is abolished between two unchanged flanking sites. What can happen?

  1. A longer restriction fragment appears
  2. All DNA becomes shorter
  3. The chromosome necessarily duplicates
  4. The marker must become lethal
Answer and explanation

A. The formerly adjacent fragments remain joined after digestion. Total length of the genomic region need not change.

6. Which is typical of RAPD?

  1. Known primers flanking a defined repeat only
  2. Arbitrary short primers sampling multiple loci
  3. Detection exclusively by Southern hybridization
  4. A requirement for a complete reference genome
Answer and explanation

B. RAPD surveys amplifiable regions using arbitrary primers; a finished reference genome is not required.

7. Which sequence configuration permits a conventional RAPD product?

  1. One isolated binding site
  2. Two compatible sites oriented toward one another
  3. Two sites oriented outward
  4. Only an RNA target
Answer and explanation

B. Opposing primer sites within an amplifiable interval define a product.

8. What differentiates AFLP from RAPD?

  1. AFLP uses restriction fragments and selective amplification
  2. AFLP cannot use PCR
  3. RAPD always requires a known locus sequence
  4. AFLP detects protein variants
Answer and explanation

A. AFLP defines fragments by restriction and adaptor-associated amplification; RAPD uses arbitrary priming.

9. What is a microsatellite?

  1. A tandem array of short DNA motifs
  2. A chromosome-specific protein
  3. A single restriction enzyme
  4. A whole mitochondrial genome
Answer and explanation

A. SSRs are tandem short sequence repeats, often polymorphic in copy number.

10. What is a common molecular origin of SSR length variation?

  1. Ribosomal translocation
  2. Replication-associated slippage
  3. Protein phosphorylation
  4. Independent assortment without mutation
Answer and explanation

B. Slippage during DNA replication can change repeat copy number. Assortment redistributes alleles rather than generating this change.

11. Which is a typical SNP genotype at a diploid A/G locus?

  1. AG
  2. AAGGG
  3. CA repeated twelve times
  4. An 8 kb Southern fragment only
Answer and explanation

A. AG is a heterozygous genotype at a biallelic nucleotide position.

12. Which marker class is commonly multiallelic at a single locus?

  1. SSR
  2. Typical biallelic SNP
  3. Dominant RAPD band
  4. Dominant AFLP band
Answer and explanation

A. Many repeat-length alleles can exist at one SSR locus.

13. What does a physical map measure?

  1. Distance in nucleotide units
  2. Fraction of heterozygotes
  3. Number of marker phenotypes
  4. Probability of dominance
Answer and explanation

A. Physical maps locate genomic sequence features in base pairs, unlike genetic maps in recombination-based units.

14. A centimorgan describes what type of distance?

  1. Recombination-based genetic distance
  2. Fixed number of base pairs
  3. Protein molecular mass
  4. Chromosome count
Answer and explanation

A. It is a genetic map unit; approximately 1% recombinant gametes only in a sufficiently short interval.

15. What is the usual scoring of anonymous RAPD bands?

  1. Dominant presence or absence
  2. Always fully codominant
  3. A four-allele genotype at every band
  4. A continuous enzyme assay
Answer and explanation

A. A single band generally cannot distinguish one versus two copies of its amplifiable allele.

16. Which feature commonly improves locus specificity in SSR scoring?

  1. Primers in unique flanking DNA
  2. No primers
  3. Random chromosome loss
  4. Ignoring product size
Answer and explanation

A. Defined flanking sequences target the repeat locus and permit allele-size comparisons.

17. Why does bacterial mapping require special assumptions?

  1. Bacteria generally lack diploid meiosis
  2. Bacterial DNA contains no markers
  3. All bacteria have identical sequences
  4. Bacteria cannot recombine
Answer and explanation

A. Marker relationships may be inferred from transfer and recombination or physical coordinates rather than conventional meiotic segregation.

18. Somatic cell hybrid mapping primarily uses which evidence?

  1. Chromosome-retention and marker concordance
  2. F2 dominance ratios
  3. SSR stutter intensity only
  4. Independent assortment in bacterial meiosis
Answer and explanation

A. Hybrid clones retain different chromosome subsets; marker concordance supports assignment.

19. What is a radiation hybrid map based on?

  1. Fragment breakage and co-retention
  2. Frequency of meiotic tetratypes
  3. Protein mass
  4. A universal cM-to-bp conversion
Answer and explanation

A. Nearby sequences tend to co-retain following fragment formation under the radiation hybrid model.

20. What does a missing SNP genotype call mean?

  1. Confirmed absence of every allele
  2. Insufficient evidence for a reliable genotype
  3. Confirmed reference homozygote
  4. A new deletion in every case
Answer and explanation

B. Missing calls are uncertainty, not established allelic state.

Part C — analytical reasoning

21. An F2 shows 26 AA, 48 AB and 26 BB at one SSR locus. Which inference is most appropriate?

  1. Grossly incompatible with Mendelian segregation
  2. Consistent with approximately 1:2:1 scoring
  3. Dominant presence/absence only
  4. Evidence of complete linkage to an unknown locus
Answer and explanation

B. The expected counts are 25, 50, 25. Chi-square = 0.04 + 0.08 + 0.04 = 0.16, so this sample gives little evidence against the expected single-locus ratio.

22. A dominant marker segregates 75 present and 25 absent in 100 F2 progeny. How many of the positive individuals are expected to be heterozygotes?

  1. 25
  2. 50
  3. 75
  4. 100
Answer and explanation

B. Among the expected 100 progeny, 25 DD, 50 Dd and 25 dd occur. The 75 positive individuals include 50 expected heterozygotes.

23. A Dd × dd testcross gives 52 positive and 48 negative progeny. What can the positive calls identify assuming error-free scoring?

  1. DD and Dd together
  2. Dd offspring
  3. dd offspring
  4. Neither parental allele
Answer and explanation

B. The dd parent always contributes d. Positive offspring must carry D from the other parent and therefore are Dd.

24. An 8 kb region is cut into 3 and 5 kb fragments in allele A, remains intact in B, and the unique probe lies in the 5 kb region. What appears in AB?

  1. 3 and 5 kb
  2. 5 and 8 kb
  3. 3 and 8 kb
  4. 8 kb only
Answer and explanation

B. The probe binds the 5 kb fragment from A and the intact 8 kb fragment from B. The 3 kb fragment lacks the probe target.

25. A known homozygous cut-site allele unexpectedly gives a faint intact fragment as well as its usual product. Which check should precede a heterozygote call?

  1. Confirm digestion completeness
  2. Assign a new gene immediately
  3. Assume linkage equilibrium
  4. Ignore controls
Answer and explanation

A. Incomplete digestion can preserve a fraction of intact DNA, mimicking an uncut allele. A controlled repeat is necessary before genetic interpretation.

26. An SSR assay has a constant 120 bp flanking contribution and a trinucleotide repeat. Alleles are 141 and 153 bp. What are their repeat counts?

  1. 7 and 11
  2. 21 and 33
  3. 7 and 15
  4. 47 and 51
Answer and explanation

A. Subtract 120 to obtain 21 and 33 bp of repeat DNA. Divide each by three, yielding seven and eleven copies.

27. Two SSR alleles are both 150 bp but sequencing finds different internal motifs. What does this establish?

  1. Equal size guarantees sequence identity
  2. The size assay may merge distinct sequence alleles
  3. Both alleles must be null
  4. SSR loci cannot be polymorphic
Answer and explanation

B. Equal electrophoretic lengths do not imply identical sequence; the assay distinguishes size classes rather than every sequence variant.

28. A four-allele SSR has frequencies 0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.125. Expected heterozygosity is?

  1. 0.34375
  2. 0.65625
  3. 0.75
  4. 0.875
Answer and explanation

B. The squared frequencies sum to 0.25 + 0.0625 + 0.015625 + 0.015625 = 0.34375. Subtraction from one yields 0.65625.

29. An SSR is highly variable globally, but both mapping parents are AA. A biallelic SNP is CC in one parent and TT in the other. Which is informative for parental ancestry?

  1. SSR only
  2. SNP only
  3. Both automatically
  4. Neither because SNPs are biallelic
Answer and explanation

B. The SNP distinguishes the two parental chromosomes; the identical SSR alleles cannot track parental origin.

30. A testcross records 170 AB, 170 ab, 30 Ab and 30 aB. What is the recombinant fraction assuming AB/ab phase?

  1. 0.075
  2. 0.15
  3. 0.425
  4. 0.85
Answer and explanation

B. Ab and aB are recombinant. The fraction is (30 + 30)/400 = 0.15; a simple 15 cM estimate is only approximate.

31. For 200 informative gametes and true recombinant fraction 0.005, what is the probability of observing none?

  1. About 0.367
  2. Exactly zero
  3. Exactly 0.005
  4. About 0.995
Answer and explanation

A. Under independent sampling, the probability is 0.995^200, approximately 0.367. No observed recombinants is compatible with low nonzero recombination.

32. Two intervals both span 1 Mb. Genetic lengths are 0.2 and 5 cM. Which inference follows?

  1. Physical map is necessarily erroneous
  2. Mean recombination rate differs 25-fold
  3. Both have equal genetic length
  4. Each has the same crossover probability per base
Answer and explanation

B. The average rates are 0.2 and 5 cM/Mb. Their ratio is 25; the observation does not specify individual base-level probabilities.

33. Three markers have identical segregation across every member of a cross. Which intervention directly increases opportunities for observing a separation?

  1. More informative meioses
  2. Renaming alleles
  3. Changing units to kb
  4. Discarding heterozygotes
Answer and explanation

A. More sampled meioses increase the chance of detecting recombinants. Marker renaming and unit changes add no biological information.

34. A RAPD band appears in one of three independent assays of the same DNA. What is the best initial interpretation?

  1. A stable inherited variant has been proven
  2. The band is technically unreliable pending validation
  3. A new chromosome arose
  4. The sample is always heterozygous
Answer and explanation

B. Repeated assays of the same template should support consistent scoring. Inconsistent bands cannot yet be used as stable genetic landmarks.

35. Two AFLP products from distantly related samples migrate to the same position. What remains uncertain?

  1. Whether they represent homologous genomic loci
  2. Whether they have finite length
  3. Whether they can migrate
  4. Whether DNA has nucleotides
Answer and explanation

A. Distinct fragments can co-migrate. Size agreement alone does not establish sequence homology or allelism.

36. A SNP panel has many missing calls exclusively in one parental genotype. What is the principal mapping concern?

  1. Biased recovery of genotype classes
  2. Increased crossover rate proved
  3. Universal physical map accuracy
  4. Loss of all polymorphism
Answer and explanation

A. Genotype-dependent missingness can distort segregation and estimated linkage. Missing calls should not be treated as a confirmed reference genotype.

37. In six hybrids, marker states are +,+,−,−,+,−. Chromosome X states are +,−,−,−,+,+. How many discordant hybrids occur?

  1. 0
  2. 1
  3. 2
  4. 4
Answer and explanation

C. Lines 2 and 6 disagree; the remaining four agree. Discordance is 2/6.

38. Two human chromosomes have identical retention profiles in a hybrid panel, both matching the marker perfectly. What is required to distinguish them?

  1. Additional informative hybrids or independent chromosomal evidence
  2. An arbitrary choice of the smaller chromosome
  3. Convert concordance to cM
  4. Ignore marker-negative clones
Answer and explanation

A. Identical profiles contain no distinguishing evidence. An independent observation must break the tie.

39. A chromosome-positive hybrid lacks the expected human gene product but contains its DNA sequence. What explanation must be considered?

  1. Expression failure in the hybrid context
  2. Gene absence is definitively established
  3. A meiotic crossover necessarily occurred
  4. The marker is always causal
Answer and explanation

A. The DNA assay indicates retention of the sequence; expression may be reduced or absent. Product detection and DNA detection measure different properties.

40. A marker predicts a trait in one pedigree but the opposite marker allele predicts it in another. Which explanation is plausible?

  1. Different parental linkage phases
  2. The law of segregation never applies
  3. The SNP must change function between pedigrees
  4. Physical distance is always zero
Answer and explanation

A. A neutral marker can be in coupling with different causal alleles in different families. Marker association does not establish functional causation.

Additional applied and assertion–reason questions

41. Assertion–reason: A highly polymorphic SSR may be uninformative in a particular cross. Reason: Both mapping parents may have the same allele.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Both false
Answer and explanation

A. Population variability does not guarantee parental contrast; identical alleles cannot distinguish ancestry.

42. Assertion–reason: Dominant marker scoring loses heterozygote information in an F2. Reason: DD and Dd can produce the same signal.

  1. Both true and reason explains assertion
  2. Both true but unrelated
  3. Assertion false
  4. Reason false
Answer and explanation

A. The observation merges two underlying genotype classes; this is the direct source of information loss.

43. Which pair is correctly matched?

  1. RFLP — fragment length after restriction
  2. SSR — antibody affinity
  3. SNP — chromosome number
  4. RAPD — obligatory Southern probe
Answer and explanation

A. RFLP detects restriction-fragment size differences. The other pairings confuse distinct methods or biological properties.

44. A marker and phenotype co-segregate in every offspring examined. Which next evidence most directly supports causality?

  1. Functional evidence linking the candidate variant to the phenotype
  2. Increasing the marker's font size
  3. Calling all offspring homozygous
  4. Converting physical distance into cM by a universal constant
Answer and explanation

A. Functional evidence can distinguish the causal variant from a nearby marker that merely travels with it.

45. A marker band segregates 1:1 in a haploid sample. Which conclusion is defensible?

  1. It may distinguish two haploid allele states
  2. It proves diploid codominance
  3. It proves all samples are heterozygous
  4. It proves a 3:1 F2 ratio
Answer and explanation

A. Haploids have one allele at a single-copy locus. A 1:1 segregation pattern does not demonstrate diploid scoring properties.

46. A plant researcher wants to recognize AA, AB and BB in an F2. Which is generally preferable if informative and reliable?

  1. Codominant SSR
  2. Anonymous dominant RAPD band
  3. An invariant SNP
  4. A missing call
Answer and explanation

A. A codominant SSR can separate the three genotypes. A dominant band merges AA and AB, and an invariant marker has no parental contrast.

47. A researcher compares 20 kb of DNA from two bacterial strains. What is the natural unit for the sequence distance between two identified sites?

  1. Base pairs
  2. Centimorgans inferred from diploid meiosis
  3. Centirays without hybrid data
  4. Phenotypic variance
Answer and explanation

A. Sequence coordinates define physical distance. The other units require different evidence or are unrelated.

48. A radiation hybrid map shows frequent co-retention of two loci. Which inference is conditional on the mapping model?

  1. The loci may be physically close
  2. The loci are always the same allele
  3. The loci are necessarily causally related
  4. The loci must have zero meiotic recombination
Answer and explanation

A. Co-retention suggests proximity given breakage and retention assumptions, but it is not proof of identity or causation.

49. A marker allele is detectable in a parent but absent from every offspring expected to inherit it. Which possibilities need investigation?

  1. Scoring error, non-Mendelian recovery or incorrect pedigree assumptions
  2. Only a universal change in genetic distance
  3. Only SNP causation
  4. No possible explanation
Answer and explanation

A. Unexpected segregation has multiple potential causes; assay validity, biological transmission and sample relationships must be checked.

50. Which approach best distinguishes a reproducible marker from a transient laboratory artifact?

  1. Independent scoring and consistent inheritance
  2. A single weak band
  3. Assuming all extra bands are genes
  4. Ignoring negative controls
Answer and explanation

A. Reproducibility and inheritance provide complementary evidence of a reliable genetic marker.
