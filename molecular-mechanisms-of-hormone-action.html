1. Hormone action: converting a circulating signal into cell behaviour

After a meal, an increase in circulating insulin can alter the transport of glucose into skeletal muscle and change the synthesis and breakdown of glycogen in liver. During fasting, glucagon promotes hepatic glucose production. These hormones circulate through many of the same blood vessels, but they do not produce identical effects in every tissue. The distinction arises because responsive cells express different receptors, signalling proteins, enzymes and regulated genes. A hormone is therefore not a universal command that directly changes every cell it contacts. It provides information that must be interpreted by a molecular system already present in the target cell.

A hormone molecule that binds a receptor is called a ligand. Binding is based on chemical interactions between the ligand and a complementary region of the receptor. This interaction may favour one conformational state of the receptor over another. A change in conformation can alter catalytic activity, expose a binding surface for a partner protein or change the way a receptor regulates transcription. Binding and activation are related but not identical: some substances occupy a receptor without producing its normal activating conformational change. An agonist produces receptor activation, whereas an antagonist blocks the effects of agonist binding without necessarily activating the receptor itself.

The first messenger is the extracellular hormone. When a receptor stimulates the generation or release of an intracellular small molecule, that molecule may serve as a second messenger. Cyclic AMP, cyclic GMP, inositol trisphosphate, diacylglycerol and calcium ions illustrate different chemical strategies for transmitting information. They are not all freely diffusible in the same way. Diacylglycerol remains associated with lipid membranes, whereas soluble inositol trisphosphate can reach intracellular calcium stores. Calcium ions are already present in cells and become a signal when their local concentration changes.

A kinase catalyses the transfer of a phosphate group, commonly from ATP, to a suitable substrate. Protein kinases modify specific residues in protein substrates, whereas lipid kinases modify lipids. A phosphatase removes phosphate groups. The two types of enzyme allow the phosphorylation state of a protein or lipid to change reversibly. Phosphorylation does not automatically activate a protein. In some cases it stimulates activity, in others it suppresses activity or alters interaction with another molecule. The physiological consequence depends on the identity and function of the substrate.

The membrane separates extracellular fluid from cytoplasm. Hydrophilic peptide hormones generally signal through receptors at the cell surface because the hydrophobic interior of the lipid bilayer strongly restricts their direct entry. Steroid hormones commonly reach intracellular receptors after crossing the membrane. Thyroid hormones are a useful exception to simplistic rules about lipid-soluble signals: their cellular access depends on transporters, and their activity can be modified by intracellular conversion of T4 to T3. Neither hormone chemistry nor receptor location alone determines the complete response. Membrane receptors can ultimately change gene transcription, and some steroid-related signals occur rapidly.

Endocrine signalling is distinct from the organism-level feedback that regulates hormone secretion. An intracellular phosphatase can terminate a signal within one target cell without directly altering secretion by the gland. Conversely, a feedback decrease in hormone secretion can reduce stimulation of many cells even if their signalling machinery remains unchanged. Both levels contribute to homeostasis, but they are experimentally separable. In this chapter, emphasis is on the target-cell mechanism; the organisation of the endocrine axes follows in the later chapters.

Hormone concentration and the cellular response

The concentration of hormone measured in a blood sample is not always the concentration that is available to bind receptors. Some hormones circulate largely bound to carrier proteins. A free hormone molecule can dissociate from the surrounding solution and interact with an accessible receptor; a hormone held in a binding protein complex generally does not participate in the same simple binding equilibrium until it dissociates. Carrier proteins can buffer changes in free concentration and influence distribution and clearance. Changes in the total quantity of circulating hormone can consequently occur with relatively small changes in receptor stimulation. This distinction becomes particularly important in the physiology of thyroid and steroid hormones, whereas many peptide hormones circulate without comparably extensive specific binding.

The temporal pattern of delivery also matters. Suppose a hormone is released in short pulses, with concentrations returning towards baseline between pulses. If receptor activation and recovery occur within each interval, the target may experience repeated episodes of signalling. Another target with slow messenger degradation may integrate the pulses into an approximately sustained signal. If receptor desensitisation is strong during continued exposure, an equally large average concentration supplied continuously may produce less signalling than a pulsatile stimulus. An examination problem can therefore ask whether two secretion patterns are functionally equivalent even when their total hormone exposure over an hour is identical. The correct answer depends on the kinetics of the receiving system, not merely the hormone quantity.

Endocrine signals commonly alter the activity of existing proteins before they alter protein abundance. A cell that possesses a transporter in an intracellular vesicle may deliver the transporter to the membrane within minutes. A cell that must synthesise an entirely new transporter has an additional requirement for transcription, translation and processing. A change in surface transporter number can thus occur while the total transporter content remains unchanged. By measuring both surface and total abundance before and after hormonal stimulation, an investigator can distinguish redistribution of existing protein from a substantial change in synthesis or degradation. The two processes may also operate on different timescales in the same cell.

A target cell's response should be specified in operational terms. An increase in kinase phosphorylation, a rise in messenger concentration and a change in organ function are three different measurements. A response at the first level does not establish that the last has occurred. For example, a mutation affecting the machinery that inserts aquaporin-2 into the membrane may prevent a renal water permeability response even if the relevant GPCR generates normal cAMP. Conversely, altering water transport by another mechanism need not activate that receptor at all. A mechanistic explanation connects these observations by demonstrating dependencies between them. This is the central approach to interpreting the experiments and perturbation questions in the following sections.

2. Gs and Gi: controlling cyclic AMP production

A G protein-coupled receptor, or GPCR, contains seven transmembrane helices and interacts with heterotrimeric G proteins at the cytoplasmic surface. The G protein has an alpha subunit associated with beta and gamma subunits. In the resting state, GDP is bound to the alpha subunit. An activated receptor promotes release of GDP, permitting GTP to bind. The GTP-bound alpha subunit and the beta-gamma complex can change their interactions with downstream targets. These targets need not all be enzymes; some are ion channels. The alpha subunit eventually hydrolyses GTP, returning to a state that favours reassociation with beta-gamma.

Gs and Gi are functionally distinguished by their effects on susceptible forms of adenylyl cyclase. Active G alpha s stimulates the cyclase, which produces cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) from ATP. Active G alpha i inhibits several forms of the enzyme. A cell expressing both receptor types can therefore adjust its cAMP concentration in response to competing inputs. The magnitude of the change also depends on the cyclase isoforms present and on the activity of phosphodiesterases, which hydrolyse cyclic nucleotides.

Glucagon acting on hepatocytes provides a physiological example of the Gs pathway. During fasting, glucagon receptor activation increases cAMP, promoting activation of protein kinase A. A typical inactive PKA complex contains regulatory subunits associated with catalytic subunits. Binding of cAMP to the regulatory components releases or exposes catalytic activity. PKA can phosphorylate phosphorylase kinase, which in turn activates glycogen phosphorylase. The result is mobilisation of glycogen in support of hepatic glucose output. PKA also phosphorylates other metabolic regulators, helping to coordinate glycogen breakdown with reduced glycogen synthesis. The hormone is not consumed in proportion to glycogen molecules mobilised.

In the heart, beta adrenergic receptor stimulation increases cAMP and PKA activity. PKA-dependent changes in calcium handling contribute to an increase in the force of contraction. In contrast, in pancreatic beta cells, alpha2 adrenergic receptor activation can inhibit secretion in part through Gi-linked processes. It is misleading to describe epinephrine as inherently a stimulatory or inhibitory hormone: different receptor subtypes convert its presence into different signals. Even within one receptor category, differences in downstream proteins can produce different functional outcomes.

The renal response to vasopressin also illustrates tissue specificity. A V2 receptor on collecting duct principal cells activates Gs-dependent signalling, ultimately increasing aquaporin-2 delivery to the apical membrane. Increased water permeability permits water movement when an appropriate osmotic gradient exists. The signal controls the availability of a water pathway rather than supplying energy to pump water across the epithelium. V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle instead commonly engage Gq-linked signalling and promote contraction.

cAMP concentration reflects the balance between production and degradation. A receptor can cease activating a G protein while cAMP remains above its resting value for a short time. A phosphodiesterase inhibitor can extend that signal without keeping the receptor occupied. This distinction predicts why an experiment measuring cAMP at only one time point cannot automatically identify whether a drug stimulated synthesis or slowed removal. Both mechanisms can raise the measured concentration.

The classical work of Earl Sutherland and colleagues established the importance of a diffusible intracellular mediator of hormonal stimulation of glycogen breakdown. An important conceptual distinction was between hormone recognition at cellular membranes and stimulation of intracellular enzyme activity by a substance generated in response to that recognition. The principle can be tested in a simplified fractionation experiment: a membrane preparation containing the receptor and cyclase is exposed to hormone and ATP, then a suitably prepared soluble fraction containing responsive enzymes is supplied with the reaction products. Activation in the second preparation supports transfer of a chemical signal. Controls without hormone, without ATP or with inactive membranes help distinguish specific messenger production from nonspecific activation. Such a teaching reconstruction illustrates the logic rather than reporting a particular historical dataset.

The biochemical life of a cyclic AMP signal

Formation of cAMP involves a reaction at the nucleotide rather than at a protein substrate. Adenylyl cyclase rearranges ATP to form an intramolecular cyclic phosphate linkage, releasing pyrophosphate. This explains the distinction between ATP as a substrate for messenger synthesis and ATP as a phosphate donor in a subsequent protein kinase reaction. Hydrolysis of cAMP produces AMP. AMP is not simply an inactive copy of cAMP: the absence of the cyclic linkage changes its ability to interact with cAMP-responsive proteins. The production and destruction reactions therefore alter the number of molecules capable of carrying the relevant signal.

Although diagrams often depict cAMP as a homogeneous cytoplasmic pool, a cell contains spatially organised signalling machinery. Adenylyl cyclases produce cAMP near particular membranes, and phosphodiesterases can restrict its accumulation in some regions. A-kinase anchoring proteins associate with PKA and help position it near particular substrates or regulatory complexes. Consequently, two stimuli that generate similar average cAMP concentrations across an entire cell need not produce identical phosphorylation of every target. The useful examination-level principle is that location, as well as quantity, determines a messenger's effect. A bulk biochemical assay is informative but may hide important local changes.

PKA is not the only cAMP-responsive protein. Exchange proteins directly activated by cAMP, or EPAC proteins, can alter the state of certain small GTPases. Some ion channels also respond to cyclic nucleotides. These alternatives clarify why inhibiting PKA may fail to eliminate every consequence of elevating cAMP. If an experiment shows that a cell retains one cAMP-dependent response after selective PKA inhibition, this does not prove that cAMP is irrelevant; another cAMP-responsive mechanism could remain functional. A student should trace which particular output the question measures before attributing it to a single effector.

The cAMP pathway also influences gene expression. In an appropriate context, PKA can phosphorylate the transcription factor CREB at a regulatory site, which facilitates interactions with coactivator machinery. CREB binds particular regulatory sequences, called cAMP response elements. The transcriptional outcome depends on additional signals and the state of the target promoter or enhancer. This provides a molecular example of how a membrane receptor can change nuclear activity without the hormone itself entering the nucleus. Immediate control of enzymes and longer-term adjustment of enzyme expression can support the same physiological objective at different times.

Consider the relationship between glycogen mobilisation and the cAMP response in liver. Glycogen is a branched polymer of glucose residues that can be mobilised to support glucose availability. Activation of phosphorylase kinase and glycogen phosphorylase changes the rate of degradation, while hormonal control of glycogen synthase can reduce concurrent storage. This coordinated regulation helps prevent futile cycling between synthesis and breakdown. A kinase cascade can act quickly on existing enzymes, making it useful during a transition between feeding and fasting. Long-term changes in enzyme expression help maintain a metabolic state when the nutritional condition persists. The detailed pathways of carbohydrate metabolism belong to the biomolecule and metabolic chapters, but the hormonal control of pathway flux illustrates why amplification and reversible phosphorylation are physiologically valuable.

Distinguishing receptor blockade from downstream inhibition

Imagine an isolated tissue preparation containing a Gs-coupled hormone receptor, cyclase and the cellular machinery needed to produce a measured response. The normal hormone increases both cAMP and the response. A competitive antagonist of the receptor is expected to reduce these effects at a fixed agonist concentration if occupancy by agonist falls. If cyclase can be activated independently of the receptor, direct activation should still increase cAMP and may restore the response. This is a useful experimental bypass: the investigator stimulates the pathway at a point downstream of the suspected defect.

Forskolin is widely used as an activator of several mammalian transmembrane adenylyl cyclase isoforms. Suppose receptor stimulation fails to elevate cAMP in a mutant cell, while forskolin produces a substantial increase. The results support the presence of cyclase activity and suggest a defect at the receptor, G protein or their coupling, although isoform-specific pharmacology and other variables must be considered. A failure to respond to forskolin would be less informative by itself because different cyclase isoforms and experimental conditions can affect its action. Including a separate control for the ability of the cell to respond to elevated cAMP strengthens the investigation.

A suitable membrane-permeable cAMP analogue may activate cAMP-responsive proteins without requiring normal synthesis of endogenous cAMP. If a downstream response is restored by such an analogue despite defective hormone-dependent messenger production, some of the machinery beyond the cyclase remains functional. If cAMP rises normally but the analogue fails to restore the response, the suspected defect moves further downstream. The strategy is analogous to genetic epistasis: introduce an intervention at a known point and ask whether bypassing an earlier step restores the final phenotype. Neither genetic nor pharmacological rescue is absolute proof of a single lesion, because supraphysiological stimulation can sometimes compensate for incomplete defects.

A critical control is the basal response in the absence of any intervention. A mutation that raises basal cAMP could make the additional hormone-induced increment appear small even though the receptor retains activity. Reporting both baseline and stimulated values can distinguish a low absolute signal from an apparently small change imposed on a high baseline. In the same way, a reporter that saturates at modest messenger concentrations may obscure differences between strong stimulation conditions. Measurements should be interpreted within their useful dynamic range. This principle applies equally to calcium fluorescence, enzyme activity assays and measures of receptor phosphorylation.

3. Gq, phospholipase C and calcium-dependent responses

In many endocrine target cells, receptor activation controls the breakdown of a membrane lipid rather than the conversion of ATP into a cyclic nucleotide. The Gq family of G proteins activates phospholipase C beta. One important substrate is phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate, commonly abbreviated PIP2. Hydrolysis produces two signalling molecules: inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). Because the products have different chemical properties, they convey the information through different parts of the cell.

IP3 diffuses through the cytoplasm and binds IP3 receptors on the membrane of the endoplasmic reticulum. These receptors function as calcium release channels. Calcium is stored in the endoplasmic reticulum at a much higher free concentration than in the resting cytosol. Channel opening therefore allows calcium to move into the cytoplasm along its electrochemical gradient. This rapid movement changes the availability of calcium for regulatory proteins. It is not necessary for the extracellular hormone to carry calcium into the cell, nor is the release of calcium an active pumping process.

DAG remains within the membrane. Conventional protein kinase C isoforms require DAG and calcium, together with appropriate membrane lipids, for effective activation. Other PKC isoforms differ in their requirements, so it is incorrect to say that every form of PKC responds identically to calcium. The two messenger branches provide a way for simultaneous changes in membrane composition and cytoplasmic ion concentration to influence a common target.

Calmodulin is a calcium-binding protein that changes its interaction with targets when calcium binds. In smooth muscle, calcium-calmodulin activates myosin light-chain kinase, which phosphorylates regulatory light chains and permits increased interaction between myosin and actin. A vasoconstrictor such as angiotensin II can therefore activate a membrane receptor, release calcium from stores and alter contractile force. Calcium entry through plasma membrane channels can contribute to sustained contraction. Calcium sensitivity can also change independently of the amount of calcium released, so contractile force is not a perfect surrogate for cytosolic calcium concentration.

Several mechanisms return intracellular calcium towards resting levels. SERCA pumps use ATP to move calcium into the endoplasmic or sarcoplasmic reticulum. Plasma membrane calcium ATPases and sodium-calcium exchange contribute to removal of calcium from the cytoplasm. IP3 metabolism and receptor closure reduce further release. These processes explain why a single brief stimulus often produces a transient calcium elevation rather than permanently high cytosolic calcium. Sustained hormonal stimulation may generate repeated calcium oscillations when release and recovery interact.

An experiment can distinguish calcium entry from release of intracellular stores by changing extracellular calcium and measuring the immediate cytosolic response. Persistence of a short initial transient in calcium-free external solution supports a contribution from intracellular stores, provided the stores were initially filled and the experimental conditions are otherwise comparable. Loss of the prolonged phase may indicate a dependence on extracellular calcium entry. A more direct demonstration combines cytosolic measurements with store measurements or selective manipulation of the release channel. It is important to avoid inferring an exact channel identity from one pharmacological treatment alone.

The Gq pathway is used by multiple hormones, including GnRH in pituitary gonadotrophs and oxytocin in uterine smooth muscle. These cells respond differently because a gonadotroph is specialised for hormone production and release, while a smooth muscle cell contains contractile machinery. The shared messenger system does not impose a shared final cellular behaviour. In later endocrine chapters, this distinction allows the same molecular mechanism to explain different organ-level responses without memorising an entirely new signalling pathway for each gland.

Calcium signals as changes in space and time

A calcium ion is chemically the same whether it participates in signalling, mineral formation or contraction. Its role as a second messenger arises from regulation of its free concentration and from the ability of proteins to respond to that concentration. The total cellular calcium content includes ions bound to proteins or stored within organelles. A measurement of total calcium is therefore not equivalent to a measurement of free cytosolic calcium. The resting cytoplasm is maintained at a much lower free calcium concentration than the extracellular fluid or the lumen of intracellular stores. This arrangement permits a relatively small movement of ions to produce a substantial fold change in free cytosolic concentration.

Calcium-binding proteins have different affinities and kinetics. A relatively high-affinity calcium sensor may respond to small increases, while a lower-affinity target may require a larger local transient. The geometry of a cell is also relevant. Calcium released close to an effector can act before the ion diffuses, binds to a buffer or is returned to a store. Thus, the timing and location of calcium elevation influence which molecular processes respond. A fluorescence image that reveals regional calcium increases may be more informative than an average value obtained from the entire cell.

Several mechanisms contribute to the temporal character of calcium signalling. IP3 receptors are regulated by ligands and by calcium itself, so release from a store can change nonlinearly as conditions change. Reuptake can refill stores during intervals between transients. When the hormone remains present, interaction between production of IP3, store depletion, channel regulation and refilling can generate repeated elevations instead of a single plateau. Different patterns may influence downstream calcium-sensitive enzymes in different ways. There is no need to memorise a single universal oscillation frequency; the important principle is that the pattern is generated by the properties of the particular cell.

A ratiometric calcium indicator can help estimate relative changes in free cytosolic calcium. In a suitable experiment, fluorescence is recorded before, during and after hormone application. Comparing traces requires attention to dye loading, calibration, cell viability and baseline conditions. A higher fluorescence signal does not automatically prove that calcium release from an organelle increased; entry, buffering and removal can also change the observed concentration. An inhibitor of the SERCA pump, for instance, may alter the initial cytoplasmic baseline and prevent refilling of stores. Interpreting its effect requires knowing the order and duration of treatment.

In endocrine physiology, calcium can participate in hormone secretion as well as hormone action. A pituitary cell receiving a releasing hormone may elevate cytosolic calcium and alter exocytosis of its own peptide hormone. A smooth muscle cell responding to a circulating vasoconstrictor may elevate calcium and activate contraction. Although the second messenger is the same, the terminal processes differ. An integrated question may therefore present a calcium trace from an endocrine secretory cell and ask about secretion, while a similar trace from muscle is used to reason about force. A mechanistic answer connects the trace to the effector available in that cell rather than assigning a fixed response to calcium.

4. Insulin receptor: intrinsic tyrosine kinase and metabolic control

The insulin receptor differs fundamentally from a GPCR. The mature receptor is a disulfide-linked complex containing two extracellular alpha subunits and two beta subunits that span the membrane and carry intracellular tyrosine kinase domains. It is already assembled before insulin binds. Binding produces conformational rearrangements that favour activation of the kinase domains. Thus, describing insulin receptor activation merely as association of two previously independent monomers is inaccurate for this receptor.

When activated, the kinase domains phosphorylate tyrosine residues in the receptor and on intracellular substrates. Insulin receptor substrate proteins, particularly IRS proteins, provide docking sites for downstream molecules when appropriately phosphorylated. The phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway is a central example. PI3K converts membrane PIP2 to phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate, abbreviated PIP3. This modification is a phosphorylation of a membrane lipid, not cleavage of it. PIP3 and PIP2 therefore distinguish two different operations on related substrates: phosphoinositide phosphorylation in insulin signalling and phospholipase-mediated cleavage in Gq signalling.

PIP3 recruits proteins with appropriate lipid-binding domains to the membrane, including Akt and PDK1. PDK1 and mTORC2 contribute to full activation of Akt by phosphorylation at distinct regulatory sites. Akt, also called protein kinase B, phosphorylates targets that control the localisation and activity of other cellular proteins. In skeletal muscle and adipose cells, one major consequence is increased surface availability of GLUT4. These cells store much of their GLUT4 in intracellular vesicles under basal conditions. Akt-dependent phosphorylation of the Rab-regulating protein TBC1D4, also called AS160, helps relieve constraints on vesicle delivery and fusion with the plasma membrane.

GLUT4 transports glucose by facilitated diffusion. Increasing surface GLUT4 can increase glucose influx when the concentration gradient favours movement into the cell. Insulin does not make GLUT4 into an ATP-driven glucose pump. Intracellular glucose phosphorylation can help maintain the gradient by reducing free glucose concentration inside the cell. This reasoning is also important when distinguishing membrane transport from subsequent metabolism.

Hepatocytes do not use insulin-stimulated GLUT4 recruitment as their primary mechanism for regulating glucose entry. Their glucose transport depends importantly on GLUT2, and insulin changes hepatic metabolism through effects on glycogen synthesis and glucose-producing pathways. Akt-mediated inhibition of glycogen synthase kinase 3 can favour glycogen synthase activity under suitable conditions. Akt also phosphorylates the transcription factor FOXO1 and promotes its exclusion from the nucleus, reducing expression of some glucose-producing enzymes. This example connects short-term phosphorylation events with slower transcriptional regulation within a membrane receptor pathway.

Insulin receptors can also engage the Ras–MAP kinase pathway through adaptor proteins, contributing to growth-related and transcriptional responses. The two branches are not wholly isolated; they use overlapping regulatory machinery and can influence each other. It is useful to distinguish their major physiological roles without treating the diagram as an exhaustive account of all signal interactions.

Several mechanisms oppose the insulin signal. Protein tyrosine phosphatases can remove phosphate groups from receptor-associated targets. PTEN converts PIP3 back towards PIP2 by removing the phosphate at the 3 position. Receptor internalisation, trafficking and changes in IRS function also affect the signal. A decrease in glucose uptake may therefore arise from a receptor defect, impaired signalling downstream of the receptor, a failure of GLUT4 trafficking or limitations in glucose metabolism. These alternatives require different tests.

Imagine that muscle cells display normal receptor phosphorylation after insulin exposure but markedly reduced Akt phosphorylation and surface GLUT4. Adding insulin at a higher concentration fails to restore uptake. These observations suggest that impaired receptor binding is not the only possible explanation; a defect between IRS and Akt or increased opposing phosphatase activity could account for the results. Measurement of IRS phosphorylation and PIP3, alongside total Akt abundance, would help identify the stage at which the response becomes abnormal. An experiment in which active Akt restores GLUT4 delivery provides stronger functional evidence that at least some downstream trafficking machinery remains usable.

The molecular concept of insulin resistance should be distinguished from insulin deficiency. In deficiency the stimulus is insufficient, even if the target cells are capable of responding. In resistance, a given hormone concentration produces a smaller response than expected, though the defect may vary by tissue or signalling branch. The distinction is revisited in the endocrine pancreas chapter. A single measure of circulating insulin cannot by itself establish which molecular process is defective.

Receptor phosphorylation as a regulated docking system

The insulin receptor's catalytic domain transfers a phosphate group to tyrosine residues of suitable substrates. Autophosphorylation refers to phosphorylation of the receptor by its own kinase activity, including phosphorylation between the kinase domains of the assembled receptor complex. Some regulatory tyrosines contribute to changes in kinase activity, whereas others create recognition sites for interacting proteins. The exact location of a phosphorylation event therefore matters. A receptor described as phosphorylated in an experiment may have been examined at only one site; this does not establish that every necessary regulatory interaction has occurred.

IRS proteins act as scaffolds and substrates rather than as transmembrane hormone receptors. Multiple phosphorylated tyrosines on an IRS protein can recruit proteins with compatible recognition domains. The regulatory subunit of class IA PI3K recognises appropriate phosphotyrosine-containing sequences, helping bring the catalytic machinery to a location where its membrane lipid substrate is available. This spatial recruitment is central to the pathway. The existence of a soluble kinase in a cell does not guarantee efficient phosphorylation of a membrane lipid if the enzyme cannot reach the substrate.

PIP3 changes the composition of a limited region of the inner membrane leaflet. Proteins carrying appropriate pleckstrin homology domains may bind to this region. Akt recruitment allows its regulatory phosphorylation by other kinases, and activation changes the probability that it phosphorylates its substrates. Because PTEN removes the regulatory phosphate from PIP3, the duration and spatial extent of the lipid signal depend on both its synthesis and removal. This is directly analogous to the earlier distinction between cyclase and phosphodiesterase control of cyclic AMP, though the chemical substrate and subcellular localisation are different.

One consequence of Akt signalling is control of the Rab family of small GTPases associated with vesicle trafficking. TBC1D4 is a regulatory protein that has GTPase-activating functions in relevant pathways. Its phosphorylation can reduce constraints on the trafficking machinery that normally limits GLUT4 delivery at rest. The process also involves physical movement of vesicles, tethering, docking and membrane fusion. A block in any of these processes can lower the number of transporters exposed to extracellular glucose. It would be incorrect to place GLUT4 delivery entirely within the catalytic activity of Akt; the kinase regulates a pre-existing membrane trafficking system.

The rates of delivery and retrieval determine the surface transporter population. If the rate of vesicle fusion increases while endocytosis continues at the same rate, surface GLUT4 rises until the rates again balance or an available pool becomes limiting. If removal is inhibited, surface abundance can also rise without increased delivery. The two mechanisms are experimentally distinguishable by labelling transporters at the surface and observing their appearance or disappearance over time. Such experiments illustrate that a single measurement of steady surface abundance cannot identify the individual trafficking rate that changed.

Muscle contraction provides an instructive contrast. Contracting skeletal muscle can increase glucose uptake through pathways that are not identical to insulin receptor signalling, including responses associated with changes in cellular energy state. Some of those pathways converge on glucose transporter trafficking. A muscle cell with impaired insulin responsiveness may therefore retain a capacity to increase glucose uptake through an alternative stimulus. The comparison illustrates how two upstream inputs can converge on a shared effector, and how a downstream defect in transporter availability can impair both inputs. Detailed exercise physiology is outside the present scope, but the logical distinction is important for integrated metabolism questions.

5. Growth hormone and prolactin: receptor-associated JAK–STAT

Growth hormone and prolactin are peptide hormones whose receptors belong to the class I cytokine receptor family. Their receptors lack the intrinsic kinase catalytic domain characteristic of the insulin receptor. Instead, the cytoplasmic regions associate with Janus kinases, particularly JAK2. Hormone binding favours an active arrangement of receptor subunits and associated kinases. This can occur through conformational reorganisation of pre-associated receptors and should not always be described as formation of an entirely new dimer.

Activated JAK molecules phosphorylate each other and tyrosine residues on the receptor. The phosphorylated receptor provides docking sites for STAT proteins. STAT stands for signal transducer and activator of transcription. STAT proteins contain domains that allow recognition of suitable phosphotyrosine-containing regions. JAK-dependent phosphorylation of STAT promotes dimer formation through reciprocal recognition of phosphorylated sites. The activated STAT complex accumulates in the nucleus and regulates transcription at appropriate regulatory DNA sequences with other factors.

STAT5 is an important mediator of both growth hormone and prolactin responses. In hepatocytes, growth hormone influences expression of insulin-like growth factor 1, providing an endocrine and local growth-related output of the signal. In mammary epithelial cells, prolactin-dependent STAT5 activity participates in regulation of milk-protein genes in a suitable differentiated and hormonal context. It would be inaccurate to conclude that STAT5 phosphorylation alone guarantees milk production in every cell. The availability of transcriptional cofactors, developmental state, chromatin accessibility and other endocrine inputs determines which genes can respond.

The direct connection from the activated receptor-associated kinase to a transcriptional regulator explains why JAK–STAT is often presented as a relatively short signalling route. Nevertheless, short does not mean instantaneous. Receptor and STAT phosphorylation may appear quickly, while accumulation of messenger RNA, synthesis of protein and changes in physiological function require additional time. Distinguish the onset of molecular signalling from the onset of the measured biological response.

Suppressors of cytokine signalling, or SOCS proteins, contribute to negative regulation. Some are induced by the very signals they inhibit, creating intracellular negative feedback. Protein phosphatases and receptor turnover also limit activity. A cell exposed to repeated or sustained growth hormone can respond differently from one exposed to a single pulse even at a similar average hormone concentration. Such temporal differences provide a bridge to the following chapter on hypothalamic and pituitary control.

A useful experimental comparison involves cells expressing a normal growth hormone receptor and cells expressing a receptor that binds hormone but cannot associate effectively with JAK2. Equal hormone binding in the two groups would not imply equal function. Reduced STAT phosphorylation and loss of the transcriptional response in the mutant group would support the requirement for the kinase association. Reintroducing the functional receptor and restoring the response would strengthen the causal interpretation. Controls for receptor abundance and cell viability are necessary because loss of signalling may otherwise reflect general cellular damage rather than a specific molecular defect.

Nuclear accumulation and specificity of STAT signalling

STAT proteins provide an example of a regulatory protein whose state can be changed at the plasma membrane and whose function is expressed in the nucleus. Their phosphorylation exposes or creates favourable interaction surfaces for association with other activated STAT molecules. Dimer formation allows particular STAT complexes to recognise regulatory DNA sequences and recruit transcriptional machinery. Nuclear accumulation reflects a balance of import, export, phosphorylation and dephosphorylation rather than irreversible transport. The response can diminish as STAT proteins are dephosphorylated and receptor activity falls.

Several members of the STAT family exist, and particular receptors may activate more than one. The prominence of STAT5 in GH and prolactin action is a useful physiological association, not an assertion that every JAK–STAT receptor uses only STAT5. Even within the GH pathway, other signalling branches contribute to the response. A question that specifies a mutation in STAT5 therefore requires careful attention to the output under consideration. Loss of a STAT5-dependent transcriptional response does not necessarily eliminate every kinase-dependent effect of the receptor.

The relationship between GH signalling and IGF-1 provides a particularly useful distinction between a direct and an indirect hormonal response. GH can act directly through its receptor on several tissues, and it can stimulate production of IGF-1, which in turn binds a different receptor with intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity. A biological effect that appears after GH administration may be mediated directly by GH, indirectly by IGF-1 or by a combination of the two. To distinguish the possibilities, an investigator can compare the timing of GH receptor activation, IGF-1 production and IGF receptor activation, then selectively disrupt the intermediate signal. The presence of an endocrine cascade makes the final response dependent on multiple cell types.

Negative regulation of JAK–STAT can occur through SOCS-mediated interactions with the receptor or kinase and through dephosphorylation of signalling proteins. When an inhibitory protein is induced by a hormone-responsive transcriptional pathway, the inhibitory response may lag behind initial activation because synthesis requires time. This delayed negative feedback can produce an early strong signal followed by attenuation. Such attenuation is not automatically evidence that circulating hormone disappeared. Measuring extracellular hormone along with phosphorylation and inhibitory protein abundance helps separate hormone clearance from adaptation within the target cell.

6. Natriuretic peptides and cyclic GMP signalling

Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP) is released from the heart in response to conditions associated with atrial stretch. It contributes to regulation of blood volume and pressure through renal and vascular effects. Its principal signalling receptor, NPR-A, also called guanylyl cyclase A, contains a membrane-spanning segment and an intracellular catalytic region that converts GTP into cyclic GMP. The receptor therefore combines ligand recognition with direct production of a second messenger.

cGMP activates suitable cGMP-dependent protein kinases, commonly called PKG, and also influences some phosphodiesterases and cyclic nucleotide-regulated channels. In vascular smooth muscle, PKG-dependent signalling can favour relaxation by reducing calcium availability and modifying the contractile response to calcium. Renal actions contribute to natriuresis through several physiological mechanisms; this chapter emphasises the receptor mechanism rather than repeating nephron transport details.

The ANP receptor should be distinguished from soluble guanylyl cyclase. Soluble guanylyl cyclase is activated by nitric oxide, which can diffuse into nearby target cells and bind the enzyme's haem-containing regulatory machinery. Both the ANP receptor and the nitric oxide-sensitive enzyme produce cGMP, but the initial recognition events differ. A drug that blocks ANP binding to NPR-A need not prevent nitric oxide from increasing cGMP in a cell with intact soluble cyclase. Conversely, a defect shared by both pathways at the level of cGMP-responsive targets could reduce both responses.

The existence of cGMP as an intracellular messenger does not make ANP a G protein-coupled receptor ligand in its principal NPR-A pathway. GTP is the catalytic substrate for guanylyl cyclase, whereas GTP bound to the alpha subunit of a G protein serves as a regulatory nucleotide. The two uses of GTP must be kept separate. NPR-C is a distinct natriuretic peptide receptor with important clearance functions and can participate in other signalling mechanisms; it should not be substituted for NPR-A in the canonical ANP–cGMP pathway.

An experiment comparing ANP and nitric oxide can localise a failure in signalling. If ANP fails to raise cGMP but nitric oxide increases it normally, the findings are compatible with impairment of the ANP receptor system. They are less compatible with complete failure of all cellular cGMP production. If both stimuli raise cGMP but neither relaxes a muscle preparation, the defect may lie downstream at PKG or another part of the contractile regulation. Measuring a messenger and an organ-level response together is more informative than measuring either alone.

Production and action of cyclic GMP in endocrine coordination

Like cAMP, cyclic GMP contains a cyclic phosphate linkage, but it is derived from guanosine triphosphate rather than adenosine triphosphate. The two cyclic nucleotides are chemically related yet interact with proteins that possess different selectivities. A kinase responsive to one cyclic nucleotide should not be presumed to respond equally to the other at physiological concentrations. Some phosphodiesterases degrade one nucleotide preferentially, while others can act on both or be regulated by them. Consequently, communication between cAMP and cGMP pathways may occur through changes in messenger degradation as well as through downstream targets.

Consider two neighbouring vascular smooth muscle cells exposed to signals associated with changes in cardiovascular conditions. Nitric oxide released by endothelial cells can stimulate soluble guanylyl cyclase, while a circulating natriuretic peptide can stimulate membrane guanylyl cyclase. The resulting cGMP signals can overlap in function but need not occupy identical spatial compartments or exhibit identical kinetics. The origin of the messenger is therefore not irrelevant even when the molecular product is the same. A selective failure of one receptor pathway may leave the other available to regulate vascular tone.

The effect of PKG on smooth muscle depends on the relationship between cytosolic calcium and the phosphorylation state of proteins involved in contraction. A lower calcium concentration can reduce activation of calcium-calmodulin-dependent myosin light-chain kinase. Additional changes can favour reduction of myosin light-chain phosphorylation or modify membrane conductance. Relaxation can therefore occur with more than one pattern of calcium change. In an examination, if cGMP increases but the measured calcium concentration remains nearly unchanged while force falls, it is reasonable to consider an alteration in the sensitivity of the contractile machinery rather than assuming that the cGMP response was absent.

ANP contributes to homeostasis at the level of the whole organism as well as through the immediate response of a target cell. Changes in sodium excretion influence extracellular fluid volume over time. Changes in vascular tone influence pressure more rapidly. Both effects can follow activation of related signalling machinery in different tissues, but they operate on different timescales and involve different downstream proteins. The complete renal and cardiovascular responses are addressed in their respective chapters. Here, the shared cGMP signal is an example of how an endocrine hormone coordinates distinct physiological outputs without requiring a distinct receptor class for each output.

7. Steroid and thyroid hormones: transcriptional regulation

Nuclear receptors are ligand-regulated transcriptional regulators. A typical member contains a DNA-binding domain with zinc-coordinating structures, a ligand-binding region and regulatory surfaces that interact with other proteins. The receptor recognises particular regulatory DNA sequences, called hormone response elements. Recognition alone is not equivalent to transcriptional activation; transcription depends on the receptor's conformational state, partner proteins and the surrounding chromatin.

In the classical glucocorticoid pathway, a substantial pool of unliganded glucocorticoid receptor is maintained in a complex with chaperone proteins. Cortisol binding alters the receptor complex, promoting changes in localisation and interactions. Activated receptors can accumulate in the nucleus, bind appropriate glucocorticoid response elements and influence transcription. Receptors can also interact with other transcription factors. The outcome can be activation of some genes and repression of others. A hormone response element is a DNA sequence rather than an enzyme or a receptor protein.

The mineralocorticoid receptor illustrates the significance of hormone metabolism at the target tissue. Cortisol and aldosterone can both bind the receptor with substantial affinity. In aldosterone-responsive epithelial tissues, 11 beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 converts cortisol into cortisone, which has much weaker activity at the receptor. This mechanism helps aldosterone produce selective responses despite exposure to circulating glucocorticoids. Loss of the enzyme can consequently permit excessive mineralocorticoid receptor activation without a primary increase in aldosterone. This is an example of local control of ligand availability rather than a change in receptor abundance.

Estrogen receptors commonly reside in the nucleus, although their distribution and membrane-associated signalling vary with receptor type and cell context. It is therefore incorrect to generalise the cytoplasmic starting state of glucocorticoid receptors to all steroid receptors. Ligand binding can alter the receptor's interactions with coactivators and corepressors. A coactivator supports transcription, often by modifying chromatin or recruiting other transcriptional machinery. A corepressor supports reduced transcription. The availability of such proteins helps explain why identical receptors can regulate different genes in different tissues.

Thyroid hormone signalling introduces a distinct route to an intracellular receptor. Transporters contribute to movement of thyroid hormones into target cells. Deiodinases regulate the local balance of T4, T3 and inactive metabolites. T3 is the major high-affinity ligand for classical thyroid hormone receptors. Thyroid receptors commonly form heterodimers with retinoid X receptor (RXR) on thyroid response elements. At many positively regulated genes, unliganded thyroid receptor recruits corepressors, whereas T3 binding changes the complex towards coactivator recruitment. The usual model should not be extrapolated indiscriminately to negatively regulated thyroid target genes.

The time required for a genomic hormone response depends on the process measured. Receptor binding and changes in regulatory protein interactions may occur relatively early. Altered transcription, mRNA turnover, translation and accumulation of functional proteins introduce delays. Existing proteins may persist even after the hormone signal falls. By contrast, membrane-associated actions of steroids and other rapid non-genomic mechanisms can change kinase activity or intracellular calcium before measurable production of a new protein. Rapid effects do not disprove nuclear receptor signalling; they indicate that more than one mode of response may exist.

A reporter-gene assay tests whether a candidate DNA regulatory sequence contributes to hormone-dependent transcription. A construct containing a response element upstream of a suitable reporter can be compared with an otherwise matched construct carrying a mutated element. If hormone increases reporter expression only in the intact construct, the result supports a role for the sequence in that experimental context. It does not automatically prove that every natural gene near such a sequence responds identically. Chromatin organisation, other regulatory elements and receptor abundance may differ between a reporter construct and an endogenous gene.

From DNA recognition to transcriptional output

To understand a nuclear receptor response, begin with the distinction between a gene's coding sequence and its regulatory sequences. The coding sequence determines the amino acid sequence of a protein after appropriate processing of the RNA transcript. Regulatory DNA contributes to the determination of when, where and at what rate that RNA is produced. A hormone-responsive sequence can lie in a promoter or in a more distant regulatory region. The physical association of regulatory regions with the transcription machinery enables receptors and other regulatory proteins to alter the rate of transcription without changing the gene's coding sequence.

A hormone response element typically has a characteristic arrangement of short DNA sequence motifs that are recognised by the DNA-binding domains of a particular receptor or receptor complex. Some receptors preferentially bind pairs of related sequence motifs arranged in opposite orientations; others recognise direct repeats with appropriate spacing. The spacing and orientation influence which receptor complexes can form stable interactions. These sequence preferences help produce specificity, but a short sequence match alone is not sufficient to establish that a region regulates a gene in a living cell. Chromatin accessibility, neighbouring regulatory elements and interactions with other transcription factors also determine functional consequences.

The DNA-binding domains of many nuclear receptors contain zinc-coordinating motifs. Zinc helps stabilise a protein structure that positions amino acid residues for interactions with DNA. The metal does not itself encode which gene must be activated. Changes in the structure of the domain can prevent appropriate DNA recognition while leaving ligand binding relatively intact. By contrast, alterations in the ligand-binding domain may impair the receptor's ability to respond to hormone even when the protein retains a functional DNA-binding region. This modular organisation permits experimental separation of ligand recognition, DNA interaction and transcriptional regulation.

The ligand-binding domain also contains regulatory surfaces that change their interactions upon hormone binding. When a suitable agonist stabilises an active receptor conformation, some coactivator proteins can associate more favourably with the receptor. Other ligands may stabilise conformations that do not recruit the same proteins effectively. Thus, the biological activity of a ligand depends not merely on whether it occupies a binding pocket, but on the receptor state it favours. A competitive ligand can reduce the effects of an agonist by occupying sites without producing the same regulatory interactions.

Chromatin presents another important layer of regulation. Eukaryotic DNA is associated with histones and other proteins, and access to particular regulatory sequences can vary between cell types. Coactivator complexes may help alter histone modifications, reposition nucleosomes or recruit general transcriptional machinery. Some corepressor complexes favour conditions associated with reduced transcription. It is misleading to assume that the receptor simply switches a naked DNA strand on or off; transcription occurs within a structured and dynamically regulated nuclear environment.

A receptor can influence transcription without binding directly to a response element in every affected gene. Interactions with other transcription factors can alter their regulatory activity, and hormone-induced changes in the expression of one regulator can have secondary effects on many additional genes. A list of all transcripts that change after hormone treatment therefore includes potential direct targets and indirect targets. To establish a direct regulatory mechanism, an investigator needs more than a change in messenger RNA abundance. Demonstrating receptor association with a relevant DNA region, testing the role of that region and analysing the timing of the response provide complementary evidence.

Hormones can increase the expression of particular genes while decreasing expression of others. An increase in receptor occupancy is therefore not synonymous with a general increase in transcription. Consider a receptor that directly activates the expression of an inhibitory regulatory protein. Even if the receptor increases transcription of the inhibitor, the inhibitor may subsequently reduce transcription of another gene. The observed response of the second gene is negative despite an initially positive transcriptional event. Distinguishing primary and secondary responses is essential when reasoning from expression datasets.

The effect of a change in transcription also depends on the rates at which RNA and protein are removed. An mRNA with a short lifetime may adjust rapidly to a change in transcription, while a stable mRNA can persist after transcription has been reduced. Proteins translated from these transcripts have their own lifetimes. If a hormone-induced protein remains functional for many hours, the physiological response can continue after the receptor has returned towards its basal state. This persistence represents a property of the regulated cellular components rather than necessarily prolonged hormone binding.

Nuclear hormone receptor action is especially suited to changes that require coordinated adjustment of cellular composition. A sustained change in thyroid hormone action can influence metabolic capacity partly through altered expression of multiple proteins. A sustained glucocorticoid signal can influence the expression of proteins involved in metabolism and stress responses. These broad outcomes should not be confused with a simple one-enzyme switch. The receptor can influence a network of genes, and the final physiological result depends on the combined effects and the metabolic environment of the cell.

Thyroid hormone action and local conversion

T4 and T3 differ in the number of iodine atoms incorporated into their chemical structures. T4 is abundant in circulation and can serve as a precursor for T3. Local deiodinase activity can therefore regulate the amount of active ligand in a cell without requiring an immediate change in hormone secretion from the thyroid gland. Deiodinase types differ in the reactions they catalyse. Some reactions support formation of active T3 from T4, whereas others contribute to inactivation of thyroid hormones. The balance of uptake, conversion and removal determines the exposure of intracellular receptors to active ligand.

Transport across the plasma membrane is also important. Thyroid hormones are not adequately described by a model in which every molecule simply dissolves into any cell membrane at the same rate. Specific transport systems help govern cellular uptake and efflux. If a cell's transporter activity falls, its intracellular thyroid hormone concentration may be reduced despite a normal extracellular concentration. If conversion to T3 is impaired, the cell may fail to generate its usual active ligand concentration from available T4. These mechanisms illustrate how tissue responsiveness can differ without a primary defect in the receptor's DNA-binding domain.

At many positively regulated thyroid hormone target genes, TR can occupy regulatory DNA before ligand is available. The unliganded receptor can associate with corepressor complexes, contributing to reduced transcription. Ligand binding changes receptor conformation and can favour release of repressors and recruitment of coactivators. This mechanism differs from a model in which hormone action always requires the appearance of a receptor on DNA for the first time. A DNA occupancy experiment might consequently detect similar receptor association before and after hormone exposure even when transcription changes substantially.

A useful experimental design compares several observations in cells treated with T4 or T3. If the T4 response is weak but the T3 response is normal, impaired conversion of T4 to T3 is one candidate explanation, provided transport and exposure are comparable. If both responses are weak, impaired receptor function, ligand access or downstream transcriptional machinery becomes a possibility. Measuring intracellular hormone concentrations and receptor-dependent reporter activity would help resolve the alternatives. The experiment must not assume that extracellular T3 and T4 necessarily enter cells at the same rate. This caution distinguishes an informative comparison from an unsupported diagnostic conclusion.

A change in receptor sensitivity can produce different effects in different organs because thyroid receptor isoforms and regulatory contexts differ between tissues. Inherited alterations affecting receptor function can lead to resistance to thyroid hormone in relevant target tissues. At the level of endocrine feedback, reduced responsiveness in tissues participating in feedback can alter the relationship between circulating thyroid hormones and pituitary stimulation. The details of thyroid secretion and disease belong to the thyroid physiology chapter. For the present mechanism, the central lesson is that a hormone concentration is not itself an unambiguous measure of receptor-mediated transcription.

An inhibitor of transcription can be useful for distinguishing mechanisms, but its interpretation requires care. If an early hormone response persists when new RNA synthesis is blocked under conditions that preserve cell viability, the early response need not depend on newly transcribed RNA. Existing proteins may mediate it. If a later response disappears, that result supports a requirement for transcription somewhere in the process. It does not establish that the receptor bound directly to a particular response element. An inhibitor could interfere with many genes that are needed for the response. A reporter mutation or a more targeted genetic perturbation is needed to localise the role of a specific DNA sequence.

Rapid steroid-associated effects and genomic responses

The classical contrast between membrane receptors and nuclear receptors is useful as a first model, but the boundary between rapid and slow responses is not identical to the boundary between the two receptor locations. A membrane receptor can activate a transcriptional regulator, as seen with the GH–JAK–STAT route. A steroid hormone may influence signalling at the membrane or in the cytoplasm through mechanisms that do not initially require transcription. In both cases, the position and molecular mechanism of the initial recognition event must be distinguished from the position and timescale of the final response.

An estrogen-dependent change in kinase activity, for example, may occur before detectable synthesis of new protein. Such a response can arise through membrane-associated receptor mechanisms and their interactions with existing signalling proteins. This does not eliminate the importance of classical estrogen receptor-dependent transcription. The same cell may show an early change in enzyme activity followed by a later alteration in gene expression. An experimental time course can reveal both components if observations are made at appropriate intervals.

The term non-genomic is best used to describe a response whose initial mechanism does not require changes in transcription. It should not imply that a rapid signal can never influence gene expression later. A rapidly activated kinase can phosphorylate transcription factors or modify nuclear receptor function. In this way an initial non-genomic response can contribute to a subsequent genomic response. Similarly, a transcription-dependent change in abundance of signalling proteins can alter the size of later rapid responses. The two modes of action can interact over time.

To investigate whether a measured response requires protein synthesis, it is useful to compare hormone-treated cells with and without a suitable translation inhibitor. A preserved early response is consistent with mediation by pre-existing proteins, provided the inhibitor is effective and the cells remain functional. Loss of a late response could indicate a requirement for a newly made protein. Because blocking translation also affects general cell maintenance, a prolonged inhibition experiment requires stronger viability controls. Timing alone is not enough to identify a molecular receptor class; it can guide hypotheses, which must then be tested by appropriate targeted observations.

8. Quantitative reasoning: occupancy, affinity, potency and amplification

Consider a reversible association between hormone H and receptor R to form HR. At equilibrium, the dissociation constant Kd is defined as the free hormone concentration multiplied by the free receptor concentration, divided by the complex concentration. If all receptors have identical independent binding sites and the free hormone concentration remains effectively constant, total receptor concentration equals free receptor plus complex. Substitution and rearrangement give the fraction occupied as [H]/(Kd + [H]). Kd and free hormone concentration must be expressed in the same units.

When free hormone equals Kd, the model predicts half occupancy. If Kd is 2 nanomolar and free hormone is 6 nanomolar, the fraction occupied is 6 divided by 8, or 0.75. If 40,000 receptor sites are available, 30,000 are occupied under the model assumptions. If receptor abundance doubles while the free ligand concentration and Kd remain unchanged, fractional occupancy is still 0.75, but the number of occupied sites doubles. This calculation assumes the ligand reservoir is large enough that additional binding does not substantially lower free hormone concentration.

A binding experiment measures interaction with receptor sites, whereas a functional dose-response experiment measures a downstream response, such as cAMP production, glucose uptake or contraction. The EC50 is the concentration giving half of the maximal measured response under the conditions of the functional experiment. It need not equal Kd. Signal amplification can allow near-maximal response before all receptors are occupied, a phenomenon associated with receptor reserve. Conversely, a response requiring multiple coordinated downstream events can be relatively weak despite high occupancy.

Potency concerns the concentration required for a specified response, while efficacy concerns the capacity of an agonist to produce a response in the experimental system. A partial agonist can occupy a large fraction of receptors yet produce a smaller maximum than a full agonist in the same system. A reversible competitive antagonist can shift the agonist concentration-response curve to higher concentrations without reducing the maximum when sufficient agonist can overcome competition and other conditions remain appropriate. Such an interpretation must distinguish a functional curve from a binding curve and avoid treating every rightward shift as proof of competitive antagonism.

For a simple model of second messenger dynamics, let c be the concentration of cAMP, v the constant production rate in concentration per unit time, and k the first-order degradation constant in inverse time. The rate of change is dc/dt = v − kc. At steady state, production equals degradation and c = v/k. If production is 8 micromolar per minute and k is 2 per minute, steady cAMP is 4 micromolar. Halving k while keeping v constant doubles the steady concentration to 8 micromolar. If production ceases, the same simplified model predicts exponential decay with half-life ln(2)/k. The model ignores compartmentalisation, saturating degradation, feedback and changing rates, but it helps distinguish messenger production from removal.

Amplification follows when activated molecules act catalytically on multiple substrates. If one activated receptor stimulates several G proteins during its active lifetime and each activated effector produces numerous messenger molecules, an intracellular signal can greatly exceed the number of original ligand molecules. This multiplication is limited by substrate availability, enzyme kinetics, opposing reactions, diffusion and the lifetime of the active species. It should not be interpreted as creation of energy. ATP and GTP consumption, pre-existing ion gradients and cellular metabolism make the cascade possible.

Illustrative binding measurements can help separate receptor number from affinity. Suppose two membrane samples have equal protein amounts. At a saturating ligand concentration the specific binding is 100 units in sample A and 50 units in sample B, but the free concentration giving half of each sample's own maximal specific binding is 4 nanomolar in both. The simplest independent-site interpretation is reduced available binding sites in B with similar apparent affinity. Alternative explanations such as altered receptor accessibility or loss of membrane integrity require experimental controls. A functional response curve alone cannot make this distinction.

Equilibrium binding and experimental design

The receptor occupancy equation used earlier rests on a conservation relationship. Let the concentration of free receptor be R, the concentration of free hormone be H, and the concentration of complex be HR. At equilibrium, association and dissociation occur at equal rates, so k_on times H times R equals k_off times HR. The association rate constant has dimensions of inverse concentration per unit time, while the dissociation rate constant has dimensions of inverse time. The ratio k_off/k_on therefore has dimensions of concentration and is equal to Kd in this simple model.

Total receptor sites are the sum of free and ligand-bound receptor. Dividing the complex concentration by that total gives the fractional occupancy. Substituting the equilibrium expression for free receptor produces the hyperbolic equation f = H/(Kd + H). The concentration H in this expression refers to free ligand, not necessarily the total amount added to an experimental tube. If receptor concentration is high relative to the amount of ligand added, the ligand bound to receptors can substantially deplete the free pool. In that situation an estimate based only on total added ligand may give an incorrect occupancy.

The limiting values of the equation make biological sense. When free hormone is far below Kd, occupancy is approximately proportional to hormone concentration. When free hormone greatly exceeds Kd, nearly all binding sites are occupied and further increases produce only small changes in occupancy. At H equal to Kd, half of the sites are occupied. The simple model is not appropriate without modification for multiple classes of binding sites with different affinities, cooperative interactions, substantial ligand depletion or systems that have not reached equilibrium.

A useful binding experiment exposes comparable membrane preparations or cells to a range of concentrations of labelled ligand. Total measured association may include specific binding to receptors and nonspecific association with other components of the sample. A parallel condition containing a sufficiently large excess of suitable unlabelled ligand can help estimate nonspecific binding, assuming the excess ligand competes for the relevant specific sites. Subtracting the estimated nonspecific component provides an estimate of specific binding. The concentration dependence of specific binding can then be analysed to estimate affinity and binding capacity.

At saturation, the estimated maximal specific binding is often denoted Bmax. Its units depend on how the data are reported, for example femtomoles of ligand bound per milligram of membrane protein. Bmax cannot be directly compared between samples without an appropriate basis for normalisation. If one sample contains twice as much membrane material, a higher total binding capacity need not indicate higher receptor density. Similarly, membrane damage or loss during sample preparation can change accessible receptor abundance. These factors must be controlled before inferring that a difference in Bmax represents biological receptor regulation.

The rate of dissociation can help distinguish ligands that have similar equilibrium affinity but different kinetics. For example, two ligands may have the same ratio of k_off to k_on even if both rate constants are much smaller for one of them. Their equilibrium occupancy curves can be similar, yet the time needed to approach equilibrium or return towards baseline after removal of free ligand can differ. This observation connects receptor binding measurements with the interpretation of hormone pulses and the duration of target-cell activation.

Suppose one ligand binds with an association rate constant of one million per molar per second and a dissociation rate constant of 0.01 per second. The ratio gives Kd = 0.00000001 molar, or 10 nanomolar. Under simple first-order dissociation after free ligand has been effectively removed, the dissociation half-life is approximately 69.3 seconds, calculated as ln(2) divided by 0.01 per second. A second ligand with both constants tenfold smaller has the same Kd but a dissociation half-life of approximately 693 seconds. This illustrates how affinity alone cannot predict the time course of receptor occupancy.

The same equilibrium framework explains why changes in circulating binding proteins can affect hormone availability. If a hormone binds to a carrier in solution, the free fraction depends on the concentration and binding properties of the carrier. A receptor at the target surface experiences the free concentration near its binding site. Even for a simple receptor with a known Kd, it is necessary to distinguish changes in total hormone from changes in free hormone. This is particularly important when comparing tissues or physiological states in which carrier abundance or local hormone metabolism differs.

Interpreting response curves and receptor reserve

A functional response curve is obtained by measuring an output at different hormone concentrations. The output might be an increase in enzyme activity, the amount of calcium released, the fraction of transporters present at the surface or the production of a reporter protein. Before comparing curves, determine whether the plotted values are absolute measurements or values normalised to each condition's own maximum. If each curve has been normalised to its own maximum, a reduction in absolute maximal response may be hidden.

A receptor reserve exists when the signalling system can achieve its maximal measured response without occupation of all accessible receptors. Consider a simplified system in which the downstream effector reaches its maximum activity once 20% of receptors are occupied. Increasing occupancy above that fraction has little effect on the selected output. A modest reduction in receptor abundance may then shift the concentration needed to achieve a given response while leaving the maximum initially unchanged. Further reduction can eventually lower the maximum when there are too few active receptors to saturate downstream machinery.

In this situation the functional response depends not only on ligand affinity but on receptor density and the efficiency of coupling between receptor and effector. A full agonist in one tissue can display a smaller maximum in another tissue with less efficient coupling. The designation of an agonist as full or partial therefore refers to a defined receptor system and response, rather than a universal property that is independent of experimental conditions. This is one reason that comparing hormone responses in different cell lines requires attention to receptor expression and downstream components.

A rightward shift in a dose-response curve indicates that a higher ligand concentration is required to achieve a specified response within the range measured. A reduction in maximum indicates that the system cannot achieve its previous maximal output under those conditions. Competitive antagonism is a familiar mechanism that can cause a rightward shift without a loss of maximum in a suitable system. However, receptor downregulation in a system with receptor reserve can also initially produce a shift without necessarily reducing the maximum. A graph of functional response alone does not uniquely identify the molecular mechanism.

A combination of binding and functional assays can resolve the ambiguity. If equilibrium specific binding reveals a reduction in Bmax without a detectable change in Kd, fewer available sites are implicated under the single-class model. If Bmax and Kd remain unchanged while the functional curve shifts, a change in receptor coupling or downstream regulation becomes a plausible explanation. If the receptor's binding affinity changes, then altered occupancy at a given free concentration may account for part of the change in response. None of these observations removes the need to consider sample quality and the limitations of the measurement.

An illustrative dataset can make these distinctions concrete. Suppose two cell preparations have the same receptor binding capacity of 100 arbitrary units and apparent Kd of 10 nanomolar. Their maximal measured responses are also both 100 response units. Preparation A has an EC50 of 1 nanomolar, while preparation B has an EC50 of 10 nanomolar. At 1 nanomolar, the predicted fractional occupancy is about 0.091 in both preparations. Yet A produces half of its maximum at that concentration. The difference is consistent with more efficient coupling or greater functional amplification in A. It does not demonstrate which specific kinase or messenger creates that difference.

Graph axes should also be read carefully. Hormone dose is often plotted on a logarithmic axis because biologically active concentrations span several orders of magnitude. The occupancy relationship appears as a sigmoidal curve when free concentration is represented logarithmically, even when the underlying binding model has independent, non-cooperative sites. A sigmoidal curve on a logarithmic concentration axis is therefore not sufficient proof of cooperative binding. Compare the data with the appropriate mathematical model and inspect the axis scale before drawing a conclusion about receptor interactions.

An examination may ask a student to choose between a reduction in hormone affinity and a reduction in maximum signalling capacity. A binding assay directly addresses the interaction between ligand and receptor; a response assay addresses the behaviour of the downstream system. The most useful first question is whether the graph measures binding, receptor activation, a messenger concentration or a distant physiological endpoint. The same curve shape can have different implications depending on which variable is plotted. This method of identifying the measurement before assigning a mechanism avoids many common errors.

Time-dependent messenger production and degradation

The simplified messenger equation dc/dt = v − kc can also describe the approach to a new steady state when hormone stimulation abruptly changes production from one constant rate to another. If the starting concentration is c0 and the new steady concentration is c_ss, the solution is c(t) = c_ss + (c0 − c_ss) exp(−kt). The sign of the difference between the starting and new steady concentrations determines whether the curve rises or falls. The time constant is 1/k, which has dimensions of time. The half-time for closing the gap to the new steady concentration is ln(2)/k.

Assume a cell initially maintains a cAMP concentration of 1 micromolar. Hormone exposure changes production so that the new steady concentration is 9 micromolar, while k remains 0.5 per minute. The initial difference from the new steady state is 8 micromolar. After a time equal to ln(2)/0.5, approximately 1.39 minutes, this gap is halved, so concentration becomes 5 micromolar. After another such interval the gap becomes 2 micromolar, and concentration reaches 7 micromolar. The example is illustrative rather than a measurement of any specific tissue.

If hormone exposure then ends and production returns to its original basal value, the signal declines towards the original steady state of 1 micromolar rather than necessarily reaching zero. This distinction is important in interpreting decay curves. When basal production persists, the experimentally observed messenger concentration approaches a nonzero asymptote. The half-time refers to the decline of the excess above that baseline. Ignoring basal production may lead to a wrong estimate of the degradation constant.

An increase in production rate raises the steady messenger concentration in the simple model. A decrease in the degradation constant also raises it, but the latter additionally increases the time constant. Therefore, two interventions can produce the same final messenger concentration while yielding different rates of approach and decay. For example, a twofold increase in production at constant degradation and a twofold decrease in degradation at constant production both double the steady concentration. The second intervention slows the approach to equilibrium and slows removal after production ceases. A time course can therefore discriminate mechanisms that a single steady-state observation cannot.

Real cellular signalling is more complicated because production and removal rates can themselves respond to messenger concentration and other signals. An activated protein kinase may alter the activity of a phosphodiesterase. A receptor may become less effective over time through desensitisation. Messenger concentrations in different compartments may have distinct kinetics. The simple model should be viewed as a reference prediction: systematic deviations from it suggest additional regulation, not necessarily an experimental mistake.

The model illustrates how a hormone pulse can be filtered by a target cell. If messenger degradation is slow compared with the interval between pulses, residual messenger from one pulse persists into the next. Repeated pulses may then produce accumulation towards a sustained level. If degradation is rapid, the messenger can return near its basal level between pulses. In either case, the average hormone concentration alone does not determine the temporal shape of the intracellular response. This principle will be useful when studying the endocrine control of pituitary and reproductive functions.

9. Termination, desensitisation and hormone resistance

A signal must end or change when its physiological context changes. Otherwise, a brief hormone pulse could continuously activate target cells. Signal termination can occur at the ligand, receptor, G protein, effector, messenger, substrate and transcriptional levels. Hormone clearance reduces the available stimulus. Receptor dissociation reduces occupancy. GTP hydrolysis switches off G protein alpha subunits. Phosphodiesterases degrade cyclic nucleotides, phosphatases remove regulatory phosphate groups, and calcium transport systems restore low resting cytosolic calcium.

Desensitisation refers to a reduced response during or following stimulation. An important GPCR mechanism involves phosphorylation of activated receptors by G protein-coupled receptor kinases (GRKs). Beta-arrestin binding can reduce coupling to G proteins and promote receptor internalisation. Internalised receptors may recycle to the cell surface after dephosphorylation or may be directed towards degradation. Arrestins can also participate in signalling, so their binding is not always equivalent to complete loss of every receptor-dependent effect.

Homologous desensitisation preferentially affects the stimulated receptor system. Heterologous desensitisation can affect more than one receptor pathway, for example through a kinase activated downstream of one receptor that modifies other components. A test using two receptors that share an intracellular effector can help distinguish these possibilities. If prolonged stimulation of receptor A reduces the subsequent response to A but not B, selective regulation of A is plausible. If responses to both are reduced, a shared effector or a broadly acting regulatory mechanism becomes a stronger possibility.

Downregulation refers to reduction of available receptor abundance, often over a longer interval, and is distinguishable from rapid functional uncoupling. Upregulation can increase available receptors in some physiological circumstances. A measurement of total receptor protein does not necessarily represent surface-accessible functional receptor number. Combining surface binding, total protein abundance and response measurements provides more information than any single measurement. A receptor internalised from the surface may remain present in the cell and may subsequently recycle.

Hormone resistance may result from receptor alterations, changes in downstream proteins, inhibitory signalling or inadequate availability of active ligand at the target. Some forms of growth hormone insensitivity involve defects in the growth hormone receptor or related signalling, reducing appropriate IGF-1 production despite abundant growth hormone. The feedback consequences illustrate an endocrine principle: a high concentration of hormone is compatible with low biological action when the target is unable to respond adequately. The diagnosis of the molecular defect, however, requires more evidence than this concentration pattern alone.

Finally, cells often receive signals from several hormones simultaneously. Insulin and glucagon can favour opposing metabolic states in the liver; their relative levels, receptor systems and the availability of metabolic substrates determine the resulting flux. In one pathway, phosphorylation may favour mobilising glycogen, whereas another pathway favours its synthesis. The final outcome reflects the balance of active enzymes and substrate concentrations rather than the existence of a single dominant hormone name. The same conceptual framework will support the study of hypothalamic and pituitary feedback, thyroid regulation, glucose homeostasis and reproductive endocrine physiology.

The difference between signal termination and adaptation

Signal termination can occur because the original stimulus disappears. Adaptation occurs when the response changes while the stimulus remains present. These two processes may produce superficially similar time courses. A messenger concentration that rises and then falls could reflect disappearance of the extracellular hormone, activation of an opposing intracellular process or depletion of a limiting substrate. Measuring the hormone concentration during the experiment is one way to distinguish a change in input from a change in target-cell sensitivity.

An activated GPCR may be phosphorylated by a GRK in a way that favours arrestin binding. The resulting receptor complex is often less capable of activating heterotrimeric G proteins. The receptor can still be present at the plasma membrane during the early stage of this uncoupling. Arrestin can also interact with components of endocytic machinery, contributing to receptor movement into intracellular vesicles. The physical removal of receptor from the membrane can further reduce its availability to an extracellular ligand. These steps are related but are not identical observations.

After internalisation, receptors can follow different routes. Some undergo dephosphorylation and are returned to the surface, allowing renewed responsiveness. Others are transported towards compartments in which they can be degraded. The balance between recycling and degradation affects how quickly the cell recovers after hormone exposure. If a cell becomes responsive again after a relatively brief hormone-free interval without an increase in total receptor protein, receptor recycling or reversal of functional uncoupling is a plausible explanation. If recovery requires synthesis of new receptors, a prior decrease in receptor protein may have been more important.

A simple protocol for studying desensitisation uses an initial hormone challenge, a period of continued or repeated stimulation, a washout and a second challenge. The initial response establishes that the preparation can signal. The second response reveals whether responsiveness changed. A time-matched control that undergoes the same handling without the first hormone exposure is essential, because changes in cell health or depletion of intracellular stores can alter responses independently of receptor regulation. If the experiment measures calcium release, the time allowed for refilling of stores should also be considered.

Two measurements of receptor abundance can help refine the interpretation. A surface-selective labelling method estimates receptors accessible at the plasma membrane, while measurement of total receptor protein includes intracellular pools. A decrease in surface labelling with unchanged total protein is consistent with redistribution. A decrease in both may support loss of receptor protein, assuming the assays detect comparable forms of the receptor. If neither changes but function declines, receptor uncoupling or changes in a downstream component become stronger possibilities. These observations should not be collapsed into a single undefined statement that a receptor is downregulated.

Desensitisation may also occur because an effector becomes less responsive or because inhibitory proteins accumulate. In a cAMP pathway, increased phosphodiesterase activity could reduce messenger concentration even when receptor coupling to G proteins remains intact. A kinase can phosphorylate components of pathways other than the one that originally activated it. This provides a possible basis for heterologous desensitisation. The distinction between a receptor-specific and a more general mechanism is especially useful when several hormones converge on a common effector.

A student may be tempted to assume that receptor desensitisation is always harmful. In normal physiology it can protect the cell from excessive or prolonged stimulation and make the response sensitive to changes in input rather than simply its continuous presence. Recovery mechanisms can restore responsiveness when conditions change. On the other hand, persistent maladaptive reductions in signalling may contribute to physiological disturbances. The functional significance depends on the context, the duration of the change and the consequences for the tissue's role in homeostasis.

Distinguishing classes of hormone insensitivity

A reduced physiological effect despite hormone availability can originate at several molecular levels. Before receptor binding, transport across a membrane or enzymatic conversion of a precursor hormone may be impaired. At the receptor, the ligand-binding domain may fail to recognise hormone efficiently, or the receptor may be poorly expressed or retained in an inaccessible cellular compartment. Even normal binding may fail to activate the receptor if the conformational transition or catalytic domain is defective. Beyond the receptor, altered signalling proteins, excessive activity of opposing enzymes and defects in final effectors can all reduce the response.

The distinction between a binding defect and a signalling defect can be explored with receptor occupancy and downstream activation measurements. Suppose two cell types contain similar numbers of receptor proteins. One responds weakly to hormone and has substantially reduced specific binding; a defect in binding affinity or accessibility is a candidate explanation. The other binds hormone normally but fails to activate the relevant messenger pathway. In the second case, the lesion may involve receptor activation or signalling beyond it. A biochemical measurement of total receptor protein alone cannot distinguish these alternatives.

The distinction between a receptor defect and a post-receptor defect can sometimes be investigated through functional rescue. In a pathway that normally uses cAMP, an intervention that raises cAMP independently of receptor activation can bypass a failure before messenger production. If the final response is restored, it supports the presence of usable downstream machinery. In an insulin pathway, direct activation of a suitable downstream component may help test whether impaired glucose uptake is due to a defect in an upstream step. Such experiments must be interpreted with care because artificial activation can exceed normal physiological levels or engage additional pathways.

Endocrine feedback further complicates interpretation of circulating hormone measurements. A normal feedback loop adjusts secretion in response to the biological state of the organism. If a target response is insufficient, compensatory changes can increase hormone secretion. A patient or experimental animal can therefore have a high hormone concentration despite reduced biological action at one or more target tissues. The concentration pattern may suggest resistance but cannot by itself identify a receptor mutation. Alternative explanations include altered hormone clearance, altered binding proteins or a defect elsewhere in the feedback loop.

Insulin resistance provides a useful example of tissue-specific and output-specific effects. A skeletal muscle cell may show reduced insulin-stimulated glucose uptake while some other insulin-dependent responses remain detectable. Liver and adipose tissue may exhibit different degrees of impaired response because their receptor networks, metabolic conditions and feedback relationships differ. Thus, a global phrase such as reduced insulin sensitivity should be connected to the particular tissue and output measured. The endocrine pancreas and interorgan metabolism chapters examine these relationships at the organism level.

A gain-of-function change can be as informative as a loss-of-function change. If a receptor activates its downstream machinery even when little ligand is present, the target cell may show an abnormally high basal response. In a negative-feedback endocrine axis, this can reduce secretion of the stimulating hormone. A low circulating hormone concentration can therefore coexist with excessive receptor-mediated action. The distinction between ligand concentration and biological effect is central to interpreting both hormone deficiency and inappropriate hormone signalling.

Receptor antibodies illustrate another mechanism of altered endocrine action. Antibodies directed towards an extracellular receptor can sometimes stimulate or block receptor function depending on the interaction. An antibody that stimulates a receptor may imitate important aspects of the normal hormone signal without being the hormone itself. An antibody that blocks receptor activation can reduce the response despite normal hormone concentration. The molecular principle is ligand-independent alteration of receptor function. Specific endocrine disorders are examined in the corresponding gland chapters rather than treated here as an exhaustive clinical classification.

Integrating opposing and convergent hormone pathways

A target cell rarely receives only one hormonal signal. In a liver cell, glucagon may favour pathways that increase glucose availability during fasting, whereas insulin favours storage and suppresses some glucose-producing processes after feeding. The two hormones act through different receptor families, yet they influence overlapping sets of metabolic processes. Their opposition is therefore not simply a physical competition for the same receptor. It arises because their intracellular pathways regulate processes with different physiological directions.

At a point of convergence, one kinase may favour phosphorylation of a regulatory substrate while a phosphatase or opposing pathway reduces that modification. The state of the substrate reflects the relative rates of the reactions. If a hormone changes the activity of an upstream kinase, its effect on substrate phosphorylation depends in part on the activity of opposing enzymes. A constant hormone concentration need not produce constant phosphorylation when metabolic conditions or other regulatory inputs change.

Hormones may also exhibit permissive effects. One hormone can influence the abundance of receptors, enzymes or other cellular components that determine responsiveness to a second hormone. This interaction can develop over a relatively long interval because a change in gene expression and protein abundance may be involved. The second hormone may then produce an enhanced response through its existing rapid signalling pathway. The two signals do not have to converge directly on the same second messenger to interact functionally.

Synergistic effects occur when combined hormonal actions produce an output greater than expected under an explicitly defined additive comparison. A distinction must be made between a genuinely cooperative effect and an apparent increase caused by the nonlinear nature of the response measurement. If two stimuli each move a messenger concentration closer to a threshold, their combination may cross that threshold and produce a large physiological change even without direct interaction between their receptors. A careful experiment compares individual and combined treatments under matched conditions and states how additivity is assessed.

Consider a teaching experiment in which a receptor A agonist increases cytosolic calcium but produces only modest activation of conventional PKC because DAG availability is limited. An independent intervention increases membrane DAG without producing a large calcium transient. The combined stimuli may produce stronger PKC activation than either alone because conventional PKC responds to both signals. The physiological output depends on the convergence of regulatory requirements at the effector. This example demonstrates how signal integration can occur without identical upstream receptor classes.

Antagonistic, permissive and synergistic interactions are physiological descriptions of the combined effects of signals. They do not automatically identify the molecular step at which the interaction occurs. To investigate that step, an experimental design measures early signals, intermediate protein activation and the final cellular response for each stimulus separately and together. If early messenger changes are independent but the combined physiological response is nonlinear, convergence downstream of the messenger level becomes a plausible hypothesis. If one hormone changes the messenger produced by the other, interaction may occur at an earlier stage. Mapping the point of interaction is more informative than assigning a descriptive term alone.

The major receptor classes studied in this chapter can now be compared through a shared set of questions. First determine where the hormone is recognised and whether access to the receptor requires a transport process. Then identify the first biochemical event that transmits the signal. Next distinguish the regulatory switch from the messenger and identify the molecule that executes the response. Finally, identify how the pathway is limited and how it may interact with another physiological input. These questions provide a consistent strategy for analysing new endocrine mechanisms without treating each hormone as an unrelated set of facts.

Part B conceptual practice

Original short-response practice. Attempt each question before opening the explanation.

1. Which hormone class most commonly signals through surface receptors?

Answer and reasoning

Peptide hormones

Their hydrophilic character restricts passage through the hydrophobic membrane interior; the receptor transmits information without hormone entry.

2. What event directly activates a heterotrimeric Gα switch?

Answer and reasoning

Exchange of GDP for GTP

The receptor promotes GDP release; GTP binding favours the active conformation. Hydrolysis reverses activation.

3. Which enzyme converts ATP to cAMP?

Answer and reasoning

Adenylyl cyclase

It is an effector of susceptible Gs and Gi pathways. PKA responds to cAMP rather than synthesising it.

4. What is the canonical effect of Gαi on susceptible adenylyl cyclase isoforms?

Answer and reasoning

Inhibition

Inhibition lowers production rate, although the net concentration also depends on degradation and other inputs.

5. Which molecule is cleaved by PLCβ?

Answer and reasoning

PIP2

Hydrolysis generates IP3 and DAG, which have different localisation and functions.

6. Which intracellular structure supplies calcium following IP3 receptor activation?

Answer and reasoning

Endoplasmic reticulum

IP3 receptors are release channels; calcium moves down its electrochemical gradient.

7. Which molecule remains membrane-associated after PLC-mediated cleavage?

Answer and reasoning

DAG

Its lipid character retains it in the bilayer, unlike soluble IP3.

8. Which PKC group typically responds to both calcium and DAG?

Answer and reasoning

Conventional PKC

Novel and atypical isoforms differ in their cofactor requirements.

9. What is the intrinsic catalytic activity of the insulin receptor?

Answer and reasoning

Protein tyrosine kinase

Its transmembrane beta subunits carry the catalytic domains.

10. Which lipid does PI3K generate from PIP2 in insulin signalling?

Answer and reasoning

PIP3

PI3K adds a phosphate at the 3 position; it does not cleave PIP2.

11. Which transporter is recruited to the surface in insulin-responsive muscle and adipose cells?

Answer and reasoning

GLUT4

Its surface abundance controls facilitated diffusion of glucose.

12. Which protein removes the 3-phosphate from PIP3?

Answer and reasoning

PTEN

It opposes the lipid signal by converting PIP3 to PIP2.

13. What distinguishes a growth hormone receptor from the insulin receptor?

Answer and reasoning

It uses an associated kinase rather than an intrinsic kinase domain

JAK2 associates with the receptor and transmits the signal to STAT proteins.

14. Which regulator is especially important in GH and prolactin signalling?

Answer and reasoning

STAT5

Phosphorylated STAT5 can dimerise and influence transcription.

15. Which receptor principally mediates ANP-dependent cGMP production?

Answer and reasoning

NPR-A (GC-A)

Its intracellular catalytic domain converts GTP to cGMP.

16. Which enzyme is directly activated by nitric oxide in vascular smooth muscle?

Answer and reasoning

Soluble guanylyl cyclase

Its activation also produces cGMP, but the recognition event differs from ANP binding to NPR-A.

17. Which partner commonly forms a DNA-binding heterodimer with thyroid hormone receptor?

Answer and reasoning

RXR

The TR–RXR complex can recruit different coregulators depending on ligand and context.

18. What is the major high-affinity ligand of the classical thyroid hormone receptor?

Answer and reasoning

T3

Transport and local deiodination help determine T3 availability.

19. What is the principal enzymatic activity of a phosphodiesterase in cyclic nucleotide signalling?

Answer and reasoning

Hydrolysis of cyclic nucleotides

It contributes to termination rather than receptor activation.

20. What is receptor downregulation?

Answer and reasoning

Reduction in available receptor abundance

It differs from rapid uncoupling, which can occur before total receptor protein declines.

Part C analytical practice

Original short-response practice. Attempt each question before opening the explanation.

1. A hepatocyte has normal glucagon binding but a mutated Gαs that cannot exchange GDP efficiently. Predict the cAMP response.

Answer and reasoning

Reduced glucagon-stimulated cAMP production

Binding alone is not sufficient; impaired nucleotide exchange blocks efficient stimulation of cyclase.

2. A Gαs mutation preserves GTP binding but prevents GTP hydrolysis. What occurs after a short stimulus?

Answer and reasoning

Prolonged Gαs activity and potentially prolonged cAMP production

The normal intrinsic shutoff step is defective, although other regulatory processes can limit the downstream response.

3. Cyclase production remains 6 μM/min and first-order degradation changes from 3 to 1.5 min⁻¹. Calculate steady cAMP.

Answer and reasoning

2 μM initially; 4 μM after the change

At steady state c=v/k. Halving k doubles c if production is constant.

4. A Gq agonist produces a transient calcium rise without extracellular calcium, but the sustained phase disappears. Infer the source of the initial and sustained phases.

Answer and reasoning

Initial release from internal stores; sustained response depends partly on external calcium entry

The initial transient persists despite absent extracellular calcium, provided internal stores were initially loaded.

5. A cell releases normal IP3 after hormonal stimulation but cannot release ER calcium. Name a likely downstream site to investigate.

Answer and reasoning

IP3 receptor function or ER calcium loading

Normal IP3 production suggests the upstream Gq–PLC machinery is at least partly intact.

6. A drug blocks PLC but leaves PI3K intact. Predict the direct effects on IP3 and insulin-induced PIP3.

Answer and reasoning

Reduced PLC-derived IP3; PI3K-dependent PIP3 may remain intact

The enzymes use related lipid substrates but catalyse different reactions.

7. Muscle cells display normal insulin receptor and IRS phosphorylation but no rise in PIP3. Which step is a candidate defect?

Answer and reasoning

PI3K activation or excessive PIP3 removal

The defect occurs after IRS or at the level of the lipid signal; measuring PI3K activity and PTEN can distinguish alternatives.

8. Cells have normal insulin-induced Akt phosphorylation but no GLUT4 surface increase. Which stage should be examined?

Answer and reasoning

Downstream vesicle trafficking or GLUT4 availability

A functional transporter delivery defect can persist despite normal upstream kinase activation.

9. A mutant insulin receptor binds insulin normally but its kinase domain is inactive. Can normal binding establish normal signalling?

Answer and reasoning

No

IRS phosphorylation requires catalytic receptor activity. Binding does not prove kinase activation.

10. Growth hormone binding and receptor abundance are normal but JAK2 cannot associate with receptor. What is predicted for STAT5 phosphorylation?

Answer and reasoning

Reduced STAT5 phosphorylation

The growth hormone receptor lacks its own kinase domain and needs associated JAK activity.

11. Prolactin triggers normal STAT5 phosphorylation but a reporter containing a mutated STAT-responsive sequence fails to respond. Localise the likely defect.

Answer and reasoning

At the DNA regulatory element

The upstream phosphorylation step remains intact; compare an otherwise matched wild-type reporter to isolate the sequence contribution.

12. ANP fails to increase cGMP while nitric oxide increases it normally. Which mechanism is most likely affected?

Answer and reasoning

The NPR-A signalling route

Preserved NO responsiveness argues against a universal inability to generate cGMP.

13. Both ANP and nitric oxide raise cGMP normally, but smooth muscle does not relax. What should be investigated?

Answer and reasoning

PKG and downstream regulation of contractility

Messenger production is intact; an impaired response to cGMP or another contractile defect can account for the phenotype.

14. Two tissues have equal T4 exposure but different local T3 concentrations. Give one explanation.

Answer and reasoning

Different transporter expression or deiodinase activity

Local hormone access and conversion can alter receptor exposure independently of total circulating T4.

15. A nuclear receptor binds hormone but its DNA-binding domain is defective. Predict the direct response of a conventional response-element reporter.

Answer and reasoning

Reduced hormone-dependent reporter transcription

Ligand recognition does not substitute for sequence-specific DNA binding; indirect effects may persist.

16. A target cell loses 11β-HSD2 activity. Why might mineralocorticoid signalling increase without increased aldosterone?

Answer and reasoning

Cortisol can activate the mineralocorticoid receptor when local conversion to cortisone is impaired

The normal enzyme restricts cortisol availability at the receptor.

17. Kd is 5 nM and free ligand is 15 nM. Calculate occupancy and bound sites if 12,000 sites are present.

Answer and reasoning

75%; 9,000 sites

Fraction = 15/(5+15)=0.75; multiply by total sites under the independent-site model.

18. Two binding curves reach maxima of 80 and 40 units but both have half-maximal binding at 3 nM. Interpret.

Answer and reasoning

Lower apparent binding capacity in the second sample with similar apparent affinity

Bmax differs while Kd is similar under the stated model; control for sample integrity and protein loading.

19. A functional response reaches half-maximum at 1 nM, whereas equilibrium binding has Kd=10 nM. Is this necessarily an error?

Answer and reasoning

No; amplification or receptor reserve can account for the difference

EC50 depends on the pathway and readout; it need not equal the binding constant.

20. Prolonged stimulation of receptor A reduces its subsequent response while receptor B, which shares cyclase, responds normally. What is suggested?

Answer and reasoning

Preferential homologous desensitisation

Preserved B response supports a selective A defect rather than global loss of cyclase, but direct tests are required.

Additional SET and integrated practice

Original short-response practice. Attempt each question before opening the explanation.

1. Assertion: all steroid hormone responses require new protein synthesis. Reason: some steroid-associated effects activate pre-existing signalling proteins rapidly.

Answer and reasoning

Assertion false; reason true

Rapid responses can occur without a newly synthesised protein, although classical genomic effects are important.

2. A 4 μM cAMP pool decays with k = 0.693 min⁻¹ and no production. Estimate its concentration after two minutes.

Answer and reasoning

1 μM

The half-life is approximately 1 minute, so two half-lives reduce the pool to one quarter.

3. A receptor population has Kd=4 nM. At what free ligand concentration is 80% of the population occupied?

Answer and reasoning

16 nM

Rearrange f=H/(Kd+H): H=fKd/(1−f)=0.8×4/0.2.

4. Match glucagon, GnRH, insulin, GH and ANP with their canonical receptor pathways.

Answer and reasoning

Gs–cAMP; Gq–PLC; intrinsic tyrosine kinase; JAK–STAT; receptor guanylyl cyclase

The messenger and catalytic arrangement distinguish the pathways.

5. A researcher measures only total receptor protein after agonist exposure. What complementary test helps identify receptor internalisation?

Answer and reasoning

Surface-specific binding or surface labelling

Total protein can remain constant while the surface population falls.

6. An antagonist shifts a dose-response curve rightward without reducing its maximum. What mechanism is compatible, but not uniquely established?

Answer and reasoning

Reversible competitive antagonism

More agonist can overcome competition in a simple equilibrium model. Other mechanisms can also change potency.

7. Which experimental control is essential when comparing hormone responses of mutant and wild-type receptor-expressing cells?

Answer and reasoning

Comparable receptor expression and cell viability

Differences in expression or viability could explain an apparent signalling defect without altered receptor mechanism.

8. What response distinguishes a membrane-receptor-mediated genomic effect from the claim that only nuclear receptors affect transcription?

Answer and reasoning

STAT-mediated gene regulation after GH stimulation

A cell-surface receptor can activate a transcription factor through kinase signalling.

9. An agonist causes messenger accumulation. Which two kinetic measurements help distinguish increased production from reduced degradation?

Answer and reasoning

Production rate and decay after production ceases

Steady concentration alone cannot identify which flux changed.

10. A hormone is abundant in circulation yet its physiological output is weak. Give two distinct molecular hypotheses.

Answer and reasoning

Defective receptor activation or impaired post-receptor signalling

Both reduce action despite hormone availability. Target access, ligand metabolism and other factors also matter.

Misconceptions and examination traps

  1. Binding means activation. An antagonist can bind without activating the receptor. In an exam separate receptor occupancy from efficacy; look for catalytic or functional readouts.
  2. All water-soluble hormones use GPCRs. Insulin and GH are peptides but act via intrinsic and associated kinases, respectively. Chemistry predicts access, not exact receptor family.
  3. GDP activates Gα. GTP binding favours the active state; GDP release precedes exchange. Predict loss of signalling when exchange is blocked.
  4. Gi destroys cAMP. Gi reduces production by inhibiting susceptible cyclases; phosphodiesterase hydrolyses cAMP. Separate production and removal.
  5. IP3 brings extracellular calcium in directly. IP3 opens ER release channels. External calcium entry can sustain the signal later.
  6. DAG diffuses freely through cytosol. DAG remains membrane-associated. Its spatial organisation helps direct PKC signalling.
  7. All PKC isoforms require calcium. Conventional PKC requires calcium and DAG, but other groups differ. Read isoform-specific questions carefully.
  8. Insulin receptor assembles from two separate monomers at every activation. The mature insulin receptor is a preassembled disulfide-linked complex. Ligand changes its conformation.
  9. PI3K cleaves PIP2. PI3K phosphorylates PIP2; PLC cleaves it. Distinguish PIP3 from IP3.
  10. GLUT4 pumps glucose using ATP. GLUT4 mediates facilitated diffusion; insulin controls surface availability. Determine whether a concentration gradient is present.
  11. GH receptor is itself a tyrosine kinase. It associates with JAK2. A catalytic partner and intrinsic receptor kinase are different architectures.
  12. ANP signals through a G protein simply because its pathway uses GTP. NPR-A converts GTP to cGMP as a catalytic substrate. GTP bound to Gα has a distinct role.
  13. Every steroid receptor begins in the cytoplasm. Glucocorticoid receptors commonly have a cytoplasmic pool, while estrogen receptors may already be nuclear. Receptor identity matters.
  14. T4 freely enters all cells and directly determines receptor occupancy. Transporters and deiodinases regulate local T3. Circulating total T4 is not a measure of all target receptor exposure.
  15. Kd must equal EC50. Binding affinity and functional potency describe different phenomena. Amplification can separate them.
  16. Desensitisation always means receptors have been destroyed. Rapid uncoupling can precede receptor internalisation or degradation. Compare surface abundance, total abundance and function.
