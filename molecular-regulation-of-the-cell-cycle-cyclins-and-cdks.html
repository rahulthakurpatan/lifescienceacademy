1. Why the cell needs a molecular control system

A newly formed cell contains an entire genome, but possessing that genome does not tell the cell when it should copy it or when it should separate the copies. During G1 the cell can grow and respond to its surroundings; during S phase it duplicates DNA; during G2 it prepares for chromosome segregation; during M phase the duplicated chromosomes are distributed. These phases describe what happens, not what causes a transition. A cell could in principle contain all the enzymes for both replication and mitosis at the same time. If those enzymes were indiscriminately active, replication might restart on a chromosome already copied, or the cell might attempt segregation while DNA synthesis was unfinished. A mechanism that changes the permission to perform each operation is therefore indispensable.

A useful way to separate the problem is to distinguish the machinery that performs an event from the machinery that regulates it. DNA polymerases extend DNA chains; microtubules and motors contribute to chromosome movement. Neither activity by itself explains why an entire population of replication factors becomes ready at the beginning of S phase or why a nucleus reorganizes at mitotic entry. A regulatory enzyme can instead alter several target proteins in parallel. If its activity is increased at an appropriate point, its targets can coordinate a transition; if activity subsequently declines, an opposing set of changes becomes possible. This strategy links events that occur in different physical parts of the cell.

The central regulators of most eukaryotic cell cycles are cyclin-dependent kinases, abbreviated CDKs, and their cyclin partners. A cyclin is not the enzyme that directly copies DNA or moves chromosomes. It is a regulatory protein that associates with a kinase, changing its ability to act on target proteins. In many textbook examples the amount of a CDK changes comparatively little, whereas the amount and identity of its cyclin partner change substantially. Even that observation is only a beginning: the activity of a complex can be held low despite abundant cyclin. To understand timing, it is necessary to distinguish protein abundance, complex formation, inhibitory and activating modifications, and the availability of targets.

There is a further distinction between the engine and the monitoring system. Cyclin–CDK activity can make a transition possible, whereas a checkpoint can prevent that transition when a prerequisite is not satisfied. A cell with normal cyclin production but damaged DNA may therefore remain outside mitosis because its kinase is inhibited. Conversely, a defective kinase can delay the cycle even if DNA is undamaged. The present chapter explains the engine and the molecular switches that control it. The next chapter examines how surveillance systems communicate with those switches. A basic understanding of G1, S, G2 and M, chromosome duplication, protein structure and enzyme catalysis is sufficient to follow the argument.

2. How experiments revealed transferable cell-cycle regulators

The idea of a regulatory molecule became compelling when investigators learned that a change in cell state could be transmitted through cytoplasm. Frog oocytes are particularly useful because an immature oocyte is large, arrested in meiotic prophase and capable of undergoing maturation after an appropriate hormonal stimulus. In 1971, Yoshio Masui and Clement Markert showed that material from a maturing oocyte could induce maturation after transfer into a recipient oocyte that had not received the original hormonal stimulus. The transferable activity was called maturation-promoting factor, or MPF. Later the name M-phase-promoting factor was used for the related activity that promotes entry into M phase.

The logic of cytoplasmic transfer is more important than the name. If a recipient undergoes characteristic nuclear changes after receiving cytoplasm from a maturing donor, a cytoplasmic activity can act downstream of the initial stimulus. An appropriate comparison is transfer of cytoplasm from an immature donor under comparable handling conditions. Such a comparison separates a maturation-associated activity from a nonspecific response to injection. It does not, however, identify the molecule in the active preparation: cytoplasm contains many proteins and small molecules. Biochemical separation and genetic experiments were needed to establish the identity of MPF.

A different approach began with yeast cells. A temperature-sensitive mutant can make a protein that works sufficiently well at a permissive temperature but loses its function at a restrictive temperature. When such cells grow normally at the first temperature and repeatedly fail at a similar cell-cycle stage after the shift, their common failure suggests a gene product required for that transition. Leland Hartwell and colleagues used this strategy in budding yeast to identify cell-division-cycle genes, including CDC28. The accumulation of a characteristic budding morphology was a clue to the stage affected; it was not proof that the gene encoded an enzyme acting directly on the bud. Wild-type cells exposed to the same temperature provide an important comparison because temperature itself can alter cell physiology.

In fission yeast, Paul Nurse and coworkers identified cdc2 as a key regulator. The elongated shape of certain mutants reflects continued cell growth without timely division; other alleles that reduce the restraint on mitosis can cause cells to divide at unusually small size. These findings separated growth from division and helped establish that kinase control was a conserved organizing principle. The budding-yeast Cdc28 and fission-yeast Cdc2 proteins belong to the CDK family; the corresponding mammalian mitotic kinase is CDK1. Functional conservation does not imply that every regulatory partner has the same name or that different organisms regulate division using identical upstream signals.

Tim Hunt and colleagues studied protein synthesis in early marine invertebrate embryos. During the rapid division cycles of fertilized eggs, radioactive amino acid labeling followed by electrophoretic separation revealed that a particular protein accumulated and then abruptly disappeared in relation to division. The periodic behavior was striking because many proteins did not undergo the same disappearance. The observation, published by Evans and colleagues in 1983, gave rise to the name cyclin. A pulse or continuous-labeling experiment can distinguish some aspects of synthesis and loss, but the intensity of a band alone depends on labeling, detection and loading; it does not by itself measure kinase activity or establish causation.

Subsequent biochemical studies connected the cyclin and genetic observations. Purified MPF contained a Cdc2-related catalytic subunit and cyclin B, its regulatory partner. Addition of active material to an appropriate recipient can induce M-phase changes, whereas removal or inhibition of the corresponding activity can prevent them. Together, these independent approaches explain why the same problem could be recognized as a transferable activity, a genetic requirement and an oscillating protein. The discoveries by Hartwell, Nurse and Hunt were recognized with the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The important inference is not that a single protein oscillates everywhere in exactly the same way, but that periodic regulation of a kinase can organize cell-cycle transitions.

One biochemical readout used in classic cell-cycle studies is histone H1 phosphorylation. Histone H1 is a chromatin-associated protein that can serve as a substrate in a kinase assay. If a fraction of egg extract phosphorylates histone H1 strongly only during M phase, the result identifies an M-phase-associated kinase activity. If cyclin co-purifies with that activity or removal of cyclin from the fraction reduces activity, the results support the idea of a cyclin-associated kinase. A comparison with inactive fractions and measurement of equal amounts of recovered protein are essential; a histone H1 assay is not inherently specific for CDK1 in every biological preparation. The convergence of association, kinase activity and reconstitution is more informative than an oscillating protein band alone. In particular, a protein that disappears during division might simply be a downstream substrate of mitosis. Demonstrating that active cyclin-dependent kinase can induce mitotic changes establishes a causal role rather than merely a correlation with the phase.

3. What a cyclin-dependent kinase actually does

A protein kinase catalyzes the transfer of a phosphoryl group, typically from ATP, onto an amino acid residue in a target protein. Cell-cycle CDKs are principally serine/threonine kinases. The hydroxyl oxygen of an appropriate serine or threonine becomes linked to the phosphoryl group; ADP is released. ATP is thus a chemical substrate for catalysis, not a message indicating that the cell has entered a particular phase. The kinase is regenerated after each catalytic cycle and can act on many substrate molecules. A phosphatase can hydrolyze the phosphate ester and release inorganic phosphate, thereby reversing the modification without reversing the original ATP-consuming reaction.

The addition of a negatively charged group can alter the shape, binding partners, subcellular location or activity of a protein. For instance, phosphorylation of proteins involved in the nuclear lamina helps dismantle a nuclear support structure during mitotic entry in cells with open mitosis. A kinase need not physically pull the nuclear envelope apart; changing the interactions among structural proteins allows a collective reorganization. Other targets respond in different ways: phosphorylation can activate an enzyme, inhibit it, or generate a recognition site for a protein that targets it for destruction. The biological effect must be established for the particular site and substrate.

An unbound cell-cycle CDK usually has an active-site arrangement that is poorly suited for productive substrate binding. Its activation segment, often called the T-loop, and nearby structural elements influence access to the catalytic region. Binding of an appropriate cyclin reorganizes this region and provides surfaces that help select particular substrates. The cyclin is therefore both an allosteric regulator and, in many cases, a contributor to target recruitment. Cyclin association is not simply a means of increasing the concentration of the CDK; an unchanged number of kinase molecules can acquire a new ability to phosphorylate targets.

Activation-loop phosphorylation by a CDK-activating kinase can further stabilize a catalytically competent conformation. In the familiar mammalian model, CAK contains CDK7, cyclin H and MAT1. The activating residue is Thr161 in CDK1 and Thr160 in CDK2. The exact requirement and regulatory arrangement vary among CDKs and organisms. These examples are especially useful because they prevent a common false generalization: phosphorylation of a CDK does not necessarily inhibit it. The outcome depends on the site and on what the phosphate does to the enzyme.

A substrate's amino acid sequence and its interaction with the cyclin–CDK complex help determine whether it will be phosphorylated. Many CDK sites include a serine or threonine followed by proline, but the presence of such a motif does not guarantee phosphorylation in a living cell. Docking interactions, accessibility of the site, location of the enzyme and opposition by phosphatases all matter. Consequently, a change in the identity of a cyclin may alter both the magnitude and the range of kinase action. A kinase assay performed with an artificial substrate measures activity under specified conditions; it cannot automatically reveal which endogenous proteins are modified in a cell.

4. Several independent controls determine CDK activity

Imagine collecting three G2-phase samples with equal amounts of CDK1 protein. In the first, cyclin B is absent; in the second, cyclin B is bound but the complex carries inhibitory phosphorylation; in the third, the complex is appropriately activated and the inhibitory sites are unmodified. An immunoblot for total CDK1 could look identical for all three samples, yet their ability to trigger mitotic target phosphorylation would differ. The relevant variable is the concentration of catalytically competent complex, not merely the quantity of one component.

Cyclin binding is one layer. An available cyclin must bind a compatible CDK; excess cyclin cannot create additional active complexes once every available compatible kinase is occupied. CAK-mediated activation-loop phosphorylation is another layer. In the classic CDK1–cyclin B model, inhibitory phosphorylation near the ATP-binding region provides a third layer: Wee1 chiefly modifies Tyr15, while the related Myt1 kinase can modify Thr14 and Tyr15 in vertebrate systems. The presence of the activating phosphate on Thr161 does not cancel the inhibition at Tyr15. A complex can carry both modifications and remain inactive.

Cdc25 is a phosphatase that removes the inhibitory phosphates from CDK1. This is a particularly instructive example because dephosphorylation increases kinase activity. Cdc25 does not synthesize cyclin B, does not replace CDK1 and does not remove the activating T-loop phosphate as its relevant mitotic function. Instead, it releases a restraint on complexes already available for activation. The cell can therefore accumulate mitotic cyclin during interphase and hold much of the potential mitotic activity in reserve. Once the conditions for entry are met, activation can occur rapidly without waiting for an entirely new protein population to be synthesized.

Inhibitor proteins offer an additional mode of control. An inhibitor bound to an otherwise suitable complex may obstruct catalysis or substrate recognition. Protein localization also matters: if a kinase is confined away from its targets, a lysate may display activity that was not available to those targets in vivo. Finally, even when a kinase is active, the amount of phosphorylated substrate depends on how rapidly phosphatases remove the modification. Thus neither a kinase assay nor a substrate phosphorylation measurement is a universal substitute for the other. The first tells us about catalytic potential under assay conditions; the second integrates at least the opposing rates and cellular context.

This layered control explains why a graph of cyclin concentration can rise gradually while a graph of mitotic CDK activity rises abruptly. The protein accumulates first, the inhibitory restraint is released later, and a population of complexes becomes active together. A student who understands these layers can reason through mutants without memorizing a separate answer for each one: first ask whether cyclin is present, then whether a compatible complex forms, then whether activating modifications and inhibitory restraints permit catalysis, and finally whether the relevant substrates are accessible.

It is helpful to distinguish the state of the kinase from the state of its target by imagining a constant pool of target proteins. When the kinase phosphorylates more target molecules per unit time than phosphatases dephosphorylate, the phosphorylated fraction rises; when dephosphorylation becomes faster, it falls. At a steady phosphorylation fraction the two fluxes can be equal while both reactions continue. Thus a drug that inhibits an opposing phosphatase can increase a mitotic phosphoprotein signal without increasing CDK catalytic activity. Conversely, an increase in kinase activity can produce little change in a particular target if an opposing phosphatase remains very active or the target is inaccessible. During mitotic entry and exit, regulation of substrate-directed phosphatases contributes to the sharp transitions seen in cells, alongside CDK regulation itself. The simplified Wee1–Cdc25 diagram describes regulation of CDK1's own inhibitory sites; it does not represent all the phosphatases acting on its many substrates.

5. Phase-specific cyclins and CDKs in different organisms

Cells can use successive kinase activities to give different groups of proteins permission to act at different times. The conventional names G1-CDK, G1/S-CDK, S-CDK and M-CDK refer to a functional window, not a set of universally conserved protein names. In a typical mammalian somatic cell, D-type cyclins work with CDK4 and CDK6 in G1. Cyclin E associated with CDK2 contributes to the late-G1/S transition. Cyclin A associated with CDK2 contributes to S phase and later can associate with CDK1. B-type cyclins associated with CDK1 provide major mitotic activity. These windows overlap, and a complex may perform more than one function.

Cyclin D is especially connected to external proliferation signals. Its transcription, translation, turnover and assembly can respond to signals received by the cell. This allows a cell to link a decision to proliferate with environmental conditions rather than moving through G1 simply because time has elapsed. Cyclin E and cyclin A have a stronger association with the transition into DNA synthesis and progression through interphase. The accumulation of cyclin B creates the capacity for mitotic activity but, as discussed above, does not guarantee that mitosis has begun.

Budding yeast illustrates an alternative use of the same basic principle. One major cell-cycle kinase, Cdc28, associates with Cln cyclins during G1 and Clb cyclins in later phases. The Clb family contains members that contribute preferentially to DNA replication and others that contribute more strongly to mitosis. Fission yeast uses Cdc2 and cyclins including Cdc13, a major B-type cyclin, with additional cyclins contributing to progression. Plant cells have CDKA and plant-specific CDKB family members and several classes of cyclins. Their regulatory system integrates developmental and environmental cues; it should not be described by simply replacing plant protein names with the mammalian list.

Even among mammalian cells the conventional pairing chart is a model, not an absolute statement of genetic necessity. Experiments reported by Santamaría and colleagues in 2007 found that CDK1 could support the mammalian cell cycle in particular genetic contexts lacking the major interphase CDKs. This does not imply that those other kinases have no functions in an intact organism, nor that all tissues can dispense with them. It demonstrates that cyclin binding and substrate overlap allow compensatory behavior. For examination questions, identify whether the question asks for the normal preferred partner or the outcome in a specially engineered mutant. The answers need not be identical.

The most transferable idea is that specificity can be achieved through the choice of partner, the amount of activity, substrate properties and the timing of that activity. A cell with one major catalytic kinase and multiple partners is not necessarily less regulated than a cell with several kinases. Conversely, a list of many CDK proteins does not prove that each one controls an independent obligatory switch.

6. From extracellular signals to the G1/S transition

A cell in a multicellular organism should not interpret the mere presence of DNA replication enzymes as a command to divide. Growth factors, also called mitogens when they promote entry into the cycle, can stimulate signaling pathways that support synthesis and assembly of D-type cyclins. The detailed receptor pathways are not required here. What matters is the conversion of an extracellular input into increased G1-CDK activity, and from that activity into altered transcription of genes needed for DNA synthesis.

The retinoblastoma protein, Rb, illustrates this conversion. In a familiar simplified mammalian model, a low-phosphorylation form of Rb restrains E2F-dependent transcription. E2F refers to a family of transcription factors; the activator E2Fs promote expression of genes whose products support S phase. Rb binds to these factors and helps establish a transcriptionally repressive state. If the E2F-dependent genes are not expressed sufficiently, simply supplying nucleotides is unlikely to move a normal quiescent cell into a coordinated S phase.

Mitogenic signaling increases the availability of cyclin D–CDK4/6. Changes in Rb phosphorylation and associated regulatory events weaken the Rb-mediated restraint. Increasing E2F-dependent expression helps produce proteins including cyclin E and other S-phase components. Cyclin E–CDK2 activity can reinforce the transition through additional Rb phosphorylation and by acting on other substrates. This arrangement produces reinforcing interactions: a modest initiating signal promotes changes that increase the ability of the cell to proceed further. The precise sequence of Rb modifications varies among models and cell types; the essential exam-level conclusion is that the cyclin–CDK system can convert extracellular signals into a sustained change in transcriptional state.

The classical restriction point is defined operationally by the observation that cells in early G1 often require continued mitogenic stimulation, whereas cells that have progressed sufficiently far can continue toward division after mitogen withdrawal. This is not a clock mark that every cell passes at exactly the same moment. A population can contain cells with differing histories, and some mammalian cells make important commitment decisions in the preceding cell cycle. The next node considers checkpoint and restriction-point logic in detail. Here the crucial link is that increased CDK activity and reinforcing transcription can make a short-lived external signal have a longer-lived cellular consequence.

A strong G1/S activity is not the only condition for DNA replication. Origins must have been prepared earlier, nucleotides and replication factors must be available, and inhibitory responses can still intervene. Removing Rb, for example, can reduce one transcriptional restraint without abolishing every other barrier to S phase. A useful prediction follows: if Rb is the principal relevant downstream target in a specified experimental context, a CDK4/6 inhibitor may have less effect in cells lacking functional Rb than in otherwise comparable Rb-positive cells. That prediction is conditional on the model and is not a universal statement about every tissue or drug.

7. CDK inhibitors: separating a brake from the accelerator

An increase in cyclin expression has little effect if the resulting complex cannot act. Mammalian cells contain two familiar families of CDK inhibitor proteins (CKIs). INK4 proteins, such as p16, act mainly on CDK4 and CDK6; the Cip/Kip family includes p21, p27 and p57. The term inhibitor describes a regulatory function rather than a universal chemical mechanism. Binding of an INK4 protein changes the ability of CDK4/6 to cooperate with D-type cyclins. Cip/Kip proteins can bind cyclin–CDK complexes and are well-known inhibitors of cyclin E/A–CDK2 activity.

The name p21 is often encountered in DNA-damage questions because p53-dependent transcription can increase p21 abundance. The accumulated p21 can bind relevant cyclin–CDK complexes and reduce their activity. The cell then has less ability to phosphorylate targets necessary for progression. If p21 increases before S entry, a delay in G1 is a common prediction. However, p21 can act at other stages and has other functions; a question must specify the phase and biological context. The full p53 response is part of the next chapter, rather than the main subject here.

p27 provides an example of how abundance of an inhibitor can vary with growth state. A quiescent cell may contain relatively high inhibitory capacity, limiting cyclin E–CDK2. Mitogen-dependent changes can promote accumulation of D-type cyclin complexes, alter how p27 is distributed among complexes, and favor its removal. Phosphorylation of p27 on Thr187 by active CDK2 can facilitate recognition by the SCF complex containing Skp2 in an appropriate setting. Destruction of p27 releases further CDK2 activity, generating a reinforcing interaction. This is not a reason to assume that phosphorylation of every CKI sends it to the proteasome; the target recognition step is selective.

An important qualification is that p21 and p27 can assist the assembly and stabilization of cyclin D–CDK4/6 complexes in some contexts, and their effect on those complexes can depend on stoichiometry and modification state. Thus it is scientifically inaccurate to claim that every molecule named a CDK inhibitor necessarily suppresses every CDK it encounters. The textbook distinction remains useful: p16 is relatively specific for CDK4/6, while p21 and p27 commonly restrain CDK2-containing complexes. In interpreting a problem about cyclin D–CDK4, read the experimental assumptions rather than applying a blanket inhibitory label.

Because CKIs act through association and turnover, total inhibitor abundance does not necessarily reveal the amount of free inhibitory protein. A cell with a large pool of inhibitor sequestered in one complex may behave differently from a cell with the same total amount available to bind another complex. An immunoprecipitation experiment, in which a specific protein is isolated together with its associated partners, can help reveal this distinction. Co-isolation establishes association under the conditions of the experiment, but catalytic inhibition still needs an activity assay or a suitable functional readout.

8. How S-phase CDK activity supports one round of DNA replication

The start of S phase presents a problem that cannot be solved merely by switching DNA polymerases on. Eukaryotic chromosomes generally possess many potential origins of replication. If an origin that has already fired could freely prepare itself and fire again within the same cycle, some regions might be copied more than once. If origins failed to fire or neighboring forks failed to complete the work, other regions could remain unreplicated. The control system therefore separates origin preparation, called licensing, from the activation of licensed origins, called firing.

When CDK activity is low, especially in late mitosis and G1, the origin recognition complex and associated factors including Cdc6 and Cdt1 can cooperate to load MCM helicase complexes onto DNA. A helicase is an enzyme that separates the paired DNA strands during replication. Loading the helicase at an origin establishes the capacity for later initiation; loading alone does not mean that the helicase is actively unwinding DNA or that a replication fork has formed. The loaded, inactive machinery is a licensed origin.

At the transition into S phase, S-CDK and another kinase activity called DDK (Dbf4-dependent kinase) promote the activation of licensed replication origins. Their targets contribute to the recruitment or activation of initiation factors and the functional helicase. Once an origin has fired, the active helicase travels with replication forks and the original licensed state is consumed. Replication from neighboring origins can also pass through potential origins that did not fire independently. It is therefore more precise to say that a particular DNA segment should be replicated once than to claim every licensed origin must fire once.

S-CDK activity also helps prevent formation of new licenses while DNA synthesis proceeds. Cells achieve this using a combination of controls on licensing proteins, their localization or degradation, and inhibitors of licensing factors. In metazoans the protein geminin inhibits Cdt1 during much of S and G2; its removal at mitotic exit contributes to a new opportunity for licensing. Different eukaryotes use partly different mechanisms, so geminin should not be treated as a mandatory universal feature. The underlying logic is shared: the same state that permits firing should restrict reloading of the machinery.

Suppose a cell is allowed to license origins in G1 but S-CDK is then inhibited before the normal start of S phase. Licensed origins can remain ready without efficiently initiating replication. In a different experiment, if an already replicated chromosome is exposed to abnormal relicensing while conditions for firing persist, DNA may be copied again. These perturbations can both damage proliferation, but they are mechanistically different. The first is failure to start replication; the second is failure to limit replication to one round. The later DNA replication unit develops the individual enzymes and molecular assembly steps in detail.

The low CDK state after mitotic exit is consequently a necessary part of the cell cycle, not a wasteful pause. It makes the next round possible by resetting the control of origins. Constantly high kinase activity could prevent this reset; constantly low kinase activity could allow preparation without firing. The transition between these states is essential for both initiation and prevention of rereplication.

9. The G2/M switch: why entry can be abrupt

During S and G2, a cell accumulates mitotic cyclins. Yet it must not begin chromosome segregation merely because a small amount of cyclin B is present. Inhibitory phosphorylation of CDK1 allows a pool of complexes to accumulate before a rapid activation event. Once some Cdc25 molecules are active and remove inhibitory phosphates, a fraction of CDK1–cyclin B becomes catalytically competent. Active CDK1 in turn contributes to the activation of Cdc25 and inhibition of Wee1 through regulatory phosphorylation in the classic model. The first change promotes more release from inhibitory phosphorylation; the second reduces the rate at which that inhibition is restored.

The two feedback routes reinforce one another. Cdc25 activates CDK1; CDK1 helps activate Cdc25. Wee1 inhibits CDK1; CDK1 helps inhibit Wee1. The latter is sometimes called a double-negative feedback loop because inhibiting an inhibitor has a positive effect on the original activity. With sufficient cyclin and a favorable regulatory balance, a modest increase in activity can accelerate further increases. This offers a mechanistic explanation for why mitotic entry can be much sharper than the underlying change in total cyclin amount.

A switch can also exhibit hysteresis, meaning that its current state depends on its previous state. In a simplified bistable model, the cyclin concentration necessary to turn a kinase system on when starting from a low-activity state can exceed the concentration at which an already active system turns off. This is not merely another word for a steep response: a steep single-valued curve can still assign the same activity to a particular concentration regardless of history. Bistability requires two stable states in a range of inputs, with the actual state depending on the starting condition. Positive feedback can help produce this behavior but does not guarantee it for every set of reaction rates.

Experiments with Xenopus egg extracts have demonstrated history-dependent CDK1 activation in an experimentally tractable system. To test such a prediction, one can compare a preparation starting in interphase with another starting in mitosis at equivalent controlled cyclin amounts. A higher threshold for entry than for maintaining M phase supports hysteresis. Appropriate interpretation requires control of the preparation, the amount of cyclin and the marker used to infer mitosis. A single time-course curve in which kinase rises steeply is insufficient on its own to prove hysteresis.

Once mitotic CDK activity is high, target phosphorylation coordinates several visible events. In cells with open mitosis, phosphorylation of nuclear lamins contributes to nuclear envelope disassembly. Mitotic kinase activity also helps establish conditions for chromosome condensation and spindle reorganization, together with other enzymes and structural factors. CDK1 does not itself constitute the spindle or directly sever all cohesin. Its role is to change regulatory and structural targets so that these events can occur in a coordinated manner. The mechanisms of chromosome behavior and physical division are developed in 2.D.04 and 2.D.05.

If Wee1 function is reduced in a cell where its inhibition is a major brake, mitotic CDK activity may rise too early. In fission yeast, early division can yield unusually small cells because the interval for growth has been shortened. If Cdc25 activity is severely reduced, mitotic entry can be delayed while cells continue to grow. Those are predictions about a specified regulatory system, not universal morphological diagnoses: a mammalian cell with additional safeguards may arrest or die instead of showing the same shape as fission yeast.

The 2003 study by Sha and colleagues tested this logic using frog egg extracts. They controlled cyclin availability and compared the cyclin requirement of preparations with different starting cell-cycle states. The cyclin level needed to induce mitosis exceeded the level needed to maintain an established mitotic state. This is direct evidence for history-dependent switching in that experimental system. The investigators also found that unfinished DNA replication changed the activation requirement, illustrating how a surveillance input can act on the switch rather than replacing it. The extract is a simplified preparation rather than an intact tissue, so the experiment is evidence for the regulatory principle, not a measurement of the thresholds of every mammalian cell. 10. Why selective protein destruction drives the cycle forward

Phosphorylation is reversible and can modify an existing protein quickly. For some transitions, however, the control system benefits from removing a regulator entirely. A cell can then stay in the new state even if the previous activating signal briefly returns. To regain the destroyed regulator it must make more protein. Selective proteolysis thus provides a different type of directionality from the rapid exchange of phosphate groups.

Ubiquitin is a small protein that can be attached covalently to other proteins. An activating enzyme, E1, uses ATP to prepare ubiquitin. A conjugating enzyme, E2, carries activated ubiquitin, while an E3 ubiquitin ligase contributes specificity for the substrate and promotes ubiquitin transfer. Repeated addition can produce a chain that is recognized by the proteasome, a large proteolytic complex that unfolds and degrades suitable substrates. Ubiquitin itself can be recycled. Not every ubiquitin modification means destruction; the nature of the modification and the recognition machinery determine the outcome. For cell-cycle ordering the relevant cases are those in which ubiquitylation promotes degradation.

The SCF complex contains Skp1, a Cullin scaffold and an F-box substrate-recognition protein, together with a RING component. Different F-box proteins recognize different substrates. In many classical cell-cycle examples, phosphorylation of a substrate creates or exposes a recognition motif, called a phosphodegron. This provides a link between kinase activity and protein removal. In budding yeast, phosphorylation of the inhibitor Sic1 contributes to its recognition by SCF containing Cdc4; its degradation releases S-phase CDK activity. In mammalian cells SCF containing Skp2 can target appropriately phosphorylated p27 with the assistance of recognition factors. These examples use different proteins but share a functional logic: a kinase helps remove a brake that otherwise limits further kinase activity.

The anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome, abbreviated APC/C, is another E3 ligase. Its cell-cycle activity and substrate selection depend on associated coactivators such as Cdc20 and Cdh1. APC/C recognizes short sequence motifs in certain substrates, including destruction boxes and KEN boxes, in a context-dependent manner. In contrast to the commonly taught SCF examples, the periodic activation and coactivator state of APC/C are major determinants of when its substrates are destroyed. This distinction is useful but is not absolute: SCF components and APC/C substrates can also be regulated in other ways.

It is crucial to distinguish tagging from degradation. Neither SCF nor APC/C is the protease that cleaves cyclin into peptides. They help attach ubiquitin to selected proteins, and the proteasome performs degradation. A mutation preventing recognition of a cyclin by APC/C can stabilize that specific cyclin even when the proteasome is intact. A proteasome inhibitor, in contrast, affects the turnover of numerous substrates, potentially producing a mixture of direct and indirect cell-cycle effects. The two perturbations can give superficially similar accumulation of cyclin but are not molecularly equivalent.

Selective destruction is also experimentally distinguishable from reduced synthesis. In a pulse–chase experiment a labeled pool of cyclin is followed after the labeling pulse ends. If the labeled pool decreases more slowly in a mutant than in control cells, with comparable sample loading and initial labeling, the result supports reduced turnover. An increase in total steady-state cyclin could instead arise from increased synthesis, so measuring total abundance alone cannot separate these possibilities. The decay of a previously labeled pool provides information about the fate of protein already made.

11. APC/C, anaphase and the return to low kinase activity

High mitotic CDK activity prepares the cell for segregation but cannot remain high indefinitely. The cell needs a way to begin sister chromatid separation and a way to reverse the phosphorylation state associated with mitosis. APC/C contributes to both. Its association with Cdc20 is regulated around the metaphase-to-anaphase transition; signals from unattached kinetochores can restrain APC/C–Cdc20 until chromosome attachment conditions are met. The detailed surveillance mechanism is reserved for 2.D.03, and the physical mechanics of separation for 2.D.05.

One important APC/C substrate is securin, which restrains the protease separase. When securin is appropriately ubiquitylated and degraded, separase can become active, subject to other restraints. Separase cleaves a component of the cohesin complex that holds sister chromatids together. Removing this linkage permits sisters to separate under the forces generated by the spindle. The ubiquitin ligase therefore initiates a regulatory sequence rather than directly cutting chromosome cohesion. In vertebrate cells, mitotic cyclin–CDK can also contribute to separase inhibition, so elimination of securin need not represent the only regulatory input.

Another important substrate is mitotic cyclin B. Its destruction reduces the concentration of CDK1–cyclin B complexes and hence the ability to maintain the mitotic phosphorylation state. Phosphatases such as PP1 and PP2A then help reverse modifications of mitotic substrates. The nuclear envelope can reform in cells with open mitosis, chromosome organization changes and other late mitotic events proceed. The precise timing of individual substrates differs, and destruction of cyclin B need not wait until every chromosome has arrived at a spindle pole. What matters is the coordinated decline of mitotic activity during exit.

There is a striking logic here: the regulatory machinery responsible for establishing M phase contributes to activation of a system that eventually removes the mitotic cyclin. This creates delayed negative feedback. Positive feedback helps make entry decisive; subsequent negative feedback helps terminate the high-activity state. The timing of destruction depends on the state of the APC/C system and substrate recognition rather than on a fixed chemical lifetime of every cyclin molecule.

APC/C associated with Cdh1 is important in maintaining low mitotic cyclin abundance during late mitosis and G1 in many systems. High CDK activity can restrain Cdh1-dependent APC/C function; decreasing kinase activity at mitotic exit helps release that restraint. This reinforces the low-CDK state. As the next cell cycle proceeds, APC/C–Cdh1 activity is reduced and interphase cyclins can accumulate; the detailed mechanisms differ among organisms. The point is that both high- and low-activity states can be maintained by feedback rather than depending on a continuously supplied external command.

Consider a cell expressing a cyclin B variant that still activates CDK1 but lacks an effective degradation signal. It may enter mitosis normally if cyclin accumulation and activation are otherwise intact. When APC/C is activated, normal securin and other susceptible substrates can still be destroyed, but the mutant cyclin B persists. Continued mitotic kinase activity can prevent orderly exit or delay completion of division. This is a different prediction from blocking all APC/C function: the broader block can also prevent securin loss and sister separation. The specific mutant thus separates the two branches of APC/C action and demonstrates why removal of mitotic activity is necessary in its own right.

The transition also explains the behavior of a continuously supplied mitotic cyclin in a simplified oscillator. While cyclin production is greater than its loss, potential kinase activity accumulates. Once kinase activation and APC/C regulation lead to rapid substrate destruction, cyclin loss can temporarily exceed production; kinase activity then falls despite continued synthesis. As the low-activity state is re-established, the machinery that destroys mitotic cyclin can eventually become less active, depending on the particular cycle. Continued synthesis can again build a cyclin pool. The delay between activation and destruction is important: a feedback mechanism that removed every cyclin molecule immediately upon synthesis could prevent the high-activity phase from developing. Early embryonic extracts illustrate this principle especially well; many somatic cells superimpose growth-dependent decisions and checkpoint controls on it.

12. Reading cyclin abundance and kinase-activity time courses

A time-course graph can test whether a proposed control mechanism fits the sequence of events. First identify what is on the vertical axis. An immunoblot band quantified against a loading control measures relative protein abundance. A kinase assay measures the rate of phosphate transfer under the chosen conditions. A phosphorylation-specific antibody measures a particular modified form of a protein, not necessarily the total amount of the protein. These three measurements can display different curves even when they are made from the same samples.

In the illustrative graph below, cyclin B accumulates during S and G2, reaches a high value around early mitosis and declines during exit. Total CDK1 is drawn approximately constant, whereas activity remains low until late G2 and then rises steeply. This idealized shape is consistent with cyclin accumulation followed by relief of inhibition. The later decline in activity is consistent with cyclin destruction. A student should not infer from the graph alone that Wee1, Cdc25 or APC/C is responsible. To distinguish those mechanisms experimentally one would need to measure the relevant modifications and compare appropriately controlled perturbations.

Now imagine that a second curve is obtained after treatment with a Wee1 inhibitor in a model cell where Wee1 provides the dominant inhibitory input. If kinase activity rises earlier than in controls while the cyclin curve changes little, that suggests the existing complexes have been activated sooner. Measuring inhibitory Tyr15 phosphorylation would provide a further test. Alternatively, if the cyclin curve declines normally but the kinase assay remains high, one should consider whether another cyclin partner contributes to the assay, whether the assay is specific for CDK1 and whether the protein measurement captures every relevant cyclin species. An unexpected curve is a reason to test assumptions, not to force every result into a familiar textbook diagram.

Bulk time courses have another limitation: cells in an asynchronous population are at different stages. A mean protein abundance can conceal sharp changes occurring in individual cells. Synchronizing cells or following single cells can help distinguish an intrinsically gradual response from an abrupt response that occurs at different times in different cells. Synchronization itself may disturb cycle progression, so an untreated population and a method appropriate to the question remain useful comparisons.

DNA-content measurement provides complementary information. A 2C population enriched after a G1 perturbation is consistent with failure to enter S phase; a 4C population enriched after inhibiting Cdc25 is consistent with failure after replication. However, a 4C peak does not distinguish G2 from M. To distinguish these, one may use chromosome morphology, mitotic markers such as phosphorylated histone H3 in the appropriate context, or nuclear-envelope behavior. Even a mitotic marker alone may not distinguish prometaphase from anaphase. Combining molecular activity, DNA content and structural observations gives a much stronger interpretation than any one readout.

13. Quantitative reasoning: binding, activity and turnover

Quantitative questions in this topic usually ask for interpretation of a small set of measurements rather than derivation of a universal cell-cycle equation. A useful starting point is stoichiometry. In a simplified model in which each cyclin–CDK complex contains one cyclin and one kinase, a sample with 80 nM cyclin B and 120 nM compatible CDK1 can form at most 80 nM complexes. This is an upper bound, not an equilibrium prediction, because incomplete association or competition for partners can reduce the actual value. If 60 nM complexes are observed, the fraction of all CDK1 in those complexes is 60/120 = 0.50, or 50%, while the fraction of cyclin B in those complexes is 60/80 = 0.75, or 75%. The denominators differ because the question refers to different protein pools.

A second problem separates binding from activation. Suppose 80 nM of complexes have actually formed, and measurements show that 70% of them carry inhibitory phosphorylation. If every other complex is catalytically competent and no additional restraint is present, the concentration of active complexes is 80 × (1 − 0.70) = 24 nM. If inhibitory phosphorylation falls to 20% without a change in total complex concentration, the active concentration becomes 80 × 0.80 = 64 nM. The fold increase is 64/24 = 2.67, whereas the increase in percentage points of the active fraction is 80 − 30 = 50. This comparison explains how activity can change substantially without additional cyclin synthesis. The assumptions must be stated: the assay must distinguish the inhibitory site, and absence of that phosphate must correspond to catalytic competence in the example.

An enzyme assay can be interpreted in the same way. If a normalized preparation transfers 12 nmol of phosphate in four minutes under initial-rate conditions, its rate is 3 nmol per minute. If another transfers 30 nmol in four minutes with equal sample protein, sufficient ATP and substrate, and otherwise identical conditions, its rate is 7.5 nmol per minute and the ratio is 2.5. This ratio reports activity under those conditions. It cannot distinguish a 2.5-fold increase in active enzyme concentration from a change in catalytic behavior per active complex unless additional measurements are made.

Protein turnover can be studied with a simple first-order approximation. Let C denote the concentration of an existing cyclin pool in nM, and let k be a positive first-order degradation constant in min⁻¹. If synthesis of that pool is absent and the removal rate is proportional to C, then dC/dt = −kC. Separating variables gives dC/C = −k dt. Integrating from initial concentration C₀ at time zero to C at time t yields ln(C/C₀) = −kt. Exponentiation gives C = C₀e−kt. This is a model for a defined decay interval, not a claim that APC/C-dependent cyclin loss is always first order.

At the half-life t½, C/C₀ = 1/2, hence −ln 2 = −kt½ and t½ = ln 2/k. Suppose a pulse-labeled cyclin pool drops from 160 nM to 40 nM in 12 min. The remaining fraction is 1/4, equivalent to two half-lives. Thus t½ = 6 min and k = 0.693/6 = 0.1155 min⁻¹. After 18 min, three half-lives have elapsed, giving 160/8 = 20 nM. The calculation predicts the remaining labeled pool under the assumed constant degradation rate; a real time course with abrupt ligase activation or saturable degradation need not follow that equation throughout.

If synthesis and degradation both occur continuously at constant rates in a different simplified model, the net rate is dC/dt = s − kC, where s is production in nM min⁻¹. At steady state the net rate is zero, so C* = s/k. If s = 10 nM min⁻¹ and k = 0.20 min⁻¹, the steady-state concentration is 50 nM. Halving k while leaving synthesis unchanged gives 100 nM. This is an equilibrium of fluxes, not an absence of turnover. A cell-cycle cyclin may never reach that steady state because both s and k can vary as the cycle progresses, but the relation helps explain why inhibiting degradation can raise concentration without increasing transcription.

Finally consider a hypothetical threshold experiment. A controlled extract starts with low CDK activity; activity becomes high only when cyclin reaches 60 nM. An extract starting with high activity retains that state until cyclin falls below 30 nM. At 45 nM, the first extract can be low and the second high. The interval from 30 to 60 nM illustrates history dependence. These numbers are teaching values, not measurements. A single sigmoidal concentration–response relation would assign a unique activity to 45 nM and could not explain the specified observations without an additional state variable.

14. Using perturbations to infer which regulatory layer has failed

Consider a model culture in which total cyclin B is normal but cells accumulate with a 4C DNA content and low mitotic CDK activity. A first hypothesis is excess inhibitory phosphorylation. An immunoblot specific for phosphorylated Tyr15 of CDK1, interpreted relative to total CDK1, can test whether that modified form is enriched. If it is enriched, the result is compatible with increased Wee1 function or reduced Cdc25 function, among other causes. It does not by itself distinguish those alternatives. A matched functional assay of each regulator or a controlled rescue experiment can help resolve them.

Suppose an otherwise comparable cell expresses a CDK1 variant in which the relevant inhibitory residues cannot accept phosphorylation. If the variant is expressed at an appropriate level and remains able to bind cyclin and catalyze phosphorylation, it may bypass the need for Cdc25 to remove those particular inhibitory phosphates. It would not bypass a requirement for cyclin binding, activating phosphorylation or other restrictions. The result places the effect of Cdc25 upstream of the inhibitory sites in this model. A catalytic-dead variant is a useful negative control because simply supplying more CDK1 protein should not substitute for enzyme activity.

Conditional mutants help distinguish direct and secondary defects. A cell held for a long time in an abnormal state can change transcription, protein turnover and metabolism. Comparing a short interval after a controlled loss of kinase function with later outcomes helps identify which changes appear earliest. Even the earliest measured change may be indirect if the true target is not measured, so temporal order supports but does not prove direct phosphorylation. Demonstrating modification of a purified target by a kinase in vitro can establish biochemical capacity; a complementary cellular perturbation is needed to establish biological relevance.

A rescue experiment is particularly powerful when it tests a defined causal prediction. If a G1 delay arises because an inhibitor remains bound to cyclin E–CDK2, reducing that inhibitor might restore activity. If the kinase catalytic site itself is inactive, removing the inhibitor cannot restore the catalytic reaction. If the relevant replication factors are absent downstream, even an active CDK may not restore DNA synthesis. Thus failure of a rescue can reflect an additional requirement rather than disproving every aspect of the proposed pathway. The strongest inference comes from combining a defined genetic change, a measurement of the affected activity and an independent readout of the cellular event.

Several controls recur in these experiments. Equal protein loading is necessary for comparison of immunoblot bands; immunoprecipitated kinase amounts should be measured when comparing specific activity; a no-enzyme or catalytically inactive comparison helps estimate background in kinase assays; and cell viability should be monitored when interpreting a failure to progress. A toxic treatment can reduce DNA synthesis for many reasons. A phase-specific accumulation, a matching molecular change and a targeted rescue collectively make a more convincing case for a cell-cycle regulatory mechanism than growth inhibition alone.

For a concrete hypothetical comparison, consider three equal-protein lysates: untreated cells show a high CDK1-associated kinase signal, treated cells show a low signal, and treated cells carrying a suitable inhibitory-site-resistant CDK1 show restored activity. Suppose the treated cells retain the same cyclin B abundance as the control. This pattern supports regulation of the inhibitory phosphorylation layer rather than a simple deficiency in cyclin synthesis. To strengthen the conclusion, quantify the kinase recovered from each lysate and test whether the rescued cells actually complete the relevant transition. A kinase signal restored in vitro is not sufficient evidence for successful division: chromosome segregation or another necessary process could still be defective. Conversely, an apparent rescue of DNA content alone may conceal abnormal chromosome inheritance. A molecular measurement and an independent cellular readout are complementary rather than interchangeable.

15. A complete regulatory cycle and its important exceptions

The central regulatory logic can now be followed from one division to the next. Mitotic cyclin loss permits a low-CDK state, which helps prepare replication origins and allows cells to respond to proliferation signals. Increasing G1-associated activity helps alter transcription, leading to greater S-phase competence. S-phase kinase activity both initiates replication and contributes to protection against relicensing. Mitotic cyclin can accumulate while inhibitory phosphorylation restrains premature entry. Once the activating and inhibitory inputs favor the switch, reinforcing interactions produce a rapid increase in mitotic activity. Finally, controlled protein destruction and phosphatase action terminate this state. The resulting low-CDK interval resets the system rather than immediately repeating a high-activity transition.

The directionality is not produced by one molecule acting as an autonomous clock independent of the cell. Cyclin production, degradation, target modification, growth conditions and surveillance signals are coupled. Early embryonic divisions provide an example of a different emphasis: many rapid early cycles alternate mainly between DNA synthesis and mitosis, without the lengthy G1 and G2 phases typical of many somatic cells. Stored maternal components and the regulation of cyclin accumulation and destruction help support such cycles. A timing mechanism identified in an egg extract can reveal a general regulatory principle without reproducing every feature of an adult mammalian cell.

In endocycles, certain differentiated cells replicate DNA without completing normal mitosis, generating increased DNA content. This occurs in particular developmental contexts, such as many plant tissues. Regulatory cycles that separate licensing and firing can continue even when mitotic activity is suppressed. The normal mitotic-cycle rule that S follows a preceding mitotic exit therefore has biologically important exceptions. The more fundamental requirement for a new replication round is establishment of a licensing-compatible state followed by a firing-compatible state, rather than an obligatory change of cell shape in every type of cycle. Unscheduled rereplication within a normal S phase is not the same as a developmentally regulated endocycle.

Another important distinction concerns growth and proliferation. Loss of a mitotic activator can prevent division without immediately blocking all biosynthesis; this accounts for elongated conditional mutants in appropriate yeast systems. Conversely, a cell may enter mitosis prematurely with too little time for growth. In animal tissues, cell-cycle regulation interacts with signals controlling growth and survival, but those are related processes rather than synonyms. A cell may stop dividing and remain metabolically active; a nondividing cell is not necessarily dead.

A final distinction is between CDK activity and a checkpoint. A checkpoint is a monitoring mechanism capable of delaying progression when a condition is unsatisfied. Cdc25 and Wee1 are targets through which some such signals act, but their normal contribution to the mitotic switch is not itself evidence that DNA damage has occurred. In an exam question, identify the perturbation, the molecular layer affected, the next cellular event that requires the affected activity, and the readout that would actually discriminate the proposed state. This approach allows the same regulatory framework to explain normal ordering, abnormal arrest and experimental rescue without memorizing isolated protein names.

16. Part B — concepts and assertion–reason

These are original practice questions. Choose the best answer under the stated assumptions before reading the explanation.

1. Which component catalyzes phosphorylation of a mitotic substrate?

  1. Cyclin B alone
  2. CDK1 within an active complex
  3. APC/C
  4. Cdc25
Answer and reasoning

B. CDK1 within an active complex

CDK1 is the kinase; cyclin B regulates it. APC/C is an E3 ligase, and Cdc25 removes inhibitory phosphates.

2. Which modification is activating in the classical CDK1 model?

  1. Tyr15 phosphorylation
  2. Thr14 phosphorylation
  3. Thr161 phosphorylation by CAK
  4. Ubiquitylation of cyclin B
Answer and reasoning

C. Thr161 phosphorylation by CAK

Thr161 supports catalytic activation. Tyr15 and Thr14 are inhibitory; cyclin destruction reduces M-CDK.

3. Which mammalian pairing is typical of late G1/S entry?

  1. Cyclin E–CDK2
  2. Cyclin B–CDK1
  3. Cyclin D–Cdc25
  4. Cyclin H–APC/C
Answer and reasoning

A. Cyclin E–CDK2

Cyclin E–CDK2 contributes to S entry; B–CDK1 is mitotic. Cdc25 is a phosphatase, and APC/C is not a kinase partner.

4. Which mechanism best explains a decline of cyclin B at mitotic exit?

  1. APC/C-mediated tagging followed by proteasomal degradation
  2. DNA replication by CDK1
  3. Wee1-mediated cyclin synthesis
  4. Cdc25 hydrolysis of cyclin
Answer and reasoning

A. APC/C-mediated tagging followed by proteasomal degradation

APC/C selects cyclin for degradation. CDK1 does not replicate DNA, Wee1 modifies kinase, and Cdc25 removes phosphates.

5. Which statement about SCF is most accurate?

  1. It is a cyclin protease
  2. It is a CDK
  3. It is an E3 ligase with interchangeable substrate-recognition F-box proteins
  4. It adds the activating phosphate to CDK1
Answer and reasoning

C. It is an E3 ligase with interchangeable substrate-recognition F-box proteins

SCF selects substrates for ubiquitylation. It neither cleaves them nor catalyzes protein phosphorylation.

6. In the standard model, when is origin licensing favored?

  1. When mitotic CDK activity is persistently maximal
  2. During a low-CDK interval
  3. Only after an origin has fired in S phase
  4. Only during anaphase chromosome movement
Answer and reasoning

B. During a low-CDK interval

Low CDK favors licensing. High activity helps prevent relicensing, and firing consumes the licensed state. Anaphase movement is not the defining requirement.

7. Which inference follows from a 4C DNA-content peak alone?

  1. All cells are in G2
  2. All cells are in metaphase
  3. Cells have approximately twice the DNA of G1 cells, but G2 and M are not distinguished
  4. All cells have doubled chromosome number
Answer and reasoning

C. Cells have approximately twice the DNA of G1 cells, but G2 and M are not distinguished

DNA content is not a stage-specific mitotic marker and does not by itself specify chromosome count.

8. Which statement about Cdc25 is correct?

  1. It synthesizes cyclin B
  2. It removes inhibitory CDK phosphorylation
  3. It is the protease that degrades securin
  4. It adds inhibitory Tyr15 phosphorylation
Answer and reasoning

B. It removes inhibitory CDK phosphorylation

Cdc25 is a phosphatase. Cyclin is synthesized by translation, the proteasome degrades securin, and Wee1 adds the Tyr15 phosphate.

9. Assertion: Total CDK1 can remain constant while kinase activity oscillates. Reason: Cyclin binding and reversible modification regulate the active fraction.

  1. Both true, reason explains assertion
  2. Both true, reason does not explain assertion
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

A. Both true, reason explains assertion

The active fraction can vary independently of total CDK1. Thus the reason directly explains the assertion and excludes the other combinations.

10. Assertion: All eukaryotes require mammalian CDK4 and CDK6 for G1. Reason: A single major kinase can associate with different cyclins in yeast.

  1. Both true
  2. Both false
  3. Assertion true, reason false
  4. Assertion false, reason true
Answer and reasoning

D. Assertion false, reason true

Yeast does not use the mammalian partner list, and Cdc28 can work with different cyclins. The assertion wrongly universalizes mammalian nomenclature.

17. Part C — experiments, perturbations and calculations

These are original practice questions. Choose the best answer under the stated assumptions before reading the explanation.

1. An extract has 150 nM CDK1 and 90 nM cyclin B. If 75 nM complexes form and 40% are inhibited, what is the active concentration?

  1. 30 nM
  2. 45 nM
  3. 75 nM
  4. 90 nM
Answer and reasoning

B. 45 nM

75 × 0.60 = 45 nM. 30 nM is the inhibited pool; 75 is total complex; 90 is total cyclin, not active kinase.

2. A labeled cyclin pool falls from 240 to 60 nM in 10 min with first-order decay. How much remains at 15 min?

  1. 120 nM
  2. 30 nM
  3. 40 nM
  4. 15 nM
Answer and reasoning

B. 30 nM

Two half-lives occur in 10 min, so each is 5 min. Three half-lives give 240/8 = 30 nM. The other values do not represent three successive halvings.

3. A Cdc25-deficient cell accumulates cyclin B and inhibitory Tyr15 phosphorylation. Which engineered CDK1 is most likely to bypass this specific restraint?

  1. A kinase-dead CDK1
  2. A cyclin-binding-defective CDK1
  3. A catalytically competent CDK1 lacking the inhibitory phosphoacceptor sites
  4. A CDK1 lacking all activating modifications
Answer and reasoning

C. A catalytically competent CDK1 lacking the inhibitory phosphoacceptor sites

Removing relevant inhibitory acceptor sites can bypass their dephosphorylation requirement if other activation conditions hold. The other variants remove necessary activity or association rather than bypass inhibition.

4. A cyclin B degradation-resistant variant binds CDK1 normally, while APC/C can destroy securin. Predict the primary late-cycle consequence.

  1. No replication origin ever licenses before the first M phase
  2. Anaphase permission may occur but exit is impaired
  3. Securin must remain stable
  4. CDK1 activity must fall normally
Answer and reasoning

B. Anaphase permission may occur but exit is impaired

The variant separates APC/C outputs. Securin can decline while cyclin-dependent activity persists. The remaining options incorrectly equate loss of one substrate with all APC/C function or normal exit.

5. A culture exposed to a mitotic CDK inhibitor has a large 4C peak. Which additional observation distinguishes G2 from mitotic arrest most directly?

  1. Total DNA concentration only
  2. Cell count only
  3. Chromosome condensation and suitable mitotic markers
  4. Total ATP concentration only
Answer and reasoning

C. Chromosome condensation and suitable mitotic markers

Morphology and mitotic markers identify the mitotic state; DNA content, cell number and ATP alone do not discriminate G2 from M.

6. A drug causes loss of p27 from cyclin E–CDK2 complexes without changing cyclin E abundance. Which result supports the direct hypothesis?

  1. Decreased CDK2 activity necessarily
  2. Higher kinase activity with equal recovered CDK2
  3. All APC/C substrates stabilized
  4. CDK1 protein absent
Answer and reasoning

B. Higher kinase activity with equal recovered CDK2

Release of p27 can increase CDK2 catalytic activity. Equal recovered kinase helps distinguish activity from abundance. The other results do not follow from the specified change.

7. An extract has an activation threshold of 80 nM and an inactivation threshold of 35 nM. At 50 nM, which observation supports hysteresis?

  1. Both histories must be inactive
  2. Both histories must be active
  3. The previously active preparation remains high, the previously inactive one remains low
  4. Cyclin concentration must be zero
Answer and reasoning

C. The previously active preparation remains high, the previously inactive one remains low

50 lies between thresholds, so previous state matters. A unique activity for all histories would not establish bistability in this model.

8. In a first-order steady-state model, synthesis is 8 nM/min and degradation constant is 0.1 min⁻¹. What happens if the degradation constant doubles?

  1. Steady-state cyclin rises from 80 to 160 nM
  2. Falls from 80 to 40 nM
  3. Stays 80 nM
  4. Falls to zero instantly
Answer and reasoning

B. Falls from 80 to 40 nM

C*=s/k: 8/0.1=80, then 8/0.2=40 nM. Concentration approaches the new steady state; it does not instantly vanish. The other numerical relationships reverse or ignore the change.

9. A population accumulates cyclin B but kinase activity remains low; an antibody shows increased inhibitory CDK1 phosphorylation. What is justified?

  1. Cdc25 protein is definitely absent
  2. Wee1 is definitely overexpressed
  3. Increased inhibitory phosphorylation is consistent with low activity; the upstream cause needs more evidence
  4. Cyclin B is definitely inactive as a protein
Answer and reasoning

C. Increased inhibitory phosphorylation is consistent with low activity; the upstream cause needs more evidence

Both stronger phosphorylation and weaker dephosphorylation could generate the state. The data do not prove abundance or activity of either regulator individually, nor a cyclin defect.

10. In a licensing model, Cdt1 inhibition fails while firing remains possible. What outcome is a concern?

  1. Repeated licensing and possible rereplication
  2. A mandatory absence of all DNA synthesis
  3. Direct cleavage of cohesin by Cdt1
  4. Loss of CDK1 catalysis in every cell
Answer and reasoning

A. Repeated licensing and possible rereplication

Failure to restrict licensing may enable new origins to fire on copied DNA. The other choices assign unrelated actions to Cdt1 or incorrectly predict an obligatory block.

11. A kinase assay gives 24 nmol product in 6 minutes for sample A and 54 nmol in 6 minutes for B. What is B/A under initial-rate conditions?

  1. 1.5
  2. 2.25
  3. 4
  4. 9
Answer and reasoning

B. 2.25

A=4 and B=9 nmol/min; 9/4=2.25. Four and nine are individual rates, not ratios; 1.5 is not supported by these numbers.

12. Two experiments stabilize cyclin B: one deletes its APC/C recognition motif, the other inhibits the proteasome. Which statement is best?

  1. Both must have identical effects on every protein
  2. The motif deletion can selectively stabilize cyclin, whereas proteasome inhibition affects many substrates
  3. Neither affects protein turnover
  4. Only the proteasome inhibitor can alter cyclin half-life
Answer and reasoning

B. The motif deletion can selectively stabilize cyclin, whereas proteasome inhibition affects many substrates

Substrate recognition and degradation machinery are different layers. Both perturbations can alter cyclin lifetime, but broad proteasome inhibition affects many proteins.

13. A CDK4/6 inhibitor causes G1 delay in Rb-positive cells. In a model where the primary relevant CDK4/6 output is Rb inactivation, what may occur after Rb loss?

  1. The same delay must increase
  2. The delay may be reduced
  3. All cells must stop at anaphase
  4. The inhibitor becomes a cyclin B ligase
Answer and reasoning

B. The delay may be reduced

Removing a downstream restraint can reduce dependence on the upstream kinase in the stated context. Other restraints may still operate; no universal effect or change in drug identity follows.

14. A mutant has normal cyclin–CDK association but no detectable kinase activity. Which test most directly separates failure of activation from catalytic-site damage?

  1. Count chromosomes only
  2. Measure activating/inhibitory phosphorylation and assay purified complex after controlled activation
  3. Measure cyclin mRNA only
  4. Observe cell size only
Answer and reasoning

B. Measure activating/inhibitory phosphorylation and assay purified complex after controlled activation

This tests both modification state and capacity for catalysis. Chromosome count, transcript and size are downstream or indirect measurements and cannot resolve the two hypotheses.
